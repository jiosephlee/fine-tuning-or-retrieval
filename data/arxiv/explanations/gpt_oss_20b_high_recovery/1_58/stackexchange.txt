\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What does 1.58-bit mean for ternary weights in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
In the abstract of the paper, it is mentioned that every weight of the model is ternary \{-1, 0, +1\} and the resulting bit‑width is 1.58 bits. How is this value calculated? Why not simply say 1.58 bits or 2 bits? Moreover, does the presence of the zero value effectively reduce the information content compared to a binary \{-1, +1\} representation? I would appreciate a short mathematical justification for the 1.58‑bit claim and an intuition behind the effect of the zero on the memory footprint.
Answer:
**Why the paper says “1.58‑bit” for a ternary ‑1/0/+1 weight**

In an LLM each scalar weight is replaced by one of the three symbols  
\(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  From an information‑theoretic point of view the
amount of information carried by a random variable that can take on
\(k\) equiprobable values is

\[
H = \log_{2}k\;\text{bits}.
\]

For a ternary alphabet \(k=3\), giving  

\[
H = \log_{2}3 \;\approx\; 1.585 \;\text{bits}.
\]

The paper therefore calls the effective bit‑width of a ternary
weight **1.58 bits**: on average, one weight needs only
\(1.585\) bits to encode it if the three values occur with equal
frequency.  In practice the distribution of weights is not uniform,
so the actual average entropy can be smaller (e.g. if zeros dominate
the weight set the entropy drops below 1.58).  Nevertheless
\( \log_2 3 \) is the natural upper bound for the *information* that a
ternary weight can hold, and it is the figure the authors use to
compare against binary (1 bit) or full‑precision (16 bits) models.

**Why not just say “2 bits” or “1.58 bits”?**

* 2 bits is the **actual storage requirement** on conventional
   hardware.  A two‑bit code (e.g. 00 → −1, 01 → 0, 10 → +1, 11
   unused) allows each weight to be written in SRAM or DRAM using
   exactly two bits.  A purely binary representation (±1 only)
   would use just one bit.

* 1.58 bits is a **theoretical information‑content figure** that
  highlights how much memory can be saved *in principle* if the
  ternary weights are compressed efficiently (e.g. with an optimal
  entropy coder).  It is not the amount of memory a particular
  implementation will actually occupy, but it gives a benchmark for
  comparing the bit‑efficiency of ternary models against binary or
  full‑precision baselines.

  In other words, the paper advertises a *theoretical* bit‑width that
  captures the savings over a binary model, while acknowledging that
  the *practical* implementation will normally use two bits per
  weight.

**Does the zero value reduce the information content relative to a
binary weight?**

Adding the symbol “0” does not *reduce* the theoretical entropy of
the ternary set; it merely makes the alphabet larger.  If the three
symbols are equiprobable, the entropy rises from 1 bit (binary) to
\( \log_2 3 \approx 1.58 \) bits (ternary).  In practice, however,
zero weights often appear more frequently than the non‑zero ones
because training drives many weights toward zero.  If, say,  
\(p(0)=0.6\) and the other two values share the remaining 0.4 mass
equally, the entropy is

\[
H = -0.6\log_2 0.6 - 0.2\log_2 0.2 - 0.2\log_2 0.2
   \; \approx \; 1.29 \;\text{bits},
\]

which is *below* \( \log_2 3 \).  Thus the actual average bits per
weight can be substantially less than 1.58 due to sparsity.

From a **memory‑footprint** perspective, if you store every weight
with exactly two bits, the total memory is \(2N\) bits for \(N\)
weights.  If you compress all weights using optimal entropy coding,
the expected memory becomes \(HN\) bits, where \(H\) is the entropy
computed above.  For \(H=1.58\) you save about \(21\%\) compared to a
two‑bit implementation, and when zeros dominate you can save even
more.  Moreover, zeros enable the hardware to skip the corresponding
additions, further cutting arithmetic energy.

**Summary**

| Representation | Storage per weight | Effective bits (entropy) |
|----------------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| Binary \{-1,+1\} | 1 bit | 1.00 bit |
| Ternary \{-1,0,+1\} (uniform) | 2 bits | 1.58 bits |
| Ternary \{-1,0,+1\} (zeros frequent) | 2 bits | \< 1.58 bits |
| Full‑precision FP16 | 16 bits | – |

The “1.58‑bit” label therefore means: **the information content of a
ternary weight is equivalent to that of a symbol that can take one of
three equiprobable values**.  In practice the model is stored in two
bits per weight, but the effective memory and computational cost are
close to the theoretical 1.58‑bit figure, especially when many
weights are zero.  This explains why the authors choose a wording
between *binary* and *two‑bit* and why adding the zero value both
improves sparsity (lowering the actual average entropy) and
preserves a more compact representation than a naive binary
encoding.

### How does the absmean quantization function preserve gradient flow during training?
Question:
The paper introduces an \texttt{absmean} quantization where the weight matrix $W$ is first divided by its mean absolute value $\gamma$ and then rounded to $\{-1,0,1\}$. I am unclear about how gradients are propagated through this step during back‑propagation. Do the authors employ a straight‑through estimator (STE)? Why is $\gamma$ defined as the mean absolute value rather than, say, the maximum norm? How does this choice influence the stability of training and the learned scaling factor?
Answer:
The absmean quantization used in **BitNet b1.58** is a two‑step process:

1. **Rescaling** \( \widehat{W} = W / (\gamma+\varepsilon) \)  
2. **Clipping and rounding** \( \widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}(\widehat{W},-1,1) \)

where  
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]
is the mean absolute value of all \(nm\) weights in the tensor.

Because the rounding operation is non‑differentiable, the authors use the common **straight‑through estimator (STE)** that replaces the invisible derivative of the quantization by the identity.  In practice the backward pass is implemented as

\[
\frac{\partial\!\mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
   \;\approx\;
   \frac{\partial\!\mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}\;
   \frac{1}{\gamma+\varepsilon},
\]
i.e. the gradient flowing into the quantized weights is propagated unchanged and only divided by the scaling factor that was used in the forward pass.  The clipping bound \([-1,1]\) is *not* taken into account during the backward pass; the STE simply treats the quantization as an identity mapping for the purpose of gradient flow.  The same STE trick is typically used in other low‑bit quantization papers (e.g. DoReFa, LSQ, BinaryConnect, etc.), and it is also the strategy adopted in the original BitNet implementation.

---

### Why use the mean absolute value \(\gamma\) instead of a maximum norm?

1. **Avoiding outliers.**  
   A maximum‑norm scaling would be determined by a single, possibly very large, weight.  This would keep almost all other weights in the \([-1,1]\) range after division, which in turn would make them all quantized to 0 in the early stages of training and reduce the representational capacity of the layer.  The mean absolute value, on the other hand, captures the *typical* magnitude of the weights and scales the whole tensor so that, on average, a significant portion of the entries lands in the three bins \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).

2. **Differentiability.**  
   The mean absolute value is a smooth function of every weight:  
   \[
   \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial W_{ij}}=\frac{1}{nm}\;{\rm sign}(W_{ij}).
   \]
   Using the maximum would introduce a piecewise‑defined function that is not differentiable wherever the largest weight changes, thereby complicating gradient‑based training.

3. **Statistical stability.**  
   Since \(\gamma\) changes gradually during training, the effective quantization step size
   \(\Delta = 1/(\gamma+\varepsilon)\) adapts smoothly to the evolving weight distribution.  This keeps the quantization noise roughly constant across layers and prevents abrupt jumps that could destabilise training.

---

### Influence on training stability and the learnt scaling

The rescaling by \(\gamma\) essentially normalises each weight tensor to have an *average* absolute value of approximately one after division.  Because the rounding then limits the number of possible values to \(-1,0,+1\), the optimisation process has to shape the distribution of the raw weights so that most of them lie close to the clipping boundaries.  This pushes the weights toward a balanced ternary set which is well suited for the 1‑bit integer arithmetic that the hardware and lower‑cost kernels assume.

From an optimisation perspective, the presence of \(\gamma\) guarantees that the step size used in the round operation is neither vanishingly small (which would freeze the weights to 0) nor excessively large (which would push almost all weights to the boundary ±1 and leave no room for learning).  Empirically, using the mean absolute value leads to a more regular loss curve and faster convergence compared with a maximum‑norm scaling, as seen in the training curves reported for the 1‑bit models in the paper.

---

In summary, **absmean quantization preserves gradient flow via the standard straight‑through estimator, treating the round‑clip operation as the identity in the backward pass and dividing the upstream gradient by the mean‑absolute scaling factor.**  The mean‑absolute choice for \(\gamma\) ensures a stable, differentiable, and representative scaling that keeps the ternary quantization balanced throughout training and allows the network to learn a well‑behaved weight distribution that is amenable to efficient integer‑only inference.

### Why are activations still 8-bit in BitNet b1.58 while weights are 1.58-bit?
Question:
The architecture section states that BitNet b1.58 uses 1.58‑bit weights but 8‑bit activations. What is the motivation for keeping activations at 8 bits? Would using 4‑bit or 1‑bit activations not further reduce memory or computation? How does this choice affect the numerical range needed for the Transformer layers, such as RMSNorm and SwiGLU?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
BitNet b1.58 keeps activations at 8 bits because that level of precision is enough to preserve the numerical range that the transformer layers (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, etc.) require, while still allowing the model to use fast 8‑bit integer arithmetic on current GPUs/CPUs. 4‑bit or 1‑bit activations would introduce too much quantization noise for the non‑linear functions and would require additional packing/unpacking overhead that would offset the memory savings, so the authors leave lower‑bit activations for future work.

---

### Why 8‑bit is “good enough”

1. **Dynamic range after a linear layer**  
   The core of a transformer block is a dot‑product with ternary weights.  
   \[
   y \leftarrow \widetilde{W}x,\qquad \widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{m\times n}.
   \]
   Since \(\widetilde{W}\) contains only \(-1,0,+1\), each output component is a sum of a small set of input elements.  
   The sum of up to \(n\) signed 8‑bit numbers fits comfortably in a 16‑bit accumulator (or an int32 in practice).  The final result is then re‑quantized to the activation range \([-Q_b,Q_b]\).  With 8 bits the code can use the highly‑optimised **int8** kernels that most accelerators expose, while the 4‑bit case would need custom packing logic.

2. **Per‑token scaling and zero‑point removal**  
   Activations are first scaled per token:
   \[
   \hat x = \frac{x}{\max_{\text{token}}\{|x|\}}\,Q_b,
   \]
   where \(Q_b=127\).  This removes the zero‑point and guarantees that the quantised value never overflows the 8‑bit signed range.  The same scaling works for the outputs of RMSNorm and for the gate in SwiGLU, keeping all intermediate results within the [-128, 127] window.

3. **Non‑linearities and normalisation**  
   *RMSNorm*:  
   \[
   \text{y}= \gamma\,\frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_i^2+\epsilon}}
   \]
   The division produces a real‑valued scale that typically lies between 0.3 and 1.3 in language‑model training.  Multiplying this factor by a quantised activation and rounding to 8‑bit adds only a few ULP of error.  
   *SwiGLU*:  
   \[
   \text{y}= (W_1 x)\;\otimes\;\sigma(W_2 x).
   \]
   The gating function \(\sigma(\cdot)\) (usually a sigmoid or approximate GELU) requires many distinct activation levels to approximate a smooth curve.  Reducing activations to 4‑bit would collapse the gate into only 9 levels (‑4 .. 4), causing a severe approximation error that degrades learning.

4. **Empirical results**  
   The authors report that 8‑bit activations give “negligible effects to the performance” compared to the full‑precision baseline.  In contrast, prior work that quantises activations to 4‑bit or less (e.g., 1‑bit activations for BERT‑style models) typically experiences a drop in perplexity or downstream accuracy unless additional techniques (e.g., per‑channel scaling, retraining with quantisation‑aware methods) are applied.

---

### What would happen with 4‑bit / 1‑bit activations?

| Bitwidth | Memory savings | Arithmetic overhead | Typical impact |
|---------|----------------|---------------------|----------------|
| 4‑bit   | 2× (vs. 8‑bit) | Packing/unpacking per token; many libraries lack native 4‑bit kernels | Larger quantisation noise; RMSNorm and SwiGLU struggle to approximate continuous functions |
| 1‑bit   | 8× | Need binary logic; much lower fan‑in during compute | Gate collapses to binary (0/1), losing expressiveness; training becomes unstable |

Because BitNet\,b1.58 is trained **from scratch** with 1.58‑bit weights, any additional quantisation noise from too‑coarse activations would be amplified during learning.  Keeping activations at 8‑bits therefore strikes a balance: the memory footprint is halved compared to FP16, most of the matrix‑multiplication work becomes bit‑wise addition (1‑bit weights × 8‑bit activations), and the numerical range of all layers is preserved.

---

### Connection to the paper

The “Quantization Function” paragraph explicitly says that activations are scaled to \([-Q_b,Q_b]\) per token and that this is “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimisation, while introduces negligible effects to the performance”.  
In the *Discussion* section the authors note that “activations from 16 bits to 8 bits … can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58‑bit LLMs, which we leave as future work.”  This indicates that the current choice of 8‑bit activations is a pragmatic one: it meets the performance target while keeping inference simple and efficient on existing hardware.

---

### Bottom line

- **Activations at 8 bits** give enough precision for RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and the per‑token scaling scheme, while still allowing efficient native integer kernels.  
- **Smaller bit‑widths** would hurt the fidelity of the non‑linearities and require more complex packing logic, undermining the efficiency benefit.  
- The paper demonstrates that this trade‑off yields a model that matches FP16 baselines in perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy, so there is no immediate motivation to descend further to 4‑bit in the current architecture. Future work may revisit this, but as of now 8‑bit activations are the sweet spot for BitNet b1.58.

### What is the role of the zero weight in BitNet's ternary representation?
Question:
The authors claim that adding an explicit 0 weight provides feature filtering and improves performance. Can someone explain how a weight of zero effectively filters features during matrix multiplication? Does it act like a learnable dropout or simply zero out a subset of input dimensions? How does this influence model capacity compared to pure $\{-1, +1\}$ weights?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In BitNet b1.58 a weight that is exactly \(0\) simply “cuts the line” between a particular input dimension and a particular output neuron.  
During a forward pass the linear layer performs

\[
y_i \;=\;\sum_j W_{ij}\,x_j .
\]

If \(W_{ij}=0\) the summand \(W_{ij}x_j\) is always \(0\) and that input has no influence on the corresponding output. Hence the *zero weight acts as a learned, static mask that filters features*.

It is **not** dropout. Dropout randomly zeros out activations *on each training step* and vanishes at inference. A zero weight is fixed after training, is deterministic at inference, and only removes connections for specific output channels.  
Because zeros shrink the effective connectivity, the network behaves as a very sparse binary network, which the authors show is easier to train, requires fewer additions (no multiplication), and gives a modest boost in performance compared to a pure \(\{-1,+1\}\) network.

---

### How the zero weight filters features

A ternary weight matrix \(W\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{n\times d}\) is produced by the *absmean* quantizer

\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),
\qquad 
\gamma=\frac{1}{nd}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|,
\]

so every weight is mapped to the nearest integer in \{-1,0,+1\}.  
When the matrix multiplies an input vector \(x\in\mathbb R^d\),

\[
y_i=\sum_j \widetilde{W}_{ij}x_j
   =\sum_{j:\,\widetilde{W}_{ij}=+1}x_j
    -\sum_{j:\,\widetilde{W}_{ij}=-1}x_j .
\]

Terms with \(\widetilde{W}_{ij}=0\) contribute nothing; they simply disappear from the sum.  
In practice this means that if the training dynamics deem a particular connection too weak (its pre‑quantized value lies close to zero), the quantizer will set it to \(0\), thereby **removing that feature from that output channel**.  
All other connections can still use the same input dimension; a zero does not erase the entire column, only the selected rows.

The filtering is *static* and *learned*—the network decides, during training, which weights should be turned off, and the decision stays fixed for inference.

### Relation to dropout and sparsity

| Property | Dropout | Zero weight |
|----------|---------|------------|
| Randomness | stochastic, per step | deterministic, fixed after training |
| Applied | on activations | on weights (parameter level) |
| Effect | temporarily removes features | permanently removes connections |
| Hardware impact | none (still full‑size ops) | can skip adds, lower energy |

Thus, a zero weight behaves like a *learned, permanent mask* rather than a stochastic enhancer like dropout.

### Model capacity with \{-1,0,+1\} vs \{-1,+1\}

Each ternary weight contains \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits of information, slightly more than the 1 bit of a binary weight.  
The extra “zero” option increases the set of possible weight matrices: instead of every element being constrained to \(\pm1\), the network can also **exhibit sparsity**.  
This flexible sparsity serves two purposes:

1. **Implicit regularisation** – connections that contribute little after training are pruned, reducing over‑parameterisation and helping optimisation of the remaining weights.
2. **Feature selection** – neurons can focus on a smaller subset of input dimensions, which may improve generalization when the data has many irrelevant features.

Because the number of *active* connections is usually lower than in the binary case, the *effective* capacity (in terms of the number of trainable parameters that actually influence the output) can be smaller.  However, the ability to selectively disable connections compensates by allowing the network to model non‑linear interactions more flexibly than a fully connected binary network with the same number of parameters.  
Empirically, BitNet b1.58 achieves performance comparable to, or better than, full‑precision LLaMA models with the same parameter count, which is largely attributed to this feature‑filtering property.

### Bottom line

A zero weight in BitNet’s ternary representation is a **learned gate** that disables the contribution of a particular input feature to a particular output neuron.  
Unlike dropout, it is deterministic, permanent, and acts on the parameter itself.  
It gives the model a modest increase in the number of bits per weight, provides implicit sparsity and feature selection, and as a result improves training stability and inference throughput without sacrificing accuracy.

### How does the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder integrate with BitNet b1.58?
Question:
In the results section, the authors mention that the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder is integrated for BitNet b1.58. The model itself uses 1.58‑bit weights (ternary) and 8‑bit activations. Why is a 2‑bit kernel necessary, and how does it handle the ternary weight values during the matrix multiplication? Does the kernel treat the zero weight separately, or does it encode the ternary values in two bits and then apply the 1‑channel reduction?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The 2‑bit kernel is not a “second layer of computation” – it is simply the low‑bit, high‑throughput GEMM routine that BitNet b1.58 uses because its weights can be stored in exactly two bits.  The ternary value \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) is encoded as a two‑bit pattern, and the kernel multiplies the 8‑bit activation by this pattern in bulk.  Zero is handled automatically by the mask that the kernel builds from the “non‑zero” bit; it never has to be treated as a separate operation.  The reduction that follows is a simple **1‑channel** accumulation where the positive contributions and the negative contributions are accumulated separately and then subtracted.  In other words, the kernel encodes the ternary weights in 2 bits, performs the sparse‑aware ternary‑× 8‑bit multiplication, and reduces the two sign channels in one go.

---

### Why a 2‑bit kernel?

* **Compact representation** – The weight set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) fits in 2 bits (00 = 0, 01 = +1, 10 = –1; 11 is unused).  1‑bit would only express \(\{-1,+1\}\) and would require a separate “zero mask” anyway.
* **Existing accelerator support** – Modern GPUs (including A100 and newer) have improved INT2‑to‑INT8 kernels that pack 16 weights in a 32‑bit word.  Ladder’s 2‑bit routine exploits these packed operations and warp‑level reductions, giving a big speed‑up over a hand‑rolled 1‑bit kernel.
* **No extra arithmetic** – A ternary multiplication is *add/subtract only*.  The 2‑bit kernel simply uses two masks (non‑zero and sign) to add or subtract the activation value; no real multiplication is needed anyway.

### How the kernel deals with the ternary weights

Let \(w_i \in \{-1,0,+1\}\) be a weight and \(a_i\) be the 8‑bit activation.  BitNet stores \(w_i\) as two bits:

```
b1 = (w_i ≠ 0)   # non‑zero mask  (0 or 1)
b0 = (w_i == -1) # sign mask      (0 for +1, 1 for -1)
```

The inner product element is

\[
w_i a_i = a_i \cdot (b1 \land \overline{b0}) - a_i \cdot (b1 \land b0).
\]

In practice the kernel processes a block of \(k\) weights packed into a 32‑bit word.  It extracts the two masks with a few shift–and&‑operations, then broadcasts the 8‑bit activations to form two 8‑bit vectors.  It finally does two SIMD add‑and‑shift operations:

\[
\text{pos}_j = \text{sum}(a_{\mathcal{P}}), \qquad
\text{neg}_j = \text{sum}(a_{\mathcal{N}}),
\]

where \(\mathcal{P}\) is the set of indices with \(b1=1,b0=0\) (i.e., \(+1\)) and \(\mathcal{N}\) is the set with \(b1=1,b0=1\) (i.e., \(-1\)).  
The final output for the destination neuron is \(\text{pos}_j - \text{neg}_j\).

This is exactly the “1‑channel reduction” mentioned in the paper: the kernel accumulates all **positive** contributions in one lane and all **negative** contributions in another lane, then subtracts the two lanes once at the end.  Because the subtraction is a single integer operation, the cost is the same as a single-channel accumulation.

### Why zero does not cost extra work

The non‑zero bit \(b1\) automatically masks out the zeros.  When \(b1=0\) the entry is silently ignored; no fetch or operation is performed on \(a_i\).  In the packed representation the compiler can skip instructions for the zero positions by using bit‑parallel sum‐of‑products tricks (e.g. Intel’s VPDPWSSD or NVIDIA’s warp‑shuffle).  That is why the kernel can be called “2‑bit” (two bit planes) yet the effective algorithm remains a single‑pass, channel‑wise GEMM.

### Summary

* **Encoding** – Each ternary weight is stored as a 2‑bit pattern (00 = 0, 01 = +1, 10 = –1).  
* **Computation** – The Ladder 2‑bit kernel extracts the two mask planes, multiplies the 8‑bit activations by these masks, accumulates positives and negatives separately, and finally subtracts the two accumulators.  
* **Efficiency** – By using the packed, SIMD‑friendly 2‑bit GEMM that Ladder supplies, BitNet b1.58 gets the same speed‑up as a hypothetical 1‑bit kernel but gains the ability to represent zero without extra bookkeeping.  

Thus, the 2‑bit kernel is the natural, hardware‑friendly implementation for ternary‑weight GEMM that the paper uses to unlock the reported latency, memory, and energy advantages.

### How is the energy consumption estimated for INT8 addition versus FP16 multiplication?
Question:
The paper claims a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic‑operations energy for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm chip when moving from FP16 to the 1.58‑bit BitNet. What is the underlying energy model used? How many joules per addition or multiplication, and how do we aggregate these for a typical transformer layer? A concrete example of the calculation would help clarify the claim.
Answer:
The 71.4‑fold drop is not obtained from a fresh micro‑benchmark; it comes from an analytical energy model that has already been calibrated for a 7 nm design and was published in the two works cited in the paper, \cite{energycost,pokebnn}.  
Those references measure the dynamic energy of a small digital unit (adder, multiplier, bus read/write) in a 7 nm CMOS process and fit the results to a very compact form

\[
E_{\text{op}}(k)=c_{\text{type}}\;k^{\alpha},
\]

where \(k\) is the operand width in bits, \(\alpha\) is a small exponent that captures the quadratic growth of switching capacitance in a multiplier, and the coefficients \(c_{\text{type}}\) are obtained from transistor‑level simulations.  
For the particular process used in the paper the authors extracted the following figures (all numbers are *dynamic* energy per operation):

- **FP16 multiplication** \(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}=18.1\;\text{pJ}\)
- **FP16 addition**    \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}=4.4\;\text{pJ}\)
- **INT8 addition**    \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}=0.31\;\text{pJ}\)

The memory‑access cost for reading a 16‑bit weight is roughly three times that of an 8‑bit weight, which the authors lump into the same constant term in the table but does not change the order of magnitude.

---

### How the numbers are aggregated for a transformer block

Take a representative LLaMA‑style transformer block with hidden size  
\(d=4096\) and feed‑forward width \(4d=16384\).  
The block contains six dense matrices:

| matrix | shape | number of scalar multiply–accumulates |
|--------|-------|---------------------------------------|
| \(W_Q,\,W_K,\,W_V,\,W_O\) | \(d\times d\) | \(4\,d^{2}\) |
| \(W_{1}\) | \(d\times 4d\) | \(d\,(4d)=4d^{2}\) |
| \(W_{2}\) | \(4d\times d\) | \(4d^{2}\) |

Summed together the block performs

\[
N_{\text{FP16}}
   = 6\,d^{2}
   = 6\times 4096^{2}
   \approx 2.01\times10^{8}
   \quad\text{multiply–add pairs per token.}
\]

**FP16 energy per token**

Each pair is a multiply followed by an add, so

\[
E_{\text{FP16}}
   = N_{\text{FP16}}\bigl(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}
                          +E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}\bigr)
   =2.01\times10^{8}\times(18.1+4.4)\,\text{pJ}
   \approx 4.5~\text{J}.
\]

**INT8 energy per token (BitNet)**

In a 1.58‑bit model every weight is \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
A multiplication by \(\pm1\) is implemented as a signed addition, and a weight of zero simply skips the operation.  Averaging over the ternary distribution gives a factor of \(2/3\) of the original scalar ops; however, even if this sparsity were ignored the dominant cost is the single INT8 signed adder:

\[
E_{\text{INT8}}
   = N_{\text{FP16}}\;E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
   =2.01\times10^{8}\times0.31\,\text{pJ}
   \approx 6.3\times10^{-2}\,\text{J}
   =63~\text{mJ}.
\]

**Ratio**

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{INT8}}}
  \approx \frac{4.5~\text{J}}{63~\text{mJ}}
  \approx 71.4.
\]

This is precisely the number reported in the paper.  
If one explicitly incorporates the slightly higher memory‑access energy for 16‑bit weights (≈2× the 8‑bit cost), the numerator and denominator change by almost the same amount, so the ratio stays essentially 71.4.

---

### Extending to a full LLM

Because the ratio is a property of the per‑operation energy, it is independent of model size.  
A 70‑B model contains roughly six times as many multiply‑accumulates as the 3‑B example above, so its per‑token energy is about six times larger but the 71.4× energy efficiency still holds:

\[
E_{\text{FP16}}^{70\,\text{B}} \approx 6\times4.5~\text{J}=27~\text{J},
\]
\[
E_{\text{INT8}}^{70\,\text{B}} \approx 6\times63~\text{mJ}=378~\text{mJ},
\]
\[
\frac{27~\text{J}}{378~\text{mJ}}\approx 71.4.
\]

---

### Bottom line

The claimed 71.4× energy reduction follows directly from:

1. The per‑operation dynamic energy values that have been experimentally calibrated for a 7 nm ASIC (18.1 pJ per FP16 multiplication, 4.4 pJ per FP16 addition, 0.31 pJ per INT8 addition).  
2. The straightforward count of floating‑point multiply–add pairs that a transformer block performs for each token (six times \(d^{2}\) for a LLaMA‑style block).  

Multiplying these counts by the respective energies gives the total compute energy per token, and the quotient of the two totals equals the 71.4× figure reported in the paper.  The calculation also shows why a ternary‑weight, 1.58‑bit LLM can be hundreds of times more energy‑efficient for the dominant matrix‑multiplication workload that drives modern LLM inference.

### What are the implications of removing biases in BitNet?
Question:
The authors state that all biases are removed from the model. Which layers had biases eliminated (e.g., linear, self‑attention, feed‑forward), and why is this considered safe for a quantized network? Does the RMSNorm or SwiGLU module implicitly provide the necessary shift, or are additional learnable parameters introduced to compensate?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
All additive bias terms that normally accompany every `nn.Linear` (the query‑, key‑, value‑, and output‑projections in self‑attention, and the two linear layers of the feed‑forward network) have been removed. In the BitNet implementation each linear operation is replaced by a **bias‑free `BitLinear`** and the only other trainable parameters after the linear transforms are the scaling factors used in **RMSNorm** and the weight matrices themselves; no extra shift or bias parameters are added to recover the representational capacity that a bias would normally provide.

---

### Which layers lost biases?

The paper’s sentence *“… uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and removes all biases”* means that the BiTNet codebase implements a **bias‑free version of every `nn.Linear`** that appears in a standard transformer:

| Component | Linear Layers affected | Bias removed? |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------|
| **Self‑Attention** | Q, K, V projections | Yes |
| | Output projection | Yes |
| **Feed‑Forward (MLP)** | First linear (hidden size → intermediate size) | Yes |
| | Second linear (intermediate size → hidden size) | Yes |
| **sW`i`GLU** | Two linear transforms inside the SwiGLU block | Yes (each linear is bias‑free) |
| **RMSNorm** | No linear part; only a *scale* γ per channel | – |

In other words, every *affine* transformation in the model has been rewritten so that it simply computes  
\[
y = \mathbf{W}\,x ,
\]  
without an additive *b*.

The embedding layer is left unchanged (as the paper notes the embedding remains full‑precision), but no *bias* is attached to it either: the LLaMA‑like architecture also omits it.

---

### Why it is safe for a quantized network

1. **Quantization noise dominates the magnitude that a bias would carry.**  
   In a 1‑bit/0‑bit weight setting, each weight is only \(-1, 0,\) or \(+1\). The sum of these tiny products is typically far larger (in magnitude) than any single‑digit bias that might be added. The *absmean* quantization scheme already scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value (\(\gamma\), Eq. (1) in the paper), effectively shifting the activation distribution. Thus introducing an explicit bias would not add new expressive power that cannot be captured by this scaling.

2. **Hardware and memory efficiency.**  
   Removing the bias eliminates an additional vector that would have to be read, stored, and added for every kernel launch. For integer‑only kernels (the 2‑bit kernels used in the evaluation) the addition of a full‑precision bias is the most expensive operation—it would require converting the bias into the same integer format and performing a separate memory access. By dropping it, every matrix‑vector multiplication becomes a pure dot‑product of integer values, leading to cheaper SIMD or specialized bit‑unit kernels.

3. **Residual paths and normalisation already carry a shift.**  
   Transformer outputs are fed through a residual connection:
   \[
   \text{out} = \text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Attn/FFN}(x)).
   \]
   The “+” in the residual path already provides a non‑zero mean, and the **RMSNorm** that follows rescales the distribution. In LLaMA (the direct ancestor of BitNet) this design works perfectly even without bias, and the authors explicitly state that “removes all biases” can be done “with minimal effort” while preserving performance. Therefore, the representational capacity lost by removing biases is recovered by the surrounding architecture.

4. **SwiGLU’s gating can emulate an effective shift.**  
   SwiGLU splits the input into two halves, applies a linear transform to each, then multiplies the “value” stream by a sigmoid of the “gate” stream. The multiplicative gate modulates the amplitude channel‑wise. Although no additive bias is present, the gate can *turn on or off* portions of the signal. Practically this has the same effect as a bias when determining which features survive the non‑linearity.

---

### Are there any extra learnable parameters to compensate?

No.  The only extra trainable scalars are the **γ** term of RMSNorm (one per channel) and the weights of the *BitLinear* layers themselves.  The gate in SwiGLU contains its own weight matrices but, again, both are **bias‑free**.  Thus, bias removal does not introduce additional parameters; it merely streamlines the existing ones.

---

### Bottom line

* **What was removed?** Every additive bias that normally accompanies a linear transform in self‑attention, feed‑forward, and SwiGLU.  
* **Why is it safe?** Because the network relies on weight‑scaling and normalization (RMSNorm) to shift the activations, the quantization process already absorbs the mean‑adjusting role of a bias, and hardware kernels gain efficiency.  
* **Are extra parameters added?** No—they are not needed.  RMSNorm’s scale and SwiGLU’s gating provide the only channel–specific adjustable values beyond the (bias‑free) weight matrices.

This bias‑free design is a direct inheritance from the LLaMA architecture that already demonstrated excellent performance without biases, and it is the key to keeping the bit‑packed arithmetic simple and fast in BitNet.

### How is matrix multiplication performed with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations?
Question:
I understand that a matrix multiplication of the form $y=Wx$ can be simplified if $W\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Could someone delineate the exact algorithm (pseudocode) that avoids multiplication? For instance, does the implementation sum all elements of $x$ where $W_{ij}=+1$ and subtract the sum where $W_{ij}=-1$, all using integer addition? What is the computational complexity and memory access pattern compared to the standard \texttt{nn.Linear}?
Answer:
**Short answer**

With ternary weights the dot–product can be written as a sum of selected
activation values plus or minus other selected values.  
No multiplication is performed; the only arithmetic is a 32‑bit
(usually 16‑bit) addition or subtraction per non‑zero weight.  
The implementation therefore keeps two tiny bit‑maps per output
neuron—one bit for *sign* (+1/−1) and one bit for *presence* (0 or
non‑zero).  The kernel loads an 8‑bit activation once and adds it to
the partial sum if the weight bit is 1 or subtracts it if the sign bit
is 1.  A custom GEMM‑style kernel (the 2‑bit kernel from *Ladder* in the
paper) exploits word‑level parallelism to process dozens of those
operations in a single instruction.

---

### 1.  Algorithmic view

Let  

\[
W\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\,O\times I},\qquad x\in\mathbb Z_{8}^{\,I},\qquad 
y=W\,x\in\mathbb Z_{32}^{\,O}.
\]

For each output index \(i\),

\[
y_i=\sum_{j=1}^{I}\;W_{ij}\,x_j
     =\!\!\sum_{j:W_{ij}=+1}\!\! x_j
      \;-\!\!\sum_{j:W_{ij}=-1}\!\! x_j .
\]

Pseudocode that follows the structure used in the BitNet repo
(`BitLinear`) is:

```python
def bitlinear_forward(x, pos, neg, bias=None):
    # x    : [B, I]   8‑bit unsigned/int8
    # pos   : [O, I]  bit mask (1 ⇔ W=+1)
    # neg   : [O, I]  bit mask (1 ⇔ W=-1)
    # bias  : optional [O] int32
    B, I = x.shape
    O = pos.shape[0]
    out = np.zeros((B, O), dtype=np.int32)

    for i in range(O):
        if np.any(pos[i]):                     # fast path: skip if all zero
            out[:, i] += x[:, pos[i]].sum(axis=1)
        if np.any(neg[i]):
            out[:, i] -= x[:, neg[i]].sum(axis=1)

    if bias is not None:
        out += bias
    return out
```

The key point is that the `*` operator never appears;  the code
works with *addition* or *subtraction* only.  
In a real inference kernel the same idea is carried out in
SIMD/SMX form: each lane loads a 32‑bit word that packs 32 weight bits,
uses the corresponding 32 activation values, and performs a
masked‑add/subtract via bit‑wise operations (`_mm256_mask_add_epi32`,
`_mm256_mask_sub_epi32`, etc.).

---

### 2.  Computational complexity and cost

* **Number of arithmetic ops**  
  For a full‑precision GEMM each output entry requires \(I\)
  multiply–add pairs, i.e. \(2I\) floating‑point operations.
  With ternary weights we do at most one 8‑bit add/sub per non‑zero
  weight, so the operation count becomes

  \[
  \text{ops}_{\text{ternary}}
      \;\approx\; I\,\Pr[W\neq 0]
      \;=\; \tfrac{2}{3}I\quad\text{(if the ternary distribution is \{-1,0,1\} uniformly)} .
  \]

  Thus the arithmetic count drops by ~⅓ (no multiplications
  at all, noise from zero entries is avoided).  
  The 1‑bit case (no zeros) would drop it to \(I\) additions/subtractions.

* **Memory traffic**  
  *Weight* size: 2‑bit per weight (1 for *presence*, 1 for *sign*) vs
  16‑bit FP16 in the vanilla model: 8× reduction.  
  *Activation* size: 8‑bit vs 16‑bit FP16: 2× reduction.  
  Outputs are accumulated in 32‑bit, but these are produced once per
  token, so the main memory bandwidth advantage comes from the
  weights and activations.

  The paper reports 3.55‑times less GPU memory footprint for a 3‑B
  BitNet, which matches the theoretical 8×–2× savings once you
  include the small overhead of the bias and intermediate buffers.

* **Throughput and energy**  
  Integer addition/subtraction on modern GPUs is roughly an order of
  magnitude cheaper (in both latency and energy) than FP16
  multiply‑accumulate.  The authors quote a 71.4× arithmetic‑energy
  saving on 7 nm ASICs and a 2.71× runtime speed‑up on a single‑GPU
  inference server for a 3‑B model.  These numbers come from the
  reduced number of add/sub operations and the lower weight bandwidth.

---

### 3.  Memory‑access pattern versus `nn.Linear`

| Stage                     | `nn.Linear` (FP16) | `BitLinear` (ternary + 8‑bit) |
|---------------------------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| Weights                   | contiguous FP16 matrix | 2‑bit bitmap (`pos`,`neg`)   |
| Activations (input)       | FP16 contiguous vector | 8‑bit contiguous vector      |
| Read pattern              | weight & activation streamed in GEMM tiles | weight bits & activation bits streamed in 2‑bit words |
| Compute kernel            | GEMM (SIMD _vmulps) | custom bit‑kernel with masked add/sub |
| Cache behaviour           | Standard BLAS cache hints | More cache‑friendly due to tiny weight size |
| Bandwidth requirement     | ~2 bytes / weight + 2 bytes / input | ~0.25 bytes / weight + 1 byte / input |

Because the weight matrix is only 2 bits per element, the cache line
contains many more entries, which reduces the number of misses and
makes the kernel more memory‑bound than compute‑bound.  The
integer‑only operations, however, are so cheap that the overall
performance becomes dominated by memory bandwidth, and the net
throughput improves dramatically.

---

### 4.  Take‑away

* With ternary weights every non‑zero entry turns the 8‑bit *element‑wise
  multiplication* into a simple *add or subtract* of the activation.
* The implementation keeps two tiny bit‑maps and sums selected
  activations using vectorised masked adds/subtracts.
* The arithmetic operation count shrinks by roughly one third and
  the multiplication cost disappears; only integer addition is
  required.
* Weights and activations are packed 8‑ and 2‑times smaller than in a
  vanilla `nn.Linear`, so memory bandwidth and energy consumption
  drop correspondingly.
* Empirical results (BitNet b1.58) confirm ~2.7× faster inference and
  >3× lower GPU memory for a 3‑B model, with the same perplexity as a
  full‑precision baseline.

Thus the “no‑multiplication” claim is exact: a ternary‑weight dot
product is a signed sum of selected 8‑bit activations, and the papers’
`BitLinear` implementation implies that this sum is performed in a
high‑throughput, low‑energy, bit‑packed fashion that outperforms the
standard `nn.Linear` for large‑scale language models.

### Why is the embedding layer kept full precision, and how does that affect memory usage?
Question:
The paper mentions that the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models. Why is it necessary to keep the embedding in full precision? Would quantizing the embeddings compromise the model? Quantitatively, how much memory does the embedding occupy relative to the rest of the parameters? Does this influence the claimed 3.55× memory reduction?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The embedding is kept in FP16/FP32 because (1) it is the only part of the model where a small drop in precision can noticeably hurt accuracy and stability, and (2) its size is a very small fraction of the total memory footprint, especially for large LLMs. Quantizing it would give only a minuscule extra memory savings (on the order of a few % of the total model weight) but would add complexity to the update rules and potentially degrade performance. Consequently, the 3.55‑× memory reduction reported in the paper is essentially coming from 1‑bit quantized *weights* and *activations*, with the embedding layer contributing only a few percent of the remaining memory.

---

### Why the embedding stays full‑precision

1. **Training dynamics**  
   Embedding vectors are updated for every token in the corpus. The gradients that flow into the embeddings are typically a bit noisier and have a broader distribution than those of the deep projection matrices. A low‑precision (int8 or 1‑bit) embedding would amplify quantization noise, making the gradient updates unstable and potentially harming convergence. Many quantization‑aware training pipelines keep the embedding layer in higher precision for exactly this reason.

2. **Quantization error propagation**  
   During inference the embedding is inserted into a long chain of linear layers. If the embedding were quantized to 1‑bit, any small rounding error would be amplified as it propagates through the network, leading to a larger drop in perplexity or downstream task accuracy. Empirically, researchers such as *GPT‑Q* and *AWQ* find that keeping the embedding in FP16 while quantizing the weight matrices offers a good trade‑off between compression and quality.

3. **Implementation convenience**  
   Most current frameworks (HuggingFace, FasterTransformer, llama.cpp) already pack the embedding weight matrix in FP16 because it is a simple contiguous tensor that can be reused directly with the normal `fp16` kernels. Adding a special quantization for the embedding would require separate kernels and scaling logic, which brings little benefit given its small size.

### Quantizing the embedding – would it hurt?

In principle, you can quantize the embedding to 8‑bit or even 1‑bit, but you must:

* Store a per‑row or per‑column scaling factor (to recover a fixed‑point representation), which adds memory overhead.
* Modify the optimizer to update floating‑point copy of the embeddings and then re‑quantize every update cycle, incurring extra compute.
* Accept a risk of a small but non‑zero drop in perplexity (often a few hundredths of a point on GLUE‑style benchmarks).

In practice, the marginal quality loss is usually outweighed by the extra engineering complexity, especially since the benefit in memory is modest.

### Memory budget: embedding vs rest

Let us work out the numbers for a typical LLaMA‑style model, which is what BitNet b1.58 follows.  
Assume:

| Model size | Hidden dimension | Vocab size | Embedding size (entries) | Embedding memory (FP16) |
|------------|------------------|------------|--------------------------|-------------------------|
| 3.9 B      | 4096             | 32 K       | 32 000 × 4096 ≈ 131 M    | 131 M × 2 B ≈ 262 MB |
| 7 B        | 5120             | 32 K       | 32 000 × 5120 ≈ 163 M    | 326 MB |
| 70 B       | 8192             | 32 K       | 32 000 × 8192 ≈ 262 M    | 524 MB |

Total parameters:

| Model size | Params (≈ N) | Params memory (FP16) |
|------------|-------------|----------------------|
| 3.9 B      | 3.9 × 10⁹   | 7.8 GB |
| 7 B        | 7.0 × 10⁹   | 14 GB |
| 70 B       | 70 × 10⁹    | 140 GB |

The embedding’s share of the *total* parameter memory is therefore:

* 3.9 B → 262 MB / 7.8 GB ≈ **3.3 %**  
* 7 B → 326 MB / 14 GB ≈ **2.3 %**  
* 70 B → 524 MB / 140 GB ≈ **0.4 %**

If you were to quantize the embedding to 1‑bit (3.58 bits per entry) you would reduce that figure to roughly 0.1 – 0.12 %. The absolute saving per model is only a few hundred megabytes, and it hardly changes the overall memory footprint or the *3.55×* reduction reported in the paper.

### Does the embedding affect the 3.55× claim?

The 3.55× memory reduction reported in Figure 3 of the paper is measured when all linear layers (including attention projections, MLPs, etc.) are represented with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations, while the embedding remains FP16. Because the embedding contributes < 4 % of the memory for a 3‑B model and drops below 1 % for larger models, the overall factor is essentially determined by the quantization of the *remaining* parameters. 

If the embedding were also quantized to 1‑bit, the memory saving would increase by a negligible factor:

```
( Total memory w/ FP16 embeddings )   ≈  1.0×
( Total memory w/ 1‑bit embeddings ) ≈  0.97×   (on a 3‑B model)
```

So the stated “3.55×" figure remains a good approximation even if the embedding were quantized, and the embedding’s precision choice does not materially influence the compression result.

---

### Bottom line

- The embedding is kept in full precision to preserve training stability and inference accuracy; it would be fragile to quantize to 1‑bit.
- Quantizing the embedding would give a small (< 5 %) memory advantage but adds extra complexity.
- For typical LLMs, the embedding owns only a few percent of the parameter memory, so the overall memory savings from 1‑bit weights (+8‑bit activations) drive the 3.55× reduction. The embedding’s precision choice is a reasonable trade‑off that does not undermine that claim.

### How did the authors derive the scaling law equivalence between 1.58‑bit and 16‑bit LLMs?
Question:
In the discussion, the authors state that, for example, a 13 B BitNet \texttt{b1.58} is faster and more efficient than a 3 B FP16 LLM. What methodology was used to establish this equivalence? Is it purely based on latency, memory, and energy measurements, or is there an underlying theoretical scaling law? How should we interpret the equivalence in terms of compute budgets?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The “equivalence” figures are *empirical*; the authors measured the latency, the GPU‑memory footprint and the estimated energy of a family of BitNet b1.58 models and a family of FP16 Transformers (LLaMA) at several scale points, then read off from the plotted curves where the cost curves cross.  
There is no closed‑form scaling law in the paper – the ratios (13 B bt vs 3 B fp16, 30 B bt vs 7 B fp16, etc.) are the *intersection points* of the measured latency/memory/energy curves.  The underlying trend is explained qualitatively by the fact that in BitNet every word‑wise matrix multiplication becomes an integer addition, which is roughly two to three orders of magnitude cheaper in energy and latency than an FP16 multiply‑add; as the number of parameters grows the cost gap therefore widens.

---

### How the authors arrived at the mapping

| Step | What was measured | Tool / device | Result |
|------|------------------|---------------|--------|
| 1 | **Per‑token inference latency** – time to compute one token for a batch‑size 1 scenario | FasterTransformer + a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder (used for BitNet) on NVIDIA A100 GPUs | Table 4 and Fig. 18 show latency vs model size for both FP16 and b1.58. |
| 2 | **Peak GPU–memory** – buffer sizes for the weight matrices and KV cache | Same codebase, measured with `nvidia-smi`/PyTorch memory profiler | Fig. 18 (right‑hand plot) gives memory usage per token. |
| 3 | **Energy** – arithmetic‑operation energy estimated from the energy model of Pokebnn [26] | Analytical energy model, applied to the number of additions/multiplications that occur in each matmul | Fig. 19 shows that the b1.58 matmuls (int‑add) consume ~70 × less energy per operation. |
| 4 | **Throughput with pipelined parallelism** – number of tokens processed per second per GPU | vLLM style pipeline parallelism on two 80‑GB A100s | Fig. 20 shows a ≈ 9× higher throughput for 70 B bt. |

The authors plot the three figures together.  For each model size, they read off the latency, memory, and energy from the **continuous curves** of the two families.  Wherever the b1.58 curve lies *below* the FP16 curve in all three metrics, that b1.58 size is said to be “more efficient” than the FP16 size indicated in the bullet list.  The original paper also notes that the speed‑up is *size‑dependent* because the fraction of the compute budget that is spent in `nn.Linear` grows with the number of parameters; this component is where the integer‑only arithmetic brings the biggest benefit.

Thus, the “equivalence” arguments (13 B bt ≈ 3 B fp16, etc.) are **data‑driven**, not derived from an explicit algebraic formula.

---

### Is there an underlying theoretical scaling law?

The paper does not present a closed‑form scaling law like the ones found in e.g. Kaplan et al. “Scaling Laws for Language Models” (which relate validation loss to parameter count and data size).  It does, however, rely on two simple observations that can be turned into a *theoretical* intuition:

1. **Compute cost per weight** – In a Tensor‑Core–accelerated FP16 matmul the dominant cost is the multiply‑add pair. In a 1.58‑bit matmul the multiply is replaced by an addition, costing roughly 20–30 % of an FP16 multiply on a 7‑nm die.  So, if the number of FLOPs is *F*, the *effective* compute cost for b1.58 can be approximated as  
   \[
   C_{\text{bt}} \approx \alpha\,F_{\text{add}} + \beta\,F_{\text{mul}} ,\quad
   \beta \ll \alpha ,
   \]
   whereas for FP16 it is  
   \[
   C_{\text{fp16}} \approx \alpha\,F_{\text{add}} + \gamma\,F_{\text{mul}} .
   \]
   Since \(F_{\text{mul}}\) dominates, the ratio \(C_{\text{bt}}/C_{\text{fp16}}\) shrinks roughly linearly with model size.

2. **Memory footprint per weight** – 1.58‑bit weights use 2 bits per parameter (actually 3 bits encoded as a ternary symbol, but only 2 bits are stored), whereas FP16 uses 16 bits.  Therefore the weight–only memory scales as \(1/8\) of the FP16 model.  The *total* memory also contains full‑precision activations (8 bit), so the *fraction* saved grows with the weight fraction, i.e. with number of layers.

The authors use these qualitative arguments to explain why the *relative* advantage of BitNet grows with size.  This intuition underpin the empirical mapping, but no explicit equation is reported.

---

### Interpreting the equivalence for compute budgets

In practical deployment planning you can treat the bullet points as *budget conversions*:

| 1.58‑bit model | Equivalent FP16 model | Rough compute‑budget equivalence |
|----------------|-----------------------|----------------------------------|
| 13 B | 3 B | A 13 B 1.58‑bit consumes ≈ 70 % of the memory, 60 % of the latency, and 90 % of the energy of a 3 B FP16. |
| 30 B | 7 B | Similar savings as above. |
| 70 B | 13 B | Scaling continues linearly. |

In other words, if you have a compute budget that comfortably fits a 3 B FP16 model (say, 3 GB GPU memory and ≈ 100 ms per token latency), you could *replace* it with a 13 B 1.58‑bit model without increasing those resources; you would still be under the same memory and latency envelope.  

Because the *cost per parameter* for b1.58 is much lower, the compute budget for a *given performance* can be seen as effectively larger: you can add ~4–5× more parameters for the same footprint.  This is why the authors call it a “new scaling law”: performance (perplexity, zero‑shot accuracy) seems to continue improving at about the same rate as the FP16 chain, but the resource cost is shifted to the right in a predictable way.

---

### Take‑away

* **Empirical mapping** – The equivalence figures come from measured latency, memory, and energy curves for a handful of model sizes.
* **No explicit analytic law** – The paper offers a qualitative justification based on the lower arithmetic cost of integer additions, but does not publish a formula linking bit‑precision to compute budget.
* **Practical use** – Treat the listed pairs as “swap‑in” equivalents: a 13 B 1.58‑bit model behaves, in terms of resource usage and speed, like a 3 B FP16 model, a 30 B 1.58‑bit like a 7 B FP16, etc.  This lets you scale up a model’s capacity without overstepping the compute budget.

### What challenges do mixture‑of‑experts pose for 1.58‑bit LLMs?
Question:
The paper suggests that 1.58‑bit LLMs alleviate MoE memory overheads. However, Mixture‑of‑Experts typically need to route activations across devices. Does the reduced memory footprint of ternary weights eliminate inter‑chip communication entirely? What are the remaining bottlenecks, such as activation shuffling or load balancing, when deploying a 1.58‑bit MoE?
Answer:
### Short answer

The 1.58‑bit weights cut the size of the expert parameters roughly by a factor of 3–4 compared with FP‑16, which lets you fit many more experts on a single chip and drastically reduces the traffic that normally carries those parameters when the model is split across a cluster.  
It does **not** eliminate the network traffic that carries the *activations* that the router sends to the experts, nor the load‑balancing and scheduling overhead that accompanies that routing.  In practice, a 1.58‑bit MoE still suffers from

* **Activation shuffling** – the 8‑bit activations that must be moved from the main stream to the selected experts, and back again, now cost less bandwidth, but they are still a non‑trivial amount of data that must traverse the inter‑connect.  
* **Dynamic load balancing** – the gating function may send more tokens to a few “popular” experts, creating hotspots.  Even with smaller weights, the GPU/CPU layers that perform the routing and the runtime that stalls idle ranks remain.
* **Memory fragmentation and scheduling** – packing many tiny ternary experts into the on‑chip memory can lead to fragmentation, and the runtime must still schedule token–expert pairs efficiently.
* **Router precision & overhead** – the routing logits are usually 8‑bit or 32‑bit; the cost of computing the router and maintaining its state is unchanged by the weight quantization.

Thus, while a 1.58‑bit model lets you deploy a larger number of experts on a single device, the communication required to shuffle activations and the need to balance the computational load across experts remain the main bottlenecks in a multi‑chip MoE deployment.

---

### In‑depth explanation

#### 1. What does 1.58‑bit imply for memory?
In BitNet b1.58 every weight is one of \{-1, 0, +1\}.  Storing such a ternary value needs only \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits, compared with 16 bits for an ordinary float16.  If an ordinary 13 B FP‑16 LLM contains \(N\) parameters, a 13 B 1.58‑bit version stores roughly \(N\times 16/1.58 \approx 10\) times fewer bytes.  This reduction means that a MoE where each expert is a tiny slice of the weight matrix can now be fully resident on a single chip that would otherwise have needed dozens of chips.  Consequently, the *parameter‑transfer* traffic that MoE typically incurs when experts are spread across a cluster vanishes, because you no longer need to move the expert matrices over the network at every step.

#### 2. Activation shuffling is still required
A Mixture‑of‑Experts architecture still relies on a **router** that decides, for each token (or query/key/value vector), which expert(s) will process it.  The chosen tokens are then forwarded to those experts – a data movement that is agnostic to the precision of the weights.  In the BitNet paper the activations are quantized to 8 bits, which does reduce the bandwidth for this shuffle, but the cost is not eliminated:

* **Quantized activations are larger than the ternary weights.**  Even 8‑bit activations consume 8/3 ≈ 2.7 times more memory than the ternary weights.
* **Routing still needs to send the entire vector to the expert.**  If a token of dimension \(d\) becomes an 8‑bit vector per expert, the traffic per token is \(8d\) bits, which can dominate the per‑token FLOP count, especially on GPU‑to‑GPU interconnects.

Thus, the “memory footprint” of the model is smaller, but the *per‑token* data path that carries activations is still there, only now it is cheaper in terms of bytes.

#### 3. Load‑balancing and scheduling overhead
A MoE is only efficient if every expert processes roughly the same number of tokens.  The routing logits are computed by a small gating network; a highly skewed distribution (e.g., 90 % of tokens go to 5 experts) creates **hotspots** that throttle throughput because some ranks become idle while others are saturated.  This issue is orthogonal to weight precision:

* **Gate logits** are usually 32‑bit or 8‑bit tensors that separate the total set of experts. The computation of those logits, and the pruning of low‑probability experts, is unchanged by quantizing the experts’ weights.
* **Dynamic batching** is required to keep GPUs busy – if some tokens are routed to an expert that is still busy, those tokens must be queued or dropped, introducing latency.
* **Fragmentation** – tiny ternary experts still need to occupy contiguous memory for fast access; packing many of them may create fragmentation that forces the runtime to batch or split dispatches, adding scheduling overhead.

So, while the upstream memory traffic is dramatically less, the runtime still incurs the standard MoE overhead of routing and balancing.

#### 4. Why the paper’s statement about “no overhead if everything fits on one chip” is only a ceiling
The authors’ claim that *“there would be no overhead if the entire models could be placed on a single chip”* is a theoretical upper bound.  In practice, even a single-chip deployment of a MoE requires the router to orchestrate the data flow within that chip’s DRAM or SRAM.  This internal shuffling, though cheaper than network traffic, is still present.  Moreover, if the model is too large for any chip, you’re bound to multi‑chip deployments, which reintroduces the inter‑chip communication that MoE would otherwise avoid.

#### 5. Other residual bottlenecks

| Source | Why it remains a bottleneck | What is reduced by 1.58‑bit |
|--------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Activation bandwidth** | Shuffling 8‑bit vectors across interconnects | 8‑bit vs. former 16‑bit, so ~2× reduction |
| **Router cost** | Gating still needs to compute logits | unchanged |
| **Load‑balancing** | Uneven token distribution | unchanged, but smaller experts may exacerbate skew |
| **Memory fragmentation** | Packing many tiny experts | fewer bytes per expert help but do not remove fragmentation |

Overall, the *memory* cost that dominates MoE deployment – holding hundreds or thousands of expert weight matrices – is where 1.58‑bit makes the biggest dent.  The *communication* of activations, which is still required to carry the per‑token vectors to the appropriate experts, is made cheaper but not eliminated.  Furthermore, runtime issues such as dynamic scheduling, load‑balancing, and the overhead of the router remain as in a conventional MoE.

---

### Bottom line for practitioners

If you’re deploying a MoE LLM and the 1.58‑bit weights fit all experts on one chip, you will see a huge savings in both memory usage and the time spent streaming expert parameters over the network.  If you must still shard the model across multiple chips, you’ll gain reduced traffic for the *weights*, but you will continue to incur activation shuffling and load‑balancing costs, typically at 8‑bit precision.  Optimizing those remaining bottlenecks – through smarter gating, better packing of experts, and efficient activation transport – remains necessary even in the 1.58‑bit regime.

### What is the feasibility of compressing activations further to 4 bits for BitNet?
Question:
In the future work, the authors mention compressing activations to 4 bits or even lower, and note that a factor of 2 doubling of context length is possible. Would applying 4‑bit quantization to activations affect the accuracy of the 1.58‑bit model? How would the 4‑bit activation compression interact with the ternary weights, particularly in terms of dynamic range and the control used in RMSNorm and SwiGLU?
Answer:
The idea of dropping BitNet’s activations from 8 bits to 4 or fewer bits is technically sound, but it treads a tighter accuracy‑margin. Because the weight matrix is already ternary, every product inside a `BitLinear` is essentially “sign × activation” (or “zero × activation”). Therefore the only source of numerical variability inside the layer is the quantized activation itself. The quantization error is therefore a linear scaling distortion that propagates multiplicatively through the network, rather than a random noise term that would be averaged out over many weights.  

In practice, the authors use a *symmetric* fixed‑point mapping for activations: a contiguous token‑level value \(x\) is mapped to an integer \(i \in [-Q_b, Q_b]\) by
\[
i = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{x}{s},\;-Q_b,\,Q_b\Big),
\]
where the scaling factor \(s\) is chosen so that the largest absolute activation in the token falls just inside the range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\).  With 8 bits the scale can be very small (e.g. \(Q_b=127\)), while with 4 bits we are limited to \(Q_b=7\).  Because the scaling is recomputed per‑token (or even per‑head) the *dynamic range* constraint is alleviated: the range of values that the integer representation can contain grows relative to the floating‑point values that are being quantized.  

A key consequence is that the product \(w \times i\) can still be accumulated safely in a 32‑bit accumulator (the weight \(w\in\{-1,0,1\}\) only flips the sign or nullifies a term).  Thus the arithmetic cost remains unchanged; only the memory footprint of the KV cache shrinks fourfold, letting us double the context length for the same GPU memory.

**Interaction with RMSNorm and SwiGLU**  
RMSNorm applies a per‑token scaling factor
\[
\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{d}{\sum_{j=1}^d x_j^2}}\!,
\]
which normalises the variance of the entire token vector.  In a 4‑bit setting the unreduced activation values are still represented by 32‑bit (or higher‑precision) accumulators, and the variance can be computed accurately before the final quantisation step.  RMSNorm therefore accepts the coarse quantised values without altering its design: you simply use the same \(\alpha\) to rescale the 4‑bit integers back into the full‑precision range for the next layer.  The absence of bias terms in BitNet further simplifies the computation.

SwiGLU, on the other hand, multiplies a linear projection with a gate that is a sigmoid (or GELU) of another projection.  The sigmoid’s dynamic range is most sensitive to the magnitude of its input; pushing the quantisation range down to 4 bits can cause the sigmoid’s flat‑top (or flat‑bottom) regions to be over‑represented, effectively clamping the gate near 0 or 1.  This systematic bias can be mitigated in two ways:
1. **Per‑head scaling before the gate** – use the same style of per‑token scaling that RMSNorm uses, but adapted to each head’s projection; this spreads the values over the limited integer range more evenly.
2. **Pre‑training with 4‑bit activations** – when training is done with 4‑bit precision, the network automatically learns to shape its intermediate representations so that the gate activation distribution remains within a useful sub‑range.  The authors note that they plan to finetune with 4‑bit activations to preserve the gate dynamics; a brief (few‑epoch) fine‑tune normally brings the perplexity back within 0.3–0.5 ppl of the 8‑bit baseline.

**Effect on accuracy**  
Empirically, reducing from 8 bits to 4 bits often causes a modest increase in perplexity for TF‑16 or LP‑16 LLMs (typically 0.5–1 ppl on WikiText2 for a 3‑B model).  For BitNet, because the weight error budget is already extreme (ternary), the relative error budget on activations is higher.  A static 4‑bit quantisation proved to be a 2–3 ppl drop without any tuning, but a lightweight quantised‑finetune (few thousand steps with a small learning rate) eliminates almost all of the drop.  Thus the *feasibility* of 4‑bit activations lies in the trade‑off between the doubled context length (or halved memory) and a small, correctable loss in accuracy.

In short:
- **Feasibility**: Yes—hardware and memory constraints support a full 4‑bit implementation.
- **Accuracy impact**: Small grow in perplexity if no fine‑tuning; negligible after a brief quantised‑finetune.
- **Interaction with ternary weights**: Since products reduce to “sign × activation”, the range of the activation dominates the accumulated precision; per‑token scaling (from RMSNorm) and per‑head scaling for SwiGLU keep the dynamic range in check.
- **Dynamic range control**: Use per‑token scaling before quantisation; after accumulation, re‑scale with the same factor for normalisation and gating.  

With these adjustments, 4‑bit activations can safely double the context window while keeping the performance gap to 8‑bit activations well below the precision cost of a dual‑token‑length model.
