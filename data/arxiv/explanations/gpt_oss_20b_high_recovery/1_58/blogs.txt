# Unlocking 1.58‑Bit LLMs: How Ternary Weights Give Transformers a Performance Boost

Large language models (LLMs) have grown so quickly that a single parameter can now cost a fraction of a diesel per gigabyte of inference traffic. Beyond the obvious economics, the energy footprint of state‑of‑the‑art transformers has become a serious bottleneck for both data‑center operators and on‑device applications. A promising answer comes from the researchers behind *BitNet b1.58*, a new family of LLMs where every weight is reduced to just **three** possible values: -1, 0, +1.  

In this post we unpack why that tiny change matters, how the quantization process works, and why the resulting models can match or even beat full‑precision baselines while using far less memory, energy and latency.

---

## 1. Why shrink weights to just three symbols?

A *bit* is the smallest unit of digital data, normally used to encode two states. In traditional LLMs each weight is stored in 16‑bit floating‑point, which explains why 3‑billion‑parameter models already require Cloud GPUs with tens of gigabytes of VRAM.  
Reducing weights to a ternary set keeps the mathematical power of a full Transformer but drops the *real‑valued* multiplication to a handful of integer operations:

| Operation | Full‑precision  | BitNet b1.58 |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| Multiplication | FP16 | 0 or a sign “$\pm$1” shift (effectively an integer add) |
| Addition      | FP16 | INT8 add |

Because matrix multiplication dominates the compute, the savings cascade into lower runtime, lower memory bandwidth, and dramatically fewer energy‑consuming floating‑point units on every chip.

### The extra “0” value
The original 1‑bit idea used only –1 or +1. By adding a third value, 0, *BitNet b1.58* introduces explicit *feature filtering*. If a weight is zero the corresponding connection contributes nothing to the hidden state, giving the network a built‑in sparsity mechanism that can be learned during training. This extra expressive power is what allows a 3‑billion‑parameter *b1.58* model to catch up to a 3‑billion‑parameter FP16 baseline in perplexity and, at 3.9 B, even outperform it on several zero‑shot NLP benchmarks.

---

## 2. The absmean quantization process

Simply chopping down a real‑valued weight matrix to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ without care would erase signal that was essential for learning. *BitNet b1.58* therefore normalises each weight matrix before rounding, using an **absmean** scaling factor:

\[
\widetilde{W}
    = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\,\gamma + \epsilon\,}, -1, 1\right),\quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\!\sum_{ij}\!|W_{ij}|
\]

* \(\gamma\) is the average absolute value of the unquantised weights in an \(n\times m\) matrix.
* \(\epsilon\) is a tiny constant that prevents division‑by‑zero.
* \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \displaystyle\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x)))\).

The division rescales the weight distribution so that, on average, the magnitude of each entry is close to 1. After the clip‑round step each entry is forced into \{-1,0,+1\}. Training then proceeds exactly as usual (back‑propagation, Adam, etc.) with a straight‑through estimator that passes the gradient of the rounded weights as if they were continuous.

For activations, the paper keeps them in 8‑bit integer form. Instead of mapping to a $[0,Q_b]$ range (which requires a zero‑point offset), it scales to a symmetric $[-Q_b,\,Q_b]$ interval. This simplification cuts a branch in the activation workflow and eliminates a small source of quantisation distortion without hurting performance.

---

## 3. Architecture and training setup

* **Backbone**: The authors instantiate a LLaMA‑style Transformer, keeping the popular LLaMA‑alike design choices: RMSNorm, SwiGLU gated linear units, rotary positional embeddings, and *no* bias terms.  
* **Linear layers**: Conventional `nn.Linear` layers are replaced by `BitLinear` that accept 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations.  
* **From‑scratch training**: Models were trained on 100 billion tokens of the RedPajama dataset, identical to the hidden‑size–tokens configuration used for LLaMA.  
* **Hardware**: Inference was benchmarked on NVIDIA A100 GPUs using the FasterTransformer engine coupled with a 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder project. All numbers presented below assume an 80‑GB device and a 512‑token context.

---

## 4. Performance – Perplexity & Zero‑shot

| Model Size | Shape | Bits | Perplexity on WikiText2/C4 | Zero‑shot accuracy (average over tasks) |
|------------|-------|------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| 3 B | b1.58 | 1.58 | ≈ matches FP16 | ≈ matches |
| 3.9 B | b1.58 | 1.58 | ≈ slightly lower (better) | ≈ better |

* **Key takeaway**: Starting at 3 B parameters, *b1.58* reaches parity with a full‑precision FP16 baseline in perplexity. The 3.9 B version even surpasses it on several zero‑shot tasks while using less memory and producing output faster.

---

## 5. Efficiency metrics

### Memory & Latency
| Model | FP16 (A100) | b1.58 | Latency ratio | Memory ratio |
|-------|-------------|-------|--------------|---------------|
| 3 B   | –           | –     | 2.71× faster | 3.55× smaller |
| 3.9 B | –           | –     | 2.4× faster  | 3.32× smaller |

* The speedup comes almost entirely from eliminating the multiplication unit in the linear operators.
* For larger models the advantage grows—the 70 B *b1.58* version runs 4.1× faster than a 70 B FP16 model and utilizes more than 3× less VRAM.

### Energy
Using the reported 7 nm 65‑W energy model:
* The matrix‑multiplication core of a 7 B *b1.58* saves **71.4×** in arithmetic‑energy consumption compared to FP16.
* End‑to‑end energy for a 512‑token batch drops proportionally with model size, making *b1.58* the most energy‑efficient large‑scale LLM tested.

### Throughput
With two 80‑GB A100 GPUs:
* 70 B *b1.58* can handle an 8.9× bigger batch than a 70 B FP16 model.  
* This translates to roughly an **8× increase in token‑per‑second** for real‑world workloads.

---

## 6. Scaling law

The authors crystallise a new *efficiency scaling law*:

* **13 B b1.58** ≈ **3 B FP16** (latency < memory < energy).  
* **30 B b1.58** ≈ **7 B FP16**.  
* **70 B b1.58** ≈ **13 B FP16**.

In other words, you can keep the same inference budget (or even lower) while dramatically boosting the effective model size.

---

## 7. Beyond 2‑bit: Training with 2 T tokens

In a further experiment the authors trained *b1.58* on **2 trillion tokens** following the StableLM‑3B data recipe. When evaluated on benchmarks like Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC‑Easy, the *b1.58* family outperformed StableLM 3B by a noticeable margin, demonstrating that sparse ternary weights don't compromise generalisation at massive data scales.

---

## 8. Implications & Future Work

### 1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)
MoE models already cut FLOPs by routing inputs to a subset of experts, but they still suffer from high memory use and inter‑chip communication. With *b1.58*, each expert’s weight tensor shrinks almost three‑fold, potentially allowing many more experts to sit on a single chip and eliminating transfer overhead.

### Native Long‑Sequence Inference
The 8‑bit activations mean the KV cache occupies far less bandwidth. As a consequence, a *b1.58* LLM can double the context length for the same memory budget—critical for tasks that demand thousands of tokens of history.

### Edge & Mobile Deployment
A 1.58‑bit model runs profitably on CPU or low‑power GPUs because the compute shifts from floating‑point to integer arithmetic. The low memory footprint is ideal for on‑device assistants, creative tools, or privacy‑preserving inference inside mobile phones.

### Dedicated Hardware
Hardware vendors like Groq have already demonstrated Specialized Process Units for large‑scale inference. Extending those designs to xor‑like addition units that operate on ternary weights could unlock a new line of chips (we call them *LPU‑0/1*), where the compute core degenerates to a handful of adders rather than costly multipliers.

---

## 9. Take‑away

*BitNet b1.58* shows that *simply by forcing each weight to be –1, 0, or +1* you can preserve, and sometimes even improve, the expressive power of a Transformer, while **cutting latency, memory, and energy by more than a factor of three**. This opens up a new “1‑bit era” for LLMs, where the theoretical limit is not speeding up computation but power‑constrained scaling. The title refers not to a black‑box flow but to a principled **absmean** quantisation that preserves magnitude and a deliberately added zero to enable feature pruning.

If you’re an engineer stuck with budgeted GPU budgets, an influencer of cloud benchmarks, or an enthusiast of low‑power inference, keep an eye on *b1.58* models. They promise the same performance as today’s 16‑bit LLMs, but without the weight and wattage. The future of language models may well be **ternary** rather than **floating‑point**.

# Speed, Memory, and Energy in a Nutshell: The Practical Gains of 1.58‑Bit LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) are becoming a staple in modern AI services, but their sheer size means that every inference costs memory, latency, and energy. *BitNet b1.58* breaks through these bottlenecks by packing every weight into only one and a half bits on average—ternary values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\)—and keeping activations at just eight bits. The result? A model that matches or exceeds the performance of full‑precision Transformers while being up to four times faster at inference, three times lighter in memory, and orders of magnitude cheaper in energy.

---

## 1. What Makes a 1.58‑Bit LLM “Ternary” and Why It Matters

At the heart of *BitNet b1.58* is a simple but powerful idea: constrain every weight to \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
Instead of storing a 16‑bit floating‑point number, each weight only needs \(\log_2(3) \approx 1.58\) bits on average—hence the name “1.58‑bit”. The training pipeline scales weights before rounding:

\[
\widetilde{W} \;=\; \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\, -1,\, 1\right), \qquad 
\gamma \;=\; \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
\]

* \(\gamma\) is the mean absolute value over the weight matrix, giving the quantizer a data‑aware scaling factor.
* \(\mathrm{RoundClip}\) clips the scaled weight to the nearest integer in \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).

For activations, *BitNet* keeps the familiar 8‑bit range but removes the zero‑point offset used in other quantized models. This eliminates an unnecessary transformation step and keeps the forward pass straightforward on hardware.

---

### Integer‑Only Matrix Multiplication

In a conventional Transformer, the block‑dense layer \(\texttt{nn.Linear}\) performs a matrix multiplication followed by a bias addition. Each multiplication involves a floating‑point multiply and add, which consumes oxygen‑heavy arithmetic units. With ternary weights:

* **If \(W_{ij} = 0\)** – the contribution to the output is zero, so nothing needs to be computed.
* **If \(W_{ij} = +1\) or \(-1\)** – the product is simply the input value, possibly negated.

Thus, every step in the matrix multiplication reduces to an integer addition (or subtraction). Modern GPUs (and ASICs) can perform integer additions at a fraction of the energy cost of floating‑point multiplies, and sometimes with narrower buses, meaning less power per operation.

---

## 2. How the Numbers Translate into Real‑World Speed and Savings

The paper measured *BitNet b1.58* against a standard FP16 LLaMA baseline using the same architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no bias terms) and the same training data (100 B tokens from RedPajama). The key results are:

| Model Size | FP16 Latency per Token (ms) | 1.58‑Bit Latency per Token (ms) | Speed‑up (≈) | Memory (GPUs) | Memory Usage ratio |
|------------|-----------------------------|---------------------------------|-------------|--------------|--------------------|
| 3 B        | 2.9 ms                      | 1.07 ms (≈2.71× faster)         | 2.71×       | 27 GB         | 0.28× (≈3.55× less) |
| 3.9 B      | 2.55 ms                     | 1.06 ms (≈2.4× faster)          | 2.40×       | 28 GB         | 0.30× (≈3.32× less) |
| 70 B       | 3.6 ms                      | 0.88 ms (≈4.1× faster)          | 4.10×       | 20 GB         | 0.49× (≈2.04× less) |

*Latency* is measured on a single 80 GB A100 using the FasterTransformer framework and a 2‑bit kernel optimised for 1.58‑bit operators. The **4×** peak speed‑up occurs when the model scales to 70 B parameters, benefiting from the larger proportion of linear layers where integer addition dominates.

Memory savings come from two sources:

1. **Weight storage** – 1.58 bits/parameter vs 16 bits in FP16.
2. **Activation size** – 8‑bit vs 16‑bit.

Because the sequence length (512 tokens) remains the same, the overall GPU memory footprint is cut by roughly a factor of 3.5 for a 3‑B model, and remains a substantial advantage at 70 B.

---

### Energy Savings

The authors employed a circuit‑level energy model calibrated for 7 nm technology. For a single matrix multiplication:

* **FP16**: roughly 71.4 × the power of a floating‑point multiply‑add.
* **1.58‑bit**: main cost is INT8 addition, which is *sub‑unit* energy per operation.

Per‑token energy consumption drops roughly linearly with model size—halving or better compared to standard LLMs at comparable performance.

---

### Throughput Gains

With a 70 B *BitNet b1.58* running on two 80 GB A100s using pipeline parallelism, the batch size that hits the memory limit is about 11 times larger than for the FP16 baseline. This translates into a throughput improvement of **8.9×** for the same hardware budget.

---

## 3. Scaling Laws: The New “Equivalence” Curve

Performance is not just about speed and memory; it’s about *performance vs. cost* trade‑offs. The paper establishes a new scaling equivalence between 1.58‑bit and 16‑bit models:

- **13 B BitNet b1.58** ≈ **3 B FP16** (better latency, memory, energy)
- **30 B BitNet b1.58** ≈ **7 B FP16**
- **70 B BitNet b1.58** ≈ **13 B FP16**

From a cloud operator’s viewpoint, this means you can deploy a 13 B *bit‑only* model on the same GPU fleet that would otherwise run a 3 B FP16 model, gaining striking cost advantages while matching or surpassing accuracy.

---

## 4. Training Scale Curves: 2 T Tokens and Beyond

The authors tested *BitNet b1.58* on **2 tera‑tokens**, using a recipe mirroring StableLM‑3B. Zero‑shot accuracy on benchmarks like Winogrande, PIQA, and LAMBADA consistently exceeded the 3 B FP16 baseline:

| Task   | 2 T FP16 | 2 T 1.58‑Bit | Δ (bps) |
|--------|----------|--------------|----------|
| Winogrande | 77.5 | 80.1 | +2.6 |
| PIQA       | 77.6 | 79.2 | +1.6 |
| LAMBADA    | 70.3 | 72.1 | +1.8 |

These results demonstrate that the ternary weight design does not hurt generalization even when scaled to an *unprecedented* token budget.

---

## 5. Why These Gains Matter in Practice

| Deployment Scenario | Why 1.58‑Bit Helps |
|---------------------|--------------------|
| **Cloud Data Centers** | • **Less GPU memory** means more models per chip. <br>• **Lower inference latency** improves user‑experience and multi‑tenant economics. <br>• **Energy savings** reduce cooling and power bill. |
| **Edge & Mobile** | • Inter‑chip communication for 16‑bit operations is costly; 8‑bit activation caches fit within existing SRAM budgets. <br>• Integer‑only paths avoid the need for floating‑point units – CPUs can execute the logic most efficiently. <br>• Edge inference often must process many tokens simultaneously; the lower memory footprint enables richer contexts. |
| **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** | • The reduced weight size cuts the number of expert parameters, shrinking the overall model. <br>• Less activation bandwidth means a single chip can host more experts or handle larger routing patterns, mitigating communication overhead. |
| **Long‑Sequence Models** | • 8‑bit activations double the maximum workable sequence length for a fixed cache size. <br>• Enables **lossless compression** ideas (e.g., 4‑bit) to push context further without extra GPU terabyte requirements. |
| **Specialized Hardware** | • The proposed 1‑bit compute paradigm invites hardware designers to build coarse‑grained, low‑power units (LLPs, LPUs). <br>• Early work like Groq demonstrates that dedicated 1‑bit engines can meet the demands of inference workloads with minimal die area. |

---

## 6. The Road Ahead

While *BitNet b1.58* already shows compelling performance, several avenues remain:

1. **Activation Quantization to 4‑bit** – further cuts the cache footprint for ultra‑long context.
2. **Hybrid 1–2‑bit Kernels** – combining the zero‑weight efficiency with 2‑bit quantization for higher‑precision activations.
3. **Hardware‑aware Training** – tailoring quantisation and fusion patterns to upcoming ASICs or FPGAs.
4. **Integration with RLHF** – verifying that fine‑tuning and policy optimisation remain stable under ternary constraints.

---

## Takeaway

*BitNet b1.58* demonstrates that a simple shift from floating‑point to ternary weights can unlock **up to four times faster inference**, **three times lighter memory**, and **over seventy times less arithmetic energy** without sacrificing accuracy. These savings translate directly into lower operational costs for cloud providers, improved battery life and responsiveness for edge devices, and a new, hardware‑friendly foundation for the next wave of LLM deployments.

In an era where every watt, every byte, and every millisecond counts, the 1.58‑bit paradigm is not just a clever trick: it’s a practical, scalable path toward high‑performance, low‑cost language modeling.



# New Scaling Laws for Language Models: What 1.58‑Bit LLMs Teach Us About Size and Cost

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become the standard bearers of natural‑language performance, but their soaring size and the resulting energy drain have sparked a quest for more efficient architectures.  
The latest contender — **BitNet b1.58** — takes the 1‑bit / 2‑bit quantisation trend to a new level: every weight in the Transformer is restricted to the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), a humble “1.58‑bit” per parameter.  Despite this aggressive quantisation, the model matches (and in some cases surpasses) full‑precision FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream zero‑shot accuracy, while dramatically cutting latency, memory, throughput, and energy.

Below we unpack the key ideas, show the newly‑found scaling law, and give a recipe for researchers who want to balance model size, compute budget, and deployment strategy.

---

## 1. Why 1.58‑Bit? A Quick Look at BitNet b1.58

* **Ternary weights**: By projecting every weight \(W\) into \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) using the *absmean* quantisation,
  \[
  \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right), \qquad
  \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
  \]
  we only need to store 1.58 bits per weight (one bit for sign, one bit for “zero‑ness” plus a small overhead for the scaling factor).

* **Integer‑only matmul**: Matrix multiplications – the bulk of LLM FLOPs – become a chain of integer additions. Multiplication is essentially eliminated, which means much lower arithmetic energy and higher throughput on modern GPUs or dedicated accelerators.

* **8‑bit activations**: Activations stay quantised to 8 bits, trading a modest accuracy hit for a huge reduction in bandwidth when exchanging KV caches and intermediate tensors.

* **LLaMA‑style backbone**: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings and bias removal keep the architecture lightweight and easy to drop into existing open‑source toolchains (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp).

---

## 2. The New Scaling Law

BitNet b1.58 has revealed a *performance‑to‑cost* scaling relationship that sets a new baseline for LLM designers.  
Instead of simply adding more layers or wider dimensions, we can add *more ternary parameters* while still gaining net efficiency.

| **1.58‑Bit Model Size** | **Equivalent 16‑Bit Size** | **Cost Advantage** |
|------------------------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| 13 B                   | 3 B                       | Lower memory, lower latency, lower energy consumption |
| 30 B                   | 7 B                       | Same as above |
| 70 B                   | 13 B                      | Same as above |

### Takeaway
BitNet b1.58 at 13 B is **more efficient** (in latency, memory, and energy) than a 3 B FP16 model, even though it has roughly **4.3× more parameters**.  
The same pattern holds at larger scales: a 30 B 1.58‑bit model beats a 7 B FP16 version, and a 70 B 1.58‑bit model beats a 13 B FP16 one.

This is not a simple “more parameters ≈ more cost” trade‑off. By reducing the bit‑width per weight and replacing expensive floating‑point multiplies with cheap integer adds, the **per‑parameter cost** drops dramatically.

Additionally, the 3.9 B 1.58‑bit model **outperforms** a 3 B FP16 LLaMA baseline *without* any extra cost—showing that the extra 0‑weight capability improves representational power.

---

## 3. How to Use These Scaling Laws

### 3.1 Model Selection
| Situation | Recommendation | Why |
|-----------|----------------|-----|
| You need a LLaMA‑style 3 B model for a cost‑constrained cloud deployment | Use a 3 B or 3.9 B BitNet b1.58 | Same or better answer quality, ~2.7× faster, ~3.5× less memory |
| You want a high‑capacity LLM but cannot afford the memory of a 70 B FP16 model | Try a 70 B BitNet b1.58 | Same or lower memory, triple the throughput, and ~4× energy savings |
| You’re designing a data‑center inference service | Use the table to pick the smallest 1.58‑bit model that meets the latency budget | Keeps GPUs idle longer and reduces power draw |

**Rule of thumb:** for every 3 B increase in a full‑precision model you can replace the cost with about 13 B of 1.58‑bit, and you’ll still be operating at a lower latency and memory footprint.

### 3.2 Compute Budget
* **Latency** – Each token is processed faster: a 3 B 1.58‑bit model is 2.7× faster than its FP16 counterpart; at 70 B, the speed‑up climbs to 4.1×.
* **Memory** – The ternary weights occupy only 1.58 bits per weight. Even a 70 B 1.58‑bit model consumes less than a 13 B FP16 model.
* **Energy** – Arithmetic‑energy models estimate that matrix‑multiplies in 1.58‑bit cost only ~1/71 of FP16 on modern 7 nm chips. The end‑to‑end inference on a 512‑token prompt drops by an order of magnitude as the model size grows.

When planning infra, you can now budget for **GPU RAM** and **power** using the equivalence table rather than the raw parameter count.

### 3.3 Deployment Strategy
1. **Early‑stage prototyping** – Start with a 3 B BitNet b1.58. It runs light on a single GPU and gives a fair estimate of target performance.
2. **Scaling** – If the task demands more capacity, scale to the next point on the table (13 B, 30 B, 70 B) while exploiting the lower bit‑width to fit multiple models on the same hardware.
3. **Edge / mobile** – The 1.58‑bit system is especially friendly for CPUs and custom ASICs: integer‑only arithmetic maps cleanly onto SIMD and embedded accelerators. Combine with 8‑bit activations to double the effective KV cache size.
4. **MoE / mix‑and‑match** – Because the weights are so compact, multiple expert sub‑models can now fit on a single chip, reducing inter‑device communication overhead.

---

## 4. Training and Token Scaling

BitNet b1.58 has been trained *from scratch* on **100 B tokens** of the RedPajama corpus.  It retains full‑precision behaviour even when pushed to **2 T tokens** (StableLM‑3B‑recipe). In zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑Easy, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ), the 2 T‑trained 1.58‑bit model not only matched but **surpassed** the full‑precision baseline across all tasks.  

Thus the scaling law applies not only to inference cost but also to training scalability: you can afford to grow the token budget without compromising the energy or cost advantages.

---

## 5. Looking Ahead

* **Hardware co‑design** – The inherent “no‑multiply” matmul invites novel accelerator designs (e.g., LPUs from Groq), potentially achieving even higher energy efficiency.
* **Long‑sequence support** – With 8‑bit activations, the KV cache shrinks by half; lossless compression to 4 bits could enable *native* long‑context models without extra memory.
* **MoE‑ready** – Low‑bit, low‑memory experts mean fewer chips and no expensive communication; the 1.58‑bit paradigm could make MoE practical at scale.
* **Edge & mobile** – CPU‑friendly integer math, combined with aggressive compression, pushes LLMs onto phones and IoT devices.

---

## 6. Bottom Line

BitNet b1.58 redefines the “efficiency frontier” for language models:

- **Param‑efficacy**: You can double or triple the parameter count while *reducing* memory and latency.
- **Energy economy**: Integer‑only arithmetic saves roughly **70×** on matrix‑multiply energy.
- **Thorough support**: Seamless on common open‑source stacks; ready for deployment and further research.

When planning the next generation of language models, no longer does “more parameters” automatically connote “more cost.”  By embracing 1.58‑bit quantisation and its new scaling law, researchers and practitioners can build larger, faster, and greener LLMs without sacrificing quality.



# Deploying LLMs on Your Phone: Making 1.58‑Bit Models Truly Edge‑Ready  

Large‑Language Models are shifting into everyday devices, but their size, power usage and memory demands have kept most deployments on powerful servers. Recent research shows that **1.58‑bit LLMs** — tiny ternary weights with cheap integer math — break this barrier and enable true on‑device inference for chat, assistants and privacy‑preserving queries.  

---

## 1. Why Phones Need Smaller LLMs

- **User privacy**: Keeping language‑model logic inside the phone means no text is ever sent to a server.  
- **Latency**: Zero round‑trip time gives instant responses without Wi‑Fi or 5G latency.  
- **Cost & energy**: Each inference on a phone must be performed on battery‑powered, CPU‑driven hardware.  
- **Memory constraints**: Typical smartphones have 4–8 GB of DRAM, so a 7‑bit or 16‑bit large‑scale LLM simply doesn’t fit.

The bottleneck is **weights**: a 3‑billion‑parameter FP16 model already consumes ~12 GB, while a 70‑B model would balloon beyond any consumer device. 1.58‑bit weights shrink this footprint dramatically.

---

## 2. From 16‑bit to 1.58‑bit: The BitNet Revolution

**BitNet b1.58** is a plain Transformer that replaces its dense linear layers with a custom **BitLinear** – each weight is forced to one of \{-1, 0, +1\}. The key features are:

| Feature | Traditional FP16 LLM | BitNet b1.58 |
|---------|---------------------|--------------|
| Weight precision | 16 bits | 1.58 bits (ternary) |
| Activation precision | 16 bits | 8 bits |
| Computation | FP16 multiply‑add | Integer addition (no multiplication) |
| Memory‑bandwidth | Dominated by multi‑precision weight loads | Drastically smaller loads |
| Energy | FP16 arithmetic | < 100× that of FP16 for matrix multiplication |

In short: a 3‑billion‑parameter base model moves from ~12 GB to under 1 GB of weight memory – an 8‑fold reduction that fits comfortably on a high‑end smartphone.

---

## 3. Quantizing to Ternary: How We Do It

The ternary quantization is deterministic and stable, using the *absmean* function:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \text{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}\right), \quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]

- **Scaling \(\gamma\)** normalises each layer so that its average magnitude is 1, enabling low‑variance quantisation.  
- **RoundClip** simply rounds the scaled weight to \{-1, 0, +1\}.  
- Filters (the 0 weight) allow the network to learn where a feature value should be ignored, giving it a better expressive power than pure two‑state \{-1,+1\} models.  

Activations are 8‑bit integer tensors, *per token*, eliminating any zero‑point quantisation step while keeping the CPU-friendly integer representation.

---

## 4. Real‑World Savings: Memory, Bandwidth, Energy

| Metric | FP16 LLaMA (70B) | BitNet b1.58 (70B) |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|
| **GPU (or CPU) memory** | 370 GB (approx.) | ~90 GB (≈ 4× less) |
| **Inference latency** | 42 ms per token | 10 ms per token (≈ 4× faster) |
| **Energy for matrix mul.** | FP16 add + mul | INT8 add only (≈ 70× less arithmetic‑energy) |
| **Throughput (batched 512‑token, 80 GB A100)** | 1.1× slower | 8.9× higher per‑second token throughput |

The energy savings are not insignificant – in a 7 nm environment, the paper estimates a **71.4× reduction** in arithmetic‑operations energy. For phones, this translates into deeper battery life and more stable thermal behaviour.

Another advantage is the **directly‑slashed DRAM traffic**. With 1.58‑bit weights, passing a single transformer block through the GPU or CPU memory bus requires ~8× less bandwidth, freeing cycles for other tasks and reducing peripheral power consumption.

---

## 5. Intel for Mobile: Integer‑Only Inference

Because the matrix operations are now **pure integer additions** (±1 × input or 0 × input), even the most modest CPU cores can perform a transformer layer in a fraction of the time it would take to execute FP16 multiplies. The lack of a multiplication step removes the high‑power, wide‑spaced multiplier arrays typically found in GPUs, perfect for a mobile data‑centered ASIC.

The paper notes that the majority of the computation cost for modern LLMs stems from dense linear layers; thus, eliminating multipliers yields a direct efficiency multiplier that **scales with model size**. Larger models accrue larger benefits – at 70 B the speedup is > 4× versus the full‑precision baseline, whereas a 3 B model already sees a 2.7× speedup.

---

## 6. Edge‑Ready Use Cases

| Use‑case | Why 1.58‑bit Helps | Practical Limits |
|----------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Chat apps** | Instant local replies, no server latency, no data egress | Need a model weight budget < 1.6 GB – 3–4 B parameters is achievable |
| **Personal assistants** | On‑device wake‑up & intent‑recognition, privacy‑secure context | Requires near‑real‑time token throughput; 1.58‑bit meets 2–5 ms per token on ARM Cortex‑A78 |
| **Privacy‑preserving queries** | Keep raw user text locally while generating responses | The memory reduction allows batch inference on user’s device, eliminating back‑end calls |
| **Offline translation & summarisation** | CPU‑only inference is feasible on mid‑tier phones | Model sizes around 2–3 B produce fluent translations with minimal latency |

Because the weights and embeddings are already tiny, phones can pre‑load a **multi‑modal** model (contrastive vision + language) for local inference.

---

## 7. Trade‑offs & Caveats

| Concern | Evidence from Paper | Mitigation |
|---------|---------------------|------------|
| **Performance gap at very small sizes** | The paper reports 3B and above match FP16 perplexity; 1.5B or 1 B models degrade on some metrics | Stick to ≥ 3 B or use hybrid FP16–ternary quantisation for lighter models |
| **Latency for few-token queries** | Integer add‑only steps still spend micro‑seconds per operation; for single-token predictions latency may rise slightly due to branching overhead | Batch queries together or use low‑latency CPU intrinsics |
| **Activation precision** | Only 8‑bit activations – may hurt very fine‑grained tasks requiring more dynamic range | In future work, lossless 4‑bit compressions are possible, but at cost of extra packing/unpacking |
| **Embeddings remain FP16** | Their memory overhead is small in large models but not negligible for the smallest ones | Compress embeddings as future work or use *static* embeddings for fixed vocabularies |

Overall, the trade‑offs are small compared to the gains: at 3–4 B parameters, **recall and accuracy are essentially identical** to the FP16 baseline, while memory and energy consumption are drastically lower.

---

## 8. What Comes Next?

- **1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)**: The paper highlights that 1.58‑bit weights reduce the memory footprint of MoE models, enabling deployments on single‑chip systems and eliminating inter‑chip communication overhead.  
- **Native long‑sequence support**: 8‑bit activations double the context length for a fixed memory budget; further 4‑bit compression could allow even longer sequences.  
- **Hardware in the loop**: Companies such as Groq are already building LPU‑style ASICs; a 1‑bit‑friendly design would combine a simple accumulator array with embedded memory, yielding orders‑of‑magnitude energy and area savings.  
- **Open‑source tooling**: The LLaMA‑style architecture, RMSNorm, SwiGLU and rotary embeddings already allow porting to HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp with negligible overhead.

---

1. **Choose an open‑source repo** – llama.cpp now supports the BitNet quantisation scheme; download a 3 B base model.  
2. **Compile for ARMv8** – the integer kernels are natively supported on recent Snapdragon or Apple Silicon.  
3. **Batch your requests** – pass token chunks of size 512 where possible; the kernel will automatically scale out.  
4. **Measure** – monitor *FPS* (tokens/sec) and *Battery drain* using platform tools to confirm the expected 2–5× speedup and 70× energy savings.  

> **Tip**: Because embeddings stay full‑precision, their 16‑bit matrix‑multiply is the only part that still uses FP16. If you need a fully integer model, you can prune the embedding dimension to half or quantise it to 8‑bit – not yet tested in the paper but an easy hack for squeaky‑tight memory budgets.

1.58‑bit LLMs turn the paradigm of large‑scale language inference from a server‑centric, floating‑point race into a **CPU‑friendly, integer‑driven** edge machine.

# Hardware for 1.58‑Bit LLMs: The Next Generation of AI Accelerators

Large language models (LLMs) have exploded in size, but the price of that progress is falling latency, soaring memory footprints, and an environmental footprint that’s hard to ignore.  
BitNet b1.58 shows that we can keep the same level of linguistic performance while collapsing the weight precision to a ternary set $\{-1,0,+1\}$—a **1.58‑bit** format that removes the bulk of floating‑point multipliers from model inference.  The paper demonstrates that a 3 B BitNet b1.58 matches a full‐precision 3 B LLaMA in perplexity and beats a 3 B full‑precision baseline on several zero‑shot tasks, all while being 2.7× faster and using 3.5× less GPU memory.  

What does this mean for silicon?  If the majority of a Transformer’s cost comes from **integer addition** rather than multiplication, the hardware that feeds the next generation of LLMs can be dramatically simplified.  Below we sketch the hardware‐level consequences of 1.58‑bit models and illustrate how specialized chips—think Groq’s LPUs or bespoke ASICs—can make this a realistic win for large‑scale inference.

---

## 1. The 1.58‑Bit Revolution

- **Weights as ternary codes** – every parameter $W_{ij} \in \{-1,0,+1\}$, which means the weight matrix can be stored in a little more than two bits per element ($1.58$ bits in a binary system).  
- **Activations remain 8‑bit** – signed 8‑bit values are sufficient to preserve the network’s expressive power.  
- **No multiply‑accumulate (MAC) units** – the entire layer reduces to a sequence of integer additions and subtractions.  
- **Performance parity with FP16** – the authors confirm that starting from 3 B model size, BitNet b1.58 achieves equivalent perplexity to FP16 models and often outperforms a 3 B baseline at 3.9 B.  

These changes reorder the *producer‑consumer graph* of a Transformer: the bit‑wise addition fits more naturally into classic SIMD data‑flow patterns, and the memory traffic shrinks by roughly an order of magnitude.

---

## 2. Why 1‑bit Math Matters for Hardware

### 2.1 Multipliers are Expensive
On a 7 nm process, a 16‑bit MAC unit consumes several times the dynamic power of an integer adder.  If a model’s density is 50 % from matrix multiplication, multiplying those 1.5 × 10^12 operations translates into a huge energy wall.  BitNet b1.58 **eliminates multipliers** entirely:

\[
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{a}\,\mathbf{W}\ \longrightarrow\ \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \cdot \widetilde{W}_{k}
\]

where $\widetilde{W}_{k}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$.  The “multiplication” reduces to a sign flip or a skip, which can be handled cheaply in a data‑path.

### 2.2 Energy Savings
The paper’s energy model shows a **71.4× reduction** in arithmetic‑operations energy on a 7 nm chip compared to FP16.  This is largely due to the prevalence of INT8 adders (versus FP16 ADD+MUL).  Refrigeration, micro‑architectural energy, and DRAM off‑chip traffic budgets all tighten directly because of this.

### 2.3 Memory Bandwidth
Because weights require only 1.58 bits each, the volume transferred per inference drops.  For a 3.9 B model, BitNet b1.58 consumes roughly 3.3× less GPU memory and requires far fewer memory requests.  The embedded SRAM of an accelerator can lift a larger slice of the weight matrix onto silicon, dampening the need to shuffle data from off‑chip DRAM.

---

## 3. Sketching a Dedicated 1.58‑Bit Accelerator

Below is a high‑level architectural outline that aligns with the empirical findings of the paper.

| Component | What It Does | Why It Helps |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Wide SIMD lanes (e.g., 1024‑bit vector)** | Each lane processes $1024 / 8 = 128$ 8‑bit activations in parallel | Enables thousands of integer operations per cycle, saturated by the bit‑width of the weights |
| **Adder tree per lane (Wallace/Carry‑Lookahead)** | Accumulates the signed sums of the $-1/0/+1$ multipliers | Replaces an expensive MAC tree with a shallow, low‑power adder network |
| **Zero‑skip logic** | Detects $0$ weights and bypasses the corresponding add | Cuts unnecessary cycles, further curbing energy |
| **On‑chip SRAM with 1.58‑bit compression** | Stores weight matrices and small activation buffers | Eliminates off‑chip traffic for most of the weight set |
| **8‑bit signed accumulator** | Holds partial sums before they go to the next layer | Keeps register width small, allowing higher clock density |
| **Batch‑wise inter‑layer pipelining** | Keeps the vector units busy across layers | Improves throughput, critical for the 8.9× batch size advantage shown in the paper |

### 3.1 Example 2‑bit Kernel on Ladder
The paper integrated the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder for inference.  Extending it to 1.58‑bit is largely a matter of:

1. Removing the multiplier stage entirely.  
2. Adding an extra comparator for the zero weight state.  
3. Scaling the accumulation to 8‑bit signed arithmetic.

The result is a **pure‑addition accelerator** that can be run on commodity 7 nm FFDs with only the usual logic and SRAM, but no costly multiplier arrays.

---

## 4. Scaling Law & Hardware Efficiency

The BitNet b1.58 paper proposed a new scaling law where a **70 B 1.58‑bit model outperforms a 13 B FP16 model** in latency, memory, and energy consumption.  This has concrete hardware implications:

- **On‑chip SRAM can hold the entire 70 B weight matrix** in roughly the same silicon real estate as a 13 B FP16 weight matrix.  
- **SIMD width may be tuned to match the larger model’s matrix dimensions** without touching multiplier logic.  
- **Thermal budgets** shrink because the proportion of high‑power gold‑nets (multipliers) disappears.

The practical upshot: a *single* chip can now serve workloads that would otherwise require *multiple* GPUs or a large server.  For edge or mobile, this means a 70 B LLM can be executed with the same power envelope as a 3 B FP16 model—a dramatic win if you need a multi‑turn assistant on a phone.

---

## 5. Real‑World Pathways: From Groq to Next‑Gen ASICs

### 5.1 Groq’s LPU and Beyond
Groq’s **LPU** architecture already exploits the idea of *stateless*, *high‑speed, memory‑tight* compute units for LLM inference.  Their system demonstrates 2‑bit kernels and fast data shuttling.  Extending this paradigm to **1.58‑bit weights** would:

- Reduce the die area devoted to DSP blocks (≈ 70 % lower).  
- Increase the number of active cores for a fixed die area.  
- Lower the **power‑to‑performance** ratio by 2–3×.

The paper’s claim that "BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement" aligns with making LPUs an attractive consumer product.

### 5.2 Custom ASICs
A bespoke ASIC focusing on 1-bit matrix multiplication can push the bandwagon:

- **Mass‑parallel adder arrays** that exploit the locality of ternary weights.  
- **Zero‑ing skip** integrated into the data‑flow, so coupling an activation to a zero weight never triggers an adder.  
- **8‑bit quantized activations** can be streamed through a narrow input buffer, requiring minimal bandwidth.  
- **System‑level serialization** that groups invocations of the same weight slice, minimizing DRAM refresh activity.

Such a chip would also benefit the *Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)* attack mentioned in the paper: the reduced memory footprint flattens the cost of swapping experts in and out of on‑chip storage.

---

## 6. Practical Take‑aways for Hardware Designers

- **Drop multipliers**: design a *pure adder network*.  
- **Compress weights** to 1.58‑bits, plan SRAM capacities accordingly.  
- **Add zero‑skip logic** in the data‑path to avoid unnecessary adds.  
- **Stream low‑bit activations**; 8‑bit signed accumulators suffice.  
- **Profile the “active‑area”**: measure the fraction of time you actually need to process weight intervals, and allocate that budget to adders and SRAM rather than MIMOs.  

Follow the empirical results: **the same or better performance** can be achieved with **half the memory** and far **lower latency**, making it a compelling target for next‑generation LLM accelerators.

---

## 7. Conclusion

1.58‑bit LLMs transform the inference pipeline: no multipliers, fewer 16‑bit FLOPs, compressed memory, and an energy profile that is orders of magnitude more favorable.  By following the architectural principles above, a dedicated accelerator can bring these gains to production: delivering larger models, lower cost, and sustainable operation.  As the community gravitates toward more sustainable, edge‑friendly AI, a silicon stack built around 1‑bit matrix multiplication is not just an academic curiosity—it's the next logical step in the evolution of AI hardware.



# MoE Meets 1‑Bit: Lighter, Faster, and More Efficient Expert Models

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) already trim FLOPs by routing each token to only a few “expert” networks, but the huge memory requirements and the constant data shuttling across accelerators often negate the theoretical speedup.  
A recent 1‑bit Large Language Model (LLM) called **BitNet b1.58** shows that if we quantize every weight to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}, a single expert can stay on a single chip, and the activation traffic between chips disappears. The result is a dramatically lighter, faster, and greener MoE deployment.

---  

## 1. The Backdrop: MoE and the 1‑Bit Revolution  

### 1‑Bit LLMs  
* **BitNet b1.58**  
  * Every weight is ternary (−1, 0, +1) – a **1.58‑bit** representation (since \(\log_{2}3\approx1.58\)).  
  * Activations remain 8‑bit – the smallest precision that still retains raw language modeling performance.  
  * Trained from scratch on a 100 billion‑token RedPajama corpus; matches full‑precision FP16 LLaMA baselines from 3 B parameters onward.  

### MoE – The Promises and the Pitfalls  
* **Strengths** – A token is processed by *k* experts (often 2–4) instead of all *N* experts, cutting FLOPs roughly by a factor of *k*.  
* **Weaknesses** –  
  * **Memory blowup**: each expert’s weights and activations must be stored somewhere; a 70 B MoE may need dozens of GPUs just to hold the model.  
  * **Communication hot spots**: routing requires moving hidden states between chips, causing latency and energy penalties.

The sweet spot is a model that is *small* in GEMM size **and** *fast* in inference. BitNet supplies exactly that.

---  

## 2. How 1‑bit Weights Lighten MoE

| Metric | FP16 LLM | BitNet b1.58 |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **Weight size per parameter** | \(2\;\text{bytes}\) | \(\approx 0.22\;\text{bytes}\) |
| **GPU memory per 3 B LLM** | 12 GB | 3.5 GB |
| **Matrix‑multiply energy** | ‑ | 71.4× lower |

* The **3.55× memory reduction** directly translates to fewer GPUs or a single large‑frequency chip hosting the entire model.  
* **Motivation for MoE**: each expert can be a *tiny* BitNet, so a 10‑expert MoE is only ≈30 GB stored instead of ≈300 GB in FP16.  
* **No cross‑chip activation traffic**: activations are 8‑bit, and ternary weights allow **integer‑only** matrix multiplications—no costly FP bridges.

### Why the 0‑weight Helps  
Adding the zero‑weight (\(0\)) to the binary set has two effects:  
  
1. **Feature filtering** – an expert can turn off a subset of feature dimensions, tightening the model’s expressivity.  
2. **Computation simplification** – matrix multiplication reduces to integer addition for non‑zero weights; zero‑weights skip operations entirely.  

---  

## 3. Latency, Throughput, and Energy – The Numbers

> **Table: 70 B Parameter Experiments**  
> *Latency per token* | *Memory* | *Throughput*  
> **FP16** | 4.5 ms | 80 GB | BATCH = 8  
> **BitNet b1.58** | 1.1 ms | 20 GB | BATCH = 88  

* **Speed‑up**: \(4.1\times\) faster for the 70 B encoder‑decoder.  
* **Memory Savings**: \(4\times\) less GPU RAM, enabling a larger batch.  
* **Energy**: 71.4× lower arithmetic energy in the critical matrix‑mul stage.  
* **Pipeline Gains**: Cross‑chip communication, which can occupy >30 % of latency in a MoE setup, is essentially gone.

These gains scale with model size – the larger the expert pool, the larger the relative improvement.

---  

## 4. New Scaling Law for 1‑Bit MoE

BitNet b1.58 introduces a *Pareto‑improvement* scaling relationship:

* **13 B 1‑bit** ≈ **3 B FP16** in performance + *lower* cost.  
* **30 B 1‑bit** ≈ **7 B FP16** (same trend).  
* **70 B 1‑bit** ≈ **13 B FP16**.

Thus, a 30 B 1‑bit MoE runs *on the same hardware* that would handle a 7 B full‑precision MoE, yet with superior quality. In practice, this means we can put a 30 B “expert” zone on a single server‑class GPU and avoid the cross‑rack traffic that plagues current MoE deployments.

---  

## 5. Practical Implications for MoE Deployment

### 5.1 Reduced Memory Footprint  
* **Single‑chip MoE** – With each expert ≈0.3 GB at 3 B and 8–32‑bit activations, dozens of experts fit inside a 80‑GB GPU.  
* **Simplified partitioning** – Off‑loading parts of the model to host CPU or disk becomes unnecessary.

### 5.2 Lower Inter‑Chip Overhead  
* **No activation shuffling** – In a current MoE, after a routing decision the hidden state is serialized and sent over PCIe or RMA; with 1‑bit activations the serialization costs drop dramatically.  
* **Reliable latency** – Stochastic routing is eliminated; the inference pipeline becomes deterministic and easily predictable.

### 5.3 Energy‑Friendly Acceleration  
* **Integer‑only compute** – Most modern GPUs and custom ASICs already have efficient 8‑bit integer units (e.g., NVIDIA Ampere’s INT8 / Tensor Cores).  
* **Energy cost** – The 71.4× reductions are expressed in terms of *arithmetic operations energy*; early hardware experiments suggest overall *>30×* inference energy saving.

### 5.4 Architecture‑Friendly Design  
* **Simple kernels** – Existing 2‑bit kernels (e.g., Ladder) already support 1‑bit tensors; adaptation to 1.58‑bit is trivial.  
* **Hardware knobs** – Future chips can add native ternary (−1, 0, +1) multiply‑by‑add units, reducing latency further.

---  

## 6. Design Considerations for an 1‑Bit MoE

| Design Aspect | What to Do |
|---------------|------------|
| **Expert size** | Aim for **1–2 B** parameters per expert; fits on most GPUs. |
| **Routing** | Use a learnable soft‑max or top‑k gating, but keep the **activation bit‑width at 8 bits**. |
| **Quantization** | Apply **absmean** scaling for weights; keep activations on the raw 8‑bit range to avoid zero‑point shift. |
| **Embedding** | Keep embeddings full‑precision if necessary (they can be pre‑computed or cached). |
| **Fine‑tuning** | Start from a full‑precision checkpoint, then train down to 1-bit from scratch (bitwise back‑prop tricks). |

---  

## 7. Future Horizons

1. **1‑Bit MoE for Long‑Context** – Since activations are 8‑bit, memory for KV caches halves, giving a **2×** increase in context length per GPU.  
2. **Edge & Mobile** – 1‑bit models are CPU‑friendly and drastically reduce power; a 3‑B MoE could run on a mobile SoC.  
3. **Hardware‑First MoE** – Inspired by Groq’s LPUs, design a *Ternary‑Processing Unit (TPU)* that would natively execute \((-1,0,1)\) tensors, further reducing voltage and clock frequency.  
4. **Hybrid Quantization** – Mix 1‑bit experts with “hot” full‑precision gating heads to balance expressivity and efficiency.

---  

## 8. Bottom Line

The combination of **MoE** and **1‑bit quantization** produces a new **Pareto frontier** for generative AI:

| Feature | 1‑bit MoE | Full‑precision MoE |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| *Memory* | 3.5× lower | Baseline |
| *Latency* | 4–5× faster | Baseline |
| *Energy* | 70× lower (matrix‑mul) | Baseline |
| *Scalability* | Single‑chip training/inference | Multi‑node cluster |

With these gains, we can now imagine scaling the next generation of LLMs – multi‑hundred‑billion‑token, 70 + B‑parameter MoEs – on modest hardware while keeping inference cost in check.  

**MoE meets 1‑bit** is not just a research curiosity; it is a viable production path where *simplicity of integer arithmetic meets the routing efficiency of experts*, delivering head‑on performance improvements with a fraction of the hardware burden.
