\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Unlocking 1.58‑Bit LLMs: How Ternary Weights Give Transformers a Performance Boost

Large language models (LLMs) have grown so quickly that a single parameter can now cost a fraction of a diesel per gigabyte of inference traffic. Beyond the obvious economics, the energy footprint of state‑of‑the‑art transformers has become a serious bottleneck for both data‑center operators and on‑device applications. A promising answer comes from the researchers behind *BitNet b1.58*, a new family of LLMs where every weight is reduced to just **three** possible values: -1, 0, +1.  

In this post we unpack why that tiny change matters, how the quantization process works, and why the resulting models can match or even beat full‑precision baselines while using far less memory, energy and latency.

---

## 1. Why shrink weights to just three symbols?

A *bit* is the smallest unit of digital data, normally used to encode two states. In traditional LLMs each weight is stored in 16‑bit floating‑point, which explains why 3‑billion‑parameter models already require Cloud GPUs with tens of gigabytes of VRAM.  
Reducing weights to a ternary set keeps the mathematical power of a full Transformer but drops the *real‑valued* multiplication to a handful of integer operations:

| Operation | Full‑precision  | BitNet b1.58 |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| Multiplication | FP16 | 0 or a sign “$\pm$1” shift (effectively an integer add) |
| Addition      | FP16 | INT8 add |

Because matrix multiplication dominates the compute, the savings cascade into lower runtime, lower memory bandwidth, and dramatically fewer energy‑consuming floating‑point units on every chip.

### The extra “0” value
The original 1‑bit idea used only –1 or +1. By adding a third value, 0, *BitNet b1.58* introduces explicit *feature filtering*. If a weight is zero the corresponding connection contributes nothing to the hidden state, giving the network a built‑in sparsity mechanism that can be learned during training. This extra expressive power is what allows a 3‑billion‑parameter *b1.58* model to catch up to a 3‑billion‑parameter FP16 baseline in perplexity and, at 3.9 B, even outperform it on several zero‑shot NLP benchmarks.

---

## 2. The absmean quantization process

Simply chopping down a real‑valued weight matrix to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ without care would erase signal that was essential for learning. *BitNet b1.58* therefore normalises each weight matrix before rounding, using an **absmean** scaling factor:

\[
\widetilde{W}
    = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\,\gamma + \epsilon\,}, -1, 1\right),\quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\!\sum_{ij}\!|W_{ij}|
\]

* \(\gamma\) is the average absolute value of the unquantised weights in an \(n\times m\) matrix.
* \(\epsilon\) is a tiny constant that prevents division‑by‑zero.
* \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \displaystyle\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x)))\).

The division rescales the weight distribution so that, on average, the magnitude of each entry is close to 1. After the clip‑round step each entry is forced into \{-1,0,+1\}. Training then proceeds exactly as usual (back‑propagation, Adam, etc.) with a straight‑through estimator that passes the gradient of the rounded weights as if they were continuous.

For activations, the paper keeps them in 8‑bit integer form. Instead of mapping to a $[0,Q_b]$ range (which requires a zero‑point offset), it scales to a symmetric $[-Q_b,\,Q_b]$ interval. This simplification cuts a branch in the activation workflow and eliminates a small source of quantisation distortion without hurting performance.

---

## 3. Architecture and training setup

* **Backbone**: The authors instantiate a LLaMA‑style Transformer, keeping the popular LLaMA‑alike design choices: RMSNorm, SwiGLU gated linear units, rotary positional embeddings, and *no* bias terms.  
* **Linear layers**: Conventional `nn.Linear` layers are replaced by `BitLinear` that accept 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations.  
* **From‑scratch training**: Models were trained on 100 billion tokens of the RedPajama dataset, identical to the hidden‑size–tokens configuration used for LLaMA.  
* **Hardware**: Inference was benchmarked on NVIDIA A100 GPUs using the FasterTransformer engine coupled with a 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder project. All numbers presented below assume an 80‑GB device and a 512‑token context.

---

## 4. Performance – Perplexity & Zero‑shot

| Model Size | Shape | Bits | Perplexity on WikiText2/C4 | Zero‑shot accuracy (average over tasks) |
|------------|-------|------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| 3 B | b1.58 | 1.58 | ≈ matches FP16 | ≈ matches |
| 3.9 B | b1.58 | 1.58 | ≈ slightly lower (better) | ≈ better |

* **Key takeaway**: Starting at 3 B parameters, *b1.58* reaches parity with a full‑precision FP16 baseline in perplexity. The 3.9 B version even surpasses it on several zero‑shot tasks while using less memory and producing output faster.

---

## 5. Efficiency metrics

### Memory & Latency
| Model | FP16 (A100) | b1.58 | Latency ratio | Memory ratio |
|-------|-------------|-------|--------------|---------------|
| 3 B   | –           | –     | 2.71× faster | 3.55× smaller |
| 3.9 B | –           | –     | 2.4× faster  | 3.32× smaller |

* The speedup comes almost entirely from eliminating the multiplication unit in the linear operators.
* For larger models the advantage grows—the 70 B *b1.58* version runs 4.1× faster than a 70 B FP16 model and utilizes more than 3× less VRAM.

### Energy
Using the reported 7 nm 65‑W energy model:
* The matrix‑multiplication core of a 7 B *b1.58* saves **71.4×** in arithmetic‑energy consumption compared to FP16.
* End‑to‑end energy for a 512‑token batch drops proportionally with model size, making *b1.58* the most energy‑efficient large‑scale LLM tested.

### Throughput
With two 80‑GB A100 GPUs:
* 70 B *b1.58* can handle an 8.9× bigger batch than a 70 B FP16 model.  
* This translates to roughly an **8× increase in token‑per‑second** for real‑world workloads.

---

## 6. Scaling law

The authors crystallise a new *efficiency scaling law*:

* **13 B b1.58** ≈ **3 B FP16** (latency < memory < energy).  
* **30 B b1.58** ≈ **7 B FP16**.  
* **70 B b1.58** ≈ **13 B FP16**.

In other words, you can keep the same inference budget (or even lower) while dramatically boosting the effective model size.

---

## 7. Beyond 2‑bit: Training with 2 T tokens

In a further experiment the authors trained *b1.58* on **2 trillion tokens** following the StableLM‑3B data recipe. When evaluated on benchmarks like Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC‑Easy, the *b1.58* family outperformed StableLM 3B by a noticeable margin, demonstrating that sparse ternary weights don't compromise generalisation at massive data scales.

---

## 8. Implications & Future Work

### 1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)
MoE models already cut FLOPs by routing inputs to a subset of experts, but they still suffer from high memory use and inter‑chip communication. With *b1.58*, each expert’s weight tensor shrinks almost three‑fold, potentially allowing many more experts to sit on a single chip and eliminating transfer overhead.

### Native Long‑Sequence Inference
The 8‑bit activations mean the KV cache occupies far less bandwidth. As a consequence, a *b1.58* LLM can double the context length for the same memory budget—critical for tasks that demand thousands of tokens of history.

### Edge & Mobile Deployment
A 1.58‑bit model runs profitably on CPU or low‑power GPUs because the compute shifts from floating‑point to integer arithmetic. The low memory footprint is ideal for on‑device assistants, creative tools, or privacy‑preserving inference inside mobile phones.

### Dedicated Hardware
Hardware vendors like Groq have already demonstrated Specialized Process Units for large‑scale inference. Extending those designs to xor‑like addition units that operate on ternary weights could unlock a new line of chips (we call them *LPU‑0/1*), where the compute core degenerates to a handful of adders rather than costly multipliers.

---

## 9. Take‑away

*BitNet b1.58* shows that *simply by forcing each weight to be –1, 0, or +1* you can preserve, and sometimes even improve, the expressive power of a Transformer, while **cutting latency, memory, and energy by more than a factor of three**. This opens up a new “1‑bit era” for LLMs, where the theoretical limit is not speeding up computation but power‑constrained scaling. The title refers not to a black‑box flow but to a principled **absmean** quantisation that preserves magnitude and a deliberately added zero to enable feature pruning.

If you’re an engineer stuck with budgeted GPU budgets, an influencer of cloud benchmarks, or an enthusiast of low‑power inference, keep an eye on *b1.58* models. They promise the same performance as today’s 16‑bit LLMs, but without the weight and wattage. The future of language models may well be **ternary** rather than **floating‑point**.
