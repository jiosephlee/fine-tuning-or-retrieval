# New Scaling Laws for Language Models: What 1.58‑Bit LLMs Teach Us About Size and Cost

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become the standard bearers of natural‑language performance, but their soaring size and the resulting energy drain have sparked a quest for more efficient architectures.  
The latest contender — **BitNet b1.58** — takes the 1‑bit / 2‑bit quantisation trend to a new level: every weight in the Transformer is restricted to the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), a humble “1.58‑bit” per parameter.  Despite this aggressive quantisation, the model matches (and in some cases surpasses) full‑precision FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream zero‑shot accuracy, while dramatically cutting latency, memory, throughput, and energy.

Below we unpack the key ideas, show the newly‑found scaling law, and give a recipe for researchers who want to balance model size, compute budget, and deployment strategy.

---

## 1. Why 1.58‑Bit? A Quick Look at BitNet b1.58

* **Ternary weights**: By projecting every weight \(W\) into \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) using the *absmean* quantisation,
  \[
  \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right), \qquad
  \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
  \]
  we only need to store 1.58 bits per weight (one bit for sign, one bit for “zero‑ness” plus a small overhead for the scaling factor).

* **Integer‑only matmul**: Matrix multiplications – the bulk of LLM FLOPs – become a chain of integer additions. Multiplication is essentially eliminated, which means much lower arithmetic energy and higher throughput on modern GPUs or dedicated accelerators.

* **8‑bit activations**: Activations stay quantised to 8 bits, trading a modest accuracy hit for a huge reduction in bandwidth when exchanging KV caches and intermediate tensors.

* **LLaMA‑style backbone**: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings and bias removal keep the architecture lightweight and easy to drop into existing open‑source toolchains (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp).

---

## 2. The New Scaling Law

BitNet b1.58 has revealed a *performance‑to‑cost* scaling relationship that sets a new baseline for LLM designers.  
Instead of simply adding more layers or wider dimensions, we can add *more ternary parameters* while still gaining net efficiency.

| **1.58‑Bit Model Size** | **Equivalent 16‑Bit Size** | **Cost Advantage** |
|------------------------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| 13 B                   | 3 B                       | Lower memory, lower latency, lower energy consumption |
| 30 B                   | 7 B                       | Same as above |
| 70 B                   | 13 B                      | Same as above |

### Takeaway
BitNet b1.58 at 13 B is **more efficient** (in latency, memory, and energy) than a 3 B FP16 model, even though it has roughly **4.3× more parameters**.  
The same pattern holds at larger scales: a 30 B 1.58‑bit model beats a 7 B FP16 version, and a 70 B 1.58‑bit model beats a 13 B FP16 one.

This is not a simple “more parameters ≈ more cost” trade‑off. By reducing the bit‑width per weight and replacing expensive floating‑point multiplies with cheap integer adds, the **per‑parameter cost** drops dramatically.

Additionally, the 3.9 B 1.58‑bit model **outperforms** a 3 B FP16 LLaMA baseline *without* any extra cost—showing that the extra 0‑weight capability improves representational power.

---

## 3. How to Use These Scaling Laws

### 3.1 Model Selection
| Situation | Recommendation | Why |
|-----------|----------------|-----|
| You need a LLaMA‑style 3 B model for a cost‑constrained cloud deployment | Use a 3 B or 3.9 B BitNet b1.58 | Same or better answer quality, ~2.7× faster, ~3.5× less memory |
| You want a high‑capacity LLM but cannot afford the memory of a 70 B FP16 model | Try a 70 B BitNet b1.58 | Same or lower memory, triple the throughput, and ~4× energy savings |
| You’re designing a data‑center inference service | Use the table to pick the smallest 1.58‑bit model that meets the latency budget | Keeps GPUs idle longer and reduces power draw |

**Rule of thumb:** for every 3 B increase in a full‑precision model you can replace the cost with about 13 B of 1.58‑bit, and you’ll still be operating at a lower latency and memory footprint.

### 3.2 Compute Budget
* **Latency** – Each token is processed faster: a 3 B 1.58‑bit model is 2.7× faster than its FP16 counterpart; at 70 B, the speed‑up climbs to 4.1×.
* **Memory** – The ternary weights occupy only 1.58 bits per weight. Even a 70 B 1.58‑bit model consumes less than a 13 B FP16 model.
* **Energy** – Arithmetic‑energy models estimate that matrix‑multiplies in 1.58‑bit cost only ~1/71 of FP16 on modern 7 nm chips. The end‑to‑end inference on a 512‑token prompt drops by an order of magnitude as the model size grows.

When planning infra, you can now budget for **GPU RAM** and **power** using the equivalence table rather than the raw parameter count.

### 3.3 Deployment Strategy
1. **Early‑stage prototyping** – Start with a 3 B BitNet b1.58. It runs light on a single GPU and gives a fair estimate of target performance.
2. **Scaling** – If the task demands more capacity, scale to the next point on the table (13 B, 30 B, 70 B) while exploiting the lower bit‑width to fit multiple models on the same hardware.
3. **Edge / mobile** – The 1.58‑bit system is especially friendly for CPUs and custom ASICs: integer‑only arithmetic maps cleanly onto SIMD and embedded accelerators. Combine with 8‑bit activations to double the effective KV cache size.
4. **MoE / mix‑and‑match** – Because the weights are so compact, multiple expert sub‑models can now fit on a single chip, reducing inter‑device communication overhead.

---

## 4. Training and Token Scaling

BitNet b1.58 has been trained *from scratch* on **100 B tokens** of the RedPajama corpus.  It retains full‑precision behaviour even when pushed to **2 T tokens** (StableLM‑3B‑recipe). In zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑Easy, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ), the 2 T‑trained 1.58‑bit model not only matched but **surpassed** the full‑precision baseline across all tasks.  

Thus the scaling law applies not only to inference cost but also to training scalability: you can afford to grow the token budget without compromising the energy or cost advantages.

---

## 5. Looking Ahead

* **Hardware co‑design** – The inherent “no‑multiply” matmul invites novel accelerator designs (e.g., LPUs from Groq), potentially achieving even higher energy efficiency.
* **Long‑sequence support** – With 8‑bit activations, the KV cache shrinks by half; lossless compression to 4 bits could enable *native* long‑context models without extra memory.
* **MoE‑ready** – Low‑bit, low‑memory experts mean fewer chips and no expensive communication; the 1.58‑bit paradigm could make MoE practical at scale.
* **Edge & mobile** – CPU‑friendly integer math, combined with aggressive compression, pushes LLMs onto phones and IoT devices.

---

## 6. Bottom Line

BitNet b1.58 redefines the “efficiency frontier” for language models:

- **Param‑efficacy**: You can double or triple the parameter count while *reducing* memory and latency.
- **Energy economy**: Integer‑only arithmetic saves roughly **70×** on matrix‑multiply energy.
- **Thorough support**: Seamless on common open‑source stacks; ready for deployment and further research.

When planning the next generation of language models, no longer does “more parameters” automatically connote “more cost.”  By embracing 1.58‑bit quantisation and its new scaling law, researchers and practitioners can build larger, faster, and greener LLMs without sacrificing quality.
