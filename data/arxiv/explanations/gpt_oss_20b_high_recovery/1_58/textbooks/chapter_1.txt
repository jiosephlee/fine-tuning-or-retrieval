\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 1: **1. Introduction and Motivation**

Large language models (LLMs) have surged in size and power over the past decade, enabling remarkable performance on a wide spectrum of natural‑language tasks. This rapid growth, however, brings mounting challenges for deployment: the sheer volume of floating‑point parameters, the cost of transferring those parameters over memory interfaces, and the significant energy required for floating‑point arithmetic. In response, researchers are exploring ultra‑low‑precision alternatives that can retain the expressive power of full‑precision models while drastically cutting latency, memory, and energy footprints. The paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits* presents a concrete instantiation of this vision with BitNet b1.58, a transformer whose every weight assumes only the values \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Below, we motivate the work by laying out the scale of modern LLMs, their computational costs, the shortcomings of conventional quantization, the emerging low‑bit research landscape, and the specific contributions of BitNet b1.58.

# Exponential scale of modern LLMs

LLMs have grown from the order of millions of parameters in early architectures to tens of billions in state‑of‑the‑art systems. This exponential increase is driven by the observation that more parameters tend to translate into more nuanced language understanding when trained on ever larger corpora. The paper highlights this trend as a core motivation: while larger models unlock new capabilities, they also demand proportionally more compute and memory, which in turn translates into higher latency for inference and greater carbon footprint during training and serving. The exponential scale thus creates a pressing need for inference–friendly architectures that can deliver comparable performance with a fraction of the resource consumption.

# Latency, memory, and energy costs of FP16/BF16 models

Full‑precision LLMs in practice operate on 16‑bit floating‑point values, either FP16 or BF16. The majority of a transformer’s runtime is occupied by matrix–matrix multiplications, each of which requires millions of floating‑point additions and multiplications. Because floating‑point units consume significant power—especially for multiplications—the energy cost scales with the number of such operations. Moreover, the latency of each operation is limited by the memory bandwidth needed to shuttle operands from DRAM to on‑chip SRAM or registers. Large models exacerbate both energy and bandwidth bottlenecks: more parameters mean larger activations, larger weight buffers, and more transfers. Consequently, state‑of‑the‑art systems report that a single inference can consume dozens of megatransfers and multiple megajoules of energy, limiting their viability in cost‑constrained or energy‑limited environments.

# Limitations of post‑training quantization

A popular way to lower precision is to perform *post‑training quantization* (PTQ). PTQ typically quantizes weights and activations from FP16 to a lower bit‑width (for example, 4‑bit) after the model has been trained. While PTQ is simple and widely deployed in industry, the paper stresses its sub‑optimality: because quantization boundaries are chosen without taking the training dynamics into account, PTQ can incur a noticeable loss in performance. Furthermore, PTQ requires a large number of calibration samples and careful handling of activation ranges, making it more of a workaround than a principled design choice. The authors point out that, despite the widespread use of techniques such as *smoothquant*, *GPTQ*, *quip*, and *quip‑sharp*, PTQ still falls short of fully matching full‑precision performance, especially for the most recent, largest models.

# Overview of low‑bit model research

Recent research has explored the feasibility of training models from scratch with very low precision. BitNet, the precursor to BitNet b1.58, demonstrated that a transformer with ternary weights \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) could be trained end‑to‑end and still perform competitively. Its key insight is that matrix multiplication can be reduced to integer addition when weights are ternary, eliminating the need for multiplication entirely and dramatically lowering arithmetic cost. The paper explains that this cheap integer operation enables new computational paradigms and specialized hardware that exploit bit‑parallelism or branchless addition. Other works in the low‑bit space—such as SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and QuIP—often rely on PTQ, whereas BitNet trains directly with a custom quantization function, thereby preserving the network’s expressive power. In addition, the authors extend the BitNet framework to a *1.58‑bit* variant, introducing an additional zero state to provide explicit feature filtering. This modification yields a quantization function based on the \emph{absmean} technique:

\[
\widetilde{W}
    = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1 \right),
\quad
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)
    = \max(a, \min(b, \operatorname{round}(x))),
\quad
\gamma
    = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\!\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|.
\]

By scaling each weight matrix by its mean absolute value before clipping, the training procedure natively steers parameters toward the ternary set, and the inclusion of zero allows the network to prune unnecessary features during forward computation.

# Contributions and objectives of BitNet b1.58

BitNet b1.58 builds upon BitNet’s low‑bit philosophy and targets full architectural parity with a standard FP16 LLM of the same size. Its primary contributions are:

1. **Ternary Weights with Performance Parity.** Every weight in BitNet b1.58 takes one of the values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), a *1.58‑bit* representation in the binary system. Training from scratch, the authors show that, starting at a 3‑billion‑parameter scale, the 1.58‑bit model achieves perplexity and zero‑shot task performance indistinguishable from a full‑precision baseline.

2. **Explicit Feature Filtering.** The optional zero weight lets the transformer selectively zero out irrelevant projections during matrix multiplication. This filtering effect improves modeling capacity beyond the benefit of mere parameter compression.

3. **Computational Efficiency.** Because the matrix multiplication reduces to integer additions, the arithmetic operation count drops dramatically; the model requires no floating‑point multipliers. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 is \(\gtrsim 2.7\times\) faster and uses \(\sim 3.5\times\) less GPU memory than an equivalent FP16 LLaMA transformer.

4. **Reduced Energy Footprint.** Energy estimates (based on 7 nm arithmetic energy models) suggest a 71.4‑fold reduction in arithmetic‑only energy for 7‑billion‑parameter models, a consequence of replacing FP16 addition/multiplication with INT8 addition.

5. **Hardware‑friendly Design.** By adopting LLaMA‑compatible components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding—and removing biases, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular frameworks (Hugging Face, vLLM, llama.cpp) with minor modifications. The new integer‑centric computation invites the design of specialized 1‑bit accelerators that can further accelerate inference.

Collectively, these contributions establish BitNet b1.58 as a *Pareto improvement* over current full‑precision LLMs: equal or better accuracy, vastly lower latency, memory, and energy consumption. The work also introduces a new scaling law: 13 billion‑parameter 1.58‑bit models outperform 3 billion‑parameter FP16 models in latency, memory, and energy, while 70 billion‑parameter 1.58‑bit models surpass 13 billion‑parameter FP16 baselines. These observations highlight the practical feasibility of deploying massive LLMs in resource‑constrained settings, and point toward a vibrant research direction in 1‑bit and low‑bit deep learning.
