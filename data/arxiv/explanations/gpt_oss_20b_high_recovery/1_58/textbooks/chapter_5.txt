\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 5: 5. Benchmarking: Perplexity, Zero‑Shot Tasks, and Experimental Setup  

# Perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 datasets  

Perplexity is a fundamental metric for evaluating language models.  It is defined as the exponential of the average negative log‑likelihood of a test sequence, and it quantifies how well a probabilistic model predicts unseen data.  If a model assigns higher probability mass to the correct tokens, the average cross‑entropy loss decreases, and the perplexity falls.  In formulae, for a sequence of length \(N\) the perplexity is  

\[
\mathrm{PPL} \;=\; \exp\!\left( - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(x_i \mid x_{<i}) \right),
\]

where \(p_{\theta}\) denotes the model’s probability estimate.  Because perplexity is an exponentiation of the cross‑entropy, it can be interpreted as the “average number of choices” the model faces at each prediction step; thus a lower perplexity signals a closer match to the true language distribution.

In the work on BitNet b1.58, two standard language modeling datasets are used to compute this value: WikiText2 and C4.  WikiText2 is a curated collection of approximately 2.8 M labeled tokens pulled from Wikipedia; it contains a relatively balanced vocabulary and serves as a benchmark for long‑context modeling.  C4, on the other hand, is a massive, publicly available corpus derived from Common Crawl, spanning roughly 800 M tokens after filtering and cleaning.  The two datasets differ in size, domain coverage, and noise level, providing a robust gauge of a model’s capacity to generalize.

The BitNet b1.58 authors pre‑train their models on the RedPajama dataset for 100 B tokens.  After training, they evaluate validation perplexity on the last checkpoint of WikiText2 and C4.  To avoid leakage, the two datasets are untouched during training.  Crucially, the perplexity computation is performed with the same tokenization and vocabulary as the model was trained on.  For each dataset, a single pass through the entire validation split is made, the log‑probabilities of successive tokens are accumulated, and the negative average is exponentiated.  This procedure produces a single scalar that can be compared across full‑precision (FP16) baselines and 1.58‑bit bit‑compressed models.

Because the BitNet b1.58 model uses 1.58‑bit ternary weights and 8‑bit activations but matches the FP16 LLaMA baseline in perplexity when the model size reaches 3 B parameters, the perplexity curves in the paper demonstrate a new scaling law.  In particular, the curve for a 3 B BitNet b1.58 lies very close to (or indistinguishable from) the 3 B FP16 curve, while the curves for 7 B, 13 B, and even 70 B show BitNet surpassing their FP16 counterparts in both perplexity and resource usage.  These results underscore that the 1.58‑bit representation is not a mere toy compression; rather, it preserves the statistical fidelity of language modeling across a wide range of scales.

# Zero‑shot evaluation suite: ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ  

Beyond perplexity, real‑world language models are judged on how well they reason, answer questions, and exhibit knowledge.  The BitNet b1.58 paper therefore adopts a battery of zero‑shot benchmarks, meaning that the model receives no task‑specific fine‑tuning and is simply evaluated on prompts designed to probe reasoning.

- **ARC‑Easy**: The “AI2 Reasoning Challenge – Easy” comprises multiple‑choice science questions drawn from middle‑school curricula.  The model must select the correct answer among four candidates without any exposure to the human‑generated hints.

- **ARC‑Challenge**: A more difficult subset of the same problem set, featuring higher‑order reasoning and ambiguous data.  Performance on this set indicates the model’s ability to handle complex inference.

- **HellaSwag**: A large collection of everyday, commonsense scenarios.  For each prompt, the model chooses from four plausible continuations.  The score is computed as the proportion of times the model picks the most intelligible choice.  In zero‑shot settings, a model’s performance reflects its grasp of trivial common sense.

- **Winogrande**: A collection built upon Winograd Schema for coreference resolution.  The task requires identifying which of two candidates (often pronouns) the model attributes a given referent to, again in a multiple‑choice format.

- **PIQA**: The “Physical Interaction Question Answering” dataset tests physical commonsense reasoning.  Prompts ask which of two actions will produce a realistic physical outcome.  The model’s predictions are evaluated against ground‑truth answer keys.

- **OpenbookQA**: A dataset that isolates factual knowledge questions that can be answered with a small set of facts (the “open book”).  The question is usually multiple‑choice and the model must demonstrate its ability to retrieve or infer facts.

- **BoolQ**: Binary (true/false) answer questions derived from open‑domain paragraphs.  The model’s predictions are turned into binary labels and compared against human annotations.

Each benchmark is assessed using the standard accuracy metric.  Because all tasks are multiple‑choice, the model’s predicted probability for each candidate is rounded to a hard choice, and the proportion of correct choices yields the accuracy.  The zero‑shot nature of the evaluation highlights that the model’s internal representations of knowledge, as gathered during training, are sufficient for generalization.

# Use of lm‑evaluation‑harness for metric extraction  

The LM‑Evaluation‑Harness is an open‑source framework that orchestrates the execution of a wide array of language‑model benchmarks.  It takes as input a model wrapper, a batch of prompts, and a set of task specifications.  For BitNet b1.58’s evaluation, the harness is configured to:

1. Load the 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations, shipping the model to the GPU as a sequence‑to‑sequence head with a vanilla decoder.
2. For each benchmark, send the pre‑tokenized prompt to the model and capture the softmax probabilities over the vocabulary.
3. Translate the token probabilities into logits over the candidate answers (e.g., mapping each choice to the corresponding token or string), often via a custom post‑processing step in the harness.
4. Compute the chosen metric (accuracy, pass@k, or a custom statistical function) defined in the harness’s task configuration.

The harness also logs GPU memory usage and inference latency per token, which the BitNet authors inherited to build their runtime profile.  The harness’s architecture ensures reproducible metric extraction across experiments—a vital feature when comparing models that differ only in precision.  Concretely, to obtain zero‑shot accuracies, the harness runs each task over the test split, records the predicted answer for each prompt, and then compares it with the ground‑truth label.  The aggregate accuracy is the ratio of correct predictions to the total number of prompts.

# Runtime measurement methodology: GPU memory, latency per output token  

Efficient deployment of language models requires not just high accuracy but also low latency and memory consumption.  The BitNet b1.58 paper measures these quantities in a systematic manner, focusing on time‑per‑token and peak GPU memory.

**Latency measurement** is performed by generating a single token at a time in autoregressive mode.  The model’s forward pass is timed from the moment the last hidden state is fed into the final linear layer until the logits for the next token are produced.  This measurement excludes any auxiliary steps (such as decoder caching or  token sequencing) that are not part of the core MMMut operation.  Because the kernel used by BitNet is a 2‑bit approximation that saves integer addition for every multiply‑accumulate, the resulting latencies are measured in the context of the same batch size and sequence length used for throughput (often a single token).  Warm‑up runs are conducted to ensure GPU pipelines are fully saturated and to eliminate startup jitter.  The final reported latency is the median across a large number of such generation steps, providing a stable benchmark.

**Memory measurement** captures the peak amount of GPU global memory allocated by the inference engine during a forward pass.  This includes the storage of the model’s weights, activation buffers, and any intermediate tensors created by the transformer blocks.  The FastTransformer profiler (via torch.cuda.memory_allocated or a dedicated query in the library’s profiling suite) records this value.  The memory footprint for BitNet is significantly lower—a factor of 3.55 times less for the 3 B model—because ternary weights require one bit per parameter while full‑precision FP16 weights require 16 bits.  However, the 8‑bit activation buffers are still needed; nevertheless, the overall compression produces a near‑linear reduction in memory.

Latency and memory are measured under identical conditions for both the BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline.  The same hardware (e.g., NVIDIA A100 GPUs), the same batch size, sequence length, and deployment setup are used so that the reported differences arise purely from the precision and architecture.

# FasterTransformer setup and 2‑bit kernel usage  

The evaluation framework is built atop FasterTransformer, a highly optimized inference engine specifically tuned for transformer workloads on NVIDIA GPUs.  FasterTransformer offers a modular architecture where mixins such as custom kernels may be swapped in, and it exposes profiling hooks for latency and memory.

For the 1.58‑bit BitNet, the authors incorporated the 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder library.  This kernel is designed to exploit the ternary weight format: because each weight can be \(-1, 0,\) or \(+1\), the matrix multiplication can be rewritten as a series of integer additions and subtractions, entirely eliminating floating‑point multiply‑accumulate operations.  The kernel works as follows:

1. **Weight packing**: The ternary weights are packed into 32‑bit integers by mapping \(-1\) to \( \{0x01\}\), \(0\) to \( \{0x00\}\), and \(+1\) to \( \{0xFF\}\).  Thus, a single 32‑bit word stores eight ternary symbols.  This compact representation reduces the number of memory fetches during computation.

2. **Matrix–vector multiplication**: The packed weights interface with a custom ATen kernel that performs a stream of add- or subtract- operations based on the packed bits.  Since all multipliers are ±1 or 0, the kernel mini‑ifies the computation into a tight loop of integer arithmetic.

3. **Quantization of activations**: 8‑bit integer activations are passed to the kernel.  The kernel performs a scaling step to map from integer product space back to the appropriate floating‑point domain before the softmax.

4. **Patch to FasterTransformer**: The kernel is integrated as a new `bitint` module within FasterTransformer’s `CublasGemmEx` alias, using a custom CUDA kernel launch configuration tuned for the target GPU architecture.  This patch ensures that both the FP16 baseline and the 1.58‑bit variant run through the same high-level inference pipeline, enabling a fair comparison of performance.

The combination of FasterTransformer and the 2‑bit kernel yields two major gains: a 2.71× speedup in latency per token for 3 B BitNet versus the FP16 baseline, and a 3.55× reduction in memory usage.  When scaled to larger models (7 B, 13 B, 70 B), the speedup climbs to 4.1× relative to FP16 LLaMA, as the linear layers—which now comprise a larger fraction of the compute budget—receive the most benefit from the integer‑only multiplication.

In summary, the benchmarking pipeline employed by BitNet b1.58’s authors is a cohesive blend of standard language‑model metrics (perplexity, accuracy across a rich zero‑shot suite), rigorous runtime profiling (latency and memory, collected with FasterTransformer and its 2‑bit kernel), and a reproducible evaluation harness (lm‑evaluation‑harness).  This methodological framework ensures that comparisons to full‑precision models reflect genuine fidelity in modeling capacity while exposing the substantial efficiency gains realized by the 1.58‑bit representation.
