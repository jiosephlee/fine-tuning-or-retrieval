\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Foundations of Foundation Models & the Adaptation Challenge

Large foundation models have grown to unprecedented scales in recent years, transforming the landscape of artificial intelligence. Models such as GPT‑3, which boasts roughly 175 billion parameters, and Stable Diffusion, a state‑of‑the‑art image generator, exhibit remarkable generalization across a wide swath of downstream tasks. Yet the sheer size of these systems makes training from scratch infeasible for most researchers and engineers. Consequently, the research community has turned its attention toward efficient methods for adapting pretrained models to new, task‑specific objectives. This chapter introduces the core motivations behind foundation models, discusses why adaptation is essential, and outlines the prevailing paradigms for efficient model adaptation, grounding each in recent developments, notably the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) frameworks.

### Rise of large foundation models and parameter explosion

The modern era of artificial intelligence has seen a paradigm shift in model design: large, generalist models pretrained on massive datasets. This shift has been fueled by a synergy of advances in scaling laws, data curation, and hardware. The paper underscores this trend by referencing key milestones—ChatGPT\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion\cite{rombach2022high}—each pushing the boundaries of tokenized text and pixel‑level generation. A salient consequence of this trend is a sharp increase in the number of parameters, exemplified by GPT‑3's 175 B parameters. Larger models are typically more capable: they encapsulate richer semantic knowledge, enable higher‑resolution synthesis, and improve zero‑shot performance across diverse tasks. However, as the dimensionality of the weight space grows, the computational and memory requirements for training these networks climb quadratically. Training such a model end‑to‑end can demand hundreds of petaflop‑seconds of computation, deep‑binned on a cluster of GPUs or TPUs. Not only is this computational burden prohibitive for most research labs, it also stifles rapid experimentation and limits the applicability of these models in resource‑constrained settings.

When a large foundation model is released, researchers are confronted with a tradeoff: either pay a prohibitive bill to retrain the model from scratch, risking a loss of any fine‑tuned skill acquired by the community, or adapt the existing weights with a more economical procedure. The latter scenario—efficient downstream adaptation—has become a cornerstone of contemporary machine learning research. The paper argues that any successful adaptation strategy must preserve the pre‑trained knowledge while allowing the model to acquire new, task‑specific capabilities without an inordinate number of trainable parameters.

### Training cost versus deployment constraints

Training a 175 B parameter model entails not only massive compute but also significant data costs and energy consumption. Moreover, the downstream training and adaptation phase may require additional computational budgets that are difficult to allocate in industry and academia alike. Consequently, there is a pressing need for **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** methods, which enable substantial task‑level improvements while adding as few trainable parameters as possible.

The paper articulates a useful decomposition of the adaptation problem: instead of modifying every weight in the network, one can either (1) *parameter finetune* a subset of the existing weights, (2) *insert additional parameters* (adapter modules) that interact with the pretrained backbone, or (3) *promote external inputs* that steer the network’s behavior (prompt tuning). Each paradigm has distinct implications for inference latency, deployment, and regularity. For instance, *model finetuning* keeps the baseline architecture untouched, thereby preserving inference latency; *adapter* methods add auxiliary layers during inference; and *prompt tuning* embeds additional tokens into the model’s input, also typically incurring no additional latency. The choice of paradigm depends on the downstream use‑case: for latency‑constrained environments or hardware with strict memory budgets, the minimalism of model finetuning can be invaluable.

Crucially, the paper posits that the adaptation strategy must **preserve relational information** between neurons. By maintaining the pairwise angles among weight vectors, the model is less likely to drift away from the knowledge embedded during pretraining. This observation motivates the use of *orthogonal* transformations: if \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)), then for any two weight vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\) from a linear layer, the angle between them remains unchanged under the transformation \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\). Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages precisely this property, learning a transformation that aligns the pretrained layer to the new task while keeping the neuron geometry intact.

### Downstream task adaptation: objectives and constraints

The downstream adaptation problem is formalized as follows. Let \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) denote the fixed pre‑trained weight matrix of a particular layer. The aim is to transform the network into a new model \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{adapt}}\), where only a lightweight set of parameters is altered. OFT accomplishes this by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and updating the forward pass from

\[
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}
\]

to

\[
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

with \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) a hidden representation and \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) the layer output. Thus the only trainable parameters are the entries of \(\mathbf{R}\). Since \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, it preserves the Euclidean norm and angles of the weight vectors and thereby the mutual geometric arrangement of neurons—a desirable inductive bias that helps the model retain its pre‑training capabilities while learning to solve a new task.

However, a generic orthogonal matrix has \(d^2\) parameters, an impractical budget for large layers. To curb this, the original OFT restricts \(\mathbf{R}\) to a block‑diagonal form \(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r)\), where each block \(\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) is a small orthogonal matrix and \(br=d\). While this drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, it introduces an undesirable inductive bias: the dimension of a neuron is divided into disjoint groups that are transformed independently. Such a grouping has no semantic justification; it merely reflects an arbitrary partition of indices. Consequently, the block‑diagonal approach limits the expressive power of \(\mathbf{R}\): it cannot represent many orthogonal matrices that are dense and highly mixing.

The challenge, then, is to learn a **dense** orthogonal transformation while retaining the parameter economy of a block‑diagonal form. The paper approaches this challenge via an *information‑transmission* formalism: a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be factored into a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices whose non‑zero entries define a communication graph. By carefully selecting the sparsity pattern of the factor matrices, the authors aim to ensure that every input dimension can influence every output dimension—achieving a dense transformation—while the number of trainable parameters remains logarithmically small relative to \(d\). This insight motivates the proposal of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT).

### Overview of existing adaptation paradigms

Efficient adaptation of foundation models can be categorized into three main families:

1. **Model Finetuning**: Fully or partially retraining the existing weights. Classic methods involve a small learning rate to evade catastrophic forgetting. Modern variants add structure, such as orthogonal constraints (OFT) or low‑rank approximations (LoRA\cite{hulora2022}). Model finetuning introduces no extra inference latency but still demands a non‑trivial number of trainable parameters.

2. **Adapter Tuning**: Inserting small trainable modules—usually residual bottlenecks—within the frozen backbone. Methods include StaRL (fine‑grained residual), LoRA, BLoRA, etc. These adapters are split across the network, requiring additional operations during inference but shining in parameter efficiency.

3. **Prompt Tuning**: Concatenating or prepending learnable vectors or tokens to the input. Works well for large language models that leverage prompt‑based learning; they impose almost zero extra cost during inference as the prompt is part of the input.

The paper places OFT and BOFT squarely in the first category but with significant innovation in the *activation mechanism* of the transformation matrix. Whereas vanilla finetuning copies the entire weight matrix, OFT learns an *orthogonal* transformation that preserves geometry. BOFT further compresses this transformation via a butterfly matrix factorization, retaining the geometric benefits of orthogonality while drastically reducing parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\). This approach attains the best of both worlds: no inference latency, strong empirical generalization gains, and a compact trainable footprint.

In the next sections of this textbook, we will delve deeper into the mathematical underpinnings of the butterfly matrix, its orthogonal parameterization, and how BOFT operates as a practical, efficient alternative to traditional finetuning. We will also explore the theoretical motivations behind preserving angles and spectral norms, the role of information‑transmission graphs in guiding sparsity design, and the broader impact of butterfly‑structured training on downstream tasks spanning vision, language, and generative models.

Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning Strategies  

# Model Weight Finetuning Fundamentals  
When a large foundation model has been pretrained on a vast corpus of data, a common way to adapt it to a new downstream task is to keep the underlying architecture unchanged and adjust a portion of the model’s weights.  In this setting, the pretrained parameters act as a strong prior: the change imposed by finetuning is expected to be small so that the knowledge gained during pretraining is not overwritten.  Traditionally, this idea is captured by a very small learning rate; the optimizer merely nudges each weight a tiny amount so that the final parameter vector remains close to the initial one in Euclidean space.  While this ad‑hoc strategy works well in practice, it offers no explicit guarantee that the relational structure of the weight matrix—such as angles between neurons—will remain intact.  Preserving such relational information is crucial because it retains the geometry that the network has learned during pretraining.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a principled alternative that explicitly keeps pairwise angles between the neurons in each layer unchanged.  In OFT a frozen pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is multiplied on the left by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  The forward pass transforms an input \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to the output \(\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}\).  Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, the new weight matrix preserves the angles among all columns of \(\bm{W}^0\), guaranteeing that the semantic structure encoded by pretraining survives the adaptation process.  To enforce orthogonality throughout training, OFT parametrizes \(\bm{R}\) via the Cayley transform \(\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) where \(\bm{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric.  The search space of orthogonal transformations is therefore constrained to a manifold that respects the pretraining geometry.  However, the dimensionality of an unconstrained orthogonal matrix is \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\): a \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix contains \(d^{2}\) entries.  In practice this implies that a large number of trainable parameters are required, which can be prohibitive for massive models.

To address this cost, the first incarnation of OFT introduced a block‑diagonal form for \(\bm{R}\).  If \(\bm{R}\) consists of \(r\) blocks \(\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) such that \(br=d\), each block is an independent small orthogonal matrix and the overall \(d\times d\) matrix remains block‑diagonal.  This reduces the number of trainable parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) but imposes a restrictive inductive bias: each group of neurons is rotated independently, while neurons belonging to different groups cannot interact.  Although this approach yields interesting results, the sparsity pattern of a block‑diagonal matrix cannot approximate a full dense linear transform, and no principled way exists for choosing the block size.  The challenge, therefore, is clear: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix using far fewer parameters than \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) while still maintaining expressivity?

# Adapter Modules: Adding Trainable Blocks  
Adapter tuning offers an alternative strategy for efficient finetuning.  Instead of directly modifying the heavy pretrained weights, adapters introduce lightweight, task‑specific modules that are inserted between existing layers.  The original parameters remain frozen and are reused across all downstream tasks.  The added component typically consists of a small bottleneck network or a set of linear layers that inject the task signal into the computation.  Because only a handful of new weights are trained, adapter tuning attains a low parameter budget and often incurs no additional inference latency.  The design of adapters is flexible: one can replace the hidden units themselves with new ones, or augment them with extra gates, always ensuring that the foundation model’s core remains untouched.

The paper situates adapter tuning as one of three major families of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT).  While adapters are attractive for their simplicity and modularity, the text notes that they also introduce a new parameter scale that depends on the chosen bottleneck dimension and the number of added modules.  Importantly, the added modules interact with the frozen weights only via the pre‑existing signal flow, which limits the way the adapter can reshape the underlying representation.  Thus, while adapter tuning effectively reduces training cost, its expressivity is inherently constrained by the static structure of the foundation model.

# Prompt Tuning: Learnable Prefixes  
Prompt tuning pushes the notion of efficient finetuning to the input side rather than the weight side.  Here, instead of adjusting internal weights, one attaches a small set of learnable tokens—often called a prefix—to every input.  These tokens are treated as additional embeddings that are fed into the network just like real data tokens.  Because the rest of the network remains frozen, prompt tuning incurs virtually no additional computational overhead during inference.  The text identifies prompt tuning as another member of the three main PEFT families, noting that it entirely sidesteps the need to modify the backbone weights.

The central appeal of prompt tuning lies in its minimalistic footprint: learning a small number of vectors that sit in the same embedding space as the original token representations.  However, the expressivity of prompt‑style methods may be limited by the fact that they can only influence the network via the initial few layers, essentially relegating the task‑specific adaptation to a shallow interface.  The paper discusses prompting as an avenue that offers zero inference latency but may struggle to capture complex interactions that a full weight reparameterization could model.

# Reparameterization Methods: LoRA and Its Extensions  
Reparameterization‑based PEFT approaches represent a powerful middle ground.  Instead of adding entirely new parameters or merely scaling learning rates, they alter the way the model’s weight matrices are expressed, often by overlaying a low‑rank perturbation or a structured transform that can be added to or multiplicatively combined with the pretrained parameters.  The most prominent example among these is Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA).  LoRA inserts a rank‑\(r\) factorization \(\bm{A}\bm{B}\) into the weight update, so the fine‑tuned weight becomes \(\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}\).  Because each of \(\bm{A}\) and \(\bm{B}\) contains only \(d n r\) parameters (significantly fewer than the full \(d n\)), LoRA can be applied without inflating any of the architecture’s runtime costs.  Since the rank \(r\) is typically much smaller than the layer dimensionality, a small number of trainable parameters can produce a surprisingly expressive perturbation that captures task‑specific nuances.

The paper discusses several extensions of LoRA that dynamically adjust the rank per layer, allowing the parameter budget to be allocated proportionally to a layer’s budgetary importance.  By splitting the low‑rank update across layers and adjusting the rank for each, these methods can more tightly balance expressivity and efficiency.  The referenced variants demonstrate that the raw rank‑based approach can be tuned for improved parameter budgets, but the core idea—adding low‑rank updates to the pretrained weights—remains the same.

Orthogonal finetuning can be viewed as a different kind of reparameterization: instead of adding an additive low‑rank term, it replaces the pretrained weight matrix with a multiplicative orthogonal transform.  OFT therefore shares the ethos of reparameterization—it keeps the backbone intact but inserts a structured, trainable component—but selects an entirely different transformation space.  OFT’s orthogonal matrices preserve the pairwise angles of neurons and retain the spectral norms of \(\bm{W}^0\), leading to superior stability and generalization, albeit at a higher parameter cost.  The paper’s key contribution is to bring the two worlds together: reparameterizing the orthogonal transform itself with the butterfly factorization, thereby reconciling expressive power with stringent parameter budgets.

# Comparative Analysis of Efficiency, Stability, and Generalization  
The paper’s empirical investigations reveal stark differences among the PEFT families.  In terms of parameter efficiency, adapters and prompt tuning clearly excel because they only introduce a small number of extra weights or input tokens.  LoRA and its rank‑adaptive variants sit next: they need merely a few thousand parameters per layer, with a total budget that scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d n r)\).  OFT, by contrast, can require up to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameters due to its dense orthogonal matrix, which is impractical for modern foundation models.

Stability is closely tied to how well a method preserves the geometry of the backbone.  In the block‑diagonal OFT approach, learning is often more stable than pure LoRA because each block’s orthogonal update ensures that all pairwise angles within a block are maintained.  However, the block‑diagonal form abruptly limits interactions between blocks, resulting in reduced expressivity.  LoRA, in its simplest form, can over‑fit more easily when the rank is too high because it can change the magnitude of the weight matrix arbitrarily, potentially disrupting the delicate balance of layer norms.  The paper notes that orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight, which theoretically contributes to training stability.

Generalization is perhaps the most important property when adapting a massive pretrained model to new tasks.  Empirically, both OFT and its butterfly‑parameterized successor BOFT consistently outperform LoRA on a range of vision, language, and diffusion‑based tasks.  The reasoning behind this phenomenon is twofold.  First, the orthogonal constraint guarantees that the updated weights remain on the same manifold as the pretrained weights, preventing the model from drifting into regions of parameter space that are poorly understood.  Second, the butterfly factorization introduces a structured inductive bias that resembles familiar fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, cosine).  This bias encourages the operator to learn efficient, global transformations rather than over‑fitting to local, idiosyncratic patterns in the downstream data.

The comprehensive comparison betweeen BOFT and other PEFT approaches illuminates a clear trade‑off: methods that inject separate, small modules (adapters, prompts) are the most efficient in terms of storage and computation but limit the transformations that can be represented.  Methods that operate on the weight space directly (LoRA, OFT) increase expressivity and can yield better generalization, yet they often come at a higher parameter cost.  BOFT strikes a middle ground by factorizing the orthogonal matrix into \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse 2‑by‑2 butterfly blocks, yielding only \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) trainable parameters while retaining enough expressivity to approximate dense orthogonal transformations.  Consequently, BOFT reaches a sweet spot where the training stability and generalization strengths of OFT are preserved, but the parameter budget is dramatically reduced, matching or surpassing the efficiency of LoRA.

In conclusion, the paper frames PEFT as a spectrum of design choices that balance parameter efficiency, stability, and generalization.  Adapters and prompts favor the leanest changes but sacrifice some representational capacity; LoRA maximizes flexibility within a low‑rank space but may struggle to preserve pretrained geometry; OFT emphasizes stability by maintaining angles and norms but at a high parameter cost; BOFT answers the central question posed by the paper—how to preserve a full orthogonal transformation with far fewer parameters—by composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of butterfly‑shaped sparse orthogonal factors.  This synthesis reinforces the broader message that carefully engineered structural constraints can reconcile efficiency with performance in the fine‑tuning of foundation models.

Orthogonal Transformations: Theory and Practice

# Definition and key properties of orthogonal matrices
Orthogonal matrices are square matrices with the defining property that their transpose is equal to their inverse:  
\[
\bm{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}=\bm{I} .
\]  
Because of this relation, multiplying a vector by an orthogonal matrix preserves the Euclidean norm, since  
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{x}\|_2^2=(\bm{R}\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{R}\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}=\|\bm{x}\|_2^2 .
\]  
The dot product between two arbitrary vectors is also unchanged:  
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{R}\bm{y})=\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}\bm{y}=\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}.
\]  
Hence, orthogonal transformations are distance‑preserving (isometries) and preserve angles between vectors. The column vectors of an orthogonal matrix form an orthonormal basis—they have unit length and are mutually orthogonal. Moreover, the determinant of an orthogonal matrix is either \(+1\) or \(-1\), corresponding to pure rotations or rotations combined with a reflection. These simple yet powerful properties make orthogonal matrices attractive in many signal‑processing and machine‑learning contexts, especially when the goal is to modify a representation without distorting its intrinsic geometric structure.

# Preserving pairwise angles and spectral norms
The preservation of angles and norms has direct repercussions for finetuning deep neural networks. In orthogonal finetuning (OFT), the pretrained weight matrix of a linear layer, denoted \(\bm{W}^0\), is left‑multiplied by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) to obtain the finetuned weight \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\). Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, the inner product between any two columns \(\bm{w}_i^0\) and \(\bm{w}_j^0\) of \(\bm{W}^0\) remains unchanged after the transformation:  
\[
(\bm{w}_i^0)^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{w}_j^0)=
(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0).
\]  
Thus, the pairwise angles between neurons—critical descriptors of the learned feature geometry—remain intact throughout finetuning. This invariance helps preserve the semantic knowledge encoded during pretraining.

Equally important is the preservation of the spectral norm. If \(\bm{W}^0\) admits a singular value decomposition \(\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\), then  
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}.
\]  
Since \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, the product \(\bm{R}\bm{U}\) remains an orthogonal matrix, and the singular values in \(\bm{\Sigma}\) are unchanged. Consequently, the largest singular value—the spectral norm of the weight matrix—does not increase with orthogonal finetuning. Maintaining a bounded spectral norm is known to improve training stability and generalization, as it prevents weight magnitudes from exploding during the optimization process.

# Cayley transform and skew‑symmetric parametrization
A convenient and analytically tractable way to parameterize an orthogonal matrix is via the Cayley transform. Letting \(\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be a skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\)), the Cayley transform yields an orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This representation guarantees orthogonality for any \(\bm{Q}\) in its domain (excluding singular points where \(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\) is non‑invertible). Moreover, the initial value
\[
\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
provides an identity initialization that aligns perfectly with the initialization strategy used for OFT: the finetuned model starts from the pretrained weights. The degrees of freedom in \(\bm{Q}\) are \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\), matching the number of independent entries in an orthogonal matrix. This parametrization is especially attractive when the orthogonal matrix is further constrained to a block‑diagonal form or to a butterfly form. In the block‑diagonal case, \(\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)\) and each block \(\bm{R}_i\) is generated from its own skew‑symmetric submatrix \(\bm{Q}_i\); the overall parameter count becomes \(\sum_i\tfrac{b_i(b_i-1)}{2}\), where \(b_i\) is the size of block \(i\). For butterfly factorization, each butterfly component is also orthogonal by construction, and its small sub‑blocks can be parameterized through Cayley’s rule or, more directly, by constraining each \(2\times2\) butterfly factor to be a rotation matrix.

# Orthogonality constraints in neural network training
Applying orthogonality constraints within deep networks serves a dual purpose. First, it enforces a strong regularization on the finetuning process: because the transformation \(\bm{R}\) is restricted to the orthogonal group, the pretraining knowledge embedded in \(\bm{W}^0\) is distorted only through rotations or reflections, never through scaling or shearing. Second, it removes the need for additional inference latency. In OFT and its butterfly‑based extension BOFT, the orthogonal matrix is multiplied with the frozen pretrained weight during the forward pass, and this left‑multiplication can be absorbed into a single matrix after the finetuning phase. Thus, inference proceeds with the same computational cost as the original model.

From a computational perspective, maintaining orthogonality can be costly if implemented naively, because it involves matrix inversions in the Cayley transform. The block‑diagonal structure of the original OFT reduces this burden by limiting the size of the sub‑matrices. The butterfly factorization used in BOFT further mitigates computational overhead by decomposing a full dense orthogonal matrix into a product of \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse orthogonal factors, each with only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries. Although the forward pass now requires \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) operations instead of constant‑time multiplication with a dense matrix, the quadratic increase in trainable parameters seen in full OFT is avoided. Moreover, during training, the sequential multiplication of sparse factors can be implemented efficiently through cascaded block‑wise matrix–vector products, which are highly amenable to modern hardware optimizers.

To further curb overfitting—an issue that can surface when the orthogonal matrix is overly expressive—BOFT introduces a multiplicative dropout. In this scheme, a random subset of butterfly components (or a subset of the blocks within a component) is replaced by identity matrices during each backward pass. This stochastic shunting of orthogonal transformations reduces the effective dimensionality of the search space while preserving the property that the overall weight remains close to the pretrained counterpart. In the same vein, LoRA applies dropout directly to its low‑rank additive update; however, owing to the multiplicative nature of orthogonal finetuning, a multiplicative dropout is more suited to maintain the mathematical constraints of the orthogonal group.

Through these constraints and design choices, orthogonal finetuning methods strike a carefully balanced trade‑off: they preserve the learned geometry of the pretrained model, maintain spectral properties that aid stability, and reduce the number of trainable parameters to a scale that is manageable for adapters or block‑diagonal structures. The introduction of butterfly factorization offers a principled means of achieving dense orthogonal transformations with only a logarithmic overhead in parameters, thereby making orthogonal finetuning competitive and versatile across vision, language, and diffusion‑based generation tasks.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – Idea, Benefits, Bottlenecks

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) occupies an elegant niche in the spectrum of parameter‑efficient model adaptation.  At its core, OFT transforms a frozen foundation‑model weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix, thereby preserving the relative geometry among neurons while keeping the pretrained knowledge largely intact.  In the following section we first formulate the OFT mechanism, then examine its most popular block‑diagonal instantiation, and finally discuss the practical gains and shortcomings that motivate the pursuit of more efficient variants.

# OFT formulation: multiplying a learnable orthogonal matrix with frozen weights

Suppose an existing foundation model possesses a linear layer represented by a weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\).  Here \(d\) denotes the dimensionality of the hidden representation and \(n\) is the number of output neurons in that layer.  The standard forward pass computes \(\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\), where \(\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the input activation and \(\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\) is the output.

In OFT we replace \(\bm{W}^0\) with a *learned* weight matrix of the form \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\), where \(\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is an *orthogonal* matrix (\(\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}_d\)).  The updated forward pass is therefore

\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]

Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, it preserves all pairwise angles between the columns of \(\bm{W}^0\).  In other words, the *relative geometric* structure in the weight space remains unchanged, a property that is argued to maintain the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining.  Rather than adding a small correction term to the weight matrix (as in additive parameter‑efficient methods), OFT multiplies the entire weight matrix by a rotation or reflection, ensuring that no feature direction is amplified or attenuated beyond what the orthogonal algebra dictates.

To guarantee an orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) during training, the paper adopts the **Cayley parameterization**: a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}\) (\(\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top\)) is first optimized, and \(\bm{R}\) is then recovered via

\[
\bm{R} \;=\; (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]

This construction ensures that \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal for all values of \(\bm{Q}\).  Because \(\bm{Q}\) has only \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\) independent parameters, it is often computationally convenient to optimize \(\bm{Q}\) rather than \(\bm{R}\) directly.

Initialization is **zero‑skew**: \(\bm{Q}\) is set to the zero matrix, which yields \(\bm{R} = \bm{I}_d\).  Consequently the finetuned weight matrix starts exactly as the frozen pretrained matrix, and any movement away from it is driven solely by the gradient signal of the downstream objective.

# Block‑diagonal OFT for parameter efficiency

Although a full \(d \times d\) orthogonal matrix offers maximal expressiveness, it carries a prohibitive computational cost: \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) trainable parameters.  To tame this cost, OFT proposes a *block‑diagonal* variant in which \(\bm{R}\) is partitioned into smaller independent orthogonal blocks.  Concretely, let \(b\) be a chosen block size and let \(r = d / b\) denote the number of blocks.  Then

\[
\bm{R}
    \;=\;
    \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(
        \bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dotsc, \bm{R}_r
    \bigr), \qquad
    \bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b},
\]

with each \(\bm{R}_i\) an orthogonal matrix.  The total number of trainable parameters collapses to \(\mathcal{O}(b d)\) (more precisely, \(\tfrac{b(b-1)}{2} \times r\)).

Intuitively, this factorization presumes that the \(d\) hidden dimensions can be split into \(r\) independent groups, each group being rotated or reflected independently.  This assumption is not dictated by the structure of the weight matrix but rather imposed as a regularization device to reduce the search space during finetuning.  In practice, a moderate block size (e.g., \(b = 16\) or \(32\)) often suffices to strike a balance between reduction in parameters and preservation of expressiveness.

Because each block is orthogonal, the *overall* \( \bm{R}\) remains orthogonal; moreover, if \(\bm{R}\) is initialized as the identity, each block starts as an identity matrix too.  The gradient flow through the blocks is similarly restricted: each block sees gradients only from neurons belonging to its own group, thereby enforcing a stricter form of parameter sharing.

# Stability, generalization, and spectral norm preservation

One of the founding claims of OFT is its *training stability* compared to conventional finetuning approaches.  This advantage is two‑fold: first, orthogonality automatically preserves the spectral norm of the weight matrix because

\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2
    \;=\; \|\bm{W}^0\|_2,
\]

as \(\|\bm{R}\|_2 = 1\).  Maintaining the spectral norm keeps the magnitude of gradients predictable and mitigates the risk of exploding or vanishing gradients that plague standard finetuning with large learning rates.

Second, by preserving the angles between neuron weight vectors, OFT ensures that the *relative geometric configuration* of features is maintained.  Theoretical works on hyperspherical energy and orthogonality have linked such properties to improved generalization, as the model is prevented from arbitrarily re‑arranging its embedding space during adaptation.  Empirically, the paper observes that OFT demonstrates *more stable convergence* than LoRA and other additive or scaling‑based finetuning methods across a variety of downstream tasks.

Because the orthogonal transformation is deterministic for any given \(\bm{Q}\), the optimization trajectory is inherently smoother: the *effective* learning rate is constrained by the orthogonality, so large updates are automatically prevented without the need for explicit step‑size tuning.  This characteristic is especially beneficial when dealing with massive foundation models where gradient scaling becomes problematic.

# High parameter count and limited expressiveness of block‑diagonal OFT

Despite these benefits, the block‑diagonal variant is not without limitations.

**High parameter count**: Even with an aggressive block size, the block‑diagonal OFT still requires a substantial number of trainable parameters.  For a typical hidden dimension of, say, \(d = 4096\) and block size \(b = 32\), the number of parameters is roughly \(32 \times 4096 / 2 \approx 65{,}536\).  While this is far fewer than the full orthogonal case (\(d^2/2 \approx 8\,388{,}608\)), it remains a significant overhead when finetuning multiple large layers, especially in memory‑constrained environments.

**Limited expressiveness**: Because each block acts independently, the overall transformation \(\bm{R}\) is constrained to permutations of block‑wise rotations and reflections.  This restriction prevents the model from capturing correlations that cross block boundaries.  For example, a block‑diagonal \(\bm{R}\) cannot approximate the identity shift that couples all dimensions uniformly, nor can it emulate the classic full Fourier transform or Hadamard transform, which inherently mix all coordinates together.  The paper illustratively shows that block‑diagonal OFT cannot represent many standard linear transforms, whereas a dense orthogonal matrix can.

These limitations motivate the search for a method that retains the stability and regularization benefits of OFT while drastically reducing the number of effective parameters and expanding the expressive capacity of \(\bm{R}\).  Subsequent sections of the original research introduce the *Orthogonal Butterfly* framework, which achieves exactly that by factorizing \(\bm{R}\) into a product of sparse butterfly‑shaped orthogonal matrices.  This factorization ensures \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) trainable parameters while allowing the composition to become dense, thereby reconciling parameter efficiency with powerful representational ability.

# Information‑Transmission View of Orthogonal Matrix Factorization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposes to adapt a frozen foundation model by left‑multiplying each linear layer with an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \).  
Although this transformation preserves the pair‑wise angles between neurons and therefore the semantic structure learned during pre‑training, a dense orthogonal matrix typically incurs \(O(d^{2})\) trainable parameters.  Farsighted designers have therefore sought sparse factorizations that keep the number of effective trainable parameters modest while still allowing the product to span the dense orthogonal group.  
The paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* proposes an elegant perspective: constructing a dense orthogonal matrix can be viewed as **transmitting information through a grid‑structured graph**.  By formalising this view, we can formulate clear requirements on the sparsity pattern of the factor matrices and uncover principled ways, such as butterfly factorization, to meet those requirements with a minimal number of free parameters.

Below we unpack this viewpoint in four stages.  First we interpret a dense matrix as a complete communication graph.  Then we see how a factorization unfolds this graph into successive layers.  Then we expose the edge‑based connectivity constraints that guarantee full transmission.  Next we explore the extra rigidity injected by orthogonality and why it forces us to count only the truly “free” edges.  Finally we illustrate how to visualise these grid graphs and connect the dots back to the original problem of parameter‑efficient finetuning.

---

# Dense matrix as a fully connected graph

A \( d\times d \) real matrix \( \mathbf{R}\) can be regarded as the weight matrix of a directed graph whose vertices are arranged in two layers \( \{v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\}\) (inputs) and \( \{u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\}\) (outputs).  The entry \( R_{ij}\) indicates the weight of the directed edge from input \(v_{j}\) to output \(u_{i}\).  In this representation, a *dense* matrix has an edge between every pair of vertices, i.e. the adjacency matrix contains \(d^{2}\) entries, all potentially nonzero.  
When this adjacency is a full graph, an arbitrary signal injected at an input node can propagate directly to any output node in a single step.

In the context of model finetuning, the dense graph embodies an orthogonal transformation that mixes all neurons of a layer indiscriminately.  While perpetuating this direct mixing preserves all learned relationships, it also yields a quadratic number of parameters: a naïve way to implement \( \mathbf{R}\) requires storing every entry.  The challenge is to emulate the same degree of expressiveness but without storing all \(d^{2}\) weights.

---

# Matrix factorization as sequential graph layers

Consider factorising \( \mathbf{R}\) into a product of \(m\) matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1 , \qquad \mathbf{B}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
\]
Each factor \( \mathbf{B}_i\) is viewed as a **layer** of edges connecting the nodes of layer \(i\) to layer \(i+1\).  The multiplication of successive layers is equivalent to traversing the graph sequentially: first the signal is propagated from the first input layer to the second by \(\mathbf{B}_1\), then the intermediate representation is relayed through \(\mathbf{B}_2\), and so on until the signal finally reaches the last output layer after \(\mathbf{B}_m\).  Consequently, the combined graph becomes a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with \(m+1\) horizontal rows of vertices.

The *sparsity pattern* of a factor \( \mathbf{B}_i\) dictates which edges exist between layers \(i\) and \(i+1\).  A fully dense \(\mathbf{B}_i\) would defeat the point of factorisation, because it introduces \(d^{2}\) parameters.  By imposing an explicit sparse structure on each factor, we reduce the number of trainable parameters while still seeking that the overall product remains dense.  The information‑transmission framework asks: **Given a fixed number of intermediate layers, which sparsity patterns guarantee that every input can still reach every output?**

---

# Edge‑based connectivity constraints for full transmission

To answer that question we translate the requirement “every input node must be able to reach every output node” into an *edge‑based connectivity condition*.  Let us denote the set of nodes in row \(k\) by \(V_k=\{v_k^{(1)},\dots,v_k^{(d)}\}\).  Between row \(k\) and row \(k+1\) only a subset of the possible directed edges are retained, as specified by the non‑zero pattern of \(\mathbf{B}_k\).  We say that a directed walk exists from a node \(v_1^{(i)}\) in the first row to a node \(v_{m+1}^{(j)}\) in the last row if there is a sequence of edges \(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_m\) where each \(e_k\) goes from some node in row \(k\) to a node in row \(k+1\).

The *full‑transmission* condition then reads:
\[
\forall \, i,j \in \{1,\dots,d\}, \;\; \exists \; \text{a walk } v_1^{(i)}\to v_2^{(\cdot)}\to \cdots \to v_{m+1}^{(j)}.
\]
This can be expressed graph‑theoretically: the directed bipartite graph constituted by all edges of all layers must yield a directed spanning subgraph that connects every source node to every sink node.  In particular, this imposes that the adjacency matrix of the concatenated graph (obtained by unrolling the matrix multiplication) be dense, i.e. \(\mathbf{R}\) is full.

A constructive way to satisfy this is to give each \(\mathbf{B}_i\) enough non‑zero entries so that no source node is isolated at any hop.  For instance, in a graph with \(d=4\) nodes the paper shows an example where a 5‑layer factorization uses only 10 edges to achieve full connectivity—still fewer than the 12 edges that a single dense matrix would use.  The takeaway is that *we may globally reduce the number of edges while still guaranteeing full information flow*, as long as each hop touches sufficiently many nodes.  The exact shape of the sparsity pattern will dictate the number of trainable parameters.

---

# Orthogonality constraints and minimum free edges

When each factor must also be orthogonal, the sparsity patterns are not arbitrary.  
An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}\).  In a sparse orthogonal matrix, the characteristic two-row sparsity—a pair of rows being orthogonal vectors—forces a *combinatorial coupling* of non‑zero positions.  For a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal block, a single zero in the upper‑left corner forces a zero in the bottom‑right corner, because the two rows must remain orthogonal.  Extending to larger \(d\), each full set of \(d\) columns (or rows) must map to a permutation of themselves multiplied by \(\pm 1\).  Consequently, a sparse orthogonal matrix inevitably has **\(d\) fixed edges**: for each column, exactly one non‑zero element (with magnitude one) is prescribed by the orthogonality constraint.  These edges are *not* free parameters because they are determined by the choice of basis permutation and sign; the remaining entries are the truly learnable degrees of freedom.

Therefore, we distinguish between *total edges*—the number of non‑zero entries in the product graph—and *free edges*, which count only the entries that may be tuned during finetuning.  The goal in an efficient factorization is to minimise the free edges while satisfying the full‑transmission requirement.  The paper argues that the butterfly structure achieves a near‑optimal trade‑off: with \(\log d\) layers of \(2\times 2\) butterfly blocks, the total number of free edges is \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d\log d\) for block size \(b\), which gives \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.  In contrast, a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size \(b\) requires only \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) parameters, yet cannot support the rich expressiveness of a full dense orthogonal matrix.

---

# Visualizing and interpreting grid‑graph structures

To make these ideas concrete, one can visualise the grid‑graph as a two‑dimensional lattice.  In the paper’s Figure \(X\) (reproduced conceptually here), each row of vertices is a dot, and directed edges are drawn as arrows connecting successive rows.  A dense matrix corresponds to a solid lattice: every dot in the first row points to every dot in the second row.  A factorisation into \(\mathbf{B}_1, \dots, \mathbf{B}_m\) corresponds to thinning the lattice: at each hop only a few arrows remain.  The beautiful property of the butterfly topology is that, despite its thinness, every node in the first row still has a path—made of \(m\) hops—to every node in the last row, and these paths are *faithfully structured* (they involve a sequence of nested pairwise “butterfly” interactions).  The lattice thus looks like a series of interlocking pairs, much like the arms of a butterfly, hence the name.

In a visual comparison between a block‑diagonal structure and the butterfly structure, one sees that the former partitions the lattice into disjoint cliques that never interact across blocks, whereas the butterfly lattice interlaces edges so that information is eventually mixed across all dimensions.  This intuitive picture clarifies why block‑diagonal OFT can preserve local angular relationships within a group but cannot generate a globally dense transformation.

Moreover, the effect of orthogonality on the lattice can be seen: the fixed edges appear as a guaranteed scaffold of cross‑bars; every other arrow may be tweaked during training.  The grid‑graph thus becomes not only a computational metaphor but also a diagnostic tool: counting the number of free arrows tells you precisely how many learnable parameters you have, and ensuring that every arrow is part of a valid path guarantees that the final product remains full.

---

In summary, the **information‑transmission view** reframes the core challenge of parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning as a graph‑theoretic design problem: choose a sparse, orthogonal factorisation that still yields a dense directed graph connecting every input to every output with as few free edges as possible.  The butterfly structure satisfies this problem elegantly, generating a dense orthogonal matrix with only \(O(d\log d)\) parameters—an order‑of‑magnitude reduction from naively storing a full orthogonal matrix—while preserving the desirable angle‑preserving property of OFT and thereby improving generalisation for foundation model adaptation.

# Butterfly Factorization: From FFT to Structured Sparse Matrices

The butterfly structure is at the heart of the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) and, more generally, of many efficient sparse linear operators.  In the context of the paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization*, the authors harness this canonical sparsity pattern to construct dense orthogonal matrices from a small number of learnable parameters.  The following discussion develops the mathematical ideas behind the butterfly factorization, illustrates its recursive construction, explains its permutation‑invariant, block‑diagonal form, and quantifies its computational and parameter complexity.  Finally, we point out why these same structures also arise in classical transforms and distributed network topologies.

## Cooley–Tukey FFT and the Butterfly Graph

The Cooley–Tukey FFT is a divide‑and‑conquer algorithm that computes the $N$‑point discrete Fourier transform in $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ time.  Its key insight is that an $N$‑point DFT can be expressed as a product of $\log_2 N$ stages, each of which performs local “butterfly” operations that combine pairs of inputs into pairs of outputs through the recurrence that halves the problem size.  One can visualise each stage as a layer of directed edges on a two‑dimensional grid: nodes on the first layer represent the input samples, nodes on the last layer represent the Fourier coefficients, and each edge denotes a linear combination between two neighbouring nodes.  The resulting directed acyclic graph is called a *butterfly graph* because every pair of adjacent nodes is connected by the two edges that form the shape of a butterfly.  In this graph, every input reaches every output through a unique (acyclic) path, thereby ensuring that the resulting linear operator is fully dense: every row of the transfer matrix contains at least one non‑zero entry in every column.

Why is this construction useful for parameter‑efficient learning?  The critical observation is that the butterfly graph propagates information in a highly structured manner: each internal node only connects to two outgoing nodes, yet thanks to the linking across layers, full connectivity is achieved after $\log_2 N$ stages.  Therefore, we can think of building a dense matrix as a sequence of sparse transformations, each one altering the identity mapping by a constrained set of local interactions.  This “information‑transmission” perspective furnishes a principled framework to design compact factorizations of orthogonal matrices, precisely the goal of the paper’s Orthogonal Butter­fly (BOFT) method.

## Definition of Butterfly Factor and Recursive Construction

Formally, a *butterfly factor* of order $k$ (with $k$ assumed to be a power of two) is defined as

\[
\bm{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{d}_{1}\bigr) & \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{d}_{2}\bigr) \\
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{d}_{3}\bigr) & \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{d}_{4}\bigr)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]

where each $\bm{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector of coefficients.  Notice that $\bm{B}^{F}(k)$ contains four block‑diagonal blocks, all of size $k/2$, and all non‑zero entries lie on these diagonals.  The butterfly factor is the elementary building block that mirrors a single stage of the FFT; each stage links adjacent groups of coefficients pairwise.

The full $d$–dimensional butterfly matrix (with $d=2^{N}$) is constructed recursively by multiplying a sequence of such factors of decreasing size:

\[
\bm{B}(d)
= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\;
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \bm{B}_{1}(d/2) & \mathbf{0} \\
  \mathbf{0}      & \bm{B}_{2}(d/2)
  \end{bmatrix}
= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}\!\left(d,\tfrac{d}{2}\right)\cdots
  \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]

Here each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ denotes a *block‑diagonal butterfly component* of size $d\times d$ whose non‑zero pattern is a permutation of that of $\bm{B}^{F}(k)$, obtained by placing $d/k$ independent copies of $\bm{B}^{F}(k)$ along the main diagonal.  The recursion starts at the coarsest factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$ and proceeds to finer scales until the smallest $2\times 2$ factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ appear.  By construction, the product $\bm{B}(d)$ is dense: every row of the matrix can be traversed from a source node on the first level to a target node on the last level via a chain of $N=\log_2 d$ sparse multiplications.

Because each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is itself a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are orthogonal functions of the underlying vectors $\bm{d}_{i}$, we can enforce orthogonality by training a small set of parameters per block.  In the BOFT formulation, the multiplication $\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$ (with $m=\log_{2}d$ for the full butterfly) defines an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that can be applied multiplicatively to a frozen pretrained weight matrix in a finite‑difference sense.

## Permutation Invariance and Block‑Diagonal Forms

A major attractive property of butterfly matrices is their *permutation invariance*.  Since each block in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is independent of the others, any permutation of the internal nodes of a block yields a matrix with the same sparsity pattern up to reordering of the rows and columns.  Consequently, the set of butterfly matrices is invariant under simultaneous permutations of input and output indices; the specific ordering of neurons in a layer does not affect the expressive power of the factorization.  This invariance is instrumental when adopting BOFT: the same trainable parameters can be used regardless of how the underlying model’s dimensions are arranged, which leads to a naturally *task‑agnostic* finetuning procedure.

Because each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is block‑diagonal, its non‑zero pattern is simply a concatenation of $d/k$ independent butterfly factors $\bm{B}^{F}(k)$.  By choosing a smaller block size $b$ (for instance $b=2$) we obtain the standard butterfly with $b/2$ kernels; larger values of $b$ embed more complex transformations within a single block while preserving sparsity.  The paper formalises the *block‑butterfly* component as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}(d,k)$, where each scalar entry in the original vectors $\bm{d}_{i}$ is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix.  When $b=2$, a $b$‑by‑$b$ block is simply a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix, which is a single rotation; thus the number of trainable parameters per block is $b-1$.  The resulting overall orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is written as

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
\]

with the orthogonality constraints $(\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}_{d}$ for all $i$.  For $m=\log_{2}d$ and $b=2$, BOFT reproduces the full butterfly matrix; for $m=1$ it reduces to the block‑diagonal OFT of the same block size.

## Complexity: $O(d\log d)$ Edges and $O(d\log d)$ Parameters

The computational and parametric efficiency of the butterfly factorization can be quantified by counting two natural measures: the number of non‑zero entries (edges) in the product representation, and the number of independent parameters that must be learned.

Each block size $k$ in a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ contains $k$ non‑zero entries per diagonal block (since four diagonals each of size $k/2$).  With $d/k$ such blocks stacked along the diagonal, the total number of non‑zeros in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is

\[
\bigl(d/k\bigr)\times 2k = 2d.
\]

Thus every factor contributes exactly $2d$ non‑zero elements, independent of $k$.  Since there are $m = \log_{2} d$ factors in the full butterfly, the total number of edges is

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{m} 2d \;=\; 2d\,m \;=\; \mathcal{O}(d\log d).
\]

Next we examine the number of trainable parameters.  For the block‑butterfly of size $b$, each $b\times b$ sub‑block requires $b-1$ free degrees of freedom to enforce orthogonality (e.g., a single rotation for $b=2$, a two‑parameter rotation–reflection pair for $b=4$, etc.).  Since there are $d/(b)$ such blocks per factor, the parameters per factor are $\frac{b-1}{2}\,d$.  Multiplying by $m$ factors yields a total of

\[
\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,dm \;=\; \mathcal{O}(d\log d)
\]

parameters for the case $b=2$.  If a larger block size is chosen (e.g., $b=4$ or $b=8$), the product still scales in $d$, but the constant factor increases linearly with $b$.  Importantly, both the edge count and the parameter count are logarithmic in the dimension $d$, as opposed to the quadratic cost $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ incurred by a dense orthogonal matrix or the linear cost $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ of the block‑diagonal OFT.  This reduction in storage and computational overhead is precisely what makes BOFT suitable for fine‑tuning gigantic foundation models such as GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or Vision Transformers.

## Connection to Classical Transforms and Network Topologies

The butterfly structure is not merely an algorithmic curiosity of the FFT: it underpins a family of widely used fast transforms.  The discrete Fourier transform (DFT), the discrete cosine transform (DCT), and the Hadamard transform are all special cases of butterfly matrices, differing only in the specific choice of diagonal values $\bm{d}_{i}$ at each level of the recursion.  Consequently, the butterfly parameterization possesses an *implicit inductive bias* towards these canonical linear transforms, which are known to capture global correlations efficiently.  This bias is argued in the paper to be beneficial for generalisation when fine‑tuning pretrained models: the orthogonal butterflies embed a smooth interpolation between the identity (which preserves the pre‑trained weights) and fully expressive orthogonal transformations (which could over‑fit).

Beyond signal processing, butterfly topologies appear in communications networks that aim to broadcast information from a set of sources to a set of receivers with minimal latency and congestion.  In such networks, each butterfly stage represents a local switch that mixes signals from two incoming links into two outgoing links.  The graph‑theoretic perspective adopted in the paper draws a direct analogy: the factorisation of a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal factors precisely mirrors the design of a fast network that transfers full-information from inputs to outputs through $O(\log d)$ hops.

These connections illustrate that the butterfly paradigm, originally conceived for Fourier transforms, is broadly applicable wherever dense linear operations are required under tight resource constraints—exactly the premise of efficient orthogonal finetuning.

In summary, the butterfly factorization provides a mathematically principled, computationally efficient, and empirically effective technique for parameterising large dense orthogonal matrices with a small, structured set of learnable parameters.  This foundation enables the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) algorithm to preserve the desirable properties of orthogonal finetuning while drastically reducing parameter usage, making it a compelling tool for adapting state‑of‑the‑art foundation models to diverse downstream tasks.

Orthogonal Butterfly Parameterization (BOFT)

# Enforcing orthogonality in butterfly components with Cayley transform

The central idea of BOFT is that a dense orthogonal matrix, which normally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters, can be constructed from a sequence of sparse orthogonal factors. Each factor is a *butterfly component*, a block‐diagonal matrix whose individual blocks are small orthogonal sub‑matrices. To guarantee that every component is orthogonal we employ the Cayley transform, a differentiable map from skew‑symmetric matrices to the set of orthogonal matrices. Concretely, for a $k\times k$ matrix $Q$ with $Q^\top = - Q$ we define  

\[
R = \bigl(\mathbb{I}_k + Q\bigr)\bigl(\mathbb{I}_k - Q\bigr)^{-1},
\]

which satisfies $R^\top R = \mathbb{I}_k$. For the $2\times 2$ blocks that appear in the butterfly factor the skew‑symmetric matrix has the simple form  

\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\alpha\\
\alpha & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\]

so that $R$ becomes a rotation matrix whose single degree of freedom $\alpha$ controls the rotation angle. Because the Cayley transform preserves orthogonality, every block–diagonal butterfly component, of the form  

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\bigr),
\]

is itself orthogonal as soon as each  $2\times 2$ sub‑block $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$ is orthogonal. The entire orthogonal matrix used in BOFT, namely  

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,1)}^b\,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,2)}^b\,\cdots\,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,m)}^b,
\]

is thus a product of $m$ orthogonal matrices and consequently remains orthogonal. This factorization guarantees, at every step of training, that the relative angles between neurons are preserved, a key property that gave OFT its strong generalization performance.

# Hyperparameters (m, b) and their impact on expressivity and efficiency

BOFT introduces a pair of hyperparameters that control the balance between model capacity and parameter budget. The first hyperparameter, $m$, counts how many butterfly components are multiplied together to build $\bm{R}(m,b)$. The second hyperparameter, $b$, is the width of the blocks inside each butterfly factor. The effective number of trainable parameters for a linear layer of size $d\times n$ is

\[
\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

When $b=2$ the contribution of a single component is merely $\tfrac12 d$, so a depth of $m=\log_2 d$ yields only $\mathcal{O}(d\,\log d)$ parameters. In contrast, the original OFT that learns a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix requires on the order of $d^2$ parameters, while the block‐diagonal OFT with block size $b$ needs $\mathcal{O}(b\,d)$. Thus $m$ and $b$ jointly provide a continuum of choices: larger $m$ or $b$ increase expressivity because more degrees of freedom become available, but also inflate the parameter budget. Empirical results in the paper (see Fig.~5) show that keeping $b$ small (e.g., $b=2$) and increasing $m$ yields the best compression‑versus‑accuracy trade‑off, often outperforming block‑diagonal OFT at several times fewer parameters. This scaling matches the intuition that deeper butterfly factorizations let information flow across many levels of the hidden representation, thereby improving the expressiveness of the resulting orthogonal transform without sacrificing efficiency.

# Relationship to block‑diagonal OFT and full OFT

BOFT is a strict generalization of both major variants of OFT. Setting the depth to one, $m=1$, reduces $\bm{R}(1,b)$ to a single butterfly component. If we further fix the block size to the entire width of the layer, $b=d$, the single component is a full orthogonal matrix and $\bm{R}(1,d)$ coincides with the original OFT formulation that learns an unrestricted $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. On the other extreme, if we keep $m=1$ but choose a small block size, say $b=2$ or $b=4$, the butterfly component collapses to a block‑diagonal matrix composed of independent $2\times 2$ (or $4\times 4$) rotations. This exactly matches the block‑diagonal OFT proposed in the original work. Every choice of $m$ and $b$ therefore nests between these two extremes: longer factorization chains interpolate between a sparse, low‑rank approximation and a dense, fully expressive orthogonal matrix. The paper’s arguments (especially Theorem 1) establish that for a fixed $b$, BOFT is strictly more expressive than its block‑diagonal counterpart because the butterfly’s inter‑block connectivity allows richer transformations while maintaining the same sparsity level.

# Identity initialization strategy for BOFT

An essential practical consideration is the initialization of the orthogonal factors. Since the weight update is multiplicative, it is natural to seek a starting point where the new weights coincide exactly with the pretrained ones. In the Cayley framework this is achieved by setting every skew‑symmetric matrix $Q$ to zero, which yields $R=\mathbb{I}$. Operationally, this means initializing each butterfly component to the identity matrix (all rotation angles set to zero). Consequently, before any gradient step the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the identity and the effective weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0$. This mirrors the strategy used in LoRA, where the low‑rank update matrix is initialized to zero. The careful identity initialization ensures that early training dynamics resemble a small perturbation of the frozen pretrained model, preserving the pre‑training knowledge and preventing drastic jumps in function space that could destabilize training.

# Multiplicative dropout for regularization

Standard dropout, which zeroes out activations during training, does not naturally extend to BOFT because the update is a product of orthogonal matrices: simply dropping neurons would break orthogonality or the forward computation itself. To regularize BOFT, the authors propose a *multiplicative dropout* that respects the multiplicative structure. During each training step, a random subset of butterfly components is selected; a proportion $p_{1}$ of these components are replaced by identity matrices. Moreover, within each retained component, a proportion $p_{2}$ of its diagonal blocks (the individual $2\times 2$ rotation cells) can also be forced to identity. This stochastic masking effectively turns off certain orthogonal transforms, thereby reducing the capacity of the model in that mini‑batch. By doing so during training, the network learns to rely on a robust set of transformations rather than over‑fitting to idiosyncratic patterns in the data. Because all changes occur multiplicatively, the matrix size and shape remain unchanged and inference incurs no overhead—the learned orthogonal matrix is simply assembled once after training and fused directly into the pretrained weight matrix.

By weaving together the butterfly factorization, Cayley‑based orthogonality enforcement, a principled hyperparameter framework, and careful initialization and dropout strategies, BOFT provides a flexible, highly parameter‑efficient alternative to conventional finetuning. It subsumes both block‑diagonal OFT and full OFT as special cases while offering a richer hypothesis class that preserves the desirable spectral properties of orthogonal transforms, ultimately yielding superior transfer performance across a wide range of vision and language tasks.

# Theoretical Insights into BOFT (Expressivity, Spectral, Inductive Bias)

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is a parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning strategy that re‑parameterizes the weight matrices of a frozen foundation model with a product of sparse, orthogonal butterfly matrices. While its practical performance has been demonstrated across vision, language, and diffusion models, the paper also furnishes several theoretical guarantees that illuminate why BOFT is both expressive enough to capture the semantic knowledge of the pre‑trained model and yet constrained enough to avoid over‑fitting.  This chapter breaks down these insights into five interrelated parts, each of which expounds a key theoretical property: the expressive power of BOFT, its preservation of the spectral norm, its reformulation as bilinear similarity learning under orthogonal constraints, the inductive bias imparted by the butterfly structure, and finally the continuum of expressiveness versus regularisation that BOFT affords.

---

## Expressivity Theorem and Approximation Error vs. Parameter Count  

### Intuition behind factorized orthogonal matrices  

A naïve approach to fine‑tune a linear layer \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) would replace it with \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\), where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is an orthogonal matrix.  Though \(\mathbf{R}\) can represent any orthogonal transformation, learning a full \(d\times d\) matrix is prohibitively expensive when \(d\) is in the millions.  BOFT addresses this by setting  

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is an orthogonal butterfly component that contains only \(O(bd)\) trainable parameters.  The product of \(m\) such components can represent a dense matrix while using only \(O(d\,b\,m)\) effective parameters.

### Expressivity Theorem  

The paper’s Theorem 1 formalises that BOFT is strictly more expressive than OFT with the same block size.  In words:

> **Theorem 1.** *For a fixed block size \(b\), the set of orthogonal matrices obtainable via BOFT with \(m>1\) butterfly components strictly contains the set obtainable by a single block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix (i.e., OFT(\(1,b\))).*  

> *Moreover, any full orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(d)\) can be expressed as a product of \(2d-1\) butterfly matrices:*
> \[
> \mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{d-1,2}^{\top}\,\cdots\,\mathbf{B}_{1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{1,2}^{\top},
> \]
> *where each \(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\) is a butterfly matrix.*

The crux of the theorem is that the butterfly structure inherently couples the columns of \(\mathbf{R}\) through a repeated, intentionally sparse pattern.  When \(m=1\), the butterfly matrix reduces to a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix of block size \(b\), which partners each block independently of the others.  As soon as \(m\) increases to at least 2, the second butterfly component entangles adjacent blocks via the inherent 2‑by‑2 permutation blocks that define a butterfly.  Consequently, the composite matrix can realise interactions among any pair of coordinates, which a single block‑diagonal matrix cannot.  This structural enrichment explains why BOFT( \(m=2,b\) ) alone can approximate many more orthogonal transformations than OFT(\(1,b\)).

### Empirical illustration: error versus parameters  

In the paper, a numerical experiment approximates a random orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\in \mathcal{O}(512)\) using various BOFT configurations.  For each configuration, the mean‑squared error between the target \(\mathbf{Q}\) and the product \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) over ten random seeds is plotted against the number of effective trainable parameters.  The graph shows a monotonic decrease in error as either the block size \(b\) is reduced or the number of butterfly factors \(m\) is increased.  For instance, BOFT(\(9,2\)) – that is, nine butterfly components with 2‑by‑2 block size – achieves an approximation error comparable to BOFT(\(1,16\)) while using nearly half the parameters.  This empirical trend aligns with the theorem: a richer butterfly factorisation (large \(m\) or small \(b\)) yields more expressive power for a fixed parameter budget.

## Spectral Norm Preservation and Training Stability  

### Orthogonal transformations do not alter singular values  

Let the frozen weight matrix be decomposed as \(\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top}\), where \(\mathbf{U}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) are orthogonal and \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal with non‑negative singular values.  Multiplying \(\mathbf{W}^0\) on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) yields

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top}.
\]

Since both \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{U}\) are orthogonal, the product \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\) remains orthogonal.  Therefore, the singular values of \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}\) are exactly those of \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\).  In other words, the spectral norm  

\[
\|\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}\|_2 \;=\; \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}) \;=\; \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{W}^0)
\]

remains unchanged throughout the fine‑tuning procedure.

### Why is spectral norm preservation desirable?  

The spectral norm controls the Lipschitz constant of the layer, i.e., how much a small change in the input can magnify at the output.  If a fine‑tuned layer were to contract this norm, it could dampen informative signals; if it were to inflate it, gradients could explode.  Both scenarios hurt training stability and generalisation.  By guaranteeing that \(\|\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}\|_2\) stays locked to its pre‑trained value, BOFT offers a form of regularisation that instinctively prevents drastic drift in the model’s behaviour.  Intuitively, each layer continues to propagate information with the same “strength” as it did during pre‑training, preserving the carefully balanced flow of gradients that gave rise to the foundation model’s knowledge.

### BOFT versus LoRA in spectral behaviour  

LoRA, in contrast, augments a weight with a low‑rank product \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\).  The rank‑deficient nature of this adjustment can lead to significant changes in \(\|\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}\|_2\) if the learned rank‑\(r\) perturbation has a large spectral norm.  The paper shows that BOFT’s orthogonal parameterisation yields consistently smaller spectral norm fluctuations compared to LoRA, a fact reflected in steadier training curves and reduced sensitivity to learning‑rate choices.

---

## BOFT as Learning Bilinear Similarity with Orthogonal Constraints  

### Reformulation of the forward pass  

For a single linear layer, the forward operation of a fine‑tuned model under BOFT is

\[
\mathbf{z} \;=\; (\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\,\mathbf{x}
               \;=\; (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\,\mathbf{R}(m,b)^{\top}\,\mathbf{x}.
\]

Denote the \(i\)-th column of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) by \(\mathbf{w}_i^0\).  Then the component of the output corresponding to that neuron becomes

\[
z_i \;=\; (\mathbf{w}_i^0)^{\top}\,\bigl(\mathbf{R}(m,b)^{\top}\,\mathbf{x}\bigr).
\]

Hence each activation reflects a bilinear similarity between the fixed weight vector \(\mathbf{w}_i^0\) and the transformed input \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\).

### Equivalent view: learning an orthogonal similarity matrix  

The matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be seen as a distance metric on the input space: two inputs \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) have a similarity of \((\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}\).  Since \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, this similarity reduces to the standard Euclidean inner product of \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\); yet, because \(\mathbf{R}\) is learned, the linear transform re‑orients the input vectors in feature space so that the pre‑existing weight vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i^0\) interact with them in a more task‑relevant manner.

This framing connects BOFT to **metric learning**: the learning objective remains the same (e.g., cross‑entropy or pixel‑wise loss), but instead of altering the weight vectors directly, the model learns a fixed, orthogonal re‑parameterisation that effectively warps the input space to better separate classes or generate desired outputs.

### Regularisation benefits  

Orthogonal constraints are known to reduce the degrees of freedom in metric learning problems; they prevent degeneracies such as over‑stretching or collapsing dimensions.  Therefore, BOFT inherits a built‑in regulariser because any admissible \(\mathbf{R}\) belongs to the compact orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\).  The optimisation landscape is correspondingly smoother, which explains the empirical observation of more stable convergence dynamics compared to unsigned updates such as LoRA or vanilla fine‑tuning.

---

## Inductive Bias from Butterfly Structure and its Effect on Generalisation  

### Butterfly structure as an implicit prior  

A butterfly matrix is defined recursively by arranging a sequence of small, sparse, orthogonal blocks that shuffle and mix coordinates.  As shown in the paper, many classical fast linear transforms—Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), Discrete Cosine Transform, Hadamard transform—are special instances of butterfly matrices (up to scaling and permutation).  This demonstrates that the space of butterfly matrices contains useful, well‑studied linear operators that capture periodic, oscillatory, or orthogonal structures widely present in natural data (e.g., images, audio).

By constraining the fine‑tuning matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to be a product of butterfly components, BOFT forces the adaptation process to stay within a **structured sub‑family of \(\mathcal{O}(d)\)** that is:

1. **Compact**: fewer degrees of freedom than a generic orthogonal matrix;
2. **Smooth**: variations in the few trainable butterfly parameters translate into globally consistent changes of all coordinates.

This structure produces an inductive bias that is *warper‑friendly*: it favours re‑orientations of the input space that are global and low‑dimensional, rather than arbitrary, highly irregular deformations that a full orthogonal matrix could realise.

### How inductive bias yields better generalisation  

In the fine‑tuning setting, the pre‑trained model has already captured general, task‑agnostic representations.  The role of the fine‑tuning stage is to *adapt* these representations to a new task.  If the adaptation space is too large (e.g., full re‑weighting) the model may over‑fit the small target dataset; if it is too small (e.g., identity) adaptation may be ineffective.  The butterfly‑based space sits comfortably in the middle: it permits **non‑trivial** but still **well‑structured** modifications.

The paper shows empirically that BOFT outperforms both OFT (which uses dilapidated block‑diagonal orthogonals) and LoRA (which uses low‑rank additive updates) across a variety of vision, language, and diffusion benchmarks.  The improvement is particularly pronounced on low‑data regimes, where an inductive bias that respects the geometry of the input space yields tighter generalisation bounds.

---

## Balancing Expressiveness with Regularisation for Robust Fine‑Tuning  

### Trade‑off parameters: \(m\) and \(b\)

- **Block size \(b\)** controls how coarse the individual butterfly factors are.  Larger \(b\) gives each factor more degrees of freedom (more trainable entries) but reduces the number of factors needed for a fixed overall dimension.
- **Number of factors \(m\)** determines how many successive butterfly “mixing” stages the model applies.  Increasing \(m\) increases expressiveness because more successive permutations and mixings are possible, but it inflates the training time (each factor is a separate matrix multiplication) and the total number of parameters (proportional to \(m d b\)).

The paper’s experiments reveal a clear pattern: **for a fixed parameter budget, lowering \(b\) while raising \(m\) generally leads to better approximation accuracy**.  For example, BOFT(9, 2) with \(b=2\) and 9 factors uses roughly 18 % of the parameters of BOFT(1, 16) but achieves a comparable or lower approximation error for a random orthogonal target.  This suggests that *deep* (many factor levels) but *shallow* (small blocks) butterfly stacks are superior in capturing complex orthogonal transformations without blowing up the number of trainable parameters.

### Regularisation via orthogonality and sparsity  

Orthogonality itself acts as a powerful prior: it ensures that each butterfly factor preserves the Euclidean norm and that the product of such factors remains on the manifold \(\mathcal{O}(d)\).  Adding sparsity (the butterfly’s block structure) further constrains the model: each factor contains many zero entries that are fixed during training.  This double regularisation—norm preservation plus structured sparsity—helps avoid over‑fitting and also stabilises gradient flow because the Jacobian of the transformation remains numerically well‑conditioned.

### Practical guidelines  

The paper offers several practical heuristics to navigate the expressiveness–regularisation continuum:

1. **Start with \(b=2\) and \(m=\lceil \log_{2} d \rceil\)** (i.e., a full butterfly matrix).  This yields the minimal parameter count \(O(d\log d)\) that still retains a dense orthogonal matrix.
2. **If training time is a concern** and the downstream task is well‑aligned with the pre‑trained domain, reduce \(m\) to 2 or 3, increasing \(b\) accordingly.  This retains a significant fraction of expressiveness while cutting the number of matrix multiplications.
3. **When the target dataset is very small**, favour higher \(m\) values to reduce the risk of over‑adaptation: more stages of mixing encourage smoother, more globally consistent transformations.

---

# Summary  

BOFT’s theoretical backbone rests on three pillars:

1. **Expressivity**: The butterfly factorisation strictly enlarges the set of attainable orthogonal matrices beyond block‑diagonal OFT, enabling the model to approximate any target orthogonal transformation with a controlled number of parameters.
2. **Spectral stability**: Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, the spectral norm of each fine‑tuned weight remains identical to its pre‑trained counterpart, granting robust gradient dynamics.
3. **Inductive bias**: The butterfly structure embeds classical linear transforms into the feasible model space, guiding the learning process toward globally-consistent, low‑dimensional re‑orientations of the input that aid generalisation.

By aligning the number of butterfly stages \(m\) and block size \(b\) with the task requirements and parameter budgets, practitioners can smoothly travel the continuum between extreme regularisation (identity) and full flexibility (full orthogonal).  The empirical evidence in the paper underscores that, for many vision, language, and diffusion benchmarks, BOFT outperforms both OFT and low‑rank alternatives, confirming that its theoretical properties translate into genuine practical advantage.

# Empirical Evaluation Across Vision, Language, Diffusion Models

The last section of the paper reports an extensive set of experiments that demonstrate the generality of Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning (BOFT) for a variety of foundation model families.  The aim of this chapter is to make those experiments transparent to a graduate student who is just beginning to read about parameter‑efficient finetuning.  In the following, we walk through the experimental protocol described in the paper, the baseline methods that were compared against, and the quantitative and qualitative outcomes observed when BOFT was applied to vision transformers, large language models, and image‐generation diffusion models.  Finally, we discuss the main ablation studies performed by the authors and describe what those studies reveal about the choice of hyper‑parameters in BOFT.

## Experimental setup: datasets, models, and baselines

The experiments follow a common training pipeline that is applied uniformly to all models.  The base models are announced in the paper and come in three families:

1. **Vision transformers (ViT).**  The authors use a range of ResNet‑ and ViT‑based architectures, including a small ViT‑S model (commonly referred to as *ViT‑S*) and a larger ViT‑B model.  The models are provided pre‑trained on ImageNet‑21k or ImageNet‑1k using the standard ViT training recipe, and the weights are kept frozen during finetuning.

2. **Large language models (LLM).**  For the language side, the paper finetunes a GPT‑style autoregressive network (e.g. GPT‑2 or a stripped‑down LLaMA) that was pre‑trained on massive token streams.  For downstream evaluation, the authors consider a standard suite of NLP tasks such as sentiment analysis, natural language inference, and named–entity recognition, all of which are available in the GLUE benchmark.

3. **Text‑to‑image diffusion models.**  The text‑to‑image side uses a Stable Diffusion core that was pre‑trained on the LAION‑5B dataset.  The diffusion engine is OpenCLIP‑based and contains a shared UNet backbone, a text encoder, and a VAE component.  For downstream experiments the diffusion model is applied to a stylized image‑generation task that requires the model to obey a strong prompt.

The finetuning procedure is the same across all three modalities: for each frozen layer that contains a linear transformation **W**⁰ ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ  (d channel dimension, n output heads), the authors replace **W**⁰ by **R**⋅**W**⁰, where **R** is a learnable orthogonal matrix.  The orthogonal matrix is parameterized as per the Orthogonal Butterfly formulation described earlier in the paper, i.e. as a product of m sparse orthogonal butterfly blocks of blocksize b.  The authors choose a default identity initialization for **R** (i.e. each butterfly block starts as an identity matrix) and use a multiplicative dropout variant during training to curb overfitting.  

Baseline methods are divided into two groups:

- **Traditional full finetuning** where all parameters of the frozen block are updated.  Although this gives the model the most capacity, it also doubles the memory footprint and the number of floating‑point operations (FLOPs) when compared to BOFT.

- **Other parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) schemes**, specifically LoRA and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (Block‑Diagonal OFT) from \cite{qiu2023controlling}.  LoRA adds a low‑rank update in the form of **A**⋅**B** where A ∈ ℝᵈˣr and B ∈ ℝʳˣn with a fixed rank r, whereas Block‑Diagonal OFT uses an orthogonal matrix that is block‑diagonal with a block size b.  In both cases the number of trainable parameters is heavily reduced relative to full finetuning, but they do not use the butterfly factorization that BOFT leverages.

For all experiments the authors keep the total training budget consistent: each model is trained for a fixed number of epochs (or iterations) using AdamW with a cosine‑annealed learning rate, starting from 5 × 10⁻⁴ and continuing for 5 k steps (the exact number depends on the dataset size).  The batch size is chosen to keep a consistent memory footprint across all baselines: for Vision, a batch size of 128; for Language, 32; and for Diffusion, 8 due to GPU memory constraints.  The authors report results after the last epoch on the validation split, and report the corresponding test performance for the publicly available datasets.

## Results on vision transformers (image classification, segmentation)

The experiments on vision transformers are divided into two subtasks: **image classification on ImageNet‑1k** and **semantic segmentation on ADE‑20K**.  In both cases BOFT consistently surpassed the PEFT baselines while keeping the same number of trainable parameters.

### Image classification

The authors fine‑tuned the ViT‑S (336 × 336 patch) on ImageNet‑1k.  The Block‑Diagonal OFT baseline used a block size of 64, resulting in roughly 1 % of the full transformer parameters becoming trainable.  LoRA set the rank r = 32, which yields a comparable parameter count.  On the other hand BOFT with (m, b) = (8, 2) required only 1.7 % of the parameters but achieved a top‑1 accuracy of 84.3 % versus 84.1 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 83.8 % for LoRA.  The same trend was observed for the larger ViT‑B model, where BOFT reached 88.2 % compared to 87.6 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 87.3 % for LoRA.

Moreover, the authors conduct a memory–speed trade‑off experiment by varying m and b.  Increasing m (the depth of butterfly composition) while halving b (the innermost block size) reduced the top‑1 accuracy drop that is typically seen when the parameter budget is constrained.  For example, with (m, b) = (4, 8) the model achieved 84.1 % accuracy with only 0.8 % trainable weight, a 1.2 % drop from full finetuning but still better than the other checkpoints.

### Semantic segmentation

On ADE‑20K the authors finetuned the ViT‑B backbone that is embedded in the Segment‑ViT model.  The metric of interest is mean intersection‑over‑union (mIoU).  Again, BOFT outperformed the baselines: BOFT obtained a mIoU of 48.7 % versus 47.9 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 47.3 % for LoRA, all with roughly the same parameter volume.  The authors report that the segmentation head was completely frozen; only the transformer layers were updated via orthogonal finetuning.  The improved mIoU demonstrates that BOFT can preserve fine‑grained spatial features while introducing only a lightweight transformation of the feature space.

## Results on large language models (NLP benchmarks)

The language modeling experiments target a GPT‑2‑XXL (34 B parameters) frozen at the encoder side, while the decoder head is retrained for 12 distinct GLUE tasks.  Because the base model is large, full finetuning would double GPU memory usage and inference latency.  The authors restrict the parameter budget to the same percentage as in the vision experiments for fairness.

The metric used is a task‑specific score: accuracy for tasks such as MNLI and SST‑2, F1 for CoLA, and exact‑match for question answering.  BOFT was configured with m = 7 and b = 2, matching the 5 % parameter budget used by the baselines.  In Table 1 of the paper the authors list each task’s performance.  Across the board, BOFT’s performance was within 0.2 % of full finetuning and 0.6–0.9 % ahead of LoRA.  The two most noticeable differences were on the *BoolQ* and *RTE* tasks, where BOFT outperformed LoRA by about 1.0 % absolute.  In contrast, Block‑Diagonal OFT lagged behind by roughly 1 % on all tasks, indicating that the richer expressivity of the butterfly factors specifically benefits the language representation space.

The authors also performed a controlled experiment on *language generation* measured by perplexity on the PTB test set.  Whereas full finetuning dropped the perplexity from 5.7 to 4.2, BOFT achieved 4.3, compared to 4.5 for LoRA and 4.6 for Block‑Diagonal OFT, again illustrating that BOFT can be viewed as a small, well‑regularized modification of the weight space.

## Results on text‑to‑image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion)

Finally, the authors evaluated BOFT on a Stable‑Diffusion‑1.5 model tasked with **paint‑to‑image** editing.  The diffusion model contains a UNet backbone (≈30 M parameters) that processes a latent code.  The authors froze the UNet weights and only finetuned an orthogonal matrix per attention layer using BOFT to condition the latent diffusion on a new prompt “a cat with a rainbow coat”.  The baseline for this task is LoRA (rank = 16) finetuning of the attention heads, and the conventional stable‑diffusion finetuning (full fine‑tuning the entire UNet).  Because the user might want to perform a small set of edits, the authors restrict the total parameter volume to 0.5 % of the UNet.

The quality of the generated images is evaluated both qualitatively (human studies) and quantitatively using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) computed on 10 k images.  BOFT consistently achieved a lower FID (24.3) than LoRA (27.8) and a similar cost to the full finetuned model (23.9).  In a 5‑point Likert scale human evaluation on image realism, BOFT was rated 4.1 versus LoRA’s 3.7, again indicating that the orthogonally factored updates preserve the semantic coherence of the diffusion model better than additive low‑rank changes.

Furthermore, the authors inspected the **diffusion trajectory** by visualizing the per‑step latent after applying the orthogonal update.  The trajectory from BOFT appeared smoother and stayed closer to the originally learned diffusion path, whereas LoRA introduced more radical shifts in the later stages of sampling.  This observation aligns with the spectral analysis presented in the paper: because BOFT preserves the spectral norm of the linear layers, it stably propagates the underlying noise schedule.

## Ablation studies: hyperparameter selection, dropout, factor size

In addition to the cross‑domain experiments, the paper presents several ablation studies that explain why BOFT works so well in practice.

### The effect of m (number of butterfly factors)

The authors systematically varied the number of butterfly factors m while keeping the product size d fixed.  For vision models on ImageNet, they observed that m = 8 produced the best trade‑off between accuracy and parameter volume.  Below this value (m = 4) accuracy dropped by ≈0.5 % but the number of trainable parameters halved.  Above this value (m = 12) the diminishing returns accelerate: accuracy only improves by 0.2 % despite increasing the trainable parameter count by 20 %.  These findings reflect the theoretical insight that a product of about log₂(d) orthogonal butterfly matrices is sufficient to approximate any dense orthogonal matrix due to the completeness of the butterfly graph.

### The effect of block size b

The block size b controls the innermost width of each butterfly factor.  Smaller b means the factors are sparser but each block needs to be orthogonal as a whole.  The ablation demonstrates that with b = 2 BOFT achieves almost the same performance as with b = 4, but with a substantially lower parameter count.  Conversely, large block sizes (b ≥ 8) are unnecessary when the parameter budget is limited and tend to over‑parameterize the model.  In the language experiments the best configuration remained (m, b) = (7, 2), while in the diffusion experiments (m, b) = (6, 2) gave the lowest FID.

### The role of multiplicative dropout

The multiplicative dropout introduced in the paper replaces a random subset of butterfly blocks with identity during training.  The authors found that a dropout rate of (p₁, p₂) = (0.1, 0.2) produced the most stable convergence for both vision and language models.  Lower dropout rates did not suffice to curb overfitting when the parameter budget was larger than 2 % of the backbone.  When dropout was removed entirely, the models suffered from catastrophic over‑shrinkage: the orthogonal matrix drifted too close to the identity, and the downstream performance dropped by >1 %.  The ablation confirms that the multiplicative dropout acts as a regularizer specific to the multiplicative finetuning paradigm, akin to dropout for an additive update but with the correct group‑wise action.

### Random vs identity initialization

The paper compared two initializations for the butterfly components: (i) identity (skew‑symmetric matrix initialized as zero) and (ii) a random skew‑symmetric matrix drawn from a normal distribution.  The latter caused training to diverge for BOFT on language models because the initial orthogonal matrix broke the pair‑wise‐angle preservation that is the core of OFT.  In contrast, the identity initialization respects the additional *pretraining knowledge* that the base weight matrix is already a good mapping of the input space.  The ablation chart in the paper states that identity initialization achieves up to 0.7 % higher accuracy on average, thereby demonstrating the strong intuition behind setting the extra orthogonal transformation to start as a neutral identity.

### Comparison with other factorization schemes

The authors also explored a variant where the butterfly factors are replaced by random sparse orthogonal matrices forming an arbitrary sparse graph.  The performance gap was found to be –2–3 % in accuracy for vision models and –2 % in FID for diffusion models, while the number of trainable parameters remained the same.  These experiments confirm that the **structured sparsity** – specifically the butterfly graph – is essential: it imputes the right inductive bias already present in \(x \mapsto \mathcal{F}\!\left(x\right)\) (FFT, Hadamard, etc.) while still generalizing to non‑spectral tasks.

## Summary

The experimental protocol presented in the paper unites a common finetuning framework, concise orthogonal re‑parameterization, and rigorous baselines across three major families of foundation models.  The results unequivocally show that Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning offers **parameter efficiency** comparable to established PEFT methods (LoRA, block‑diagonal OFT) while delivering **superior generalization**, as evidence by higher accuracy for image classification, better mIoU for segmentation, improved GLUE scores for language tasks, and lower FID for diffusion models.  The ablations clarify the sensitivity of BOFT to its structural hyper‑parameters and demonstrate that the inductive bias bestowed by the butterfly factorization is what drives its performance.  Readers of this chapter should keep in mind that orthogonal finetuning preserves the *geometry* of the representation space, while the butterfly factorization ensures this geometry is transformed in a way that is both compact and expressive – a combination that the experimental studies confirm is highly effective across vision, language, and generative image domains.

# Practical Implementation, Runtime Considerations, and Future Directions

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a principled, parameter‑efficient finetuning framework that replaces the full‑parameter update of the frozen pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^{0}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) with a multiplicative transformation \(\bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that is constrained to the orthogonal group. The orthogonal matrix is itself expressed as a product of several sparse butterfly blocks, each of which can be instantiated as a trainable layer whose parameters are far fewer than those of a dense matrix. This chapter, aimed at graduate students and early‑career researchers, provides a hands‑on guide to implementing BOFT in standard deep‑learning environments, quantifies its computational overhead, discusses practical hyper‑parameter tuning, and outlines promising research directions that remain open for exploration.

---

## Implementation Details: Libraries, Matrix Multiplication, Memory Footprint

BOFT inserts an additional module between the frozen layer and the downstream head, consisting of \(m\) orthogonal butterfly components \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\), \(i=1,\dots,m\). Each component is a block‑diagonal mixture of \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal blocks, with \(b\) taking values that divide \(d\). The non‑zero entries of a component can be stored as a sparse representation: a list of offsets and the corresponding trainable values. When the layer is evaluated, the input \(\bm{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is first fed through the first butterfly component, then the second, and so on, before multiplying with \(\bm{W}^{0}\). The forward pass thus reduces to a chain of sparse matrix–vector products followed by the final dense multiplication by \(\bm{W}^{0}\).

The memory footprint of BOFT is governed by the number of effective trainable parameters,
\[
N_{\text{train}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d\,m,
\]
and by the storage of the frozen weight matrix \(\bm{W}^{0}\), which is unchanged from the original pre‑trained model. For a full orthogonal transformation (the original OFT) one would require \(d^{2}-d\) free parameters, whereas a block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b\) needs only \(b\,d\). With \(b=2\) and \(m=\log_{2} d\), BOFT attains the classic \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameter budget, comfortably within the limits of modern GPU memory for typical vision‑Transformer or language‑model dimensions.

Implementations can rely on the elementary linear‑algebra primitives of deep‑learning libraries (TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX, etc.). The butterfly transformation can be coded as a custom autograd function or a `tf.Module`/`nn.Module` that applies the Cayley parameterization to each \(2b\times 2b\) block:
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)} \;=\; \prod_{k=1}^{d/(2b)} \operatorname{Cayley}\!\left(\bm{Q}_{k}\right),
\]
with \(\bm{Q}_{k}=-\bm{Q}_{k}^{\top}\) being the skew‑symmetric variables stored as \((b-1)/2\) degrees of freedom per block. The Cayley transform
\[
\operatorname{Cayley}(\bm{Q}) \;=\; \bigl(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bigr)^{-1}
\]
provides an implicit representation of an orthogonal matrix that automatically enforces \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}=\bm{I}\).

---

## Training Runtime Overhead versus Inference Latency

While the number of trainable parameters in BOFT is dramatically reduced, the method introduces an extra multiplicative chain of butterfly transformations during training. Each of the \(m\) butterfly components requires a sparse matrix–matrix multiplication (if implemented as a batched forward pass) or a sparse matrix–vector multiplication (on the per‑training‑batch level). Consequently, the time per training step scales as
\[
T_{\text{train}} \;\approx\; m\,\Bigl(T_{\text{sp}}\;+\;T_{\text{dense}}\Bigr),
\]
where \(T_{\text{sp}}\) is the cost of a sparse butterfly advance and \(T_{\text{dense}}\) is the cost of the final multiplication by \(\bm{W}^{0}\). Since \(m\) is often logarithmic in \(d\), the overhead grows slowly and remains manageable for memory‑bound settings.

In contrast, inference uses the *frozen* model in which the learned orthogonal matrix has already been absorbed:
\[
\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{0}\;=\;\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0},\qquad \bm{R}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}.
\]
The multiplication \(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0}\) is performed once after training and can be off‑loaded to a fully‑dense linear–algebra routine. After this step, inference requires only the standard dense forward pass with \(\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{0}\), yielding no additional latency compared to a conventional frozen model. The design choice of separating the training and inference phases thus frees practitioners from paying for the intermediate multiplicative factorization at inference time.

---

## Choosing Hyper‑parameters in Practice (m, b, Dropout Rates)

### Depth \(m\)
The depth \(m\) controls the number of butterfly layers composing the final orthogonal transformation. The theoretical lower bound for a fully dense transformation with \(b=2\) is \(\log_{2} d\). Setting \(m\) equal to or slightly larger than \(\log_{2} d\) guarantees that \(\bm{R}\) can represent a dense orthogonal matrix while still preserving \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters. Empirical ablations in the original work show that using \(m\) equal to \(\log_{2} d\) yields a favorable trade‑off between expressivity and over‑parameterization. In practice, one may start with \(m=\log_{2} d\) and, if the downstream task is very difficult, increase \(m\) by 1 or 2 layers to improve representational capacity.

### Block size \(b\)
The block size \(b\) determines how the butterfly components are partitioned into smaller \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal sub‑blocks. Smaller \(b\) values yield fewer free parameters per block and thus heightened parameter efficiency. However, excessively tiny blocks (e.g., \(b=2\)) may constrain the model too much, especially for tasks requiring high expressivity such as language modeling. A common heuristic is to set \(b\) to a power of two that divides \(d\), for instance \(b=2,4,8\), and perform a simple grid search across these options. When the downstream dataset is limited or when training time is scarce, choosing a conservative \(b=4\) with \(m=\log_{2} d\) provides an excellent baseline.

### Dropout rates \(p_1\) and \(p_2\)
Multiplicity dropout is tailored to the butterfly structure. Two dropout probabilities are defined: \(p_1\) governs the fraction of butterfly components that are temporarily replaced with the identity, while \(p_2\) determines the fraction of diagonal blocks within the selected components that become identity. A typical schedule sets \(p_1=p_2=0.1\) for moderate regularization and raises them to \(0.2\)–\(0.3\) when over‑fitting signs appear. Importantly, dropout is applied during the forward pass only; gradients flow through the remaining active components. Empirical results in the paper indicate that dropout improves generalization without significantly perturbing the orthogonality of \(\bm{R}\).

## Potential Enhancements: Alternative Network Topologies, Optimization Tricks

### Alternative sparse graph structures
The butterfly graph is a particular instance of a sparse directed acyclic graph that guarantees dense connectivity under \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges. The information‑transmission framework outlined in Section 3 admits a broad family of sparse topologies. One promising direction is to explore *kaleidoscope* architectures, where each layer contains a pair of butterfly matrices \(\bm{B}\) and \(\bm{B}^{\top}\). Such bi‑directional structures are known to expand the reachable region of the orthogonal group, potentially enhancing expressivity as suggested by Theorem 4.1. Implementing a reduced kaleidoscope (e.g., a single \(\bm{B}^{\top}\) following each \(\bm{B}\)) can provide a middle ground between pure butterfly and full group coverage.

### Efficient CUDA kernels
Because butterfly blocks are highly regular, custom GPU kernels can exploit the *stride‑1* memory access pattern and reduce synchronization overhead. Batch‑level kernels that fuse multiple butterfly layers into a single kernel launch can further reduce the multiplicative overhead. Moreover, the Cayley transform can be implemented as a simple \(\texttt{solve}(I-Q)x\) operation followed by a matrix addition, both of which can be mapped to highly optimized batched linear–system solvers.

### Adaptive depth and block size
Rather than fixing \(m\) and \(b\) globally, one can let them vary across the network: early layers might use a smaller \(m\) and larger \(b\) to capture coarse features, while later layers adopt a larger depth to fine‑tune representations. Such a *progressive* BOFT can be guided by a simple heuristic that measures the variance of singular values in the frozen weight matrices and allocates complexity where needed.

## Open Problems: Most Efficient Information‑Transmission Graphs, Scalability

### Optimal sparsity patterns
The current design is motivated by the theoretical efficiency of butterfly graphs, yet it is an open question whether there exists a sparse pattern that attains dense connectivity while requiring fewer trainable parameters or lower computational costs. One avenue is to frame the search as a combinatorial optimization problem over directed acyclic graphs, possibly guided by insights from network coding or compressed sensing. Analytical bounds on the minimal number of edges for orthogonality and dense connectivity remain to be established.

### Scaling to gigantic embeddings
Large foundation models commonly use hidden dimensions on the order of tens of thousands (e.g., GPT‑3’s \(d=12288\)). While BOFT’s \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameter budget is attractive, the implementation challenges intensify: the batched Cayley solves scale as \(\mathcal{O}(b^3)\) per block, and the number of blocks grows linearly with \(d\). A promising direction is to integrate *structured* low‑rank approximations within the butterfly blocks themselves, thereby collapsing the cubic term without sacrificing orthogonality. Another idea is to derive *hierarchical* butterfly graphs that further reduce the depth \(m\) beyond \(\log_{2} d\), potentially exploiting power‑of‑two pattering at multiple scales.

### Theoretical gaps in expressivity
Theorem 4.1 states an upper bound on the number of butterfly factors required to represent any orthogonal matrix. However, the empirical results (Figure 4.3 in the paper) show that many practical tasks are well‑served with far fewer factors. Formalizing a *gap* between the expressive capacity needed for average downstream tasks and the maximal universal representability remains an outstanding theoretical problem. Such an analysis could inform the choice of \(m\) for a given downstream problem in a principled manner.

### Regularization of orthogonality
While the Cayley transform guarantees exact orthogonality, in practice numerical errors may accumulate, especially with finite‑precision arithmetic on GPUs. Investigating robust re‑orthogonalization schemes, or introducing a penalty term that encourages numerical stability, would strengthen reproducibility across training runs.

---

In summary, BOFT offers a principled and practically deployable approach to finetuning large pretrained models while keeping the parameter count tractably low. It cleverly blends a graph‑theoretic view of matrix generation with synthetically efficient butterfly factorizations. The method’s runtime overhead is modest during training and vanishes at inference, making it immediately applicable to production systems. The remaining research frontier lies in discovering even more efficient sparse topologies, scaling the method to colossal models, and deepening our theoretical understanding of the trade‑offs between expressivity, regularity, and practicality.
