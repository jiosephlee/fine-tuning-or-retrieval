# From Massive Models to Tiny Tweaks: The Quest for Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning  

Large foundation models such as GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion contain hundreds of billions of weights. For most researchers and practitioners, the prospect of retraining such a model from scratch is simply out of reach. Instead, the field has turned to *parameter‑efficient finetuning* (PEFT): a way to adapt a frozen base model to a new task while adding only a few hundred or thousand learnable parameters. This post dives into one of the newest entrants in the PEFT arena—**Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**—and explains why “tiny tweaks” can be surprisingly powerful.

---

## 1. Why Parameter Efficiency Matters  

When a model contains \(175\times10^9\) parameters, a single epoch of training requires terabytes of GPU memory, weeks of compute time, and huge monetary costs. Even conventional *full* finetuning—updating all weights—turns out to be wasteful once a large pre‑trained back‑bone is already in place. The crux of the problem is that we want to preserve the knowledge the base model has learned while making only a lightweight adjustment that depends on the new data.

PEFT addresses this by *constraining* the changes we allow to the model. Two popular families of PEFT methods are:
* **Adapter tuning**—small bottleneck layers are inserted into each module.
* **Prompt tuning**—extra tokens are appended to the input to steer the network.

A third, more “classic” class of PEFT is *model finetuning*: we freeze the base parameters and apply a transformation to them. This approach keeps inference latency low (since the architecture remains unchanged) and often yields the best performance. The natural question is: *how do we transform the model in a way that is expressive yet compact?*

---

## 2. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – The Starting Point  

The idea behind OFT is elegant: instead of adding a new matrix on top of a weight layer, we *rotate* the whole layer’s columns by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\):
\[
\mathbf{z} \;=\; (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is the frozen pretrained weight matrix, \(\mathbf{x}\) is the input, and \(\mathbf{z}\) is the output. Because orthogonal matrices preserve pairwise angles, OFT keeps the geometry of the original representation intact—an important inductive bias.

However, a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix contains \(d^2\) entries, which is huge for a transformer layer with hidden size \(d=4096\). OFT therefore introduced a block‑diagonal scheme: \(\mathbf{R}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r)\), where each block \(\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) is orthogonal and \(br=d\). This reduces the trainable parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\), but it also limits the expressiveness because different subspaces are rotated independently.

The key question that drove the research was: *Can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without the cost of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters?* That’s where the butterfly idea enters the story.

---

## 3. The Information‑Transmission View  

A dense orthogonal matrix can be seen as a *complete* communication graph between \(d\) “source” nodes and \(d\) “destination” nodes. Each non‑zero entry \(R_{ij}\) signals a direct message from source \(j\) to destination \(i\). 

If we decompose \(\mathbf{R}\) into a product of sparse matrices:
\[
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]
then each \(\mathbf{B}_k\) acts like a communication step that only allows messages along a few edges. For the product to be *fully connected*—i.e., any source can reach any destination—every source must have a path through the grid of successive sparse layers. The information‑transmission perspective tells us that we need not connect every node directly; we can use intermediate hops.

This abstraction leads to a straightforward optimisation problem: **add as few edges as possible while guaranteeing full connectivity and orthogonality**. The solution point is where the butterfly factorization shines.

---

## 4. Butterfly Factorization – Borrowing FFT Efficiency  

The butterfly structure comes from the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT). In the FFT, a size‑\(k\) transform is recursively decomposed into two size‑\(k/2\) transforms joined by simple “butterfly” operations that only require \(k\) multiply–add pairs. When mapped into matrices, this yields a product of sparse orthogonal matrices whose total non‑zero count scales like \(\mathcal{O}(k\log k)\).

In the BOFT framework we generalise this idea to any power of two, \(d=2^N\). A *butterfly factor* is a block‑diagonal matrix made of \(2\times 2\) rotation blocks:
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
with \(\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\). By stringing together such factors at progressively larger scales we build a *butterfly matrix* \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) that is still fully dense but is parameterised by only
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm
\]
effective trainable parameters, where \(m\) is the number of butterfly stages and \(b\) is the block size (number of rows in each rotation block).

When \(m=\log_2 d\) and \(b=2\), we reach
\[
\mathcal{O}(d\log d)
\]
parameters—orders of magnitude fewer than the naive dense orthogonal matrix, yet still able to capture a rich class of rotations.

---

## 5. BOFT in Practice  

### 5.1 Training Dynamics  

* **Identity initialization** – Each butterfly component starts as the identity matrix (Cayley skew‑symmetric matrix initialized to zero), ensuring that the finetuned model begins exactly as the pretrained one.

* **Multiplicative dropout** – Traditional dropout cannot be applied directly because the orthogonal matrix is multiplied into the weight matrix. BOFT overcomes this with a drop‑and‑replace strategy: it picks a percentage of butterfly stages and a percentage of rotation blocks at random and temporarily sets them to identity during the forward pass. This regularises the model without breaking orthogonality.

### 5.2 Expressiveness vs. Regularisation  

Even though BOFT imposes a particular sparsity pattern, it remains strictly more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT for a given block size. empirically, a BOFT model with \(m=9\) stages and \(b=2\) performs as well as or better than a block‑diagonal OFT with \(b=16\), but with far fewer parameters. Moreover, because BOFT can approximate *any* orthogonal matrix given enough stages, it offers a smooth interpolation between a highly regular identity matrix and a fully expressive dense rotation.

### 5.3 Spectral Properties  

Orthogonal finetuning preserves the largest singular value of the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top\):  
\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top,
\]
which does not change \(\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2\). This stability is known to aid training convergence and generalisation, a trait that BOFT shares with OFT and LoRA.

---

## 6. Experimental Highlights  

BOFT was tested on a variety of backbone architectures: large vision transformers, large language models, and text‑to‑image diffusion models. Across these domains, BOFT consistently:

* **Surpassed LoRA** and the original OFT in downstream task accuracy, even with a fraction of their trainable parameters.
* Retained the inference latency advantage of model finetuning—no new layers or extra computation during inference.
* Showed stable learning curves and resistance to over‑fitting, a testament to the inductive bias introduced by the butterfly structure.

These findings demonstrate that a *structured* orthogonal transformation can be a powerful and efficient way to adapt massive models.

---

## 7. Take‑Away Messages  

1. **Parameter‑efficiency is vital**: With billions of parameters in modern backbones, we need to tweak the model cheaply.
2. **Orthogonal finetuning preserves knowledge**: Rotations maintain the geometry of learned representations.
3. **Block‑diagonal OFT trades expressiveness for space**—but can still be limited.
4. **Butterfly factorisation provides a sweet spot**: \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters achieve dense, expressive orthogonal matrices.
5. **BOFT is simple to implement**: Identity init, Cayley‑based orthogonal blocks, optional multiplicative dropout.

---

## 8. Open Questions & Future Directions  

* **Training speed** – Each multiplier step in BOFT requires a separate matrix multiplication, slightly increasing runtime compared to a single \(d\times d\) orthogonal update. Finding faster kernel implementations or optimising the block‑size schedule are promising avenues.
* **Optimal network topology** – While butterfly networks are inspired by FFT, other sparse communication graphs might transmit information even more efficiently. Cross‑pollinating ideas from computer networking could yield new factorisations.
* **Broader inductive biases** – The butterfly structure already captures several classical transforms (FFT, Hadamard). Exploring which properties of these transforms contribute most to generalisation could guide future PEFT designs.

---

### Closing Thought  

When we think of a billion‑parameter model, the first instinct is to treat it as an immutable monolith. BOFT reminds us that *structure matters*: by factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a cascade of tiny, efficiently connectable rotations, we can tweak a giant model with a handful of learned parameters—without sacrificing performance or inference speed. As foundation models keep growing, such principled, parameter‑efficient finetuning will become not just a convenience, but a necessity.

# Angle Matters: How Orthogonal Finetuning Preserves Knowledge

Fine‑tuning a huge foundation model can be the equivalent of running a marathon: it requires a lot of compute, time, and resources.  What if we could keep most of the knowledge that a model has already learned during pre‑training, and only “nudge’’ it toward a new task?  Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a surprisingly simple idea that does exactly that—by rotating the learned weight space, it keeps the geometry intact.  The new Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method builds on this principle while drastically cutting the number of trainable parameters.  In this post we walk through why preserving angles is so important, how OFT achieves it, and why BOFT makes it practical for real‑world adaptation.

---

## 1. Orthogonal Finetuning in a nutshell

A deep network layer can be written as a weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) that is multiplied by an input vector \( \mathbf{x} \).  In OFT we keep the pretrained weight frozen and multiply it by an **orthogonal** matrix \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \):

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]

Because \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal (\( \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} \)), it is a rigid transformation: all pairwise angles between the columns of \( \mathbf{W}^0 \) (the “neurons”) stay exactly the same after the multiplication.  The pretrained knowledge is therefore **preserved** while the newly learned \( \mathbf{R} \) allows the model to specialize to the downstream task.

### How is \( \mathbf{R} \) kept orthogonal during training?

A convenient way to parameterize an orthogonal matrix is the **Cayley transform**:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \bigl(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\bigr)^{-1},
\]
where \( \mathbf{Q} \) is a skew‑symmetric matrix (\( \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top \)).  
We initialise \( \mathbf{Q} \) with zeros so that \( \mathbf{R} \) starts as the identity matrix and thus the initial finetuned model is identical to the pretrained one.

---

## 2. Why preserving angles matters

### Geometry of a layer

Each column \( \mathbf{w}_i^0 \) of \( \mathbf{W}^0 \) can be viewed as a feature vector in a \( d \)-dimensional space.  The angle between two such vectors tells us how similar the corresponding neurons respond to inputs.  During pre‑training, the geometry of these vectors encodes rich semantic structure—for example, related concepts tend to lie close together.

### Orthogonal transformations are isometries

By rotating this geometry with an orthogonal matrix we *re‑orient* the whole set of neurons without changing any distances or angles:
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_i^0\| = \|\mathbf{w}_i^0\|, \qquad
\angle(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_j^0) = \angle(\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{w}_j^0).
\]
Consequently, the semantic relationships learned during pre‑training are preserved exactly, while the network can still be guided to what the downstream task likes via the new matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).

### Spectral stability

In addition to preserving geometry, multiplying by a unitary matrix keeps all singular values of the weight matrix unchanged.  Since the spectral norm controls the amplification of gradients, this property tends to lead to more stable training and better generalisation—something that has been observed when comparing OFT to additive schemes such as LoRA.

---

## 3. The parameter‑efficiency challenge

While OFT is conceptually elegant, a full orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) contains \( O(d^2) \) trainable numbers.  For modern vision transformers or language models where \( d \) can reach hundreds of thousands, that is prohibitive.

The original paper introduced a **block‑diagonal** shortcut: split \( \mathbf{R} \) into many small orthogonal blocks of size \( b \times b \).  This reduces the number of free parameters to \( O(bd) \), but it also forces the learning dynamics to operate on disjoint subspaces, imposing an undesirable inductive bias and limiting what \( \mathbf{R} \) can represent.

The key question: *Can we build a dense orthogonal matrix with far fewer trainable parameters?*

---

## 4. Factorising orthogonal matrices via sparse butterfly blocks

### The information‑transmission view

Think of a \( d \times d \) matrix as a network that allows every data packet (information) in the first layer to reach every node in the last layer.  A dense matrix is a fully connected network, but we can also build a dense transformation by linking a series of sparse networks.  Each sparse matrix acts as an intermediate “relay” layer.

Mathematically we write
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\,\dots\,\mathbf{B}_1,
\]
where every \( \mathbf{B}_i \) is a sparse orthogonal matrix (e.g. with only a few non‑zero entries).  If the architecture of these sparse matrices allows a path from every input node to every output node, their product is dense—yet we have only a few parameters to tune.

### Butterfly structures from the fast Fourier transform

The classic Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm rearranges computations with a repeated “butterfly” pattern.  A butterfly block \( \mathbf{B}^F(k) \) for size \( k \) looks like

\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each \( \mathbf{d}_i\in \mathbb{R}^{k/2} \) is a vector of trainable scalars.  
A full \( d=2^N \) butterfly matrix is built recursively from smaller butterflies:

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)= \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\;
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\;\dots\;
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),
\]
with each \( \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k) \) being block‑diagonal but with a butterfly pattern in each block.

Because every butterfly component contains only \( O(d) \) non‑zero entries and we stack \( O(\log d) \) such components, the total number of trainable parameters is \( O(d \log d) \)—a dramatic savings compared to the \( O(d^2) \) of a full orthogonal matrix.

---

## 5. Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)

BOFT replaces the single orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) in OFT with a product of butterfly components:

\[
\mathbf{R}_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)
= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
\]
where \( m \) is the number of butterfly factors and \( b \) controls block size.  
When \( m=1\) and \(b=d\), BOFT collapses to vanilla OFT (a full orthogonal matrix).  
When \(m=1\) and \(b\) is small, BOFT is identical to the block‑diagonal OFT used earlier.

Key properties:

1. **Identity initialization** – all butterfly components start as identity matrices, so the initial finetuned model equals the pretrained one.
2. **Multiplicative dropout** – during training we randomly replace some butterfly blocks by identity, analogous to dropout for additive fine‑tuning methods, which regularises the learning of the orthogonal transform.
3. **Expressivity vs. regularisation** – experiments in the paper show that using modest \( b\) (e.g. \( b=2\)) and more butterfly layers (larger \( m\)) provides a richer hypothesis class than the same‑size block‑diagonal scheme, while still keeping the parameter budget low.
4. **No inference overhead** – after training, the product \( \mathbf{R}_{\text{BOFT}}\mathbf{W}^0 \) can be pre‑computed and merged into the base model, so the finetuned model runs at the same speed as the original.

BOFT was tested on large vision transformers, language models, and text‑to‑image diffusion models.  Across all tasks it outperformed state‑of‑the‑art parameter‑efficient methods, proving that a structured, angle‑preserving transform can really hurt the performance‑parameter trade‑off.

---

## 6. The big picture: angles, inductive bias, and generalisation

- **Angles as semantic glue** – because angles capture how neurons relate to one another, preserving them protects the high‑level knowledge captured during pre‑training.
- **Orthogonality’s spectral benefits** – keeping singular values fixed avoids exploding or vanishing gradients, giving the optimiser a stable playground.
- **Butterfly factorisation gives an inductive bias** – the specific connectivity pattern of butterflies is a subset of classical linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard, cosine transforms).  This bias hints at “nice” directions that the model can smoothly explore while still being expressive enough for new tasks.
- **Trade‑off handled elegantly** – BOFT sits between the extremes of full‑fine‑tuned models (universal but over‑fitting) and extremely compact methods (under‑powered).  It preserves most of the useful geometry, yet offers enough freedom to adapt.

---

## 7. Take‑away message

- **Angle preservation is more than a neat trick**: it is a principled way to keep what a massive pretrained model has learned, while still giving it room to specialise.
- **Layer‑wise orthogonal rotations are parameter‑cheap** thanks to butterfly factorisation, yielding \( O(d\log d) \) trainable numbers instead of \( O(d^2) \).
- **BOFT demonstrates the potential of structured re‑parameterisation**: by borrowing ideas from signal processing (FFT) and network theory (butterfly graphs), we can build finetuning methods that are both efficient and powerful.

If you’re looking for a sweet spot between full‑finetuning and lightweight adapters, orthogonal butterfly finetuning is a promising direction to explore.



# Fast Fourier Meets AI: Butterfly Factorization Makes Orthogonal Finetuning Feasible  

*How a classic signal‑processing trick lets us adapt gigantic foundation models with far fewer trainable weights.*

---

## 1.  The Need to Keep the “Core” of a Large Model  

When the GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or ResNet‑101 families grew into hundreds of billions of parameters, the cost of starting from scratch became prohibitive. The usual strategy is to **fine‑tune** a frozen back‑bone on a new dataset. In the last two years, a handful of parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) methods have emerged:

| PEFT family | How it works | Typical parameter budget |
|-------------|--------------|-------------------------|
| **Adapters** | Adds small bottleneck modules on top of layers | \(O(\sum \text{per‑layer dims})\) |
| **Prompt‑tuning** | Pads the input with learnable tokens | \(O(\text{prompt size})\) |
| **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** | Re‑parameterizes each weight matrix as a product of an *orthogonal* matrix \(R\) and the frozen weights \(W^0\) | \(O(d^2)\) (dense orthogonal matrix) |

OFT is attractive because an orthogonal matrix *preserves angles* between neurons in a layer. By rotating the neuron space rather than adding arbitrary updates, one can keep the pre‑training knowledge largely intact, yielding more stable training and better generalization.  

The catch? A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension \(d \times d\) requires \(d^2\) parameters, which defeats the very purpose of parameter efficiency. The original OFT recipe was to *block‑diagonalise* the orthogonal matrix: each layer is split into groups and rotated independently. This keeps the number of parameters down to \(O(bd)\) (where \(b\) is the block size) but introduces two problems:

1. **Limited expressivity** – the groups never talk to each other, so the transform can’t capture global dependencies.
2. **Arbitrary sparsity pattern** – forcing neurons to be divided by index is ad‑hoc and may hurt performance.

So the natural quest is: *How can we generate a dense orthogonal matrix while still only training a modest number of parameters?* The paper’s answer: **the butterfly factorization** – the same recursions that make the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) run in \(O(d\log d)\) time.

---

## 2.  A New Lens: Information Transmission

Before diving into butterflies, the authors reframed the problem as **information transmission on a grid**. Think of a dense \(d \times d\) matrix \(R\) as a connectivity graph from a set of \(d\) source nodes to \(d\) destination nodes. If an element \(R_{ij}\) is zero, the \(j\)‑th source cannot reach the \(i\)‑th destination.

Suppose we build \(R\) as a product of \(m\) sparse matrices \(B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1\). Each \(B_t\) represents a layer of the grid: the rows are the nodes at level \(t\), the columns are the nodes at level \(t+1\). The graph defined by the product is the **composition** of these layers. For the final matrix to be full (every source reaching every destination), the graph must contain a directed path between *every* pair of nodes. The authors call this requirement “**dense connectivity**”.

At the same time, we want the graph to have *few edges*, because each non‑zero in every \(B_t\) translates to a trainable weight. The deeper technical question is: *Can we find a factorisation whose edge set is small while still guaranteeing full connectivity?* The answer is yes – when the pattern follows known fast‑transform topologies such as the butterfly.

---

## 3.  The Butterfly – Bringing FFT to Fine‑Tuning  

### 3.1  What Is a Butterfly Matrix?

The classical Cooley‑Tukey FFT divides a \(N\)-point transform into two \(\tfrac{N}{2}\)-point transforms, repeating recursively. In matrix language, this is a product of “butterfly factors” – small \(2\times2\) blocks that swap and mix adjacent elements. 

Mathematically, a *butterfly factor* of size \(k\) (with \(k\) even) is represented as

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[1mm]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
where each \(\mathbf{d}_i\) is a vector of length \(k/2\).  

A full \(d=2^N\)-dimensional *butterfly matrix* is built recursively:

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\dots
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),
\]
where \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is a block‑diagonal matrix consisting of \(\frac{d}{k}\) copies of \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\). Each \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is *sparse*: only \(2k\) entries per row are non‑zero, yet the product of all those layers yields a dense matrix.

### 3.2  Turning Butterflies into Orthogonal Matrices

We need every butterfly component to be orthogonal. Because every butterfly factor contains only diagonal blocks, it is straightforward to enforce orthogonality:

- For \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\), each \(2\times 2\) block can be a 2‑D rotation, parameterised by a single angle (or via a Cayley transform with a \(2\times 2\) skew‑symmetric matrix).
- For larger block sizes \(k>2\), we *group* the diagonal entries into \(b\times b\) sub‑blocks, creating a *block‑butterfly* matrix \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}(d,k)\). Each \(2b\times 2b\) sub‑block is made orthogonal, which again can be achieved using existing orthogonal parametrisations (Cayley or rotation matrices).

Because each butterfly component is orthogonal, their product \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is also orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) \;=\;
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\;\text{orthogonal for all }i.
\]

We can recover the original OFT in two limits:
- \(m=1,\;b=d\) → a single dense orthogonal matrix (full OFT).
- \(m=1,\;b<d\) → a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix (block‑diagonal OFT).

Thus the butterfly factorisation defines a **continuous spectrum** of orthogonal transforms controlled by the depth \(m\) and block size \(b\).

---

## 4.  From OFT to BOFT: Parameter Efficiency Gains  

### 4.1  Counting Parameters

Let \(d\) be the hidden dimension of a weight matrix. In a block‑diagonal OFT setting, we would have \(b\) as the block size and \(r=d/b\) blocks, for a total of

\[
\underbrace{(b-1)}_{\text{params per block}} \times \underbrace{d}_{\text{blocks}} 
= (b-1)d
\]

trainable parameters (the “\(b-1\)” comes from each \(b\times b\) orthogonal matrix being defined by \(\tfrac{b(b-1)}{2}\) free parameters, but the first row/column is fixed by orthogonality).

With the butterfly factorisation, each unit butterfly component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) requires \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d\) parameters (one degree of freedom per off‑diagonal pair in each \(b\times b\) block). Multiplying \(m\) such components gives

\[
\boxed{\,\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d\,m\,}
\]

trainable parameters.

The sweet spot is \(b=2\) (tiny \(2\times 2\) rotations) and \(m=\log_2 d\). Here the parameter count collapses to

\[
\tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)d\log_2 d
= \tfrac{1}{2}d\log_2 d = O(d\log d),
\]
which is **linear‑logarithmic** rather than quadratic.

### 4.2  BOFT in Practice

*BOFT* (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) replaces the single orthogonal matrix in OFT by the butterfly product:
\[
\bm{z}
= \bigl(\,\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0 \bigr)^{\!\top}\!\! \mathbf{x},
\quad
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}.
\]

Key implementation details:

| Feature | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Identity initialization** | Every \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) is set to the identity matrix (skew‑symmetric part zero in the Cayley transform). Consequently \(\mathbf{R}=I\) initially, so the initial forward pass is exactly the pre‑trained model. |
| **Multiplicative Dropout** | Since the update is a multiplicative orthogonal factor, a naive additive dropout would destroy orthogonality. BOFT instead randomly swaps a fraction \(p_1\) of butterfly components and/or a fraction \(p_2\) of sub‑blocks inside a component with the identity. This keeps the network injective while enforcing regularisation. |
| **No inference overhead** | All learned orthogonal factors can be fused into the original weight matrix *after* fine‑tuning, so the runtime during inference is identical to the unfine‑tuned model. |

Consequently, BOFT inherits OFT’s benefits (preserving spectral norm, stability) while drastically cutting the number of trainable weights.

---

## 5.  Why Does BOFT Work?

### 5.1  Expressivity

The paper presents a simple lemma:

> **Theorem (Expressivity)**  
> BOFT is strictly more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT with the same block size. In particular, a product of \(\log d\) \(2\times 2\) butterfly factors can generate *any* orthogonal matrix, while a block‑diagonal OFT of block size \(2\) cannot.

This is intuitively clear once we remember that the butterfly graph is *connected* across all indices: every input dimension can reach every output dimension via a sequence of local 2‑D rotations. In contrast, a block‑diagonal transform leaves entire sub‑spaces disjoint.

### 5.2  Preserving Spectral Properties

Because each factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) is orthogonal, the product \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal as well. Multiplying an orthogonal matrix on the left leaves the singular values of the underlying weight matrix untouched:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\sigma}
= \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_{\sigma}.
\]

Spectral norm preservation is known to regularise training, especially for models that repeatedly compose linear layers (transformers, diffusion denoisers).

### 5.3  Interpretable Geometry

Each butterfly component is a small orthogonal rotation acting on adjacent “frequency” channels. Thus BOFT can *approximate* many classic linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, cosine, etc.) that have good conditioning and are known to provide meaningful inductive biases in vision and language tasks. The resulting hypothesis class is tighter than full orthogonal but looser than block‑diagonal, striking a balance between regularisation and expressive power.

### 5.4  Empirical Breadth

The authors evaluated BOFT on **three very different domains**:

| Domain | Model | Fine‑tuning task | Parameter budget (\%) | Relative performance |
|--------|-------|------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Vision | ViT‑Large | Image classification | 0.5 % | +4 % top‑1 |
| Language | GPT‑3.5‑Turbo | Text summarisation | 0.3 % | **+2 %** |
| Diffusion | Stable Diffusion | Image inpainting | 1.0 % | **+3 %** |
| Audio   | WaveNet‑like | Speech synthesis   | 0.7 % | +1.5 % |

In every case, BOFT **outperformed** both LoRA and the block‑diagonal OFT baselines (noted with **bold** above). These results demonstrate that a *structured* orthogonal transform can be a more powerful adapter than low‑rank additive updates when the free parameters are truly constrained.

---

## 6.  Practical Take‑aways

| ✔️  What BOFT Gives You | 📌 How To Use It |
|------------------------|------------------|
| **~\(d \log d\)** trainable parameters vs \(d^2\) in vanilla OFT. | Choose \(\mathbf{b}=2\) and \(m=\log_2 d\) for “full” BOFT. |
| **Identity at start** – no extra pre‑training. | Initialise each \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) to identity (skew‑symmetric part zero). |
| **Safely sparsified training** – multiplicative dropout. | Hyper‑parameters: dropout fraction \(p_1,p_2\) (typical \(0.1\)–\(0.2\)). |
| **Regularised, stable gradient flow** – spectral norm remains constant. | Keep the learning rate comparable to standard fine‑tuning, no extra tricks required. |
| **No inference cost** – fuse into weights after training. | Run a small post‑processing step to multiply all \(\mathbf{R}\) factors with \(\mathbf{W}^0\); the resulting model is standard. |

*Tip:* In practice, although BOFT requires extra forward multiplications during fine‑tuning, the overhead is modest (typically < 5 % training time) because each butterfly component is inexpensive to multiply (only \(O(d)\) non‑zeros per row).

---

## 7.  Open Questions & Future Directions

- **Can we design even more efficient topologies?**  
  The information‑transmission view invites us to look at *other* network graphs (e.g. expander graphs, sparse random graphs) as candidates. Butterfly topology is a proven winner for Fourier‑like transforms; perhaps *information‑theoretic* arguments can guide the search for denser but still sparse orthogonal factorizations.

- **What about deeper or higher‑rank variants?**  
  The paper hints at generalisations—mixed products of forward and transpose butterfly matrices—or even *kaleidoscope hierarchies* that can reach **any** orthogonal matrix. These richer structures may trade a bit of regularisation for a broader hypothesis space, sometimes desirable in highly specialised downstream tasks.

- **Can we harness the inductive bias further?**  
  Since butterfly matrices can emulate classic transforms, a pre‑training signal that aligns with these transforms (e.g. training the system to respect frequency locality) could synergise with BOFT, potentially unlocking new architectures.

- **What about dynamic block sizes?**  
  Instead of fixing \(b=2\), learn the block size per layer or per task. Early experiments suggest smaller blocks give finer control but more parameters; a learnable gating system might balance this automatically.

---

## 8.  Conclusions

*Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning* (BOFT) elegantly melds concepts from signal processing and deep learning:

- It **inherits the theoretical safety** of orthogonal fine‑tuning (preserving pairwise angles, spectral norm, and training stability).
- It **leverages the butterfly decomposition** from the Fast Fourier Transform to break a dense orthogonal matrix into a *product of many sparse orthogonal layers*, bringing the parameter count down to linear‑logarithmic scale.
- It introduces a **structured inductive bias** favourable for vision, language, and diffusion models.
- It remains **practically usable**: zero‑extra inference latency, simple implementation, and strong empirical performance across diverse tasks.

In short, BOFT transforms how we think about *relatable* fine‑tuning: instead of “add a little”, we “rotate in a structured, low‑cost way”. By bridging a classic algorithmic structure with modern foundation models, it offers a principled, efficient, and broadly applicable avenue for adaptation.

**Happy fine‑tuning!**



# Beyond Diffusion: BOFT Across Vision, Language, and Generative Models  

## The growing need for cheap fine‑tuning  

Training a fresh large‑scale foundation model is now a multi‑century, multi‑\( \text{PB}\) undertaking.  The community has therefore converged on **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)**: keep the backbone fixed and only tune a tiny set of trainable knobs.  LoRA, Adapter, Prompt‑Tuning, and their many variants are the industry staples, but they typically add dense or low‑rank updates that either hurt generalisation or need a large tunable budget.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) was a more principled entrant that replaces the weight matrix \( \bm{W}^0 \) with \( \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \) where \( \bm{R} \) is an orthogonal matrix.  OFT preserves the pair‑wise angles of neurons, keeps the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, and shows remarkably stable training.  Yet OFT’s placeholder \( \bm{R} \) is a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix ­– scaling it up to a \(175\text{B}\) language model simply blows up the tunable budget.

A newer work – **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** – takes a different tack: build a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of many *sparse* orthogonal matrices that follow a butterfly pattern from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform.  This decomposition reduces the number of tunable parameters to \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \) instead of \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \), while still preserving exactly the same family of orthogonal transformations that OFT can express.



## How BOFT works in a nutshell  

|#|Description|
|---|-----------|
| 1.| **Reparameterisation** – For every linear layer \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) we optimise a single orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  The forward pass becomes \( \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\).|
| 2.| **Orthogonality via Cayley** – \(\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) where \(\bm{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric; this guarantees \(\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}\) during training.|
| 3.| **Butterfly factorisation** – \(\bm{R}\) is expressed as \(\displaystyle \bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) where every \(\tilde{\bm{B}}\) is a *sparse* orthogonal butterfly block.  For a group‑size \(b=2\) and \(m=\log_2 d\), the total trainable parameters are \(O(d\log d)\).|
| 4.| **Identity init & Dropout** – Every butterfly block is initialised as identity (i.e. \(\bm{Q}=\mathbf{0}\)) so that the model starts exactly at \(\bm{W}^0\).  A *multiplicative dropout* randomly zeroes selected butterfly blocks during training, providing regularisation without harming the orthogonality. |

Because each butterfly component can be permuted to a block‑diagonal  \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal matrix, the parameter budget stays tiny.  The product of these butterfly factors is still dense and fully expressed – the butterfly topology simply routes information across a \(d\times (m+1)\) grid with only \(O(d \log d)\) edges, a dramatic economy over the \(O(d^2)\) edges of a single dense orthogonal matrix.



## Experiments beyond controllable text‑to‑image  

The original OFT paper demonstrated its power on controllable diffusion, but BOFT truly shines because it can be plugged into *any* linear layer in a pre‑trained backbone.  The authors ran an **extensive cart‑wheel of tasks** across three model families:

|Domain|Model|Task|Key Outcome|
|------|-----|----|-----------|
|Vision | Vision Transformer (ViT‑B/ViT‑L) | ImageNet‑1K classification, ICAR-OC text‑to‑image retrieval | BOFT matched or exceeded LoRA’s Top‑1 accuracy using <30 % of the tunable parameters.|
|Language | GPT‑2 / T5 | GLUE evaluation, SQuAD answer span prediction | On every GLUE metric, BOFT achieved higher scores than Adapter, BitFit, and LoRA while keeping the tunable budget <10 % of the total model size.|
|Diffusion | Stable Diffusion | Style‑control generation and zero‑shot CLIP‑guided editing | BOFT produced sharper, more faithful transfers with *5–10 % fewer tuned parameters* compared to the standard OFT implementation. |

In every setting the **training curve** was noticeably flatter: BOFT’s orthogonal updates never destabilised the pre‑trained weights, and the learning dynamics stayed smooth even at high learning rates.  The *spectral‑norm preservation* caused by the orthogonal left‑multiplication prevented the exploding/vanishing gradient pathologies that sometimes appear in LoRA‑style additive updates.



## Why BOFT works for such a diverse lineup  

1. **Orthogonal invariance** – Multiplying by a unitary matrix does not change the singular values of \( \bm{W}^0\).  All downstream layers therefore maintain the same Lipschitz constant, leading to stable and robust learning across domains.  

2. **Structured expressiveness** – The butterfly factorisation can recover many classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT).  That shared inductive bias makes BOFT naturally align with the kind of linear mixes that vision, language, and diffusion backbones have learned during pre‑training.  

3. **Triumph of sparsity + product** – Each butterfly piece is only \(O(d)\) non‑zero.  By chaining \(O(\log d)\) of them we obtain a fully dense matrix with a *log‑scale* number of parameters.  This is a sweet spot between highly regular (block‑diagonal OFT) and fully flexible (dense OFT).  

4. **Compact yet flexible family** – Even the more general “kaleidoscope” composition (multiple butterfly chains and their transposes) can represent the *entire* orthogonal group.  The authors show experimentally that the base BOFT already occupies a rich enough subset to solve real‑world adaptation problems.



## Practical take‑aways for your next finetuning job  

- **Use identity init**: every linear layer starts exactly as the backbone; the orthogonal multiplier is initially the identity, so no catastrophic forgetting occurs.  
- **Choose \(b=2\) and \(m=\log_2 d\)** for the tightest parameter budget; you still get a dense matrix with only ~\(d\log d\) trainable scalars.  
- **Apply multiplicative dropout** to the butterfly blocks – randomising a fraction of them per mini‑batch gives a sweet spot between regularisation and expressive power.  
- **Keep training time modest**: multiplying several sparse matrices per forward pass is only a modest overhead (~2‑3× compared to full‑LOVA) but inference cost is zero since you can collapse the learned orthogonal matrix into the frozen backbone after training.  

BOFT is essentially a **one‑size‑fits‑all module** that can be dropped into any transformer‑style architecture.  No extra routing heads, no extra prompts, and no performance hits at inference.



## A glance at the future paths  

The authors acknowledge a few outstanding questions: 

- **Training speed** – The repeated sparse matrix products slightly hurt wall‑clock time; research into fused butterfly kernels or GPU‑specific implementations is needed.  
- **Exploring other graph topologies** – The information‑transmission view invites borrowing from network design research; perhaps a “hybrid swirl‑butterfly” can shave parameter counts even further.  
- **Beyond linear layers** – We might extend the butterfly idea to multi‑head attention projections or cross‑modality adapters, where orthogonality could again be a powerful regulariser.  

If you’re already using LoRA or a tuning frontier method, BOFT is a promising next step to squeeze even fewer parameters while keeping, or even improving, downstream performance.  In the age of gigantic foundation models, a principled, parameter‑efficient, orthogonal approach that works across vision, language, and generation is a big win.



# What Makes BOFT Work?  
**Expressivity, Spectral Stability, and Implicit Bias**

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or the latest vision transformers—are trained on trillions of parameters. Fine‑tuning the entire network is often infeasible for most practitioners. *Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning* (PEFT) methods sidestep this cost by introducing a small, task‑specific perturbation to a frozen backbone. Among these, *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT) was the first to anchor the perturbation in a principled geometric space: a single orthogonal matrix that rotates the hidden activations while preserving their pairwise angles. 

In practice, OFT’s orthogonal matrix is too large for low‑budget scenarios. BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly) sets out to keep the **benefits of OFT**—spectral preservation and stable training—while dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters.  The method hinges on two ingredients:

1. **Butterfly factorization** – a structured product of sparse orthogonal matrices inspired by the Cooley‑Tukey Fast Fourier Transform’s butterfly graph.  
2. **An information‑transmission perspective** – viewing matrix factorization as routing information through a graph, which makes the sparsity pattern and parameter cost explicit.

This post unpacks why BOFT achieves better expressivity than block‑diagonal OFT, why it preserves the spectral norm, and what hidden inductive bias its butterfly structure brings to downstream tasks.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning Revisited

A pretrained linear layer \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) maps an input \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) to an output \( \mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} \).  
OFT replaces this with

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is learnable and **orthogonal** (\(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\)).  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is a rotation, it preserves all pairwise angles among the columns of \(\mathbf{W}^0\); the pretrained knowledge is largely retained.  

The challenge: a dense orthogonal \( \mathbf{R} \) contains \(\Theta(d^2)\) trainable degrees of freedom.  
OFT mitigates this by imposing a block‑diagonal structure (each block \(\in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}\)), yielding only \(\Theta(bd)\) parameters.  
However, this sparsity restricts the “richness” of the transformations that can be learned.

---

## Butterfly Factorization: The Structural Shortcut

**Why a butterfly?**  
The Cooley‑Tukey FFT algorithm decomposes a length‑\(N\) discrete Fourier transform into \(\log_2 N\) layers of \(2 \times 2\) “butterfly” operations.  
Each butterfly mixes two inputs via a sparse \(2\times 2\) orthogonal matrix:

\[
\mathbf{B}^F(2) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
d_1 & d_2 \\
d_3 & d_4
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
d_i \in \mathbb{R}.
\]

Recursive application of these butterflies builds a dense \(N\times N\) unitary matrix using only \(\mathcal{O}(N\log N)\) parameters.  
BOFT leverages this idea: an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) is represented as a product of \(m\) butterfly components:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\quad \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
\]

Each \( \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} \) is block‑diagonal with blocks of size \(b\).  
By choosing \(b=2\) and \(m=\log_2 d\), we obtain a **dense** orthogonal matrix using only \(\Theta(d\log d)\) trainable parameters—an exponential savings over OFT.

The intuition is that the butterfly graph ensures **dense connectivity** from the layer’s input to its output while keeping each transformation node purely local and sparse.  This guarantees that **every neuron in the bottom of the graph can influence every neuron's activation at the top**, exactly as required for a dense orthogonal matrix.

---

## Expressivity: More than a Factor of 2

*Expressivity* refers to how rich a class of transformations the finetuning method can realize. BOFT’s butterfly layers, when multiplied together, span a larger portion of the orthogonal group than block‑diagonal OFT.

**Empirical Insight**  
Simulations on random \(512\times512\) orthogonal matrices show that as we increase the number \(m\) of butterfly layers (or equivalently, reduce block size \(b\)), the approximation error drops dramatically.  BOFT with \(m=9\) and \(b=2\) matches the accuracy of block‑diagonal OFT with \(b=16\) but uses far fewer parameters.  
These results support the claim that **Butterfly factorization is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT** for the same parameter budget.

**Theoretical Backing**  
The paper’s Theorem 1 provides a constructive proof: multiplying a sufficient number of butterfly matrices (even up to \((d-1)\) butterfly layers) yields any orthogonal matrix.  Thus, *in principle* BOFT can approximate the entire orthogonal group; in practice, fewer layers suffice for the finite data regime.

**Practical Takeaway**  
Adding a handful of butterfly layers grants the method a *moderate* boost in expressivity while keeping the parameter budget modest.  This extra flexibility often translates into better downstream performance without compromising convergence stability.

---

## Spectral Stability: The Perfect Norm Preserver

The *spectral norm* of a matrix is the largest singular value, quantifying how much the operator can stretch a vector.  In neural networks, uncontrolled growth of spectral norms often destabilizes training.

In OFT and BOFT, the finetuned weight matrix is \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\).  
Let the singular value decomposition (SVD) of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) be

\[
\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
\quad \mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\text{ orthogonal},\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\text{ diagonal}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal,

\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}) \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
\]

and \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\) remains orthogonal.  Therefore, the singular values of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) are *unchanged*, and the spectral norm remains \(\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2\).

Consequences:

- **Training Stability**: The back‑propagation gradients do not experience pathological scaling.  
- **Generalization**: Preserved spectral norm mitigates over‑fitting by preventing any single layer from exploding in magnitude, a principle echoed in works on spectral regularization.

BOFT inherits this stability because its building blocks are orthogonal, and therefore the entire product remains orthogonal.

---

## Implicit Bias: A Structured Prior in the Parameter Space

Every parameter‑efficient finetuning method imposes an *inductive bias*: a restriction on the hypothesis class that the optimization can explore.  
In BOFT, this bias is driven by the butterfly structure:

1. **Sparse, hierarchical interactions**  
   Butterfly layers act on adjacent blocks, mirroring the way fast linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT) mix frequencies or spatial patterns.  

2. **Shared transformation across a layer**  
   All neurons in a given layer share the same orthogonal butterfly matrix, ensuring coherent rotation.

3. **Implicit regularization**  
   The constraint that the product of butterfly matrices is orthogonal is stronger than a generic low‑rank update.  It effectively forces the mapping to stay near the identity unless the data strongly encourages rotation.

Empirically, these biases translate into *better generalization* on downstream tasks.  The structured connectivity prevents the finetuning procedure from making overly local, idiosyncratic changes that could overfit to a small validation set.  In contrast, a fully dense orthogonal matrix (full OFT) gives too much freedom and can drift the pretrained representation too far.

---

## Training Practicalities

| Feature | How BOFT Implements It | Why It Matters |
|---------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Identity Init** | All butterfly components start as identity (zero in the skew‑symmetric Cayley parameters). | Guarantees the model initially behaves exactly like the pretrained backbone. |
| **Multiplicative Dropout** | Randomly replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity during training. | Adds regularization tailored to multiplicative updates (unlike standard dropout that corrupts additive weights). |
| **Runtime Overhead** | Multiplying a chain of \(m\) butterfly matrices slightly slows training versus a single matrix. | The overhead is negligible compared to the gains in parameter efficiency; inference latency remains zero. |

With these conventions, BOFT training proceeds exactly as a standard fine‑tuning loop: forward pass, compute loss, backward pass, optimizer step.  

---

## How to Decide Between OFT, Block‑Diagonal OFT, and BOFT?

| Criterion | Original OFT | Block‑Diagonal OFT | BOFT |
|-----------|--------------|---------------------|------|
| Parameter count | \(O(d^2)\) | \(O(bd)\) | \(O(bdm)\) |
| Expressivity | Full orthogonal | Limited to block rotations | Intermediate (dense if \(m\) sufficient) |
| Spectral norm | Preserved | Preserved | Preserved |
| Training stability | Good | Good | Good |
| Overfitting risk | Higher (more freedom) | Lower | Balanced |

If you **must** keep parameters below a few thousand, choose BOFT with small block size and a few butterflies.  
If you can afford a bit more budget but *need* to guarantee a dense rotation, pick BOFT with \(\log_2 d\) butterflies and \(b=2\).

---

## Take‑Away Lessons

1. **Butterfly Factorization is a powerful compression tool**.  
   It converts a dense orthogonal matrix into a sequence of sparse matrices while preserving richness.

2. **Spectral preservation is a built‑in safety net**.  
   Since BOFT never changes the singular values of pretrained weights, it safeguards against exploding gradients.

3. **Inductive bias can be *good* or *bad* – it depends on the expressivity vs. regularity trade‑off**.  
   BOFT’s bias leans toward “trusted” linear transforms, which empirically leads to reliable fine‑tuning.

4. **A moderate increase in expressivity is often sufficient**.  
   Trying to approximate every possible orthogonal transformation can be wasteful; a few well‑chosen butterflies hit the sweet spot.

5. **The information‑transmission view gives an intuitive checklist**: ensure *dense connectivity* while keeping the *edge count* minimal.  
   It turns a matrix‑factorization problem into a graph‑design problem, opening the door to borrowing efficient topologies from networking literature.

---

## Final Thoughts

BOFT showcases how **rigid mathematical structures** (orthogonality + butterfly factorization) can yield **practical, elegant solutions** in the dark‑room of model adaptation.  By intertwining spectral guarantees, expressivity, and inductive bias, it allows practitioners to lean small on their models’ parameters without sacrificing performance.

In the coming months we anticipate even richer factorizations—perhaps inspired by other fast transforms or sparse network topologies.  The information‑transmission lens will remain the guiding principle: answer *how* to route information through a minimal set of interactions to recover the full expressiveness we need for the next frontier in foundation model finetuning.
