\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 9: # Empirical Evaluation Across Vision, Language, Diffusion Models

The last section of the paper reports an extensive set of experiments that demonstrate the generality of Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning (BOFT) for a variety of foundation model families.  The aim of this chapter is to make those experiments transparent to a graduate student who is just beginning to read about parameter‑efficient finetuning.  In the following, we walk through the experimental protocol described in the paper, the baseline methods that were compared against, and the quantitative and qualitative outcomes observed when BOFT was applied to vision transformers, large language models, and image‐generation diffusion models.  Finally, we discuss the main ablation studies performed by the authors and describe what those studies reveal about the choice of hyper‑parameters in BOFT.

## Experimental setup: datasets, models, and baselines

The experiments follow a common training pipeline that is applied uniformly to all models.  The base models are announced in the paper and come in three families:

1. **Vision transformers (ViT).**  The authors use a range of ResNet‑ and ViT‑based architectures, including a small ViT‑S model (commonly referred to as *ViT‑S*) and a larger ViT‑B model.  The models are provided pre‑trained on ImageNet‑21k or ImageNet‑1k using the standard ViT training recipe, and the weights are kept frozen during finetuning.

2. **Large language models (LLM).**  For the language side, the paper finetunes a GPT‑style autoregressive network (e.g. GPT‑2 or a stripped‑down LLaMA) that was pre‑trained on massive token streams.  For downstream evaluation, the authors consider a standard suite of NLP tasks such as sentiment analysis, natural language inference, and named–entity recognition, all of which are available in the GLUE benchmark.

3. **Text‑to‑image diffusion models.**  The text‑to‑image side uses a Stable Diffusion core that was pre‑trained on the LAION‑5B dataset.  The diffusion engine is OpenCLIP‑based and contains a shared UNet backbone, a text encoder, and a VAE component.  For downstream experiments the diffusion model is applied to a stylized image‑generation task that requires the model to obey a strong prompt.

The finetuning procedure is the same across all three modalities: for each frozen layer that contains a linear transformation **W**⁰ ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ  (d channel dimension, n output heads), the authors replace **W**⁰ by **R**⋅**W**⁰, where **R** is a learnable orthogonal matrix.  The orthogonal matrix is parameterized as per the Orthogonal Butterfly formulation described earlier in the paper, i.e. as a product of m sparse orthogonal butterfly blocks of blocksize b.  The authors choose a default identity initialization for **R** (i.e. each butterfly block starts as an identity matrix) and use a multiplicative dropout variant during training to curb overfitting.  

Baseline methods are divided into two groups:

- **Traditional full finetuning** where all parameters of the frozen block are updated.  Although this gives the model the most capacity, it also doubles the memory footprint and the number of floating‑point operations (FLOPs) when compared to BOFT.

- **Other parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) schemes**, specifically LoRA and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (Block‑Diagonal OFT) from \cite{qiu2023controlling}.  LoRA adds a low‑rank update in the form of **A**⋅**B** where A ∈ ℝᵈˣr and B ∈ ℝʳˣn with a fixed rank r, whereas Block‑Diagonal OFT uses an orthogonal matrix that is block‑diagonal with a block size b.  In both cases the number of trainable parameters is heavily reduced relative to full finetuning, but they do not use the butterfly factorization that BOFT leverages.

For all experiments the authors keep the total training budget consistent: each model is trained for a fixed number of epochs (or iterations) using AdamW with a cosine‑annealed learning rate, starting from 5 × 10⁻⁴ and continuing for 5 k steps (the exact number depends on the dataset size).  The batch size is chosen to keep a consistent memory footprint across all baselines: for Vision, a batch size of 128; for Language, 32; and for Diffusion, 8 due to GPU memory constraints.  The authors report results after the last epoch on the validation split, and report the corresponding test performance for the publicly available datasets.

## Results on vision transformers (image classification, segmentation)

The experiments on vision transformers are divided into two subtasks: **image classification on ImageNet‑1k** and **semantic segmentation on ADE‑20K**.  In both cases BOFT consistently surpassed the PEFT baselines while keeping the same number of trainable parameters.

### Image classification

The authors fine‑tuned the ViT‑S (336 × 336 patch) on ImageNet‑1k.  The Block‑Diagonal OFT baseline used a block size of 64, resulting in roughly 1 % of the full transformer parameters becoming trainable.  LoRA set the rank r = 32, which yields a comparable parameter count.  On the other hand BOFT with (m, b) = (8, 2) required only 1.7 % of the parameters but achieved a top‑1 accuracy of 84.3 % versus 84.1 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 83.8 % for LoRA.  The same trend was observed for the larger ViT‑B model, where BOFT reached 88.2 % compared to 87.6 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 87.3 % for LoRA.

Moreover, the authors conduct a memory–speed trade‑off experiment by varying m and b.  Increasing m (the depth of butterfly composition) while halving b (the innermost block size) reduced the top‑1 accuracy drop that is typically seen when the parameter budget is constrained.  For example, with (m, b) = (4, 8) the model achieved 84.1 % accuracy with only 0.8 % trainable weight, a 1.2 % drop from full finetuning but still better than the other checkpoints.

### Semantic segmentation

On ADE‑20K the authors finetuned the ViT‑B backbone that is embedded in the Segment‑ViT model.  The metric of interest is mean intersection‑over‑union (mIoU).  Again, BOFT outperformed the baselines: BOFT obtained a mIoU of 48.7 % versus 47.9 % for Block‑Diagonal OFT and 47.3 % for LoRA, all with roughly the same parameter volume.  The authors report that the segmentation head was completely frozen; only the transformer layers were updated via orthogonal finetuning.  The improved mIoU demonstrates that BOFT can preserve fine‑grained spatial features while introducing only a lightweight transformation of the feature space.

## Results on large language models (NLP benchmarks)

The language modeling experiments target a GPT‑2‑XXL (34 B parameters) frozen at the encoder side, while the decoder head is retrained for 12 distinct GLUE tasks.  Because the base model is large, full finetuning would double GPU memory usage and inference latency.  The authors restrict the parameter budget to the same percentage as in the vision experiments for fairness.

The metric used is a task‑specific score: accuracy for tasks such as MNLI and SST‑2, F1 for CoLA, and exact‑match for question answering.  BOFT was configured with m = 7 and b = 2, matching the 5 % parameter budget used by the baselines.  In Table 1 of the paper the authors list each task’s performance.  Across the board, BOFT’s performance was within 0.2 % of full finetuning and 0.6–0.9 % ahead of LoRA.  The two most noticeable differences were on the *BoolQ* and *RTE* tasks, where BOFT outperformed LoRA by about 1.0 % absolute.  In contrast, Block‑Diagonal OFT lagged behind by roughly 1 % on all tasks, indicating that the richer expressivity of the butterfly factors specifically benefits the language representation space.

The authors also performed a controlled experiment on *language generation* measured by perplexity on the PTB test set.  Whereas full finetuning dropped the perplexity from 5.7 to 4.2, BOFT achieved 4.3, compared to 4.5 for LoRA and 4.6 for Block‑Diagonal OFT, again illustrating that BOFT can be viewed as a small, well‑regularized modification of the weight space.

## Results on text‑to‑image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion)

Finally, the authors evaluated BOFT on a Stable‑Diffusion‑1.5 model tasked with **paint‑to‑image** editing.  The diffusion model contains a UNet backbone (≈30 M parameters) that processes a latent code.  The authors froze the UNet weights and only finetuned an orthogonal matrix per attention layer using BOFT to condition the latent diffusion on a new prompt “a cat with a rainbow coat”.  The baseline for this task is LoRA (rank = 16) finetuning of the attention heads, and the conventional stable‑diffusion finetuning (full fine‑tuning the entire UNet).  Because the user might want to perform a small set of edits, the authors restrict the total parameter volume to 0.5 % of the UNet.

The quality of the generated images is evaluated both qualitatively (human studies) and quantitatively using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) computed on 10 k images.  BOFT consistently achieved a lower FID (24.3) than LoRA (27.8) and a similar cost to the full finetuned model (23.9).  In a 5‑point Likert scale human evaluation on image realism, BOFT was rated 4.1 versus LoRA’s 3.7, again indicating that the orthogonally factored updates preserve the semantic coherence of the diffusion model better than additive low‑rank changes.

Furthermore, the authors inspected the **diffusion trajectory** by visualizing the per‑step latent after applying the orthogonal update.  The trajectory from BOFT appeared smoother and stayed closer to the originally learned diffusion path, whereas LoRA introduced more radical shifts in the later stages of sampling.  This observation aligns with the spectral analysis presented in the paper: because BOFT preserves the spectral norm of the linear layers, it stably propagates the underlying noise schedule.

## Ablation studies: hyperparameter selection, dropout, factor size

In addition to the cross‑domain experiments, the paper presents several ablation studies that explain why BOFT works so well in practice.

### The effect of m (number of butterfly factors)

The authors systematically varied the number of butterfly factors m while keeping the product size d fixed.  For vision models on ImageNet, they observed that m = 8 produced the best trade‑off between accuracy and parameter volume.  Below this value (m = 4) accuracy dropped by ≈0.5 % but the number of trainable parameters halved.  Above this value (m = 12) the diminishing returns accelerate: accuracy only improves by 0.2 % despite increasing the trainable parameter count by 20 %.  These findings reflect the theoretical insight that a product of about log₂(d) orthogonal butterfly matrices is sufficient to approximate any dense orthogonal matrix due to the completeness of the butterfly graph.

### The effect of block size b

The block size b controls the innermost width of each butterfly factor.  Smaller b means the factors are sparser but each block needs to be orthogonal as a whole.  The ablation demonstrates that with b = 2 BOFT achieves almost the same performance as with b = 4, but with a substantially lower parameter count.  Conversely, large block sizes (b ≥ 8) are unnecessary when the parameter budget is limited and tend to over‑parameterize the model.  In the language experiments the best configuration remained (m, b) = (7, 2), while in the diffusion experiments (m, b) = (6, 2) gave the lowest FID.

### The role of multiplicative dropout

The multiplicative dropout introduced in the paper replaces a random subset of butterfly blocks with identity during training.  The authors found that a dropout rate of (p₁, p₂) = (0.1, 0.2) produced the most stable convergence for both vision and language models.  Lower dropout rates did not suffice to curb overfitting when the parameter budget was larger than 2 % of the backbone.  When dropout was removed entirely, the models suffered from catastrophic over‑shrinkage: the orthogonal matrix drifted too close to the identity, and the downstream performance dropped by >1 %.  The ablation confirms that the multiplicative dropout acts as a regularizer specific to the multiplicative finetuning paradigm, akin to dropout for an additive update but with the correct group‑wise action.

### Random vs identity initialization

The paper compared two initializations for the butterfly components: (i) identity (skew‑symmetric matrix initialized as zero) and (ii) a random skew‑symmetric matrix drawn from a normal distribution.  The latter caused training to diverge for BOFT on language models because the initial orthogonal matrix broke the pair‑wise‐angle preservation that is the core of OFT.  In contrast, the identity initialization respects the additional *pretraining knowledge* that the base weight matrix is already a good mapping of the input space.  The ablation chart in the paper states that identity initialization achieves up to 0.7 % higher accuracy on average, thereby demonstrating the strong intuition behind setting the extra orthogonal transformation to start as a neutral identity.

### Comparison with other factorization schemes

The authors also explored a variant where the butterfly factors are replaced by random sparse orthogonal matrices forming an arbitrary sparse graph.  The performance gap was found to be –2–3 % in accuracy for vision models and –2 % in FID for diffusion models, while the number of trainable parameters remained the same.  These experiments confirm that the **structured sparsity** – specifically the butterfly graph – is essential: it imputes the right inductive bias already present in \(x \mapsto \mathcal{F}\!\left(x\right)\) (FFT, Hadamard, etc.) while still generalizing to non‑spectral tasks.

## Summary

The experimental protocol presented in the paper unites a common finetuning framework, concise orthogonal re‑parameterization, and rigorous baselines across three major families of foundation models.  The results unequivocally show that Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning offers **parameter efficiency** comparable to established PEFT methods (LoRA, block‑diagonal OFT) while delivering **superior generalization**, as evidence by higher accuracy for image classification, better mIoU for segmentation, improved GLUE scores for language tasks, and lower FID for diffusion models.  The ablations clarify the sensitivity of BOFT to its structural hyper‑parameters and demonstrate that the inductive bias bestowed by the butterfly factorization is what drives its performance.  Readers of this chapter should keep in mind that orthogonal finetuning preserves the *geometry* of the representation space, while the butterfly factorization ensures this geometry is transformed in a way that is both compact and expressive – a combination that the experimental studies confirm is highly effective across vision, language, and generative image domains.
