\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Foundation Models & the Adaptation Challenge

Large foundation models have grown to unprecedented scales in recent years, transforming the landscape of artificial intelligence. Models such as GPT‑3, which boasts roughly 175 billion parameters, and Stable Diffusion, a state‑of‑the‑art image generator, exhibit remarkable generalization across a wide swath of downstream tasks. Yet the sheer size of these systems makes training from scratch infeasible for most researchers and engineers. Consequently, the research community has turned its attention toward efficient methods for adapting pretrained models to new, task‑specific objectives. This chapter introduces the core motivations behind foundation models, discusses why adaptation is essential, and outlines the prevailing paradigms for efficient model adaptation, grounding each in recent developments, notably the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) frameworks.

### Rise of large foundation models and parameter explosion

The modern era of artificial intelligence has seen a paradigm shift in model design: large, generalist models pretrained on massive datasets. This shift has been fueled by a synergy of advances in scaling laws, data curation, and hardware. The paper underscores this trend by referencing key milestones—ChatGPT\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion\cite{rombach2022high}—each pushing the boundaries of tokenized text and pixel‑level generation. A salient consequence of this trend is a sharp increase in the number of parameters, exemplified by GPT‑3's 175 B parameters. Larger models are typically more capable: they encapsulate richer semantic knowledge, enable higher‑resolution synthesis, and improve zero‑shot performance across diverse tasks. However, as the dimensionality of the weight space grows, the computational and memory requirements for training these networks climb quadratically. Training such a model end‑to‑end can demand hundreds of petaflop‑seconds of computation, deep‑binned on a cluster of GPUs or TPUs. Not only is this computational burden prohibitive for most research labs, it also stifles rapid experimentation and limits the applicability of these models in resource‑constrained settings.

When a large foundation model is released, researchers are confronted with a tradeoff: either pay a prohibitive bill to retrain the model from scratch, risking a loss of any fine‑tuned skill acquired by the community, or adapt the existing weights with a more economical procedure. The latter scenario—efficient downstream adaptation—has become a cornerstone of contemporary machine learning research. The paper argues that any successful adaptation strategy must preserve the pre‑trained knowledge while allowing the model to acquire new, task‑specific capabilities without an inordinate number of trainable parameters.

### Training cost versus deployment constraints

Training a 175 B parameter model entails not only massive compute but also significant data costs and energy consumption. Moreover, the downstream training and adaptation phase may require additional computational budgets that are difficult to allocate in industry and academia alike. Consequently, there is a pressing need for **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** methods, which enable substantial task‑level improvements while adding as few trainable parameters as possible.

The paper articulates a useful decomposition of the adaptation problem: instead of modifying every weight in the network, one can either (1) *parameter finetune* a subset of the existing weights, (2) *insert additional parameters* (adapter modules) that interact with the pretrained backbone, or (3) *promote external inputs* that steer the network’s behavior (prompt tuning). Each paradigm has distinct implications for inference latency, deployment, and regularity. For instance, *model finetuning* keeps the baseline architecture untouched, thereby preserving inference latency; *adapter* methods add auxiliary layers during inference; and *prompt tuning* embeds additional tokens into the model’s input, also typically incurring no additional latency. The choice of paradigm depends on the downstream use‑case: for latency‑constrained environments or hardware with strict memory budgets, the minimalism of model finetuning can be invaluable.

Crucially, the paper posits that the adaptation strategy must **preserve relational information** between neurons. By maintaining the pairwise angles among weight vectors, the model is less likely to drift away from the knowledge embedded during pretraining. This observation motivates the use of *orthogonal* transformations: if \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)), then for any two weight vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\) from a linear layer, the angle between them remains unchanged under the transformation \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\). Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages precisely this property, learning a transformation that aligns the pretrained layer to the new task while keeping the neuron geometry intact.

### Downstream task adaptation: objectives and constraints

The downstream adaptation problem is formalized as follows. Let \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) denote the fixed pre‑trained weight matrix of a particular layer. The aim is to transform the network into a new model \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{adapt}}\), where only a lightweight set of parameters is altered. OFT accomplishes this by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and updating the forward pass from

\[
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}
\]

to

\[
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

with \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) a hidden representation and \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) the layer output. Thus the only trainable parameters are the entries of \(\mathbf{R}\). Since \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, it preserves the Euclidean norm and angles of the weight vectors and thereby the mutual geometric arrangement of neurons—a desirable inductive bias that helps the model retain its pre‑training capabilities while learning to solve a new task.

However, a generic orthogonal matrix has \(d^2\) parameters, an impractical budget for large layers. To curb this, the original OFT restricts \(\mathbf{R}\) to a block‑diagonal form \(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r)\), where each block \(\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) is a small orthogonal matrix and \(br=d\). While this drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, it introduces an undesirable inductive bias: the dimension of a neuron is divided into disjoint groups that are transformed independently. Such a grouping has no semantic justification; it merely reflects an arbitrary partition of indices. Consequently, the block‑diagonal approach limits the expressive power of \(\mathbf{R}\): it cannot represent many orthogonal matrices that are dense and highly mixing.

The challenge, then, is to learn a **dense** orthogonal transformation while retaining the parameter economy of a block‑diagonal form. The paper approaches this challenge via an *information‑transmission* formalism: a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be factored into a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices whose non‑zero entries define a communication graph. By carefully selecting the sparsity pattern of the factor matrices, the authors aim to ensure that every input dimension can influence every output dimension—achieving a dense transformation—while the number of trainable parameters remains logarithmically small relative to \(d\). This insight motivates the proposal of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT).

### Overview of existing adaptation paradigms

Efficient adaptation of foundation models can be categorized into three main families:

1. **Model Finetuning**: Fully or partially retraining the existing weights. Classic methods involve a small learning rate to evade catastrophic forgetting. Modern variants add structure, such as orthogonal constraints (OFT) or low‑rank approximations (LoRA\cite{hulora2022}). Model finetuning introduces no extra inference latency but still demands a non‑trivial number of trainable parameters.

2. **Adapter Tuning**: Inserting small trainable modules—usually residual bottlenecks—within the frozen backbone. Methods include StaRL (fine‑grained residual), LoRA, BLoRA, etc. These adapters are split across the network, requiring additional operations during inference but shining in parameter efficiency.

3. **Prompt Tuning**: Concatenating or prepending learnable vectors or tokens to the input. Works well for large language models that leverage prompt‑based learning; they impose almost zero extra cost during inference as the prompt is part of the input.

The paper places OFT and BOFT squarely in the first category but with significant innovation in the *activation mechanism* of the transformation matrix. Whereas vanilla finetuning copies the entire weight matrix, OFT learns an *orthogonal* transformation that preserves geometry. BOFT further compresses this transformation via a butterfly matrix factorization, retaining the geometric benefits of orthogonality while drastically reducing parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\). This approach attains the best of both worlds: no inference latency, strong empirical generalization gains, and a compact trainable footprint.

We will also explore the theoretical motivations behind preserving angles and spectral norms, the role of information‑transmission graphs in guiding sparsity design, and the broader impact of butterfly‑structured training on downstream tasks spanning vision, language, and generative models.
