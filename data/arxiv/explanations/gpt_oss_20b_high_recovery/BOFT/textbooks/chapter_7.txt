\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 7: Orthogonal Butterfly Parameterization (BOFT)

# Enforcing orthogonality in butterfly components with Cayley transform

The central idea of BOFT is that a dense orthogonal matrix, which normally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters, can be constructed from a sequence of sparse orthogonal factors. Each factor is a *butterfly component*, a block‐diagonal matrix whose individual blocks are small orthogonal sub‑matrices. To guarantee that every component is orthogonal we employ the Cayley transform, a differentiable map from skew‑symmetric matrices to the set of orthogonal matrices. Concretely, for a $k\times k$ matrix $Q$ with $Q^\top = - Q$ we define  

\[
R = \bigl(\mathbb{I}_k + Q\bigr)\bigl(\mathbb{I}_k - Q\bigr)^{-1},
\]

which satisfies $R^\top R = \mathbb{I}_k$. For the $2\times 2$ blocks that appear in the butterfly factor the skew‑symmetric matrix has the simple form  

\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\alpha\\
\alpha & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\]

so that $R$ becomes a rotation matrix whose single degree of freedom $\alpha$ controls the rotation angle. Because the Cayley transform preserves orthogonality, every block–diagonal butterfly component, of the form  

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\bigr),
\]

is itself orthogonal as soon as each  $2\times 2$ sub‑block $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$ is orthogonal. The entire orthogonal matrix used in BOFT, namely  

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,1)}^b\,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,2)}^b\,\cdots\,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,m)}^b,
\]

is thus a product of $m$ orthogonal matrices and consequently remains orthogonal. This factorization guarantees, at every step of training, that the relative angles between neurons are preserved, a key property that gave OFT its strong generalization performance.

# Hyperparameters (m, b) and their impact on expressivity and efficiency

BOFT introduces a pair of hyperparameters that control the balance between model capacity and parameter budget. The first hyperparameter, $m$, counts how many butterfly components are multiplied together to build $\bm{R}(m,b)$. The second hyperparameter, $b$, is the width of the blocks inside each butterfly factor. The effective number of trainable parameters for a linear layer of size $d\times n$ is

\[
\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

When $b=2$ the contribution of a single component is merely $\tfrac12 d$, so a depth of $m=\log_2 d$ yields only $\mathcal{O}(d\,\log d)$ parameters. In contrast, the original OFT that learns a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix requires on the order of $d^2$ parameters, while the block‐diagonal OFT with block size $b$ needs $\mathcal{O}(b\,d)$. Thus $m$ and $b$ jointly provide a continuum of choices: larger $m$ or $b$ increase expressivity because more degrees of freedom become available, but also inflate the parameter budget. Empirical results in the paper (see Fig.~5) show that keeping $b$ small (e.g., $b=2$) and increasing $m$ yields the best compression‑versus‑accuracy trade‑off, often outperforming block‑diagonal OFT at several times fewer parameters. This scaling matches the intuition that deeper butterfly factorizations let information flow across many levels of the hidden representation, thereby improving the expressiveness of the resulting orthogonal transform without sacrificing efficiency.

# Relationship to block‑diagonal OFT and full OFT

BOFT is a strict generalization of both major variants of OFT. Setting the depth to one, $m=1$, reduces $\bm{R}(1,b)$ to a single butterfly component. If we further fix the block size to the entire width of the layer, $b=d$, the single component is a full orthogonal matrix and $\bm{R}(1,d)$ coincides with the original OFT formulation that learns an unrestricted $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. On the other extreme, if we keep $m=1$ but choose a small block size, say $b=2$ or $b=4$, the butterfly component collapses to a block‑diagonal matrix composed of independent $2\times 2$ (or $4\times 4$) rotations. This exactly matches the block‑diagonal OFT proposed in the original work. Every choice of $m$ and $b$ therefore nests between these two extremes: longer factorization chains interpolate between a sparse, low‑rank approximation and a dense, fully expressive orthogonal matrix. The paper’s arguments (especially Theorem 1) establish that for a fixed $b$, BOFT is strictly more expressive than its block‑diagonal counterpart because the butterfly’s inter‑block connectivity allows richer transformations while maintaining the same sparsity level.

# Identity initialization strategy for BOFT

An essential practical consideration is the initialization of the orthogonal factors. Since the weight update is multiplicative, it is natural to seek a starting point where the new weights coincide exactly with the pretrained ones. In the Cayley framework this is achieved by setting every skew‑symmetric matrix $Q$ to zero, which yields $R=\mathbb{I}$. Operationally, this means initializing each butterfly component to the identity matrix (all rotation angles set to zero). Consequently, before any gradient step the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the identity and the effective weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0$. This mirrors the strategy used in LoRA, where the low‑rank update matrix is initialized to zero. The careful identity initialization ensures that early training dynamics resemble a small perturbation of the frozen pretrained model, preserving the pre‑training knowledge and preventing drastic jumps in function space that could destabilize training.

# Multiplicative dropout for regularization

Standard dropout, which zeroes out activations during training, does not naturally extend to BOFT because the update is a product of orthogonal matrices: simply dropping neurons would break orthogonality or the forward computation itself. To regularize BOFT, the authors propose a *multiplicative dropout* that respects the multiplicative structure. During each training step, a random subset of butterfly components is selected; a proportion $p_{1}$ of these components are replaced by identity matrices. Moreover, within each retained component, a proportion $p_{2}$ of its diagonal blocks (the individual $2\times 2$ rotation cells) can also be forced to identity. This stochastic masking effectively turns off certain orthogonal transforms, thereby reducing the capacity of the model in that mini‑batch. By doing so during training, the network learns to rely on a robust set of transformations rather than over‑fitting to idiosyncratic patterns in the data. Because all changes occur multiplicatively, the matrix size and shape remain unchanged and inference incurs no overhead—the learned orthogonal matrix is simply assembled once after training and fused directly into the pretrained weight matrix.

By weaving together the butterfly factorization, Cayley‑based orthogonality enforcement, a principled hyperparameter framework, and careful initialization and dropout strategies, BOFT provides a flexible, highly parameter‑efficient alternative to conventional finetuning. It subsumes both block‑diagonal OFT and full OFT as special cases while offering a richer hypothesis class that preserves the desirable spectral properties of orthogonal transforms, ultimately yielding superior transfer performance across a wide range of vision and language tasks.
