### "Spectral properties preserved under OFT/BOFT versus LoRA – why matters?
Question:
"Paper claims multiplying left⁑ W⁰ by ⃖Rwō preserves largest singular value  largest singular norms stay fixed.
Beyond that definition,
a wide range “singular‐value spectrum” measures (e.g., spread, min/max ratio). Does OBFTOF impose further regularisation vs LoRA–and how drastic differences affect training stability / generalisation experimentally reported?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Multiplying a frozen weight matrix \(W^{0}\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times n}\) on the **left** side with an **orthogonal** matrix \(R\in{\mathbb O}_{d}\) does not change any entry of its *singular‑value spectrum*.  
Consequently every \(L_{2}\)-norm used during back‑propagation – the Lipschitz constant of each linear layer – stays exactly the same so long as \(R\) remains unitary during training.  

LoRA replaces \(W^{0}\) with  
\(W^{0}+AB^{\top};  
the additive term can increase or decrease any singular value arbitrarily and therefore perturbs the spectrum completely (largest eigenvalue may grow up toward end‑of‑epoch, smallest may collapse).  

Because OFT/BOFT keep the full spectrum intact they obtain **built‑in spectral regularisation**:
* No single layer’s operator norm can blow up or vanish,
* The overall network retains its gating dynamics and activation statistics,
* Training curves are smoother (lower variance in gradient norms),
* Empirical results show consistently higher downstream accuracies/game‑level scores compared with pure Low‑Rank updates while using far fewer trainable parameters.

## 1. What is actually preserved?

Let  
$$
W^{0}=U\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots ,\sigma_{r})\,V^{\top}
      \qquad( \sigma_{1}\ge\dots \ge\sigma_{r},\, r=\operatorname{rank}W^{0} )
$$
be an SVD of the pretrained linear mapping.
If \(R\in{\mathbb O}_{d}\) then  
$$
RW^{0}=RU\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{i})\,V^{\top}
      =(RU)\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{i})\,V^{\top}
      =U'\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{i})\,V^{\top},
$$  
where \(U'=RU'\) is again an orthogonal matrix.
Thus **the set of non‑zero singular values \(\{\sigma_i\}\)** does not change at all – neither their magnitudes nor their spacing.
Consequences follow immediately:


| Property | Preservation under OFT/BOFT | Alteration under LOCA |
|----------|-----------------------------|-----------------------|
| Largest singlular value \(\max_i\sigma_i=\|W^0\|_2​​</span></span>|unchanged​</span>|may grow/shrink​</span></span>
| Smallest non‑zero singlular value |unchanged​</span>|may shrink​</span>
| Condition number \(\kappa(W)=S\sigma_{\max}/S\sigma_{\min}</span><span)|unchanged​</span>|can become larger​</span></span>

The proof relies solely on basic linear algebra;
every left multiplication by an element of \(O(d)\)
is simply a rotation or reflection that does not alter volumes spanned
by columns.



## 2 Why does this matter for fine‑tuning?

### Training stability  
The operator norm bounds how much each layer can amplify input gradients.
If an early layer has a very large ‖·‖₂ it may cause exploding gradients,
and if a later one has too small ‖·‖₂ it will kill gradients.
Because OFT/BOFT leave every ‖·‖₂ untouched,
no additional gradient amplification occurs during fine‑tuning itself—even when hundreds of layers are adapted sequentially.
In contrast,
additive updates in LOCA universally inject new directions;
if their ranks dominate inherited directions they can __________\
inflates ‖·‖₂ exponentially through depth—a phenomenon already observed  
in Transformer experiments where LOCA often required extra damping
(e.g., learning rate warm‐ups).

### Generalisation  
Many recent works link weighted magnitudes through associated Lipschitz bounds  
to sample complexity (e.g., Miyato et al.’s “spectral normalisation’’).
Preserving contractive behaviour keeps the effective hypothesis space tighter:
the distance between two hidden representations before/after tune varies only by rotation,
hence statistical variance stays bounded.
Empirically this manifests most cleanly on small‐data downstream settings;
Boft blends “orthogonality’’ + “efficient dense coverage” so subtler feature reorientation takes place without shaking up scale‐dependent statistics.



## 3 Extra regularisation wielded by BOFT beyond single‑ratio preservation

While plain OFT already fixes the spectrum,
each learnt orthogonal matrix lives inside a submanifold—
the *complete* group \(O(d)\)
when no sparsity mask appears,
but inside one when constraints are imposed.
Boft constructs this submanifold via consecutive butterfly factors:
$$
R(m,b)=M_mM_{m-1}\dots M_1,
$$
each factor being block diagonal composed exclusively b×b rotations  
(b=2,… ≈ d⁄log d).
Two consequences follow:


1. Reduced hypothesis space –
compared to freely chosen dense rotations Boft restricts every factor’s sparsity pattern.
Consequently optimisation trajectories cannot wander into pathological regions such
as very sharp angular variations that would otherwise overflow numerics.

2. Implicit bias toward structured transforms –
many classic fast transforms (DFT/Hadamard/etc.) arise exactly from repeated simple butterflies;
thus boft inherently favours rotations whose action looks “smooth’’ along
input dimensions,
reminiscent of low‐frequency filters commonly useful in vision/tokens,
giving Boft foundations commonly seen in early Layers’ behaviour  
(*this weak emphasis explains why Boft attains broader accuracies than vanilla OFT even when operating under far fewer parameters*).

To guard against underspecified exploration naturally performed by such structured parametrisations,
Boft introduces multiplicative dropout tailored for its product form—
a contagion style mask randomly forces whole butterfly factors or subblocks towards identity.
Unlike vanilla Dropout applied elementwise,
this operation preserves exact unitarity whenever available while stochastically relaxing connectivity—
analogous—serving serves another shrinkage mechanism preventing accidental overfitting.
