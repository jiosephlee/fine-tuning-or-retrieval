### Is there an invertibility or separation guarantee to avoid position aliasing after interpolation?
Question:
For distinct positions \(n_1 \neq n_2\), after applying non-uniform interpolation, the rotated embeddings across all dimensions should remain sufficiently distinct to let attention discriminate them. Is there any condition on \(\{\lambda_i\}\) ensuring that the mapping \(n \mapsto [\cos(\cdot),\sin(\cdot)]\) remains injective (or at least collision-resistant) over \([0,L')\)? Could bad choices of \(\{\lambda_i\}\) lead to aliased positions that are indistinguishable to the model?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not give a formal injectivity or collision-free guarantee for the mapping $n \mapsto [\cos(\ldots), \sin(\ldots)]$ after applying the per-dimension rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$. In principle collision (aliasing) is possible because RoPE is composed of periodic $\cos/\sin$ coordinates; in practice LongRoPE reduces the risk via design choices and an empirical search objective (perplexity) that favors non-aliased solutions.

Why collisions are possible (intuitive + math). RoPE represents position $n$ by the vector of angles
$[\, n/\beta^0,\, n/\beta^1,\, \ldots \,]$ (up to the paper’s scaling/notation) and then applies $\cos$ and $\sin$ to each angle. After rescaling a dimension $i$ by $1/\lambda_i$ the $i$-th angle becomes
$\theta_i(n) = \left(n / \beta^i\right)\cdot\left(1/\lambda_i\right)$.
Two distinct positions $n_1 \neq n_2$ will be indistinguishable in the entire RoPE vector if and only if for every dimension $i$ the angle difference
$\Delta\theta_i = \theta_i(n_1) - \theta_i(n_2) = (n_1 - n_2)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\right)$
is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ (or equivalently the $\cos$ and $\sin$ are equal). So mathematically the mapping is injective over $0\ldots L'$ only if for every nonzero integer $m = n_1 - n_2$ in $[-(L'-1),(L'-1)]$ there exists at least one $i$ with
$(n_1 - n_2)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\right) \neq 2\pi k \quad\text{for all integers } k$.
Because $\cos/\sin$ are $2\pi$-periodic, if the set of scaled frequencies $1/(\lambda_i\beta^i)$ aligns badly with the integer differences, you can get exact (or very near) collisions.

Why this matters practically. Exact integer-multiple equalities are rare for arbitrary real $\lambda_i$, but near-equalities cause near-identical rotary embeddings and therefore the attention mechanism may have trouble discriminating positions (crowding). Linear global downscaling (PI) makes many positions “crowded” (angles compressed into a smaller range), increasing near-collisions for nearby $n$; NTK/YaRN and LongRoPE avoid some of that by preserving higher-frequency dimensions (smaller effective scaling) so close-by positions remain separated in at least some coordinates.

What the paper does to avoid aliasing (practical mitigations). The authors do not attempt a formal number-theoretic injectivity proof; instead they mitigate aliasing by:
- Searching non-uniform $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension (and optionally leaving the first $\hat n$ positions unscaled). This preserves higher-frequency dims (smaller $\lambda_i$) that give fine-grained phase changes for nearby $n$, reducing local aliasing.
- Enforcing a monotonic constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, which keeps the frequency ordering consistent and protects high-frequency dimensions from excessive downscaling.
- Optimizing $\lambda_i$ by an evolutionary search with perplexity as the objective, so solutions that cause practical aliasing (which hurts language modeling) are disfavored.
- Recovering short-context behavior via a secondary search that reduces allowed $\lambda$ ranges when evaluating short windows, to avoid crowding in the original range.

Recommendations and checks you can run (grounded in the paper’s approach). If you worry about collisions for a chosen $\{\lambda_i\}$, you can empirically test for them cheaply before fine-tuning: sample a set of position pairs (or differences $m$) up to your target $L'$, compute the multi-dimensional rotary vectors (or just angle vectors modulo $2\pi$), and measure minimal pairwise distance (or minimal cosine similarity) across those embeddings. If you find near-zero distances for distinct positions, that indicates potential aliasing. Practical heuristics that the paper’s method already uses and you can adopt are: keep some dimensions near-unmodified ($\lambda_i$ close to $1$) for high frequencies, allow an initial block of positions unscaled, and use a search objective that penalizes downstream loss (perplexity) rather than relying on an abstract injectivity property.

Bottom line. There is no formal injectivity guarantee in LongRoPE; exact aliased positions are possible in principle because of trigonometric periodicity. The paper’s empirical strategy (non-uniform, monotonic per-dimension scaling plus selective exemption of initial tokens and a perplexity-driven search) is designed to avoid practically harmful aliasing and to preserve separability where it matters for attention. If you need stronger assurances, you must add explicit constraints or post-hoc checks (e.g., sampled pairwise embedding distances, or constraints avoiding simple rational ratios in scaled frequencies) as part of the search.
