\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: LongRoPE}

### How do you ensure numerical stability of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) at positions near 2M tokens?
Question:
With angles like \(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}\) and \(n\) up to \(2{,}048{,}000\), do you encounter numerical issues in float16/float32 evaluation of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)? For instance, precision loss for large arguments can be severe. Do you reduce angles modulo \(2\pi\) explicitly, use higher precision for angle computation, or employ stable rotary implementations to avoid catastrophic cancellation?
Answer:
The paper does not discuss numerical tricks for $ \sin/\cos $ explicitly, and it does not report numerical instability in their experiments up to the $2{,}048\text{k}$ evaluation length. That suggests they did not hit catastrophic failures in practice. That said, your concern is valid: evaluating $ \sin/\cos $ of very large raw arguments can cause precision loss in low-precision arithmetic if you naively compute $ \sin(n\cdot\theta) $ with $n$ up to millions.

Why the problem is less severe than it first appears here. In RoPE the rotary angle for dimension $i$ is not simply $n$, it is
$$
n\theta_i \quad\text{with}\quad \theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}
$$
and in the paper’s unified notation the effective per-dimension angle is $n / \beta^{i}$ (with further rescaling by the searched $\lambda_i$). Because $\beta^{i}$ grows exponentially with $i$, most dimensions have very small angles even for large $n$; only the lowest-frequency dimensions (small $i$) can have large raw arguments. LongRoPE also rescales angles (often substantially) and enforces monotonic $\lambda_i$, which tends to reduce large-angle growth for many dimensions. Empirically the authors report stable training/evaluation up to $2\text{M}$ tokens, so whatever numeric handling they used was sufficient for their setup.

Practical recommendations (consistent with what would be safe / what the paper’s implementation likely relied on):

1) Compute angles in $ \text{float32} $ (or $ \text{float64} $) and keep the $ \sin/\cos $ call in that precision. In modern GPU libraries the $ \text{sincosf}/\text{sincos} $ calls perform internal range reduction (argument reduction modulo $2\pi$) and are far more accurate than computing $ \sin/\cos $ in $ \text{float16} $. So do NOT feed raw $ \text{float16} $ angles into $ \sin/\cos $.

2) Avoid doing argument arithmetic in $ \text{float16} $. Even if model weights and activations are stored in $ \text{fp16} $, compute the position-dependent angle terms in $ \text{fp32} $, call the hardware $ \text{sincos} $ (which does range reduction), then cast the resulting $ \sin/\cos $ to $ \text{fp16} $ if you must store them in $ \text{fp16} $. This preserves the essential precision of the trig results.

3) If you implement custom kernels: use a numerically robust range-reduction routine (e.g., reduce modulo $2\pi$ in $ \text{fp64} $ or use the same algorithms as libc/CUDA $ \text{sincos} $ which split the argument) before calling $ \sin/\cos $. For extremely large $n$ and tiny $\theta_i$, consider computing angles as $(n \bmod \text{period})/\text{scale}$ where $\text{period} = 2\pi\cdot(\text{denominator scaling})$, but this is usually unnecessary if you do the trig in $ \text{fp32} $ and rely on the hardware intrinsics.

4) If you observe issues (rare): you can: (a) compute angles in $ \text{fp64} $ during the search / generation of $\lambda$ (offline) and then store produced $ \sin/\cos $ values; (b) explicitly reduce the argument modulo $2\pi$ in high precision before $ \sin/\cos $; or (c) use recurrence relations to generate $ \sin/\cos $ across positions ($\sin(n+1) = \sin(n)\cos(1)+\cos(n)\sin(1)$) in a stable, incremental fashion for blocks — though that has its own numerical tradeoffs and complexity.

In short: the paper doesn’t report numerical failures and the normal, robust approach is to compute angles/$\sin\cos$ in $ \text{fp32} $ (or $ \text{fp64} $ if you want extra safety), relying on the standard $ \text{sincos} $ intrinsics that do proper range reduction, and only cast results to $ \text{fp16} $ if necessary. That avoids the catastrophic cancellation you worry about while remaining efficient — and is consistent with the fact LongRoPE achieved stable evaluation up to $2\text{M}$ tokens in their experiments.
