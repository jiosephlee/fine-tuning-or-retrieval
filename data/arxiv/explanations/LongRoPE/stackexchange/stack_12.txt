### What exactly are the "catastrophic values" introduced by new positions, and how does interpolation prevent them?
Question:
The introduction mentions that untrained new position indices introduce many catastrophic values, making fine-tuning hard to converge. Can you spell out what these catastrophic values are at the level of attention logits or hidden states? For example, do large angles cause destructive interference in QK dot products or numerical overflow/underflow in \(\sin/\cos\) at big \(n\)? How does your non-uniform interpolation specifically mitigate these failure modes?
Answer:
Short answer: the “catastrophic values” are not numeric overflows of sine/cosine but out-of-distribution positional phases that break the geometry the model was trained on, causing attention logits and hidden-state interactions to behave unpredictably (attention becomes “crowded” or dispersed, logits spike or lose discriminability), which makes fine‑tuning hard to converge. LongRoPE’s non‑uniform interpolation reduces this by selectively preserving the RoPE dimensions and early token positions that are most informative, so the $Q\cdot K$ geometry stays closer to the pretrained regime and attention weights remain well‑behaved.

Why they’re “catastrophic” (intuitive + technical sketch)
- RoPE encodes position $n$ in each paired dimension as $\cos(n\theta_i), \sin(n\theta_i)$. That is equivalent to rotating query/key components by an angle proportional to $n\theta_i$, so relative phases between positions control the $Q\cdot K$ dot products that produce attention logits. If you suddenly introduce many new positions $n \gg L$ (where $L$ was the trained max), you produce rotation angles $n\theta_i$ far outside the range the model saw during pretraining. Although $\sin$ and $\cos$ are numerically bounded (no overflow/underflow), the resulting phases are out-of-distribution.
- In practice those out-of-distribution phases produce two harmful effects in attention/logits:
  1. Crowding / loss of discriminability: after naive downscaling (e.g., linear PI) many different positions map to similar effective angles, so $Q\cdot K$ between different positions becomes similar. Attention weights flatten or mis-assign mass across many tokens—models can no longer tell “nearby” positions apart. Formally, if rotations make $q_n, k_m$ have similar phase alignment for many $(n,m)$, then $q_n\cdot k_m$ values lose the position-dependent structure the model learned.
  2. Unpredictable destructive interference / distribution shift: changing phases non-uniformly across RoPE dimensions alters the relative contributions of dimensions to dot products. Some dimensions that carried useful high-frequency position cues may be suppressed (large rescale), causing certain hidden directions to cancel or amplify unexpectedly. This shifts activation distributions of attention logits and downstream hidden states, hurting optimization and making fine-tuning unstable or slow to converge.
- So “catastrophic values” refers to attention logits and hidden activations being moved far from the pretrained distribution (not to arithmetic overflow).

How LongRoPE’s non‑uniform interpolation mitigates these failure modes
- Preserve informative (high-frequency) dimensions: LongRoPE searches per‑dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ (with monotonic constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) so that low-index RoPE dimensions (high-frequency) receive less downscaling. This preserves the fine-grained phase differences those dimensions encode, keeping $q\cdot k$ phase patterns similar to pretraining and preventing the crowding/loss-of-discriminability effect.
- Preserve early token positions: LongRoPE allows the first $\hat{n}$ tokens to remain unscaled (no interpolation). Empirically those initial tokens receive large attention and are especially sensitive; leaving them unchanged prevents their attention contributions from being corrupted, reducing catastrophic changes to logits and hidden states.
- Targeted, not uniform, change: by searching a non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ schedule (instead of a single global $\lambda = s$), LongRoPE avoids uniformly collapsing or excessively stretching all phase scales. This keeps the overall $Q\cdot K$ geometry closer to the training-time manifold, so attention weight distributions and downstream activations stay in a regime the model can adapt from quickly (hence fewer fine‑tuning steps and better converged models).
- Progressive/second-stage search: when pushing to extreme lengths (e.g., $2048k$), LongRoPE first finds a good non‑uniform rescale for an intermediate target ($256k$) and fine-tunes, then searches again from that fine‑tuned checkpoint. This reduces the abruptness of phase shifts the model must absorb during optimization, avoiding the large distribution jump that causes catastrophic behavior.

Concretely, in terms of attention logits $q_n\cdot k_m$: RoPE effectively multiplies the rotated components so the dot product depends on differences of angles like $(n\theta_i - m\theta_i)$ after scaling. If scaling makes these differences collapse or scramble across many $(n,m)$, the matrix $[q_n\cdot k_m]$ loses the positional structure the transformer expects. LongRoPE’s per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ and preserved initial positions keep those angle differences informative where it matters, so the attention matrix retains useful structure and the model can continue to produce sensible logits and hidden states.

Bottom line: the problem is distributional (phase/geometry) damage to attention and hidden representations produced by naive interpolation, not arithmetic overflow. Non‑uniform, dimension‑ and position‑aware rescaling keeps the phase geometry close to the pretrained regime, preventing the “catastrophic” disruption of attention logits and enabling stable, efficient fine‑tuning.
