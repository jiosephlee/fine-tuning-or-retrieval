\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: LongRoPE}

### Why do Mistral fine-tuning runs use 16k training length while targeting 128k/256k windows?
Question:
In the setup, for Mistral you follow YaRN and use 16k sequence length during fine-tuning while still calling the resulting models 128k/256k. How does this training length mismatch relate to the target context window? Is the idea that the searched RoPE provides the length generalization, with relatively short fine-tuning just anchoring the model? Any downsides observed (e.g., worse performance beyond 256k for Mistral compared to LLaMA2)?
Answer:
Short answer: the Mistral runs use $16\mathrm{k}$ training chunks as a pragmatic fine‑tuning setting (following YaRN) to save compute and because long training data are scarce, while relying on LongRoPE’s searched, non‑uniform RoPE to do most of the heavy lifting of length generalization. That combination—searched RoPE + relatively short fine‑tuning—gives a good tradeoff (fast fine‑tuning, reuses existing long‑data pipelines) but is not free: the paper reports that Mistral models fine‑tuned using $16\mathrm{k}$ chunks have more trouble beyond $\sim256\mathrm{k}$ than LLaMA2 models that were fine‑tuned on longer chunks (see Sec. 4.2 and the Books3 / passkey results).

Why $16\mathrm{k}$ instead of directly training at $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$
The paper explains two practical constraints: (1) long contiguous training segments ($128\mathrm{k}$–$256\mathrm{k}$) are scarce and (2) training on very long sequences is very expensive. For Mistral the authors followed YaRN’s fine‑tuning recipe ($16\mathrm{k}$ chunks, 400 steps on Together’s long‑data) because it fits available datasets and GPU budgets (Sec. 4.1, Fine‑tuning). Using shorter chunks greatly reduces memory/compute and allows a quicker fine‑tune checkpoint to be produced.

How this relates to the target window ($128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}/2048\mathrm{k}$)
LongRoPE is explicitly designed to separate two things: (A) find a better positional rescaling (non‑uniform RoPE) via search that preserves important dimensions/positions and therefore generalizes to much longer lengths without extensive training, and (B) use cheap progressive fine‑tuning at an intermediate length to anchor the model further. Concretely, LongRoPE first searches rescale factors for the target extended length (or an intermediate $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$ target) and then fine‑tunes. For Mistral the searched RoPE provides substantial length generalization (the search can give an $\sim8\times$ non‑finetune extension in many cases), and the $16\mathrm{k}$ fine‑tuning acts as a light anchor of model weights on longer‑length behavior (Sec. 3 and Sec. 3.3).

So yes: the searched RoPE supplies a lot of the length generalization and the relatively short fine‑tuning mostly “anchors” the model rather than fully training it at $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$ sequence lengths. That is exactly the progressive strategy the paper advocates: do search → fine‑tune at a manageable length (they used $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$ for LLaMA2, but Mistral used YaRN’s $16\mathrm{k}$ setting for the fine‑tune step) → optionally run a second search on the fine‑tuned checkpoint to push to much larger windows (Sec. 3.3).

Observed downsides / empirical effects
The paper reports concrete differences between LLaMA2 and Mistral that point to the limitations of the $16\mathrm{k}$ fine‑tuning choice. On Books3 and passkey tasks (Sec. 4.2): LongRoPE‑Mistral-$2048\mathrm{k}$ (ft=$128\mathrm{k}$ but trained with $16\mathrm{k}$ chunks per YaRN) performs well at shorter lengths but perplexity rises beyond $\sim256\mathrm{k}$ and passkey accuracy falls ($100\%$ to $60\%$ at $2048\mathrm{k}$). The authors attribute this partly to the use of $16\mathrm{k}$ training length during Mistral’s fine‑tuning: it reduces the fine‑tuning signal available for very large secondary extensions, so Mistral suffers more when the secondary extension ratio is large (Sec. 4.2, paragraph discussing Books3 and the final sentence: “The main reason is that for Mistral's $128\mathrm{k}$ and $256\mathrm{k}$ fine‑tuning, we follow YaRN’s setting to use a $16\mathrm{k}$ training length, which affects Mistral's ability to further extend context window after fine‑tuning.”). In contrast, LLaMA2 fine‑tuned with actual $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$ chunks shows steadier perplexity up to $2048\mathrm{k}$.

Intuition / broader concept
Positional interpolation (RoPE rescaling) is a way to remap new, unseen position indices back into the range the model was trained on. Search‑based, non‑uniform rescaling preserves high‑frequency (important) RoPE dimensions and the early token positions that tend to receive strong attention, so the model can generalize farther with less direct long‑sequence fine‑tuning. But interpolation alone cannot fully replace signal from real long sequences: very large extension ratios introduce many “new” positions and attention patterns that the model never observed, so some amount of true long‑sequence fine‑tuning (or progressive fine‑tuning at intermediate lengths) improves robustness. Using too short fine‑tuning chunks ($16\mathrm{k}$) is cheaper but may leave a residual ceiling on performance at the extreme end of the target window.

Practical takeaway
If you must conserve compute or lack long training data, using a searched non‑uniform RoPE plus short fine‑tuning (as in YaRN/LongRoPE) is a good strategy and often sufficient up to a few hundred kilotokens. If your goal is maximal robustness at multi‑hundred‑kilotoken or million‑token lengths, the paper’s experiments indicate it helps to fine‑tune with longer chunks (e.g., $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$) or use the progressive strategy (search → fine‑tune at $128\mathrm{k}/256\mathrm{k}$ → second search) described in LongRoPE to reduce the secondary extension ratio and avoid degradation beyond $\sim256\mathrm{k}$.

References in the paper: see Sec. 3 (search and progressive extension), Sec. 3.3 (progressive extension to $2048\mathrm{k}$), and Sec. 4.2 (Books3 and passkey results) for the empirical comparisons and the explicit note that Mistral’s $16\mathrm{k}$ fine‑tuning choice affects its large‑window performance.
