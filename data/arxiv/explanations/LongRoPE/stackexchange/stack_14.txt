\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: LongRoPE}

### Does the two-stage progressive strategy amount to composing two scalings, or is the second search done from scratch?
Question:
The method fine-tunes at 128k→256k with searched factors, then performs a second search on the 256k model to reach 2048k without further fine-tuning. Is the second stage initializing from the first stage's \(\{\lambda_i\}, \hat{n}\) and then refining, or does it search from scratch over the full space again? Mathematically, if the first stage applies \(\lambda_i^{(1)}\) and the second stage \(\lambda_i^{(2)}\), is the effective mapping equivalent to a single scaling by \(\lambda_i^{(1)}\,\lambda_i^{(2)}\), or do you replace the first set entirely?
Answer:
Short answer: the second stage searches anew on the fine‑tuned 256k model and replaces the previous rescale factors; it is not implemented as a simple multiplicative composition of two scalings.

Why: in the paper the progressive pipeline is (i) search for rescale factors for 128k and for 256k, (ii) fine‑tune the model while using the 128k factors for 400 steps then replacing with the 256k factors for another 600 steps, and (iii) after you obtain the fine‑tuned 256k model you “perform a secondary search on the fine‑tuned 256k‑length LLM” to get factors for 2048k and then apply those factors without further fine‑tuning. That wording and the method description make clear that the second search is performed on the state of the fine‑tuned model and returns a fresh set of per‑dimension rescale factors to be used for 2048k. In other words, the final RoPE used at inference is the result of the second search’s chosen $\lambda_i$ values (and $\hat{n}$) applied to the fine‑tuned model, not the product of the first-stage $\lambda_i^{(1)}$ and second-stage $\lambda_i^{(2)}$.

Intuition and a bit of math: LongRoPE defines the applied rotation as $\cos\!\left(I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\cdot \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right)$ where $I(\cdot)$ is either $1$ for $n<\hat n$ or $1/\lambda_i$ for $n\ge\hat n$ (see Eq. (1)/(problem formulation)). When you fine‑tune to 256k you used a particular set of $\lambda_i$ (call them $\lambda_i^{(\mathrm{ft})}$) during training. The secondary search does not simply pick extra factors to multiply onto the already‑applied $\lambda_i^{(\mathrm{ft})}$. Rather it searches for new $\lambda_i$ (call them $\lambda_i^{(2048)}$) to be used directly in the RoPE formula at inference on the fine‑tuned model. So the final mapping used is $\cos\!\left(I(\hat\lambda_i^{(2048)},\hat n^{(2048)})\cdot \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right)$, not $\cos\!\left(I(\hat\lambda_i^{(\mathrm{ft})},\hat n^{(\mathrm{ft})})\cdot I(\hat\lambda_i^{(2048)},\hat n^{(2048)})\cdot \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right)$. The second search is performed on the model weights that have been adapted under the 256k rescaling, but the rescale factors themselves are replaced by the newly searched ones for the 2048k run.

Why they do it this way: composing scalings multiplicatively would accumulate distortions of the rotary angles and is unlikely to be optimal. The paper argues (and empirically shows) that searching a new non‑uniform rescaling on the fine‑tuned model gives a better initialization for 2048k (and avoids the need to fine‑tune directly at 2048k). The second search therefore leverages the fine‑tuned model state while selecting a fresh set of per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ (and $\hat n$) tailored for the final large extension ratio, rather than naively multiplying earlier scalings.
