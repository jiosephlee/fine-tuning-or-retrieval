### Why does keeping the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens un-interpolated help, and how should \(\hat{n}\) scale with \(L'\)?
Question:
The paper reports that preserving the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens without interpolation improves perplexity and that the optimal \(\hat{n}\) depends on the target length. Can you provide intuition or a simple model for how attention distributions make early tokens especially sensitive to interpolation? Also, is there a principled way to choose \(\hat{n}\) as a function of \(L'\) (e.g., proportional to \(\log L'\), a fixed count, or learned via search)?
Answer:
Short answer: keeping the first $\hat n$ tokens un‑interpolated helps because those early tokens tend to receive large attention mass and so small changes to their RoPE phases produce disproportionately large changes to the attention logits (and hence loss). A principled way to pick $\hat n$ is to measure the model’s actual attention footprint on short-context examples and choose $\hat n$ so that the preserved prefix captures a chosen fraction of attention mass (or equivalently tune $\hat n$ on a small held‑out perplexity set). In practice $\hat n$ grows slowly with the target length $L'$ (sublinear — often a fixed small count or slowly increasing like $\log L'$ works), which matches the paper’s empirical choice to search $\hat n$ in a small set up to 256.

Why early tokens are especially sensitive (intuitive + simple model)
RoPE changes token-wise phase/rotation angles that affect the inner products between query and key vectors. Attention logits are proportional to these inner products; for a fixed query, the logit for position $n$ depends on the relative RoPE phase between the query position and position $n$. If you interpolate (rescale) the rotation angles for positions $n\ge\hat n$, you change those phases. When a token has a large attention weight, perturbing its logit by $\delta$ produces a large change in the softmax distribution and thus a large change in loss. So perturbing the phases of tokens with high attention is more harmful than perturbing phases of tokens with near-zero attention.

A minimal analytical sketch to make this concrete. Suppose a query attends over a prefix of tokens with baseline (pre-interpolation) logits $z_n$ and attention weights $a_n=\exp(z_n)/\sum_m \exp(z_m)$. After interpolation the logit for position $n$ shifts by $\Delta z_n$ determined by the phase change; for small $\Delta z_n$ the change in attention mass on position $n$ is approximately
$$
\Delta a_n \approx a_n (1-a_n)\,\Delta z_n \approx a_n\,\Delta z_n
$$
when $a_n$ is small-medium. So the contribution of position $n$ to the change in downstream loss scales roughly with $a_n\Delta z_n$. Two consequences follow: (1) positions with large $a_n$ amplify even small $\Delta z_n$; (2) reducing $\Delta z_n$ (i.e., not interpolating those positions) is the most effective way to keep loss small. This is exactly what keeping the first $\hat n$ tokens un‑interpolated does: it sets $\Delta z_n=0$ for the tokens that the model tends to attend to most.

Why early tokens get large attention
Empirically (and argued in the paper and related works like Streaming LLM, LM-Infinite), many attention patterns place relatively large mass on a local prefix or early context (e.g., headers, prompts, beginning of dialogue turns, function boundaries). Also common attention kernels (decaying with distance or focusing on local context) concentrate mass near the start of a block. So the “important” tokens are not uniformly spread over a million tokens; they are concentrated early, making them worth preserving exactly.

How $\hat n$ should scale with target length $L'$
There are two competing effects as $L'$ grows. On one hand, more overall positions means more interpolation is needed and the model may benefit from protecting a larger initial chunk. On the other hand, attention mass is usually concentrated in a local window that does not grow linearly with $L'$. Therefore $\hat n$ typically increases slowly with $L'$ (sublinear). The paper’s empirical behavior and choices support this:

- The authors search $\hat n$ from a small discrete set $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$ and find that preserving a modest number of initial tokens improves perplexity and that the optimal $\hat n$ depends on target length. They do not pick values that scale linearly with $L'$.
- Practical recommendations consistent with the paper: use a fixed small count (e.g., tens to a few hundreds) or a slowly growing rule, not $O(L')$.

A principled, simple heuristic you can use (grounded in the paper’s approach)
Compute an empirical average attention distribution from the pre-trained model on representative short-context examples (same domain you will evaluate on). For each position index $n$ compute the mean attention mass that the model assigns to positions with index < $n$. Pick $\hat n$ as the smallest $n$ such that the cumulative attention mass up to $n$ exceeds a threshold $\alpha$ (common choices $\alpha=0.6$–$0.9$). This gives a data-driven $\hat n$ that directly targets preserving the tokens that carry most attention. This is principled because, as the small-model sensitivity analysis above shows, positions with the bulk of attention produce the largest loss when their phases are perturbed.

Practical alternative: cheap validation search
The paper uses an evolutionary search over a small candidate set for $\hat n$ together with per-dimension $\lambda_i$. If you want a simpler workflow, fix a reasonable candidate list (the same as the paper’s discrete set up to 256) and evaluate perplexity on a small held‑out set for each candidate. This is cheap (inference only) and was the method used in the paper to pick $\hat n$.

Concrete summary guidance
If you want a short recipe, do one of these:

1. Data-driven attention heuristic: compute mean attention mass and choose $\hat n$ where cumulative mass ≥ 0.7. This is principled and often yields $\hat n$ in the tens–low hundreds.
2. Lightweight grid/search: evaluate perplexity with $\hat n\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256\}$ and pick the best; this is what the paper does and is cheap.
3. Rule of thumb: pick a small fixed $\hat n$ (e.g., 32–128) for many tasks. Increase it modestly for much larger $L'$, but avoid scaling $\hat n$ linearly with $L'$.

All three recommendations are consistent with the paper’s empirical findings: preserving a relatively small initial chunk reduces interpolation-induced degradation, and the optimal $\hat n$ depends on target length but grows slowly (and is best found with a small search or attention-based measurement).
