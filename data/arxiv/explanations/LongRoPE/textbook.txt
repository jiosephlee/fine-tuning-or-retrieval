\title{A Textbook about the Paper: LongRoPE}

Why Million-Token Contexts Matter

# Use cases for long context: in-context learning with many examples, long-document reasoning, LLM agents and planning

The value of very long context windows—reaching into the millions of tokens—comes directly from the kinds of tasks we expect modern LLMs to perform. One important class is in‑context learning with many examples. Some applications benefit from giving the model a large number of calibration examples, demonstrations, or few‑shot instances in a single prompt so that it can better generalize to a complex downstream task. When the number of relevant examples grows from a handful to hundreds or thousands, a short context window (e.g., a few thousand tokens) simply cannot hold enough examples to be effective.

A second class of applications is long‑document reasoning: summarizing or analyzing entire books, codebases, legal files, or multi‑session conversations. For such applications, important dependencies and evidence can span extremely long stretches of text. If the model can attend to a million tokens rather than a few thousand, it can make inferences that depend on far‑apart facts, preserve narrative coherence across long horizons, and produce summaries or edits that respect the full document context.

Third, LLM agents and planning systems—where the model acts as an orchestrator over many steps, logs, and retrieved documents—benefit from retaining long histories. Agents may need to revisit prior decisions, consult long traces of past interactions, or inspect large retrieved contexts to plan future actions. Larger context windows let a single forward pass incorporate many more past events or candidate documents, simplifying agent architectures and reducing reliance on external retrieval or memory systems.

These use cases share the same intuition: more context can be essential to correctness, coherence, and sample efficiency. Million‑token contexts aim to make those benefits practical for a wide class of real‑world tasks.

# Limits of standard LLMs (e.g., 4k–8k in LLaMA2) and performance drop beyond trained lengths

Most pre‑trained LLMs are trained with an explicit maximum sequence length: the models learn positional encodings or positional mechanisms only for token indices up to that maximum. For example, as discussed in the paper, LLaMA2 commonly ships with a context window of roughly 4096 tokens. That training choice means the model has experience only with positional encodings and attention patterns up to that length.

When we try to use such a model past its trained range, performance degrades. Tokens at new, unseen positions receive position encodings the model has never encountered, which makes the input distribution out of sample relative to pre‑training. Empirically, this manifests as rising perplexity, failing retrieval or passkey tasks, and degraded downstream accuracy. The degradation can be severe even for modest extensions: the model’s ability to distinguish closely positioned tokens or to place high attention on early tokens may be disrupted when many additional positions are introduced.

We can formalize the notion of extension ratio with a simple quantity. If the original maximum length is $L$ and we wish to operate at a new length $L'$, define the extension ratio
\[
s=\frac{L'}{L}.
\]
For example, extending from $L=4{,}096$ to $L'=32{,}768$ corresponds to $s=8$. As $s$ grows, the fraction of token positions the model has never seen (roughly $1 - L/L'$) increases rapidly; very large $s$ therefore represents a substantial out‑of‑distribution change for the model.

# Three obstacles: catastrophic values from unseen positions, scarcity/cost of very long training texts, and short-context performance degradation

Pushing context windows well beyond what a model was trained on faces three interlocking obstacles.

1) Catastrophic values from unseen positions. Introducing new position indices leads to position encodings the model has never been trained to interpret. These new encodings can create "catastrophic values" in internal activations and attention scores, causing the model to behave unpredictably and making fine‑tuning difficult to converge. The problem is especially acute when the extension ratio is large: extending from a few thousand tokens to over a million tokens can mean that more than 90% of token positions are entirely new to the model. Without a principled way to map new positions into the model’s learned positional space, training becomes unstable.

2) Scarcity and cost of very long training texts. Fine‑tuning the model to handle longer context windows is one direct remedy, but it requires training data of the matching lengths and substantial compute. Extremely long documents (for example, documents exceeding hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens) are relatively scarce in publicly available corpora. Furthermore, even when such long data are obtainable, training on very long sequences is expensive: each training pass on long input segments consumes large amounts of GPU memory and time. These two limitations make naïve fine‑tuning to extremely long windows impractical at scale.

3) Short‑context performance degradation. Another important obstacle is that methods used to accommodate new positions can inadvertently harm performance on the original, shorter context lengths that the model must still support. For example, many positional‑rescaling approaches compress the range of rotation angles or embed many distinct short‑range positions into a narrower region of the positional manifold. This "crowding" makes it harder for the model to discriminate fine positional differences inside the original window. In attention terms, when a model is adapted to a very long window, the attention distribution can become dispersed across many positions, reducing the focused attention patterns that were useful for short‑range tasks. Thus, a naive extension can trade long‑context capability for losses on the familiar, short contexts.

These three obstacles together explain why simply increasing an LLM’s context length is fundamentally nontrivial: you must avoid catastrophic out‑of‑distribution encodings, you must do so without impractical data or compute demands, and you must preserve the model’s original short‑context strengths.

# Overview of current 32k–128k extensions and why pushing beyond 128k is hard

Recent literature has made steady progress in extending LLM context windows into the tens or low hundreds of thousands of tokens. Two broad strategies have emerged.

One strategy is fine‑tuning the model on longer sequences. If you can present the model with training segments of the target length, the model can learn to interpret the new positions directly. Works that take this approach have successfully extended windows to about 128k tokens in practice. But, as discussed, fine‑tuning at those scales requires access to long training segments and substantial compute, and it becomes progressively more expensive as target length grows.

A complementary strategy is positional interpolation: modify the positional encoding (especially for rotary positional embeddings like RoPE) so that new token indices are "mapped back" into the original learned range. Linear positional interpolation (PI) is the simplest such approach: to extend from $L$ to $L'$, PI rescales the rotation angles by the extension ratio $\lambda=s$, effectively compressing the new positions into the pre‑trained angular range. PI is simple and widely used, but it tends to crowd many positions into a small angular range at large $s$, which reduces the model’s ability to discriminate nearby tokens and therefore underperforms at high extension ratios.

More sophisticated non‑uniform interpolation schemes build on the observation that different components of RoPE encode position at different effective frequencies. NTK‑inspired methods distribute the interpolation pressure across RoPE dimensions so that lower‑index (higher‑frequency) dimensions are scaled less and higher‑index (lower‑frequency) dimensions are scaled more. YaRN takes a pragmatic middle ground: it partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency groups and applies different interpolation or extrapolation strategies to each group (for example, extrapolating the highest frequencies while interpolating the lowest). These non‑uniform methods can achieve larger extensions in non‑fine‑tuning scenarios than PI can, and they avoid some of PI’s crowding; however, they have limitations. The grouping choices in YaRN are hand‑designed and may not be optimal for all architectures, and NTK‑style formulae often only safely extend windows by modest factors (e.g., up to about 4× without fine‑tuning in practice).

Why is pushing beyond around 128k so hard? Several interrelated reasons stand out. First, as the extension ratio $s$ becomes enormous (for instance, going from $L\approx 4{,}096$ to $L'\approx 2{,}048{,}000$ yields $s\approx 512$), the number of distinct rescaling choices across RoPE dimensions and token positions explodes combinatorially. Finding a mapping that preserves the most important positional information in the original model becomes an enormous search problem. Second, the scarcity and computational cost of fine‑tuning data at those lengths makes direct training infeasible for most practitioners. Third, existing interpolation heuristics are too coarse: linear PI loses rich structure in RoPE, NTK gives a single analytic form that may not match the empirical encoding importance across dimensions and positions, and groupings like YaRN’s, while helpful, do not fully capture the complex, non‑uniform information distribution across both RoPE dimensions and token positions.

Put together, these factors set a practical ceiling near the low hundreds of thousands of tokens for many prior approaches. Overcoming that ceiling requires three ingredients: (a) a way to preserve the most critical original positional information while mapping new positions into the learned manifold, (b) a more fine‑grained, data‑guided method for allocating interpolation across RoPE dimensions and initial token positions, and (c) a training strategy that reduces the need to fine‑tune on ultra‑long segments (e.g., a progressive approach that fine‑tunes at an intermediate length and then further extends).

The work summarized in the paper—LongRoPE—targets those exact gaps: it emphasizes exploiting two distinct non‑uniformities (across RoPE dimensions and across token positions), uses an efficient search to find per‑dimension and position thresholds for rescaling, and adopts a progressive fine‑tuning strategy so that a relatively small amount of fine‑tuning (on the order of 1{,}000 steps at a 256k training length) can support an eventual, non‑fine‑tuned jump to a 2{,}048k context. The methodological details and empirical results show how addressing the obstacles described above can push context windows into the million‑token regime while largely preserving short‑context performance.

Foundations: Attention, Positional Encodings, and RoPE

# Transformer attention and why sequence order needs to be encoded
Transformers compute attention between token representations without any built‑in notion of sequence order. In a self‑attention layer each token produces query, key and value vectors; attention weights arise from inner products between queries and keys, and these weights control how information flows across positions. If the model only used token embeddings (or wordpiece ids) but no positional signal, the attention mechanism would be permutation‑invariant: permuting input tokens would produce the same set of pairwise interactions and thus the model could not reliably distinguish different token orders. For language and other sequential data the order is essential — meaning, the model must be given an explicit positional signal so that attention can depend on where each token sits in the sequence.

Accordingly, Transformer architectures use positional encodings or embeddings added to (or combined with) token representations so that the attention computation becomes order‑aware. The paper frames this as: “Transformer models require explicit positional information, often in the form of position embedding, to represent the order of input tokens,” and the rest of the work focuses on a particular family of positional encodings, Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), which is widely adopted in modern large language models.

# Absolute vs. relative positional embeddings; pros and cons
Position embeddings come in different flavors. One common summary (as used in the community and implicit in the paper’s context) is to contrast absolute and relative approaches. Absolute positional encodings associate each token position with a fixed vector (or a deterministic transform of the position) and inject that information into token representations. Relative approaches, instead, encode distance or relative offsets between token pairs so that attention can directly depend on relative displacement rather than absolute index.

The text of the paper centers on RoPE as a positional encoding mechanism that provides a compact, rotation‑based encoding of position that is applied to queries and keys. In practice, different choices trade off properties: absolute encodings give a direct index signal, but can be brittle when models are asked to operate at positions not seen during training (new absolute indices create out‑of‑distribution inputs). Relative encodings can be more robust to varying sequence lengths and emphasize pairwise offsets, but they typically require different attention formulations or architectural changes. RoPE occupies a middle ground in practice: it applies a deterministic, index‑dependent rotation to query/key vectors. As the paper explores, the way those rotation angles are scaled (for example when extending a model’s context window) has strong practical consequences for robustness, crowding of position signals, and whether fine‑tuning is needed.

# RoPE mathematics and intuition: rotation in query/key space, frequencies across dimensions
RoPE encodes positional information by rotating components of the query and key vectors by position‑dependent angles. The encoding is implemented dimensionwise using sine and cosine of rotary angles so that, when queries and keys are rotated consistently, their inner product carries position information. A convenient way to view the RoPE encoding is as pairs of sine/cosine features for each rotary frequency.

The paper presents a compact representation of RoPE for a token at position index $n$ (with embedding dimension $d$) as:
\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\; \ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right]
\]
where each $n\theta_i$ is the rotary angle for the $i$‑th (pair) dimension. Intuitively, each dimension pair behaves like a 2D rotation of a vector by angle $n\theta_i$. If the query and key are rotated by the same position‑dependent angle, the cosine of their angle difference appears in the dot product, allowing attention to reflect relative phase differences induced by positions.

The choice of the sequences of angles $\theta_i$ across dimensions determines a spectrum of positional frequencies. In RoPE the angles vary across dimensions so that different dimensions encode different spatial frequencies (some dimensions change rapidly with $n$, others slowly). This multi‑frequency representation gives the model both fine and coarse positional resolution across different dimensions.

# Key variables and notation: n (position), d (dimension), θ, β, rotation angle n/β^i
To reason precisely about RoPE we use the notation from the paper. Let:
- $n$ denote the token position index (an integer counting positions in the input sequence),
- $d$ denote the embedding dimension (so there are $d/2$ sine/cosine pairs),
- $\theta_i$ denote the base rotation frequency for the $i$‑th pair of RoPE dimensions,
- $n\theta_i$ denote the actual rotary angle applied at position $n$ for that dimension.

The paper adopts a common parametrization of the per‑dimension frequencies. A scalar base value (also called $\theta$ in the paper, with a conventional default of $10000$) defines the frequency schedule. The per‑dimension frequency is given by
\[
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\]
and the corresponding rotary angle for position $n$ is $n\theta_i$. To simplify certain expressions, the paper introduces
\[
\beta=\theta^{2/d},
\]
and writes the rotation angle compactly as
\[
\frac{n}{\beta^i}.
\]
So the $i$‑th dimension’s rotary angle at position $n$ can be expressed as $n/\beta^i$. This form makes clear the geometric progression of effective wavelengths across dimensions: increasing $i$ multiplies the denominator by powers of $\beta$, producing progressively smaller angles per unit $n$ (i.e., lower frequencies).

Using the cosine/sine pair form, RoPE represents position $n$ across the $d$ dimensions via the sequence of $\cos(n/\beta^i)$ and $\sin(n/\beta^i)$ terms (paired across $i$ from $0$ to $d/2-1$). In code or practical implementations these rotations are applied directly to slices of query and key vectors so that the attention dot products incorporate these phase shifts.

# How base frequency (e.g., 10000) governs spectrum of positional frequencies
The base scalar $\theta$ (often set to $10000$) controls the overall spacing of frequencies across the $d$ RoPE dimensions. Through the relation $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ and $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$, the per‑dimension rotary angles form an exponential sequence. Concretely, when $\theta=10000$ and the dimension index $i$ increases, $\beta^i$ grows exponentially, and hence the rotation angle $n/\beta^i$ shrinks exponentially for a fixed $n$. Early (low‑indexed) dimensions therefore have larger rotation angles per unit position (higher frequency components that change rapidly with $n$), while later dimensions have smaller angles (low frequency components that vary slowly with $n$).

This spectrum is important for two reasons emphasized in the paper. First, having multiple frequencies gives the model the ability to represent both small positional offsets (via high‑frequency dimensions) and large scale positional context (via low‑frequency dimensions). Second, when attempting to extend a model’s usable context window by rescaling or interpolating positional angles, different RoPE dimensions respond differently: low‑index (high‑frequency) dimensions are more sensitive to rescaling and often benefit from less interpolation, whereas high‑index (low‑frequency) dimensions can tolerate or even require more aggressive rescaling. The paper leverages that observation: non‑uniform interpolation across RoPE dimensions (and also different handling of early token positions) preserves key information in the original RoPE and leads to much better behavior when extending the context window.

A final practical point in the paper’s development is that many context‑extension strategies operate by rescaling the effective rotary angles. If $\lambda$ denotes a rescale factor tied to a target extension ratio $s=L'/L$, then a common interpolation scheme is to replace $n$ with $n/\lambda$ (or equivalently scale $\theta_i$). The paper unifies such schemes in the form
\[
\left[\cos\!\bigg(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\bigg),\; \sin\!\bigg(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\bigg),\; \cos\!\bigg(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\bigg),\; \ldots\right],
\]
and explores how choosing $\lambda$ uniformly or non‑uniformly across dimensions and token positions impacts model performance when the context window is extended.

In summary, RoPE builds a multi‑frequency rotation‑based positional representation parameterized by a base $\theta$ (commonly $10000$), dimension index $i$, embedding dimension $d$, and token position $n$. Understanding how these variables interact — how rotation angles scale across dimensions and how rescaling those angles affects positional resolution — is central to the paper’s investigation into extending LLM context windows without losing the model’s ability to distinguish nearby tokens or to generalize to unseen positions.

Prior Approaches to Context Extension

# Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) encode position $n$ by multiplying token features by rotation angles that grow linearly with $n$. In the simplified RoPE form used in the paper, a token at position $n$ has the positional components
\[
\big[\cos(n\theta_0),\ \sin(n\theta_0),\ \cos(n\theta_1),\ \sin(n\theta_1),\ \ldots\big],
\]
where each rotary angle is $n\theta_i$ and the frequencies satisfy $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ (with the usual base $\theta=10000$). Because a pre-trained model has only seen positions up to some $L$, naive use of larger position indices $n>L$ produces out-of-distribution rotation angles and often catastrophic behavior.

Linear Position Interpolation (PI) is the simplest, widely used strategy to extend the context window without changing model parameters: downscale the rotation angles of new positions so that the largest angle maps back into the pretrained range. If the extension ratio is $s=\tfrac{L'}{L}$ for extending from original length $L$ to target $L'$, PI uniformly rescales angles by $\lambda=s$ across all RoPE dimensions. In the unified interpolation view this replaces angle $n/\beta^i$ by $(1/\lambda)\,n/\beta^i$ with the same $\lambda$ for all dimensions.

PI has a direct intuition and is trivial to implement, but it suffers from a fundamental "crowding" problem. By compressing a much larger set of position indices into the numeric range the model was trained on, many distinct positions end up mapped to nearby rotation angles. Attention and the model's learned functions then have less ability to discriminate nearby token positions, which degrades generation and prediction for large $s$. Empirically, PI often requires fine-tuning to be useful beyond modest extension ratios; at high $s$ its compressed positional geometry becomes a bottleneck.

# NTK-based interpolation and extrapolation

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) perspective analyzes how different frequency components (i.e., different RoPE dimensions) contribute to function approximation and sensitivity. NTK-based approaches exploit this by distributing the "interpolation pressure" non-uniformly across RoPE dimensions instead of applying a single uniform scale.

Concretely, NTK-style methods scale lower-index (higher-frequency) dimensions less and higher-index (lower-frequency) dimensions more. One simple parametrization used in the literature is to set per-dimension scaling factors that grow with the dimension index, e.g. a rule like $\lambda_i = s^{\alpha(i)}$ where $\alpha(i)$ increases with $i$. The intuition is that higher-frequency dimensions encode fine-grained positional differences and should be altered minimally to preserve local discriminability, while lower-frequency dimensions can be compressed more to accommodate a larger global range.

Dynamic NTK methods further adapt the interpolation factor at each position based on the current sequence length, aiming to keep useful frequency content available for different parts of the sequence. A major practical advantage of NTK-inspired schemes is that by preserving important frequencies they can extend context without any fine-tuning in some regimes: NTK-style interpolation is commonly effective up to roughly a 4× extension ratio in non-fine-tuned settings. However, even NTK-based methods encounter limits: when the extension ratio becomes large, even dimension-wise reweighting cannot fully avoid information loss, and performance degrades unless the model is fine-tuned on longer contexts.

# YaRN: three frequency bands with different strategies

YaRN builds on the insight that different RoPE frequency bands should be treated differently, but it does so by partitioning RoPE dimensions into three groups (high-frequency, mid-frequency, low-frequency) and applying a distinct interpolation strategy to each group. The canonical assignment in YaRN is:

- High-frequency dimensions: extrapolate (i.e., do not interpolate, effectively $\lambda=1$), preserving fine-grained local position signals.
- Mid-frequency dimensions: apply NTK-style interpolation.
- Low-frequency dimensions: apply linear PI-style interpolation (larger downscaling), allowing the low-frequency components to cover the enlarged global range.

This three-way scheme interpolates between the extremes of pure PI and pure NTK. The principal advantage of YaRN is practical: it often yields better behavior at moderate extension ratios by preserving the most important frequencies while compressing those that matter less for local discrimination.

A key limitation of YaRN, emphasized in the paper, is that the grouping into three bands is a human-designed choice derived from empirical tuning. The optimal breakpoints between frequency bands can depend on the model architecture and the target extension; a fixed grouping may be suboptimal for a new LLM or a different target length. The paper also reports empirical cases where YaRN underperforms PI or NTK (for example, failing to reach the target context length in some non-finetuned evaluations), indicating that handcrafted groupings can miss better non-uniform patterns.

# Typical fine-tuning strategies and costs, need for long documents, and context–performance trade-offs

Extending an LLM's context window by changing positional embeddings is often followed by fine-tuning on longer sequences to adapt the model to the new positional geometry. Fine-tuning helps the model recover from distributional shifts introduced by new positions and adjust internal representations and attention patterns to operate effectively over longer ranges.

However, fine-tuning for long context windows carries several practical challenges described in the paper. First, training on very long sequences requires corresponding long-text data. Such texts (especially those exceeding hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens) are scarce; large publicly available corpora typically contain few documents of extreme length. Second, training on longer sequences is computationally expensive. Longer sequences increase memory and compute per step, and reaching adequate coverage of long-range behaviors can demand many optimization steps or large batches, which translates into significant GPU resources and wall-clock time. Third, when a model is suddenly exposed to many new positions, the positional embeddings for those indices can create catastrophic, out-of-distribution activations that make optimization unstable or slow to converge.

Because of these constraints, prior practical pipelines have favored moderate extensions coupled with limited fine-tuning. Many prior works achieve useful extended windows (tens to a few hundreds of thousands of tokens) but stop well short of million-token scales because of the combination of data scarcity, compute costs, and instability.

# Non-finetuned vs. finetuned extensions; practical ceilings around 4×–8× without finetune

A useful way to categorize prior approaches is whether they aim to extend context without any additional training (non-finetuned) or whether they perform fine-tuning after changing positional embeddings.

Non-finetuned extensions are attractive because they are cheap and immediate: they only change how position indices are mapped into the pretrained angular range. PI is the simplest such change but rapidly runs into the crowding problem. NTK-based and dynamic NTK approaches improve on PI by preserving important frequency components, and in practice they push the usable non-finetuned extension farther—commonly to about a 4× expansion before perplexity or generation quality collapses.

YaRN further expands the non-finetuned capability by mixing extrapolation and interpolation across frequency bands; this can improve robustness for some extension targets but, as noted, depends on human-chosen banding and can fail in other cases.

The empirical ceiling for purely non-finetuned methods reported in the paper is around 4× for many NTK-like methods, with carefully designed non-uniform schemes sometimes reaching up to 8× in favorable cases. However, going beyond an 8× extension without any fine-tuning is generally unrealistic: excessive compression of angles or overuse of extrapolation leads to loss of positional discriminability or misaligned representations that the model has no opportunity to correct.

When larger extensions are required, fine-tuning becomes necessary. Fine-tuning on intermediate long lengths (for example, training to 128k or 256k) has been the practical strategy: it reduces the jump the model must make and makes subsequent interpolation to even larger windows more stable. The trade-off is the cost and data requirements discussed above. Prior works therefore typically balance these factors—using NTK-inspired interpolation to get some non-finetuned gains, and limited fine-tuning on the longest feasible lengths to push context further—rather than attempting a single-shot extrapolation to million-token scales.

Summary

The prior RoPE-based approaches form a spectrum from uniform linear interpolation (PI) through NTK-motivated per-dimension scaling to hybrid schemes like YaRN that manually split frequency bands. PI is simple but suffers crowding; NTK methods preserve important frequencies and enable modest non-finetuned extensions (commonly up to $\sim$4×); YaRN improves robustness by mixing strategies across bands but relies on hand-tuned groupings that may be suboptimal. Fine-tuning can move the practical frontier further, but it is constrained by long-text availability, compute cost, and optimization instability when many new positions are introduced. As a result, prior practical ceilings for non-finetuned extensions cluster around 4× (with carefully designed non-uniform methods sometimes reaching 8×), while larger extensions have typically required careful staged fine-tuning and significant resources.

Empirical Findings: Non-Uniformities in Positional Interpolation

# Finding 1: Strong dimension-wise non-uniformity; per-dimension scaling $\lambda_i$ outperforms PI/NTK/YaRN

Transformers that use RoPE positional encodings represent a token at position $n$ by applying rotation angles across paired embedding dimensions. In the paper the RoPE vector for a token at position $n$ is written compactly as
\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\ \sin(n\theta_0),\ \cos(n\theta_1),\ \sin(n\theta_1),\ \ldots\right],
\]
where $\theta_i$ are the rotation frequencies (often derived from a base such as $10000$). Extending the context window is commonly done by rescaling these rotation angles: for an extension ratio $s = L'/L$ one replaces the original angles by smaller angles so that tokens with index beyond the pretrained range fall into the original angular manifold. Typical approaches set a single global rescale (linear positional interpolation, PI), or a dimension-dependent analytic schedule motivated by NTK (dynamic-NTK) or hand-designed grouping (YaRN).

The first empirical finding in the paper is that RoPE dimensions are not uniform in their sensitivity to rescaling: different embedded frequency components benefit from different rescale factors. Concretely, instead of using a single $\lambda$ for all dimensions, allowing a distinct rescale factor $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension gives substantially better language-modeling performance when evaluating long-context perplexity without further fine-tuning. A convenient way to express this per-dimension rescaling is to replace the original angle $n\theta_i$ by $n\theta_i / \lambda_i$ (equivalently scale the base frequency), i.e.
\[
\text{RoPE}_n \;\rightsquigarrow\; \left[\cos\!\left(\frac{n\theta_0}{\lambda_0}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n\theta_0}{\lambda_0}\right),\ \ldots,\ \cos\!\left(\frac{n\theta_{d/2-1}}{\lambda_{d/2-1}}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n\theta_{d/2-1}}{\lambda_{d/2-1}}\right)\right].
\]

Why does per-dimension scaling help? Intuitively, different RoPE dimensions encode different effective position "frequencies": some dimensions vary rapidly with token index (high-frequency components), others vary slowly (low-frequency components). High-frequency dimensions help distinguish very nearby token positions because small changes in $n$ yield noticeable phase differences; low-frequency dimensions help capture long-range phase structure but are more tolerant to coarse-graining. If all dimensions are uniformly downscaled by a large factor (as in PI), high-frequency components can be crushed into a narrow angular range and lose their discriminative power for near-by tokens. The NTK-inspired approaches partially address this by recommending smaller rescaling for low indices and larger for others, but they still follow fixed analytical patterns or coarse groupings.

In the empirical study (searching per-dimension $\lambda_i$ on LLaMA2-7B using perplexity as the objective), the discovered $\lambda_i$ profiles are clearly non-uniform and yield lower perplexity on long-context evaluation datasets (PG19 and Proof-pile) than PI, dynamic-NTK, and YaRN when no fine-tuning is performed. The paper reports that YaRN in particular can underperform PI/NTK on PG19 in this non-fine-tuned regime because its hand-designed grouping does not match the model- and target-length-specific optimal allocation of interpolation pressure. The successful per-dimension solutions tend to respect a monotonicity pattern (lower-index/high-frequency dimensions receive smaller $\lambda_i$, while higher-index/low-frequency dimensions receive larger $\lambda_i$), consistent with NTK intuition, but crucially they are data- and model-driven rather than fixed formulas.

# Finding 2: Positional non-uniformity among token indices; early tokens should be less interpolated or extrapolated

The second non-uniformity the paper identifies is along the token-index axis rather than the RoPE-dimension axis. Not all token positions are equally important to preserve exactly; the empirical observation is that the earliest tokens in the input (the first $\hat{n}$ tokens) tend to be especially important and should be subjected to less or no interpolation compared to later tokens.

Formally, the paper models this by a position-threshold $\hat{n}$ and a per-dimension rescale $\hat{\lambda}_i$ so that the RoPE applied to position $n$ is
\[
\text{RoPE}_n = 
\begin{cases}
\left[\cos(n\theta_i),\sin(n\theta_i)\right]_{i\,:=0}^{d/2-1} & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[6pt]
\left[\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{n\theta_i}{\lambda_i}\bigr),\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{n\theta_i}{\lambda_i}\bigr)\right]_{i\,:=0}^{d/2-1} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
\]
In other words, for the first $\hat{n}$ positions we keep the original RoPE (no rescaling), while positions beyond $\hat{n}$ use the searched rescale factors.

Why preserve early tokens? The paper points to the attention dynamics: initial tokens often receive strong attention weights and play outsized roles in the model's computation (for example, in streaming or left-context setups initial context tends to be referenced heavily). If the encoding of those tokens changes drastically due to large interpolation, the attention distribution and resulting representations can be destabilized. Empirically, the authors tested keeping various numbers of starting tokens un-interpolated (searching $\hat{n}$ among values {0, 2, 4, 8, …, 256}) and observed that retaining a small block of initial tokens without rescaling reduces perplexity versus rescaling all tokens. Moreover, the optimal $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension length: for modest extensions a small $\hat{n}$ suffices, but for larger targets different $\hat{n}$ values can be preferable.

This positional non-uniformity interacts with the dimension-wise non-uniformity: preserving both the most important dimensions (via small $\lambda_i$ for them) and the earliest token encodings (via $\hat{n}$) avoids two distinct forms of crowding that interpolation can introduce.

# Finding 3: Non-uniform interpolation helps both without fine-tuning (notably 8×) and with fine-tuning

A critical practical question is whether these non-uniform interpolations are only helpful when followed by costly fine-tuning, or whether they can already extend useful context capacity without any additional training. The paper reports two complementary empirical findings.

First, when applying the searched non-uniform per-dimension $\lambda_i$ (and the positional threshold $\hat{n}$) directly to a pretrained model without any fine-tuning, the model can reliably extend its usable context window by up to about 8× (for example, the paper shows extending a 4k context to 32k with substantially better perplexity behavior than PI, NTK, or YaRN). In contrast, applying uniform PI or the prior NTK/YaRN recipes tends to cause perplexity to spike much earlier (often after a 2× extension). The ability to extend 8× without fine-tuning is important because it avoids the need for long, expensive training runs and for long training sequences which may be scarce.

Second, for much larger extensions (hundreds of times larger), fine-tuning is still required. However, the non-uniform interpolation provides a markedly better initialization for such fine-tuning. The paper demonstrates this by searching non-uniform rescale factors and fine-tuning LLaMA2-7B to a 64k context length: models initialized with the searched non-uniform RoPE converge to lower perplexity than models initialized by PI or YaRN. Thus non-uniform interpolation reduces the "catastrophic values" introduced by new positions and makes subsequent fine-tuning easier and more effective.

Taken together these results support a practical two-stage strategy: (1) discover and apply non-uniform interpolation to obtain as large an extension as possible without training, and (2) for further extension, fine-tune only from that better initialization (which requires fewer steps and less extreme training lengths than naively fine-tuning from PI-initialized extremes).

# Metrics and datasets used for discovery: perplexity on PG19 and Proof-pile without fine-tuning

Perplexity is the primary metric used in the empirical discovery and evaluation. It directly measures next-token prediction quality, and is particularly informative when probing how well a model can exploit long-range context with modified positional encodings.

For searching per-dimension rescale factors and validating the non-uniform hypotheses, the paper used LLaMA2-7B as the testbed and computed perplexity on datasets of long texts. Specifically, the search itself used 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set as an efficiently measurable proxy objective. For broader evaluation without fine-tuning the search outcomes were compared on the PG19 and Proof-pile test sets. For larger-scale verification of extreme extensions the authors later use parts of Books3 and Pile variants, but PG19 and Proof-pile are the central datasets for the initial empirical findings.

Perplexity was computed over long windows (e.g., windows matching the target extension lengths), and sliding-window evaluation was used in later experiments to assess behavior across positions. In all cases, lower perplexity indicates that the modified positional encoding preserves useful positional information for language modeling across the extended range.

# Why preserving key dimensions and initial-token positions reduces information loss and crowding

Two related failure modes hurt naive positional interpolation. First is crowding in the angular/phase space: if you uniformly shrink angles by a large factor, many distinct token indices map to very similar phases in several RoPE dimensions. When phases become similar, the model loses the ability to distinguish nearby positions; attention weights and positional offsets that the Transformer relied on become ambiguous. Second is disruption of high-salience contexts: if tokens that are frequently attended (often those at the beginning of a sequence) suddenly have very different encodings from what the model was trained on, the attention and feedforward computations that depended on those encodings break, leading to degraded performance.

Preserving "key" RoPE dimensions (those high-frequency components that separate nearby token indices) guards against angular crowding at short distances. The per-dimension search naturally finds small $\lambda_i$ for those dimensions, keeping their angular variation large enough to discriminate nearby tokens. Preserving initial token positions by leaving the first $\hat{n}$ positions un-rescaled avoids disrupting the representations of tokens that play outsized roles in attention, reducing destabilization of the model's internal computations.

Together, these two preservations minimize the informational loss that interpolation inevitably introduces while still enabling many more token positions to be represented within the model's original angular manifold. The empirical results in the paper (reduced perplexity for long-context evaluation, robust 8× non-fine-tuned extensions, and improved fine-tuning initialization) validate that this targeted conservation is more effective than uniform or coarse-grained schemes.

# Summary

The empirical analysis in the paper reveals two orthogonal non-uniformities that matter for extending RoPE-based LLMs: (1) per-dimension sensitivity (different RoPE dimensions should be rescaled differently), and (2) positional sensitivity (the first $\hat{n}$ token positions often benefit from being left unchanged). Searching for per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ together with a small preserved prefix $\hat{n}$ yields lower perplexity on long-context language modeling tasks (PG19 and Proof-pile) than prior linear or hand-grouped interpolation methods. Practically, these non-uniform strategies enable substantial non-fine-tuned extensions (up to about 8×) and provide much better initialization when fine-tuning is applied for still larger extensions. These findings form the empirical backbone of LongRoPE’s progressive, search-driven approach to pushing LLM context windows to multi-million-token scales.

LongRoPE: Problem Formulation

# Context extension ratio s = $L'/L$ and unified RoPE representation

Transformer models do not have a built‑in notion of token order, so positional encodings are added so the model can distinguish token positions. LongRoPE builds on the widely used Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE). If a token is at position index $n$, the canonical RoPE encoding across the embedding's paired sine/cosine components can be written in the usual compact form as
\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\ \sin(n\theta_0),\ \cos(n\theta_1),\ \sin(n\theta_1),\ \ldots \right],
\]
where the per‑dimension rotary angles satisfy $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ for embedding dimension $d$ and a base constant $\theta$ (commonly $\theta=10000$ in many implementations). It is convenient to reparameterize the rotary angle for the $i$‑th (paired) component so that the angle is written as
\[
n\theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^i},
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\beta = \theta^{2/d}.
\]
This representation highlights that higher indexed dimensions correspond to progressively lower rotation frequencies (larger denominators).

When we talk about extending an LLM's context window, we compare the original (pretrained) maximum context length $L$ with a desired extended length $L'$. The context extension ratio is defined as
\[
s = \frac{L'}{L}.
\]
Intuitively, if we want tokens that previously occupied positions up to $L$ to map meaningfully into positions up to $L'$, we must adjust how position indices are converted into rotation angles. A common and simple approach is to uniformly scale down the effective position index (so that new positions map into the angular range the model was trained on). LongRoPE places such adjustments into a single, unified mathematical representation of RoPE rescaling that can express many prior methods as special cases and also enable more flexible, per‑dimension, per‑position schemes.

# Per‑dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and position threshold $\hat{n}$ for early tokens

To capture the two important forms of non‑uniformity the paper identifies, LongRoPE introduces two degrees of freedom:

1. A per‑dimension rescale factor $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE dimension index $i$. Each $\lambda_i$ controls how much the rotation angle for that particular frequency is reduced (interpolated) when mapping to the extended range. Larger $\lambda_i$ means stronger downscaling (more interpolation), while $\lambda_i=1$ means no scaling (extrapolation).

2. A position threshold $\hat{n}$ that designates a prefix of the input sequence whose rotary angles are left unchanged. The idea is that the first $\hat{n}$ tokens (the "early tokens") often receive strong attention and are thus especially important to preserve exactly; for these positions, the original pretrained RoPE should be used without interpolation.

Concretely, LongRoPE expresses the new rotary angle for token position $n$ and RoPE dimension $i$ as
\[
\text{angle}(n,i) \;=\; \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i},
\]
where the indicator/scaling function $\mathbb{I}$ depends on both the chosen per‑dimension rescale and the token position. The per‑dimension rescale values are parametrized by $\lambda_i$ (with $\hat{\lambda}_i=1/\lambda_i$ in the paper's notation), and the threshold $\hat{n}$ decides whether to apply the rescaling to a given token position. This formulation lets us treat each RoPE frequency independently, and also gives the option of protecting the earliest token positions from any rescaling.

# Indicator $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})$: original RoPE for $n<\hat{n}$; rescaled angles for $n\ge\hat{n}$

The indicator function in LongRoPE is defined simply and deterministically to combine the two non‑uniformities. For a chosen per‑dimension rescale $\lambda_i$ and a prefix length $\hat{n}$, the function is
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus, for positions in the preserved prefix ($n<\hat{n}$) the angle remains exactly $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$, i.e., the original pretrained RoPE. For positions beyond the prefix the angle becomes $\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i} = \frac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}$ — equivalently, the effective token index has been scaled down by $\lambda_i$. This scaling reduces the angular magnitude for large positions so that angles remain inside the range the model has been exposed to during pretraining.

Some important implementation and interpretive remarks follow:

- The per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ values are allowed to vary across $i$ so that high‑frequency (low index) dimensions can be scaled less (smaller $\lambda_i$) than low‑frequency (high index) dimensions. This respects the intuition that different RoPE frequencies encode positional information at different granularities; preserving high‑frequency components helps the model distinguish closely spaced tokens.

- The prefix threshold $\hat{n}$ is typically chosen much smaller than $L$; it is a way to preserve a small set of highly attended positions exactly. The optimal $\hat{n}$ is task‑ and extension‑dependent, and LongRoPE treats it as an explicit search parameter.

# Optimization target: minimize next‑token loss (perplexity) over long documents $|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'$

Having specified how the rotary angles are parameterized by the set of variables $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$, LongRoPE frames the design of those variables as a search/optimization problem. The ultimate goal is to choose rescale factors and the prefix threshold so that the resulting LLM with modified RoPE behaves well on long sequences. Concretely, given a corpus of long text samples $\mathbf{X}$ where each sample $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}$ has length at least $L'$, we want to minimize the standard next‑token prediction loss (cross‑entropy) or equivalently the perplexity of the language model on these long inputs.

If we denote the language model loss on a set of inputs when using a particular RoPE instantiation as $\mathcal{L}(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE},\mathbf{X}))$, then the optimization problem is expressed as
\[
\arg\min_{\{\lambda_i\},\,\hat{n}}\ \mathcal{L}\big(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n}),\mathbf{X})\big),
\]
subject to the RoPE mapping
\[
\text{RoPE}(n,i) = \left[\cdots,\ \cos\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),\ \sin\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),\ \cdots\right].
\]
This objective directly aligns the search for interpolation/rescaling parameters with the model's final metric of interest: how well the model predicts tokens when it must process sequences of length $L'$.

Because evaluating $\mathcal{L}$ requires running the LLM on long inputs and computing perplexity, LongRoPE treats selection of $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ as a black‑box search (the paper uses an evolutionary search) rather than attempting to derive closed‑form optimal settings. The search leverages empirical perplexity on held‑out long sequences as the fitness signal.

# How PI, NTK, and YaRN fit into the unified framework as special cases

One of the benefits of the unified rescaling formulation is that several previously proposed interpolation/extrapolation schemes appear as simple settings of the same parameters. Understanding this correspondence clarifies both the flexibility of LongRoPE and why more expressive, non‑uniform solutions can outperform earlier methods.

- Linear positional interpolation (PI). PI uniformly scales every rotary angle by the extension ratio $s$. In the LongRoPE parametrization, PI corresponds to choosing a single scalar $\lambda_i = s$ for every RoPE dimension $i$ and choosing $\hat{n}=0$ (so no prefix is preserved). Then for all positions
  \[
  \text{angle}(n,i) = \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i} = \frac{n}{s\beta^i},
  \]
  which is exactly linear downscaling of positional indices across all frequencies.

- NTK‑based interpolation / extrapolation. NTK‑inspired schemes distribute the interpolation pressure non‑uniformly across RoPE dimensions: lower‑index (higher frequency) dimensions are adjusted less, while higher‑index (lower frequency) dimensions are scaled more. In the unified notation, NTK corresponds to a monotone sequence of $\lambda_i$ such that $\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots$, often expressed in theories as a dimension‑dependent formula (e.g., $\lambda_i = s^{f(i)}$ for some increasing mapping $f$). Setting $\hat{n}=0$ yields a pure per‑dimension interpolation/extrapolation pattern. The NTK viewpoint gives the theoretical motive for per‑dimension monotonicity: preserving high frequencies reduces crowding of nearby positions.

- YaRN (three‑group strategy). YaRN partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency groups and applies different strategies per group: aggressive extrapolation (no scaling) for the highest frequencies, NTK‑style dimension‑adaptive scaling for the middle group, and linear interpolation (PI) for the lowest frequencies. In LongRoPE terms, YaRN picks piecewise constant or piecewise monotone values of $\lambda_i$ across ranges of $i$ (for example $\lambda_i=1$ for a high‑frequency block, $\lambda_i=s$ for a low‑frequency block, and intermediate $\lambda_i$ in the middle block), typically with $\hat{n}=0$. This shows YaRN is an instance of per‑dimension rescaling restricted to three groups.

Viewing these prior techniques as special cases highlights the core flexibility LongRoPE introduces: instead of committing to a single global scalar or a coarse grouping, LongRoPE allows an arbitrary monotone (or more general) per‑dimension profile $\{\lambda_i\}$ together with an optional early‑token protection $\hat{n}$. This richer family can preserve critical components of the pretrained RoPE while still mapping far‑away positions into a familiar angular range for the model, which the paper empirically shows leads to substantial improvements in perplexity and downstream retrieval/generation tasks when extending context windows to very large sizes.

In summary, the LongRoPE problem formulation unifies positional interpolation as a search over per‑dimension scale factors and an early‑token threshold, with the optimization objective of minimizing next‑token loss on long documents. This unified view both explains existing methods as constrained instances and provides the parameterization that LongRoPE searches over to find high‑quality, non‑uniform rescalings suitable for extremely large context extensions.

Searching Non-Uniform Rescale Factors

# Search space design: $\lambda_i \in [1.0,\,1.25\times s]$ with step $0.01$; $\hat{n}\in\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$

The search that LongRoPE performs is over two kinds of choices: a per-RoPE-dimension rescale $\lambda_i$ and a threshold $\hat{n}$ that controls how many initial token positions are left unscaled. Concretely, the algorithm searches the vector $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ together with a single integer $\hat{n}$. Each $\lambda_i$ controls how much the rotary angle for the $i$-th RoPE pair is scaled for positions $n\ge\hat{n}$; for $n<\hat{n}$ the original RoPE is kept. In the paper this is implemented by searching $\lambda_i$ values and setting the applied rescale factor to $\hat{\lambda}_i=1/\lambda_i$ in the RoPE formula.

To make the search concrete and finite, each $\lambda_i$ is constrained to a fixed interval and discretized. The interval used is
\[
\lambda_i \in \left[1.0,\;1.25\cdot s\right],
\]
where $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ is the extension ratio (extended length divided by original length). The interval lower bound $1.0$ corresponds to pure extrapolation (no interpolation, i.e., preserving original rotation frequencies), while the upper bound $1.25\cdot s$ allows slightly more aggressive interpolation than the simple linear positional interpolation (PI) which would use $\lambda=s$. The step size used in the search is $0.01$, so each $\lambda_i$ takes values on a regular discrete grid.

For the token-position threshold $\hat{n}$, LongRoPE restricts the candidates to the small set
\[
\hat{n}\in\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}.
\]
When $\hat{n}=0$ every token position uses the searched $\lambda_i$ scalings; for positive $\hat{n}$ the first $\hat{n}$ positions are left unmodified (i.e., original RoPE). This discrete set for $\hat{n}$ encodes the intuition that only a small number of starting positions typically need special treatment, and it keeps the search tractable.

This design produces a very large but structured search space: many degrees of freedom (one $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension) combined with a small set of $\hat{n}$ choices.

# Why the search space explodes with $s$ (example: $4\times$ leading to $\sim 4\times 10^{167}$ combinations)

It is important to see why an exhaustive search is infeasible. Each $\lambda_i$ can take roughly
\[
M(s) \approx \frac{1.25\cdot s - 1}{0.01} + 1
\]
distinct values given the interval and $0.01$ step. For example, when $s=4$ we have $1.25\cdot s = 5.0$, so the allowed values for each $\lambda_i$ are approximately the discrete set $\{1.00,1.01,\dots,5.00\}$, giving about $401$ values (the paper rounded to about $400$).

If the model has $d/2$ RoPE dimension pairs over which we search independently, and we also have $N_{\hat n}$ choices for $\hat n$ (here $N_{\hat n}=14$), the naive number of combinations is
\[
\text{combinations} \approx M(s)^{\,d/2}\times N_{\hat n}.
\]
Using the numbers in the paper as an illustrative example, for $s=4$ and $d/2=64$ the count becomes
\[
400^{64}\times 14 \approx 4\times 10^{167}.
\]
This astronomical figure illustrates why exhaustive enumeration is impossible: the number of candidate rescaling vectors grows exponentially in the number of per-dimension parameters and multiplicatively with the granularity induced by $s$. As $s$ increases, $M(s)$ grows linearly, and the exponential blow-up in $M(s)^{d/2}$ becomes rapidly overwhelming. This combinatorial explosion motivates the use of a guided search method rather than brute force.

# Evolutionary search pipeline: initialization, evaluation by perplexity, top-k selection, mutation, crossover

To explore this enormous space efficiently, LongRoPE frames the problem as a black-box optimization over discrete candidate solutions and adopts an evolutionary (genetic) search. The high-level loop of the evolutionary pipeline is:

1. Initialization. Create an initial population $\mathcal{P}_0$ of $P$ candidate solutions. Each candidate is a full specification of $\{\lambda_i\}$ and a choice of $\hat{n}$.

2. Evaluation. For each candidate in the population, apply its $\{\lambda_i,\hat{n}\}$ to the model's RoPE during inference and compute a scalar score: the perplexity (negative log-likelihood) on a small set of held-out long-document samples $\mathbf{X}$ whose lengths meet the target $L'$. Perplexity is the objective to minimize.

3. Selection. Keep the top-$k$ candidates (lowest perplexities) as parents for the next generation.

4. Variation. Generate new candidates via mutation and crossover operations applied to the selected parents. Mutations perturb $\lambda_i$ values and/or $\hat{n}$; crossover mixes parameters from two parents.

5. Constraint filtering. Reject any newly generated candidate that violates problem-specific constraints (see monotonicity below) before evaluation.

6. Next generation. The new population is formed from the selected parents plus the newly generated offspring. Repeat evaluation and selection for $\mathcal{T}$ iterations.

7. Termination. After $\mathcal{T}$ iterations return the candidate with the lowest perplexity observed.

This pipeline is summarized in Algorithm 1 of the paper. Two aspects deserve emphasis. First, the evaluation step uses real model inference and language-model perplexity as the fitness function; this directly measures how well a candidate preserves the model’s language modeling capability at the target length. Second, most of the computational cost lies in repeated inference for perplexity calculation, so everything that reduces the number of candidates requiring full evaluation (good initialization, cheap constraints) yields big speedups.

# Optimized initialization: seeding with PI, NTK, and YaRN solutions and mutating

A naive random initialization would waste many evaluation steps on very poor candidates. To accelerate convergence, the LongRoPE search seeds the initial population with known, sensible baselines: the positional interpolation solutions given by PI (linear interpolation), NTK-based scaling, and YaRN’s grouped strategy. Concretely, these three known rescale patterns are included as 3 individuals in the initial population $\mathcal{P}_0$. The remaining $P-3$ members are generated by mutating these three seeds with some mutation probability $p$.

This optimized seeding has two beneficial effects. First, it guarantees that the search starts with feasible, reasonably good solutions (the three seeded individuals are already much better than random). Second, by mutating around those baselines we bias the initial population towards regions of the search space that are more likely to contain useful non-uniform patterns. This both improves early-generation fitness and reduces the number of generations required to find high-quality candidates.

The mutation operation used at initialization (and later during evolution) is simple: randomly select a subset of dimensions and perturb each chosen $\lambda_i$ by a small discrete step (multiples of $0.01$) within the allowed interval, and possibly change $\hat{n}$ to one of its discrete options. Because seeds are near good solutions, these small local perturbations are effective at finding improvements that preserve desirable structure from the baselines.

# Monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$: theory motivation (higher frequencies less interpolation), practical speedups

A central structural constraint in the search is monotonicity across RoPE dimensions:
\[
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}.
\]
This enforces that the rescale factors are non-decreasing with the RoPE dimension index. The motivation for this constraint is twofold: theoretical and practical.

Theoretical motivation. RoPE’s rotary angles embed different frequency bands across dimensions: lower-index dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components and higher-index dimensions to lower-frequency components (the NTK and Fourier analyses underpin this view). High-frequency components are responsible for fine-grained positional discrimination and should therefore be subjected to less interpolation (i.e., smaller $\lambda$) to avoid collapsing distinguishability among nearby positions. Low-frequency components can be interpolated more aggressively because they encode coarser positional patterns. Imposing $\lambda_i$ non-decreasing aligns the searched scalings with this frequency-structured view.

Practical motivation and speedup. Enforcing monotonicity dramatically prunes the candidate space. Many randomly generated or mutated vectors would violate monotonicity; by filtering them out before evaluation the search avoids wasting expensive model inference on implausible candidates. In addition, the monotonicity constraint encourages smoother, well-behaved scalings that are easier to fine-tune during evolution (mutations will only need to tweak local slopes rather than explore arbitrary zig-zag patterns across dimensions). This makes the evolutionary operators more effective and stabilizes the fitness landscape.

Because only monotonic candidates are evaluated, the effective branching factor of the search drops substantially: rather than exploring every combination of independent per-dimension choices, the algorithm only considers non-decreasing sequences, which are a small subset of all possible vectors. This is the single most important pruning heuristic in practice.

# Hyperparameters: population size $P$, mutation/crossover sizes $N_1/N_2$, mutation prob $p$, iterations $\mathcal{T}$

The evolutionary search has a number of free hyperparameters that trade off exploration, exploitation, and compute cost. The paper uses two concrete configurations depending on the target window size.

For target windows within $256\text{k}$ the recommended settings are:
- population size: $P=64$,
- mutation offspring per generation: $N_1=16$,
- crossover offspring per generation: $N_2=16$,
- mutation probability: $p=0.3$,
- total iterations: $\mathcal{T}=40$,
- select top-$32$ candidates to form parents for mutation/crossover.

Perplexity evaluation in this regime is computed on 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set, each having at least the target length.

For windows beyond $512\text{k}$ (where each evaluation is more expensive due to longer sequences) the paper halves the population, mutation, and crossover sizes to reduce compute: i.e., $P\leftarrow P/2$, $N_1\leftarrow N_1/2$, $N_2\leftarrow N_2/2$ (the iteration count and selection strategy are similarly scaled to maintain reasonable search quality). In that regime, perplexity is measured on 3 random samples from Pile-Books3 validation because each sample is extremely long.

Choosing these hyperparameters balances two competing needs. A larger $P$ improves exploration and reduces the risk of premature convergence, but each generation requires $P$ full-model perplexity evaluations and can be very costly for long targets. A larger $\mathcal{T}$ allows more generations of refinement. The values used in the paper were found empirically to work well across LLaMA2 and Mistral experiments.

# Non-finetuned $8\times$ extension to 32k: why better initialization yields stable perplexity

One especially useful empirical finding is that LongRoPE’s search can produce non-uniform RoPE scalings that permit substantial context window extension without any fine-tuning. In particular, using the searched per-dimension $\lambda_i$ (and optimal $\hat{n}$) the paper demonstrates stable language-model perplexity when extending from 4k to 32k — an $8\times$ extension — with no further model training.

Why does this work? The answer lies in what the search preserves. Linear interpolation (PI) simply rescales all rotation angles uniformly by $s$, which “crowds” many different token positions into a narrow angular range and undermines the model’s ability to distinguish nearby tokens. NTK-style and YaRN-style methods mitigate this by spreading interpolation across dimensions, but they do so using fixed analytic rules or coarse grouping. LongRoPE’s per-dimension search discovers fine-grained, non-uniform scalings that keep the most important (high-frequency) RoPE dimensions close to their original scales while interpolating more in dimensions that can tolerate it.

Concretely, the best-found candidates often have small $\lambda_i$ (near $1.0$) for the early, high-frequency dimensions and larger $\lambda_i$ for later, low-frequency dimensions. In addition, optimal $\hat{n}$ values often protect a modest number of initial token positions from any interpolation at all, preserving the highly attended prefix behavior that many transformer layers rely on. These two forms of non-uniformity—per-dimension and per-position—work together to reduce the information loss caused by interpolation and therefore maintain low perplexity even without fine-tuning.

From the evolutionary-search perspective, initializing with PI/NTK/YaRN seeds and then locally mutating allows the optimizer to find these delicate trade-offs: it starts from sensible baselines and then refines where those baselines over- or under-interpolate. The monotonicity constraint ensures the refinements remain aligned with the frequency-based intuition. Together these elements explain why a guided, constrained evolution can produce an $8\times$ non-finetuned extension that is stable in practice.

# Summary and practical notes

Searching for non-uniform per-dimension and per-position RoPE rescale factors is an effective way to initialize models for very large context extensions. Because the discretized search space grows combinatorially with the extension ratio $s$ and the number of searched dimensions, LongRoPE combines three crucial design choices to make search practical and effective: (1) a restricted but expressive discretization $\lambda_i\in[1.0,1.25s]$ with step $0.01$ and a small set of $\hat{n}$ values, (2) an evolutionary search seeded by strong baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) and using mutation/crossover, and (3) a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ that is theoretically motivated and dramatically reduces wasted evaluations. The paper’s recommended hyperparameters (e.g., $P=64$, $N_1=N_2=16$, $p=0.3$, $\mathcal{T}=40$ for targets up to $256\text{k}$) provide a practical starting point; for larger targets the population and offspring counts are halved to control compute. The resulting searched scalings both improve non-finetuned extensions (up to $8\times$) and provide much better initializations for the modest fine-tuning used to reach extreme windows like $2048\text{k}$.

Progressive Extension to 2,048k

# Stage 1: Search for 128k and 256k on the pretrained model

The progressive extension procedure begins by searching for a high-quality, non-uniform RoPE rescaling on the original pretrained model for moderately large target lengths: first 128k, then 256k. The reason for working in these intermediate ranges is practical: directly searching or fine-tuning to a 2048k window introduces an enormous search and training burden, and the majority of the catastrophic positional artifacts appear when many previously unseen positions are introduced at once. By choosing 128k and 256k as intermediate targets we keep the per-step extension ratio manageable (e.g., 32× and 64× from a 4k base), which makes both the search and subsequent fine-tuning tractable.

Concretely, the search problem optimizes a per-RoPE-dimension rescale vector \(\{\lambda_i\}\) together with a token-threshold \(\hat{n}\) so that the modified RoPE
\[
\text{RoPE}(n) = \big[\ldots,\cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\,
\sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\ldots\big]
\]
minimizes next-token loss on samples of length \(L'\) (here \(L'\) is 128k or 256k). The indicator
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})=\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat{n},\\
1/\lambda_i & n\ge\hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
encodes the two non-uniformities discovered in the paper: (i) different RoPE dimensions can use different rescale factors \(\lambda_i\), and (ii) the first \(\hat{n}\) token positions may be left un-rescaled (i.e., extrapolated less) because they often receive large attention and are more sensitive to interpolation.

Because the space of possible \(\{\lambda_i\}\) is enormous, LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search with several practical design choices. The per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) are searched in a grid from \(1.0\) (no interpolation/extrapolation) up to \(s\times 1.25\) (where \(s=L'/L\) is the extension ratio), with a step of 0.01; \(\hat{n}\) is chosen from a small set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\). The search is seeded with known solutions (PI, NTK, YaRN) in the initial population so the evolutionary process starts from sensible baselines. To reduce wasted evaluations the method enforces a monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\), motivated by NTK reasoning that lower-frequency (higher-index) RoPE dimensions can tolerate more interpolation while higher-frequency (lower-index) dimensions should be changed less.

Empirically this targeted search on the pretrained model yields rescale factors that preserve important RoPE dimensions and the most-attended early positions, producing substantially lower perplexity at 128k and 256k than naive linear PI or prior group-wise heuristics. Importantly, the paper shows that these searched, non-uniform RoPE settings already enable an 8× extension in non-fine-tuning settings, which is why 128k/256k targets are suitable stepping stones on the path to 2,048k.

# Stage 2: Fine-tune to 256k efficiently (e.g., 400 steps at 128k RoPE, then switch RoPE and 600 steps at 256k)

After obtaining good non-uniform rescale factors for 128k and 256k from Stage 1, LongRoPE proceeds to fine-tune the model in a short, efficient manner. The guiding principle is to make the amount of fine-tuning minimal while still adapting the model weights to the newly exposed positional regimes. Instead of attempting to fine-tune directly at 256k for many epochs (which is costly and difficult because very-long training segments are scarce), the paper uses a two-phase fine-tuning recipe:

1. Fine-tune for 400 steps with the RoPE rescale factors targeted at 128k, using long segments (e.g., RedPajama chunks of length 128k for LLaMA2 in the experiments). This first phase adapts the model to a large but not extreme increase in context length and stabilizes the weights while operating under rescale factors that remain conservative compared to the final 256k target.

2. Replace the RoPE rescale factors with those searched for 256k, and then fine-tune a further 600 steps. This second, shorter adaptation step raises the context capacity to 256k while leveraging the already adjusted weights from the first phase.

For LLaMA2-7B the paper uses a learning rate of \(2\!\times\!10^{-5}\) with linear decay and global batch size 32. The two-step fine-tuning (400 then 600 steps) therefore totals about 1k fine-tuning updates while operating at training lengths within 256k, which is computationally feasible compared with direct training at multi-million token lengths. The empirical result is a model that behaves well at 256k: because the search provided a rescale initialization that minimizes the interpolation-induced information loss, the model converges quickly and robustly in these relatively few steps.

The rationale for the two-stage fine-tuning is both practical and empirical. By first adapting at a smaller intermediate rescale, optimization instability caused by exposing the model to a very different positional regime is reduced. The model needs fewer gradient steps to accommodate the larger positional range when starting from a checkpoint already adapted to 128k rescaling, compared to starting from the untouched pretrained weights and trying to learn a direct 256k positional mapping.

# Stage 3: Second search on the 256k-finetuned model to reach 2,048k without further fine-tuning

Once the model has been fine-tuned to operate reliably at 256k with the searched RoPE initialization, LongRoPE performs a second evolutionary search—but now on the fine-tuned 256k model—to find rescale factors that enable much larger extensions without additional weight updates. The key empirical observation exploited here is that the non-uniform RoPE found for an already fine-tuned extended model can support another substantial non-finetuning extension: in the paper, the second search extends the 256k-finetuned model to 2,048k (an additional 8×), resulting in an overall extension from 4k to 2,048k of \(512\times\).

Mechanically, this second search has the same structure as the first search: it optimizes per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) under the same monotonicity constraint and similar search ranges, but it evaluates perplexity on very long samples drawn from Books3 or Pile-Books3 validation splits to ensure the RoPE rescaling works across multi-million-token contexts. Because the underlying weights were already trained to handle much longer contexts (256k) and the new rescale factors are chosen to preserve critical low-dimension (high-frequency) rotation angles and early token encodings, the resulting 2,048k-capable model achieves very large effective context with no extra gradient updates beyond those used to reach 256k.

This staged sequence—(search on pretrained → light fine-tuning to 256k → search on fine-tuned model)—is the core progressive strategy that enables a 512× overall extension while keeping heavy fine-tuning limited to a manageable training length (256k) and step count (approximately 1k updates in the LLaMA2 example).

# Short-context recovery: re-search RoPE for 4k–8k to mitigate crowding and preserve original performance

A known side-effect of aggressive positional interpolation is “crowding” of positions inside the original short context window: when rescale factors compress large new positions into the pretrained angular range, the rotation angles used by tokens within the original range become more tightly packed, which can degrade the model’s original short-context performance. This is especially noticeable when the overall extension ratio is large (e.g., \(512\times\) from 4k to 2,048k).

To address this, LongRoPE performs a short-context recovery step: after the model is extended to 2,048k, the paper runs additional evolutionary searches that specifically target short evaluation contexts such as 4k and 8k. In these recovery searches the maximum allowable \(\lambda_i\) is deliberately reduced (i.e., the search is constrained to encourage less interpolation), and the objective is to find rescale factors that preserve performance in the short regime. The search still uses the monotonicity constraint and the same per-dimension parametrization, but with a tighter range for \(\lambda\) so that the rescaling applied when the runtime sequence length is small does not over-compress the original RoPE geometry.

Empirically, applying these short-length RoPE variants at inference recovers much of the original perplexity and benchmark performance on tasks that operate within the original context window. This recovery is done without changing model weights: only the per-dimension rescale factors used to compute RoPE are swapped in at inference time when short sequences are processed.

# Dynamic RoPE selection at inference based on sequence length

All of the preceding work—searches for multiple target lengths (e.g., 4k/8k, 128k, 256k, 2048k) and the monotonicity-aware rescale vectors—becomes operational at inference through dynamic RoPE selection. The paper’s practical inference policy is simple: at runtime, the model selects the RoPE rescale factors appropriate for the current input sequence length. If the incoming sequence length \(N\) is small (for example \(N<8\)k), the model uses the rescale factors obtained from the short-context recovery search; if \(N\) is in an intermediate range (e.g., between 8k and 256k) it uses the rescale vector searched for the corresponding target; and for very long sequences (up to 2048k) it applies the rescale factors found in the second search on the 256k-finetuned model.

Because the modifications are limited to the positional computation, the core model architecture and most optimizations remain unchanged. Switching RoPE parameters is cheap: it simply changes the rotation angles used to form the complex-valued or interleaved cosine–sine positional multipliers in attention. This per-input adaptation ensures the model is not forced to use the same compressed positional geometry for both very long and short sequences, thereby preserving the original short-context capabilities while enabling million-token inputs when needed.

# Summary of the progressive pipeline and its benefits

The Progressive Extension to 2,048k is a practical and empirically validated recipe that combines a search for fine-grained, non-uniform RoPE rescale factors with a short, staged fine-tuning schedule and inference-time RoPE selection:

- Stage 1: Use evolutionary search on the pretrained model to find non-uniform per-dimension rescale factors for 128k and 256k targets, including the token-threshold \(\hat{n}\) that leaves initial tokens less interpolated.
- Stage 2: Fine-tune for a small number of steps (the paper uses 400 steps at the 128k RoPE and then 600 steps after switching to the 256k RoPE for LLaMA2) to adapt model weights at manageable training lengths.
- Stage 3: Run a second evolutionary search on the 256k-finetuned model to produce rescale factors that extend usefully to 2048k without further fine-tuning, producing an overall extension ratio of \(512\times\).
- Recovery: Re-search RoPE targeted at short contexts (4k–8k) with reduced maximum \(\lambda\) to recover and preserve original short-context performance.
- Inference: Dynamically select the appropriate RoPE rescale set based on the current sequence length.

This progressive strategy addresses the three main practical obstacles highlighted in the paper—catastrophic new position values, scarcity/expense of extremely long training texts, and degradation at short contexts—by (a) providing a non-uniform, information-preserving positional initialization, (b) concentrating fine-tuning at feasible lengths and with few steps (roughly 1k updates in the LLaMA2 example), and (c) using dynamic RoPE selection to avoid crowding short-range positional encodings. The result reported in the paper is an LLM extended to 2,048k tokens while maintaining strong short-context behavior and requiring only modest fine-tuning resources.

Experimental Setup and Baselines

# Models
The experiments in the LongRoPE study focus on two modern 7-billion-parameter decoder-only language models that use RoPE positional embeddings: LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B. Both models are treated as pre-trained backbones whose positional encoding is modified during search and (in some cases) fine-tuning to extend their context windows to very large sizes (up to 2,048k tokens). All modifications in LongRoPE keep the original Transformer architecture and weights intact except for the rescaling of RoPE rotary angles; this allows reuse of existing model codepaths and optimizations.

The comparisons reported in the paper evaluate both LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B after applying LongRoPE (with different fine-tuning choices) and compare them against multiple positional-interpolation and fine-tuned baselines. In subsequent sections I summarize the datasets, evaluation tasks, the exact fine-tuning and search configurations used for these two models, and the baselines used for comparison.

# Long-sequence language modeling datasets and sliding-window evaluation
LongRoPE is primarily evaluated by measuring language modeling quality (perplexity) when models consume very long contexts. The paper uses three corpora for long-sequence evaluation:

- PG19: the PG19 test split (100 documents) is used for language-model perplexity evaluation. For search-time evaluation, small subsets of PG19 validation examples are also used (details below). For long-context evaluation up to 256k, the full PG19 test split of 100 documents is used.

- Proof-pile: for very long examples, the experiments follow YaRN and select a small set of long sequences. Specifically, they randomly select 10 Proof-pile samples each with length at least 128k tokens for evaluation in the 128k–256k regime.

- Books3 (from the Pile): to evaluate behavior beyond one million tokens, the paper samples long books from Books3. For the 2,048k evaluation, they randomly select 20 books each exceeding 2,048k tokens and run sliding-window evaluation.

Sliding-window evaluation protocols
- Within the 256k evaluation regime (for PG19 and Proof-pile) the paper computes perplexity by moving a sliding window over each long document with a stride or evaluation window of 256 tokens. This means the model is asked to predict tokens in sliding windows across the document; aggregating cross-entropy over those windows yields a perplexity curve as a function of evaluation context length.

- For the Books3 (multi-million-token) evaluation the paper uses a much larger evaluation stride: a sliding window of 256k tokens is used to move across each book. This reduces computational cost while still measuring modeling quality on substantially long text spans (up to and beyond 2,048k).

These sliding-window regimes are chosen to measure how well LLMs make next-token predictions when allowed to attend to longer and longer prefixes of a document and to produce comparable perplexity curves across different context sizes.

# Passkey retrieval task
The passkey retrieval task is a synthetic, controlled evaluation that measures whether a model can retrieve a short secret placed somewhere inside a very long document. The setup used in the paper is as follows:

- A five-digit numeric passkey is randomly chosen for each trial and inserted into a large “sea” of irrelevant text at a single position.

- For each trial, the location of the passkey is sampled uniformly at random across the evaluation context length (so the passkey may appear very early, very late, or anywhere in between).

- Each experimental point reports the retrieval accuracy over 10 independent iterations (trials). In each iteration the prompt + document are constructed with the passkey at a different random location, and the model is asked (via generation prompt) to produce the passkey.

- The reported metric is simple accuracy: the fraction of trials in which the model successfully outputs the exact five-digit passkey.

This task is designed to stress whether the model can attend to and retrieve a very small piece of information placed anywhere within a long context. It is particularly sensitive to whether positional interpolation and fine-tuning preserve the model’s ability to locate and use specific content across long spans.

# Standard short-context benchmarks
To ensure that extending the context window does not unduly harm performance within the original, shorter context sizes, LongRoPE models are also evaluated on a set of standard LLM benchmarks (the paper uses datasets from the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard). The benchmarks and shot settings used were:

- ARC-Challenge: 25-shot evaluation.
- HellaSwag: 10-shot evaluation.
- MMLU: 5-shot evaluation.
- TruthfulQA: 0-shot evaluation.

These tasks measure reasoning, commonsense, multi-domain knowledge, and truthfulness in the original (shorter) context regime. Comparing extended models on these benchmarks verifies whether LongRoPE preserves—or degrades—original capabilities designed for the native context length.

# Fine-tuning details
LongRoPE relies on a progressive fine-tuning strategy to reach extremely large target contexts without training directly at those lengths. The fine-tuning configurations differ slightly between LLaMA2 and Mistral:

LLaMA2-7B fine-tuning
- Optimizer and schedule: a learning rate of \(2\mathrm{e}{-5}\) with linear decay is used.
- Batch size: global batch size of 32.
- Data and chunking: RedPajama data is chunked into \(128\mathrm{k}\) segments; sequences are bookended with BOS and EOS tokens.
- Fine-tuning steps and progression: first fine-tune for 400 steps using the RoPE rescale factors produced for a \(128\mathrm{k}\) target length. After that checkpoint is obtained, replace the RoPE rescale factors with those computed for a \(256\mathrm{k}\) target and fine-tune for an additional 600 steps. This two-stage fine-tuning (400 + 600 steps) is more efficient and more stable than a single direct run to \(256\mathrm{k}\).
- Hardware: training the \(128\mathrm{k}\) stage used 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs; the \(256\mathrm{k}\) stage used 16 A100 GPUs.

Mistral-7B fine-tuning
- Optimizer and schedule: a constant learning rate of \(1\mathrm{e}{-6}\) is used.
- Batch size: global batch size of 64.
- Data and sequence length: the experiments followed YaRN’s setup and used Together Computer’s Long-Data Collections with a training sequence length of \(16\mathrm{k}\).
- Fine-tuning steps: 400 steps per extended-length model (for the reported 128k and 256k variants).
- Hardware: 4 A100 GPUs were used for Mistral fine-tuning.

Notes on the progressive approach
- The motivation for the staged (128k then 256k) fine-tuning is practical: extremely long direct fine-tuning (e.g., directly to 2048k) would require scarce training data at that length and prohibitively high GPU/time costs. LongRoPE therefore uses a progressive approach where a pre-trained LLM is first extended (via search) to 128k–256k, fine-tuned at those feasible lengths, and then further extended (via a second search) to 2,048k without further expensive fine-tuning.

# Search settings and evaluation during search
LongRoPE’s core is an evolution-based search that finds per-dimension RoPE rescaling factors \(\lambda_i\) and a token-threshold \(\hat{n}\) that together implement the two non-uniformities (across RoPE dimensions and across beginning token positions). The search details used in the experiments are:

Search space ranges
- Each per-dimension scale \(\lambda_i\) is searched in the range \([1.0,\; s\times 1.25]\) with step size \(0.01\), where \(s=L'/L\) is the target extension ratio. A value \(\lambda_i=1.0\) corresponds to direct extrapolation (no downscaling), while larger \(\lambda\) values correspond to stronger interpolation.
- The initial-token threshold \(\hat{n}\) (the number of leading positions left un-rescaled) is searched over the discrete set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\). When \(\hat{n}=0\) all positions are rescaled; nonzero \(\hat{n}\) means the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens keep their original (pretrained) RoPE angles.

Evolutionary algorithm specifics (for target windows up to \(256\mathrm{k}\))
- Population size: \(P=64\).
- Mutation size and crossover size: \(N_1=N_2=16\).
- Mutation probability for initializing additional individuals: \(p=0.3\).
- Iterations (generations): \(\mathcal{T}=40\).
- Parent selection for reproduction: top-32 individuals are selected each generation to produce mutation/crossover children.
- Initialization: instead of fully random initialization the initial population explicitly includes the three prior interpolation schemes (PI, NTK, YaRN) as seed individuals; the remaining \(P-3\) are random mutations of those seeds.

Monotonicity constraint
- To reduce the search space and bias toward reasonable solutions, the algorithm enforces a monotonic non-decreasing constraint on the \(\lambda_i\) across RoPE dimensions: \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\). This reflects the NTK-inspired intuition that lower RoPE dimensions (higher frequencies) should be interpolated less than higher dimensions (lower frequencies).

Perplexity-based fitness evaluation during search
- For target windows within \(256\mathrm{k}\), perplexity for each candidate RoPE is computed using 5 randomly sampled PG19 validation sequences that satisfy the minimum length requirement. The average perplexity over these validation samples is used as the fitness to drive evolution.
- For targets larger than \(512\mathrm{k}\), the algorithm reduces computation by halving population and mutation/crossover sizes, and uses 3 random validation samples from the Pile-Books3 dataset for perplexity evaluation (these samples are chosen to be long enough for the larger targets).

Reduced search budgets beyond \(512\mathrm{k}\)
- Because evaluating very large context lengths is expensive, the search hyperparameters are scaled down for very large targets: population, mutation, and crossover sizes are halved for search runs with target windows beyond \(512\mathrm{k}\).

Other practical search choices
- The initial population design, monotonicity constraint, and seeding with PI/NTK/YaRN all accelerate convergence and reduce the number of expensive model-perplexity evaluations required.
- The final selected individual is the candidate with the lowest validation perplexity found in the Top-k archive across generations.

# Baselines
LongRoPE is compared against several positional interpolation methods and models that have been previously extended to larger contexts. These baselines cover both interpolation-only changes (no fine-tuning) and fine-tuned extended models:

Positional interpolation baselines (methods applied to RoPE)
- PI (Linear Positional Interpolation): uniformly downscales rotation angles by the extension ratio \(s\).
- NTK-aware interpolation (and dynamic NTK variants): dimension-aware rescaling inspired by Neural Tangent Kernel analysis that reduces interpolation in lower-frequency dimensions.
- YaRN: a grouping-based method that partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands and applies different interpolation/extrapolation rules per group.

Published or open-source extended models and fine-tuned baselines
- Together-32k: an open model variant extended to 32k context.
- Code LLaMA: an extended variant used for code modeling (included for comparison on longer-context behavior when available).
- LongLoRA-full-FT-100k: an extended model obtained by the LongLoRA fine-tuning procedure up to a 100k context length.
- YaRN-LLaMA and YaRN-Mistral: models fine-tuned after YaRN-style positional interpolation for LLaMA and Mistral, respectively.

The paper compares LongRoPE variants (differing by whether fine-tuning was performed at 128k or 256k) to these baselines across perplexity curves, passkey retrieval, and short-context benchmark accuracy.

# Evaluation metrics and experimental sample sizes
For clarity, here is how metrics are computed in each evaluation:

- Perplexity (language modeling): aggregated over sliding-window predictions across each test document. For 256k-regime experiments the sliding step was 256 tokens; for Books3 multi-million token evaluation the sliding step was 256k tokens. Perplexity curves are reported across a range of context lengths to show how model performance changes as the allowed attention span grows.

- Passkey retrieval accuracy: fraction correct across 10 independent trials, with the passkey placed uniformly at random across the evaluation length for each trial.

- Standard benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA): reported in the conventional way for each task under the shot settings listed earlier. These metrics are used to check whether extension affects original short-context capabilities.

- Search validation sizes: during search, fitness (perplexity) is measured on small random validation sets: 5 PG19 samples for targets \(\le 256\mathrm{k}\), and 3 Books3 samples for targets \(>512\mathrm{k}\). Evaluation test sizes for final reported results are larger (e.g., 100 PG19 test documents, 10 Proof-pile long samples, 20 Books3 books).

# Putting it together: experimental pipeline and rationale
The empirical protocol used in the paper follows this overall pipeline:

1. For a desired intermediate target (e.g., \(128\mathrm{k}\) or \(256\mathrm{k}\)), run the evolution-based search to find a per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) profile and a \(\hat{n}\) value that minimize validation perplexity (the search uses the settings described above).

2. Option A (no fine-tuning extension): apply the discovered rescaling directly at inference time — this is what enables modest extensions (in the paper up to an \(8\times\) extension) without fine-tuning.

   Option B (progressive fine-tuning to larger windows): fine-tune the model at a feasible intermediate target (\(128\mathrm{k}\) then \(256\mathrm{k}\)) using the corresponding discovered RoPE rescalings and the step counts and hardware setup described above.

3. After obtaining a fine-tuned \(256\mathrm{k}\) model, perform a secondary search (using the same evolutionary search but with appropriately scaled-down budgets for very large targets) on the fine-tuned model to discover rescalings that extend the model to the final target of \(2,048\mathrm{k}\). This second search is applied without further expensive fine-tuning.

4. Finally, to reduce short-context degradation caused by extreme interpolation, run an additional short-length search on the extended model (restricting the maximum allowed \(\lambda\) during search) to recover better performance at 4k–8k; the model then dynamically uses the appropriate rescaling at inference depending on sequence length.

This combination of careful per-dimension search, seeding and monotonic constraints, staged fine-tuning, and final recovery search is the experimental strategy used to report LongRoPE’s results across perplexity, passkey retrieval, and standard benchmarks.

# Summary
The experiments in the LongRoPE paper intentionally balance practicality (search budgets, limited fine-tuning steps, and available long-text data) with thorough evaluation across multiple axes. The two model families (LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B) were extended using evolution-discovered per-dimension RoPE rescalings, fine-tuned at feasible intermediate lengths (400 + 600 steps for LLaMA2; 400 steps for Mistral), and then evaluated with sliding-window perplexity, a targeted passkey retrieval task, and several short-context LLM benchmarks. The search and evaluation regimes are tuned to keep the most expensive steps tractable (reduced population and sample sizes beyond \(512\mathrm{k}\), large sliding-window strides for multi-million-token books) while still providing meaningful comparisons to interpolation baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) and prior extended models (Together-32k, Code LLaMA, LongLoRA-full-FT-100k, YaRN-LLaMA/Mistral).

Results: From 4k to 2,048k

# Within 256k: decreasing perplexity with longer contexts; outperforming baselines despite much longer windows

One of the central empirical claims of LongRoPE is that extending an LLM's context window need not degrade language modeling performance at intermediate long lengths; instead, with the right positional interpolation and modest fine-tuning, the model can continue to benefit from additional context. This was evaluated by measuring perplexity across a range of evaluation lengths up to 256k using long-text datasets (PG19 and Proof-pile). Perplexity was computed with a sliding evaluation window of size 256 tokens, which isolates the model's next-token predictive performance as the model is exposed to progressively larger histories.

Across both LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B, models extended with LongRoPE show an overall decreasing perplexity trend as evaluation length increases from the pretraining baseline (4k) up to 256k. A decreasing perplexity as more context is available indicates that the model can make better next-token predictions when given access to longer histories — i.e., it is effectively leveraging the extra context. Crucially, LongRoPE-extended models outperform state-of-the-art baselines (models extended by PI, NTK-based strategies, and YaRN, as well as several released long-context fine-tuned models) across these lengths, despite operating with much larger nominal windows. This demonstrates that the non-uniform, empirically searched rescale factors preserve essential positional information and provide a superior initialization for fine-tuning than uniform or hand-crafted interpolation schemes.

Two practical points underlie these gains. First, LongRoPE's search prioritizes preserving dimensions and early token positions that are most information-bearing, reducing the information loss that simple linear interpolation (PI) or coarse grouped strategies (YaRN) can introduce. Second, the progressive fine-tuning strategy (fine-tune at 128k then 256k, using only a small number of steps relative to training from scratch) makes it feasible to reach large windows while still preserving short- and mid-range performance. The combination produces models that are not just capable of reaching a larger nominal context size but can actually utilize it in language modeling tasks up to 256k.

# Beyond 2M: stable perplexity on Books3 with sliding 256k window; differences between LLaMA2 and Mistral

LongRoPE's most ambitious result is extending models to a 2,048k (2.048 million) context window. To evaluate behavior at such extreme lengths, the authors measured perplexity on Books3 using a sliding evaluation window of 256k tokens across long books (20 randomly selected books each exceeding 2,048k tokens). This evaluation isolates how well the models handle very long-range dependencies in real long-form text.

For LLaMA2-7B extended with LongRoPE (with the progressive 128k/256k fine-tuning schedule and a second non-uniform interpolation applied post-finetuning), perplexity remains stable and generally improves as more context within the 256k sliding windows is provided, with only marginal increases observed around the very largest evaluation points (1024k and 2048k). This behavior indicates that LLaMA2 with LongRoPE is successfully exploiting long-range context over multi-hundred-thousand-token spans, and that the secondary non-uniform interpolation preserves sufficient positional discriminability even when the model's nominal window is expanded by hundreds of times.

Mistral-7B extended with LongRoPE shows a different profile. While Mistral achieves strong perplexity at shorter extended lengths (up to a few hundred thousand tokens) — outperforming baselines at shorter windows — its perplexity increases more notably beyond 256k, and in the Books3 runs it exceeded a perplexity of 7 for very long windows. The paper attributes this difference partly to differences in fine-tuning setup: for Mistral the authors followed YaRN's training configuration using 16k training lengths when fine-tuning at 128k and 256k, which can limit how well the model adapts to subsequent, larger secondary extensions. Operationally this means that although LongRoPE can produce a working 2,048k nominal window for both LLaMA2 and Mistral, the realized long-range modeling quality depends on the base model and fine-tuning regime; LLaMA2 benefited more from the 256k fine-tuning schedule in the paper, whereas Mistral performed better when fine-tuned at 128k under the chosen training setup.

# Passkey retrieval: ≥90% accuracy for LLaMA2 up to 2,048k; Mistral strong up to ~1,800k

Perplexity measures next-token predictive quality, but generation tasks reveal different practical constraints of long-context models. The paper uses a passkey retrieval synthetic task to assess whether the model can find a small target (a random five-digit passkey) buried somewhere in a very long document. This task is a strong test of the model’s effective context window in a retrieval-like, generation-oriented setting: success requires locating and producing the exact target token sequence despite massive distractors.

LongRoPE-extended LLaMA2-7B (fine-tuned to 256k as described) maintains high retrieval accuracy — at or above 90% — across evaluation context lengths from 4k up to 2,048k. Maintaining such a high retrieval rate over a million-level context window is striking: it shows that LongRoPE preserves not only statistical prediction performance (perplexity) but also the model’s ability to synthesize and retrieve a precise item from an extremely large context. For Mistral-7B, LongRoPE-extended models achieve perfect (100%) retrieval up to roughly 1,800k, but accuracy drops to about 60% at 2,048k. This aligns with the earlier perplexity observations: Mistral's long-window modeling degrades more at the very largest windows under the used fine-tuning regimen, and that degradation undermines retrieval performance at the extreme endpoint.

The passkey results emphasize two insights. First, a working nominal context window must be judged by the model's practical ability to find and use relevant tokens distributed anywhere in that window, not merely by a single-measure perplexity. Second, the non-uniform rescaling plus progressive fine-tuning that LongRoPE employs enables retrieval robustness far beyond what earlier interpolation-only methods accomplished (where accuracy collapsed beyond 128k in the experiments).

# Short-context benchmarks: comparable performance; slight drops mitigated by short-context recovery

An important concern when massively extending context is preserving original competence on the shorter windows for which the models were trained. Positional interpolation that aggressively compresses original positions can crowd embeddings for tokens within the original window (e.g., 4k), causing degraded performance on standard benchmarks. The paper addresses this both empirically and methodologically.

Empirically, LongRoPE-2048k models achieve comparable results to their unmodified counterparts on standard short-context benchmarks evaluated in the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard suite: ARC-Challenge (25-shot), HellaSwag (10-shot), MMLU (5-shot), and TruthfulQA (0-shot). In particular, LongRoPE models match or closely track baseline performance and even slightly improve on some tasks (for example, outperforming the original Mistral on TruthfulQA by +0.5% in the reported runs). LLaMA2-2048k models fine-tuned at 256k show modest degradation on some benchmarks compared to the base model, but the drops are small and within reasonable ranges.

To mitigate those modest short-length degradations, LongRoPE performs an additional targeted search to “recover” short-context performance. Concretely, after producing a 2,048k extended model the authors run the evolutionary search again with a reduced maximum allowed rescale factor (encouraging less interpolation) targeting short lengths like 4k and 8k. During inference the model dynamically selects the appropriate RoPE rescale factors: if the sequence length is below a configured short threshold (e.g., 8k), it uses the short-length rescale factors; otherwise it uses the long-length rescale factors. This recovery procedure substantially reduces perplexity at short lengths and restores competitive benchmark performance while retaining the long-window capabilities.

The result demonstrates that long-window modifications need not be a zero-sum tradeoff with short-window behavior; by searching independently for rescale strategies appropriate to short and long regimes and switching at inference time, one can retain strong performance across regimes.

# Effect of fine-tuning length (128k vs. 256k) on secondary extension quality

A core practical insight of LongRoPE is that aggressively large final extension ratios are best achieved via a progressive approach: search and extend non-uniformly to an intermediate length (e.g., 128k or 256k), fine-tune at that length (but only for a small number of steps), and then apply a second non-uniform interpolation (search) to reach the final massive window (2,048k). The choice of intermediate fine-tuning length affects the difficulty of the subsequent secondary extension and the final model quality.

For LLaMA2 in the paper, fine-tuning at 256k produced a better final 2,048k model than fine-tuning at 128k. The reason given is that the secondary extension ratio (the factor by which the fine-tuned model is further extended) is smaller when starting from 256k: extending from 256k to 2,048k is an $8\times$ secondary step, whereas extending from 128k would require a $16\times$ secondary extension. Smaller secondary jumps are empirically easier to manage with non-uniform interpolation and produce lower perplexity and stronger downstream behavior.

For Mistral-7B the pattern is different: the paper reports that Mistral performed better in the final extended regime when fine-tuned at 128k rather than 256k under the authors’ experimental setup. The explanation offered is that their fine-tuning configuration for Mistral (following YaRN’s setup) used 16k training sequence lengths for both 128k and 256k fine-tuning runs, and this affected Mistral’s ability to adapt to later larger secondary extensions. Thus, the optimal intermediate fine-tuning length interacts with practical choices in the fine-tuning pipeline: training sequence length, number of steps, and hardware limits. The general lesson is that a smaller secondary extension ratio (achieved by choosing a larger intermediate fine-tune length) tends to be beneficial, but training details and model-specific behavior can influence the best choice.

# Synthesis and practical implications

Taken together, the experimental results establish several actionable conclusions for practitioners aiming to extend LLM context windows:

1. Non-uniform positional interpolation matters. Searching for dimension-wise and token-position-wise rescale factors (rather than applying a single global scale or coarse groups) yields substantial perplexity and retrieval gains across large intermediate and extreme context lengths.

2. A progressive extension strategy is effective and efficient. Fine-tuning at an intermediate large length (e.g., 128k or 256k) for a modest number of steps, followed by a secondary non-uniform interpolation, enables reaching a 2,048k nominal window without fine-tuning directly at million-scale lengths.

3. Preserve short-window performance with targeted searches. Because interpolating for megascale windows can harm performance in the original short regime, running a separate search limited to smaller rescale factors and dynamically switching RoPE factors by input length at inference time recovers short-context competence.

4. Model- and training-sensitive choices matter. The best intermediate fine-tuning length and exact gains depend on the base model (LLaMA2 vs. Mistral) and on the fine-tuning regimen (training sequence length, number of steps). In practice, experimenting with $128\text{k}$ vs. $256\text{k}$ intermediate fine-tuning and with the training chunk length is important to reach the strongest final performance.

Overall, the empirical story in the paper is clear: with careful, searched non-uniform interpolation, a modest amount of targeted fine-tuning, and an inference-time mechanism to recover short-length behavior, pre-trained RoPE-based LLMs can be extended from their original 4k windows to working 2,048k windows that demonstrably improve long-context perplexity and retrieval, while preserving short-context competence.

Ablations and Analysis

# Second interpolation after fine-tuning vs. PI/YaRN: stability of perplexity under further scaling

One of the central design choices in LongRoPE is to perform a second, non-uniform positional interpolation on a model that has already been fine-tuned to an intermediate extended length (e.g., fine-tuned to 128k or 256k) rather than attempting a single giant jump from the original length to the final target (2048k). Intuitively, fine-tuning at an intermediate scale lets the model adapt its internal representations to a moderate amount of new positions; applying a second carefully searched interpolation leverages that adaptation while avoiding the severe distributional shift that would occur if we abruptly exposed the model to the full target range without intermediate training.

Empirically, the ablation reported in the paper compares the result of extending a fine-tuned 256k LLaMA2 model further to 512k, 1024k and 2048k using three methods: Plain linear positional interpolation (PI), YaRN’s group-wise non-uniform scheme, and LongRoPE’s searched non-uniform rescale factors applied as a second interpolation step. The observed behavior is that PI and YaRN produce rapidly increasing perplexities as the secondary extension ratio increases, while LongRoPE’s searched rescale factors keep perplexity at a much more stable and consistent level across the further scalings. This result highlights two points. First, naive linear interpolation (PI) crowds many new positions into a compressed angular range and therefore breaks the model’s positional discrimination when the extension ratio gets large. Second, even existing non-uniform schemes such as YaRN, which partition dimensions into groups, are not flexible enough to capture the fine-grained rescaling pattern that best preserves model behavior after fine-tuning. The second interpolation with LongRoPE, instead, uses a dimension-wise and position-aware rescaling that is tailored to the already fine-tuned model’s state; this tailored rescaling maintains the model’s ability to use very long-range context without driving perplexity upward.

From a practical perspective, this ablation justifies the progressive strategy in LongRoPE: (i) search and fine-tune at an intermediate long length (128k/256k), and (ii) search again on the fine-tuned checkpoint to reach the final large length (2048k). The second interpolation acts as a stability-preserving transform that leverages the earlier fine-tuning and is far more effective than attempting a one-shot extension or using generic interpolation rules.

# Short-context recovery: perplexity and benchmark improvements at 4k–8k after readjustment

A known side-effect of aggressive positional interpolation is degradation of model behavior at the original, shorter context lengths. When the model’s rotary angles are globally rescaled to spread over a much larger range, positions that used to occupy a comfortable angular span get “crowded” into a much narrower portion of the angular domain; this makes originally short-range distinctions harder and can increase perplexity (or harm downstream accuracy) on tasks whose relevant context is within the original window.

LongRoPE addresses this with a dedicated recovery step: after producing the extremely extended model (2048k), the method runs an additional evolution search specifically constrained for short context lengths (e.g., 4k and 8k). Concretely, the search reduces the maximum allowable $\lambda$ values so that the searched rescale factors encourage less interpolation for those short-window cases. The extended model thus stores several RoPE configurations: the one suited for very long contexts and a readjusted one to use when the input length is within the short range.

The ablations demonstrate that this readjustment substantially improves perplexity at 4k and 8k compared to using the single extreme rescaling uniformly. Table results in the paper show clear perplexity reductions on Proof-pile at both 4k and 8k after recovery, and corresponding improvements in the average scores on standard LLM benchmarks (for example in zero-shot and few-shot tasks kept within the original 4096 window). Conceptually, this recovery step is important because the goal of LongRoPE is to add very long-context capability without sacrificing the model’s original short-context competence; the readjustment provides a simple, inference-time mechanism to pick the RoPE rescaling that best matches the current length regime.

# Dimension non-uniformity vs. token-position non-uniformity: contributions at 16k–32k and at 2,048k

LongRoPE’s search targets two distinct kinds of non-uniformity in positional interpolation: (A) non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions (frequency bands), and (B) non-uniformity across token positions (leave some early tokens unscaled). The ablations isolate these two effects to quantify their relative contributions under different extension scenarios.

When extending to moderate long lengths such as 16k and 32k without fine-tuning, the results indicate that the dominant benefit comes from dimension non-uniformity. Searching for distinct rescale factors $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE dimension yields a marked perplexity reduction compared to PI, indicating that different frequency bands should be scaled differently. Adding the token-position non-uniformity (i.e., choosing a threshold $\hat{n}$ so the first $\hat{n}$ tokens use original RoPE while later tokens are rescaled) further reduces perplexity at these moderate lengths. Thus for 16k–32k, both forms are beneficial, but the largest single gain comes from letting each dimension vary its rescaling.

At the extreme extension target (2048k), the ablation shows a different pattern. Dimension non-uniformity still provides substantial benefit over PI, but the token-position non-uniformity (preserving the earliest $\hat{n}$ tokens) does not have the same positive impact and can be ineffective. The paper hypothesizes—and reports—that for extremely long sequences the usefulness of preserving only the initial tokens diminishes: when the target length is so large, the model’s attention distribution and the dynamics of long-range interactions change such that the simple strategy of leaving the earliest positions unscaled is not as helpful. In other words, token-position non-uniformity is more important when the extension is modest enough that the original early positions remain central to attention computations, whereas in ultra-long regimes the geometry of attention and relative positional relationships becomes more complex and the benefit of protecting just the initial positions reduces.

These ablation outcomes support the design choice in LongRoPE to search jointly over dimension-wise $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$: the joint search finds the configuration that best matches the target length regime, leveraging both kinds of non-uniformity where they help and de-emphasizing one when it does not.

# Sensitivity to $\hat{n}$ (number of early tokens left unscaled) and dependence on target length

The hyperparameter $\hat{n}$ controls how many of the earliest positions remain unrescaled (i.e., use original RoPE), and the ablations make two points about its sensitivity. First, leaving some initial tokens unscaled generally improves performance when extending to modest target lengths: experiments that sweep $\hat{n}$ over values like $\{0, 2, 4, 8, \dots, 256\}$ show that perplexity often drops when a small but non-zero number of initial tokens are preserved. This improvement aligns with the observation that early tokens commonly receive strong attention and influence the model’s behavior; preserving their original angular encoding avoids perturbing those high-impact signals.

Second, the optimal $\hat{n}$ depends on the extension target $L'$ (and thus on $s=L'/L$). For small extension ratios, a small $\hat{n}$ or even $\hat{n}=0$ may be adequate. For moderate ratios (e.g., extending to 8k or 16k), larger $\hat{n}$ values can yield better results. The paper’s ablation tables show that the best-performing $\hat{n}$ varies across evaluated targets. At extreme extension ratios (e.g., when ultimately targeting 2048k), preserving early tokens becomes less clearly beneficial and, as discussed above, can even be neutral. The practical take-away is that $\hat{n}$ should be treated as a length-dependent tuning knob and that LongRoPE’s search includes $\hat{n}$ as one of the variables so the optimizer can choose its value in accord with the target length.

Mathematically, $\hat{n}$ appears in the indicator function used in the rescaling:
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
1/\lambda_i & n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
so that positions $n<\hat{n}$ use the original rotation angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ (see Eq.~\ref{eq:problem} in the paper). Because the indicator is a hard threshold, the choice of $\hat{n}$ is sensitive: too small and you fail to protect genuinely important early tokens; too large and you may miss opportunities to compress interpolation pressure across positions in a way that benefits global long-range modeling.

# Why non-uniform rescaling preserves information in key frequency bands and early tokens

Understanding why non-uniform rescaling works requires revisiting how RoPE encodes position and how different RoPE dimensions correspond to different effective "frequencies." In the RoPE formulation, each dimension pair uses a rotation with an angle proportional to $n\theta_i$, where $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ (with $d$ the embedding dimension). Lower-indexed dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components (rapidly changing with position), while higher-indexed dimensions correspond to lower-frequency components (vary more slowly with position). When one rescales positions uniformly (linear PI with a single $\lambda=s$), all frequency bands are compressed equally into the pretrained angular range. That uniform compression disproportionately damages the bands that were critical for fine-grained positional discrimination (typically the higher-frequency dimensions), because their angular progress per token becomes much smaller and many token distinctions collapse.

Non-uniform rescaling addresses this by assigning smaller rescale factors to the lower-index (higher-frequency) dimensions and larger rescale factors to higher-index (lower-frequency) dimensions. Formally, the search finds a monotone sequence $\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}$ (the monotonicity constraint used in LongRoPE), so that the high-frequency components are downscaled less. The effect is to preserve phase increments where fine-grained positional distinctions are encoded, while allowing the lower-frequency components to be compressed more aggressively to accommodate the extended range. This preserves the original RoPE information in the "key" frequency bands that the pre-trained model had learned to rely on for short-range positional signals, thereby minimizing information loss from interpolation.

For early tokens, the intuition is that initial positions often have outsized influence on downstream computations because attention patterns frequently concentrate on recent or particular beginning-of-sequence tokens; preserving their original angular relationships helps keep those high-attention signals intact. The $\hat{n}$ mechanism (leave-first-$\hat{n}$-tokens-unscaled) protects these tokens’ rotational angles exactly, avoiding any interpolation-induced corruption for the positions most likely to drive attention weights.

Finally, the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ used in the search both reflects NTK-inspired theory (which predicts smaller effective smoothing for higher-frequency components) and dramatically reduces the search space, making it tractable to find a rescaling profile that preserves the most important angular information. In practice the joint effect — dimension-wise protection of high-frequency channels and optional protection of early positional indices — yields initializations that are much easier to fine-tune (when fine-tuning is needed) and that enable large non-fine-tuned extension factors (empirically up to 8$\times$) while keeping perplexity and retrieval performance stable.

# Closing remarks on the ablations

The ablations in the paper show a coherent story. Dimension-wise non-uniformity is the single most impactful element for preserving language modeling quality when extending context windows; token-position non-uniformity provides additional gains at moderate extensions by protecting influential early positions; and the two key operational strategies — progressive fine-tuning plus a second searched interpolation, and a short-context recovery search — together enable models to reach ultra-long contexts (2048k) while retaining or recovering performance on shorter-window tasks. The evolution search with monotonicity and informed initialization makes discovering these non-uniform solutions computationally practical, and the empirical results validate that these design choices materially improve perplexity stability and downstream retrieval accuracy compared to PI and prior group-wise schemes such as YaRN.

Applying LongRoPE in Practice

# When to use LongRoPE: read/write workloads, long-document QA, multi-shot prompts
LongRoPE is designed for scenarios where the model must consume or reason over extremely long contexts beyond standard LLM windows (e.g., 4k). Typical use cases include long-document question answering and summarization, multi-shot prompting with many examples, and read/write workloads where a model must produce outputs that reference distant earlier context (for example, editing or refactoring across a very large file). The paper motivates these uses by pointing out tasks where additional context matters: in-context learning with numerous examples and agent-style workflows that accumulate long interaction histories.  

Practical decision rules are simple: prefer LongRoPE when the downstream task benefits from retaining more than a few tens of thousands of tokens of context, or when you expect retrieval from or reasoning over document spans that are much longer than the model’s native window. If your workload is primarily short-turn conversational queries or isolated short documents, LongRoPE’s extra engineering and compute are not necessary; the standard RoPE-based model is sufficient.

# Integration steps: modify RoPE with searched $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$; dynamic selection by context length
The core integration change is replacing the model’s RoPE positional scaling with the searched, non-uniform rescale factors. Recall the RoPE encoding for token position $n$ in dimension index $i$ (as simplified in the paper):
\[
\left[ \cos\!\big(n\theta_0\big), \sin\!\big(n\theta_0\big), \ldots, \cos\!\big(n\theta_{d/2-1}\big), \sin\!\big(n\theta_{d/2-1}\big) \right],
\]
and the unified interpolation form:
\[
\left[\cos\!\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),\; \sin\!\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),\ldots\right],
\]
where $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})=1$ for $n<\hat{n}$ and $1/\lambda_i$ for $n\ge\hat{n}$. Integration therefore means: (1) store the vector of per-dimension scale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ and the token-threshold $\hat{n}$ produced by the search; (2) at positional-encoding time, apply the factor $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ to the rotary angle generation so that positions below $\hat{n}$ use the original angles and positions at or beyond $\hat{n}$ use the rescaled angles.

Because LongRoPE supports different rescale sets for different target lengths (e.g., separate sets for 4k/8k/256k/2048k), implement a small dispatch in inference that picks the appropriate $\{\lambda_i,\hat{n}\}$ based on the current sequence length. The paper describes a “shorter context window recovery” step: after producing the 2048k-capable model, they run a secondary search to recover short-context performance (4k and 8k) by limiting allowable $\lambda$ and saving the corresponding scale set. During inference, dynamically select the short-length rescale factors when sequence length falls below the designated threshold (the paper specifically notes applying the searched rescale factors when the sequence length is less than 8k).

# Search recipe: choosing datasets for perplexity evaluation, population sizes, iterations, and constraints
The search in LongRoPE is an evolutionary search over per-dimension scale factors and a token threshold $\hat{n}$. Design choices used in the paper are practical defaults you can reproduce:

- Search datasets: For target lengths within 256k, the paper used 5 random documents from the PG19 validation set (each sample must meet the target minimum length) to compute perplexity during search. For target lengths over 512k, they switched to 3 random samples from Pile-Books3 validation, because Books3 contains extremely long books suitable for million-token contexts. Use long samples whose lengths meet or exceed the target length so the computed perplexity reflects the intended extension.

- Search space: For an extension ratio $s=L'/L$, allow each $\lambda_i$ to range from $1.0$ (no interpolation / direct extrapolation) to $s\times 1.25$ with step $0.01$. Search the token-threshold $\hat{n}$ over the discrete set \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}. (When $\hat{n}=0$ all positions use the searched $\lambda_i$.)

- Evolutionary search hyperparameters (within 256k targets): population $P=64$, mutation size $N_1=16$, crossover size $N_2=16$, mutation probability $p=0.3$, iterations $\mathcal{T}=40$, and select the top 32 individuals per generation for mutation/crossover. For targets beyond 512k, halve the population and mutation/crossover sizes to reduce per-evaluation cost.

- Initialization and constraints: include PI, NTK, and YaRN rescale vectors as members of the initial population rather than entirely random initialization. When sampling new candidates, enforce the monotonic constraint
\[
\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\quad\text{for }i=0,\ldots,\tfrac{d}{2}-2,
\]
so scale factors are non-decreasing across RoPE dimensions (this reflects the NTK intuition that higher-frequency dimensions should be interpolated less). Only evaluate perplexity for candidates satisfying this constraint to reduce wasted computation.

- Perplexity metric: compute language-model perplexity on the chosen long samples for each candidate rescale set; use this scalar as the objective to minimize in the evolution loop.

These settings reproduce the search behavior reported in the paper and balance coverage of the extremely large combinatorial space with manageable compute.

# Fine-tuning recipe: staged training to 256k, switching RoPE factors mid-training, compute planning
For very large target windows (e.g., 2048k), LongRoPE uses a progressive recipe that minimizes fine-tuning cost while obtaining stability:

1. Search on the pre-trained LLM for intermediate target sizes (the paper searched for 128k and 256k rescale factors first). Use the search recipe above to get $\{\lambda_i,\hat{n}\}$ for 128k and 256k.

2. Fine-tune progressively to 256k. For LLaMA2-7B the paper’s recipe was:
   - Learning rate: $2\times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay.
   - Global batch size: 32.
   - First fine-tune for 400 steps using the 128k RoPE rescale factors and training with chunked sequences of length 128k (examples bookended with BOS/EOS).
   - After 400 steps, replace the RoPE rescale factors with the 256k set and continue training for another 600 steps.
   - Hardware: 128k stage used 8 A100 GPUs; the 256k stage used 16 A100 GPUs.

   For Mistral-7B the paper used:
   - Constant learning rate: $1\times 10^{-6}$.
   - Global batch size: 64.
   - 400 steps total for the intermediate stage, trained using 16k sequence length on suitable long-data collections (in the paper they followed YaRN’s setup using Together Computer’s long-data collection).
   - Hardware: 4 A100 GPUs for their 128k/256k setup.

This two-stage fine-tuning (first to a smaller extended length, then replace RoPE and continue to the next) proved more efficient and stable than directly fine-tuning to the large target length. The paper reports that this approach allowed reaching 256k length with as little as 1k fine-tuning steps (400 + 600) and provided a better checkpoint for subsequent secondary search.

3. Secondary search: after obtaining a stable 256k-fine-tuned checkpoint, run the evolutionary search again on that fine-tuned model targeting the final huge window (e.g., 2048k). The paper reports that this second searched rescale set, when applied to the fine-tuned LLM, achieved the 2048k window without additional fine-tuning.

Compute planning guidance from the paper: the intermediate fine-tuning steps are the most compute-heavy part. Use the two-stage approach to reduce required extreme-length data and to keep fine-tuning iterations small (1k steps total in the paper). Ensure you have enough GPU memory to handle the chosen training sequence lengths (they used 16k for some Mistral experiments and 128k/256k chunk sizes for LLaMA2 stages).

# Inference considerations: attention memory, batch sizing, sliding window for evaluation
When running inference with an extended context window, the primary concern is memory for attention states and throughput:

- Attention memory: model caches (past key/values) grow linearly with sequence length. Practical deployment must consider GPU or host memory capacity to hold the attention caches for the longest contexts you intend to serve. The paper does not change attention implementation; LongRoPE keeps the original transformer architecture so memory scaling is unchanged from any extended-window model.

- Batch sizing: large single examples with long contexts may require reducing batch sizes or using model-parallel/distributed memory to fit. Plan batch sizes accounting for the longest context you will supply. The paper’s training used distributed systems (Cube) and multiple A100s for long chunks; inference will similarly benefit from distributed or host-sharded caching for extreme context lengths.

- Sliding-window evaluation: when you evaluate perplexity or measure language-model quality across very long documents, follow the paper’s strategies: for PG19/Proof-pile evaluations within 256k, the authors used a sliding window stride of 256 tokens (i.e., compute loss in windows of the model’s context size with a sliding hop of 256). For Books3-scale evaluation (multi-million tokens), they used a sliding window of 256k to reduce compute while still sampling across the entire book. Use a stride appropriate to your compute budget and the resolution you want in the perplexity curve.

- Dynamic RoPE selection: during inference, pick the rescale set appropriate to the current sequence length (for example, apply short-context recovery RoPE when length < 8k; otherwise apply long-window RoPE). The model must be able to switch RoPE parameters on the fly; implement this as a lightweight pre-step before positional encoding.

# Common pitfalls: over-interpolation crowding, insufficient search seeding, evaluating on too-short texts
Several failure modes are highlighted in the paper and observed in experiments:

- Over-interpolation crowding: linear interpolation (PI) uniformly scales positions, which makes many positions “crowded” in angular space and causes the model to lose the ability to discriminate nearby tokens, especially within the original short window. This is worse at high extension ratios. LongRoPE mitigates this by preserving certain RoPE dimensions and initial token positions (via $\hat{n}$), but if your searched $\{\lambda_i\}$ over-interpolates (too large $\lambda$ for too many dimensions) you will see performance drops at short lengths. Remedy: use the short-context recovery search (with smaller maximum $\lambda$) and use the monotonic constraint during search.

- Insufficient search seeding: random-only initialization frequently wastes compute in the enormous combinatorial space. The paper’s success relied on initializing the population with PI, NTK, and YaRN individuals and then mutating them. If you skip such seeding, evolution may take many more iterations or converge to poor solutions.

- Evaluating on too-short texts: if you compute perplexity during search on samples whose lengths are much shorter than the target $L'$, the evaluation signal will not reflect behavior at the target length. Always ensure search-evaluation samples meet or exceed the target length (the paper required samples to have at least the target length).

- Fine-tuning directly to extreme ratios: the authors found direct fine-tuning to very large target lengths (e.g., directly to 2048k) is difficult to converge and costly. Use the progressive two-stage fine-tuning instead.

- Ignoring dimensional monotonicity: allowing arbitrary $\lambda_i$ shapes across dimensions (without enforcing $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) can yield solutions that violate NTK intuition and produce worse results; enforcing monotonicity reduces wasted evaluations and helps find stable solutions.

# Validation checklist: perplexity curves across lengths, passkey accuracy, short-context benchmarks
To validate a LongRoPE deployment reproduce the evaluation suite used in the paper:

1. Perplexity curves across lengths. Compute perplexity at multiple evaluation lengths (e.g., 4k, 8k, 16k, 32k, ..., up to the target 256k or 2048k). Use sliding windows with the strides used in the paper (stride 256 for within-256k evaluation; stride 256k for Books3-scale evaluation). A successful LongRoPE integration should show a non-increasing or smoothly improving perplexity trend as longer context is allowed (indicating the model can leverage longer context).

2. Passkey retrieval accuracy. Use the passkey retrieval synthetic test: hide a five-digit passkey at a random uniform location in a long document and measure whether the model retrieves it. The paper ran 10 iterations for each evaluation length. Successful LongRoPE models retained high accuracy across extremely long lengths (the paper reports LLaMA2-2048k (ft=256k) maintaining $\geq 90\%$ retrieval accuracy from 4k to 2048k; Mistral-2048k (ft=128k) kept 100\% up to 1800k then dropped to 60\% at 2048k). These numbers are illustrative of expected behavior and a useful sanity check.

3. Short-context benchmarks. Evaluate a representative set of short-context tasks to ensure you did not regress on original capabilities. The paper evaluated zero- and few-shot benchmarks used in the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard (examples include ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA). After long-extension and short-context recovery search, results should remain comparable to the base model on these tasks (the paper reports only small degradations in most cases, with some parity or slight gains on certain datasets).

4. Ablations for trust. Run ablations that isolate the two non-uniformities: (i) search only per-dimension $\lambda_i$ (no $\hat{n}$ non-uniformity), (ii) search only token-position threshold $\hat{n}$ while keeping uniform $\lambda$. The paper shows that per-dimension non-uniformity yields the largest perplexity improvements and that token-position non-uniformity helps for modest extension ratios.

If your model fails any of these checks, inspect the searched $\lambda_i$ curves, check whether the monotonicity constraint was accidentally violated, and ensure eval data lengths matched the intended target length for search.

# Wrapping up: practical tips and recommended default pipeline
To port LongRoPE to a RoPE-based model and reproduce the paper’s benefits, follow this compact pipeline:

1. Choose the target long context $L'$ and compute extension ratio $s=L'/L$.
2. Run evolutionary search on the pre-trained model to produce rescale sets for intermediate targets (e.g., 128k and 256k). Use the paper’s search hyperparameters and evaluation datasets (PG19 for up to 256k; Books3 for >512k).
3. Fine-tune progressively: 400 steps at the intermediate RoPE, then 600 steps after swapping to the next RoPE set (LLaMA2 settings shown in the paper). Adjust learning rates and batch sizes per the model (paper’s LLaMA2 and Mistral settings are practical defaults).
4. Run a secondary search on the fine-tuned extended checkpoint targeting the final huge length (e.g., 2048k). Do not necessarily fine-tune further after this search.
5. Run a short-context recovery search for 4k/8k and store those rescale sets for dynamic selection at inference.
6. Validate using perplexity curves, passkey retrieval tests, and standard short-context benchmarks.

By following the search and fine-tuning recipes, enforcing monotonicity across dimensions, and applying the progressive training strategy, LongRoPE enables extremely large context windows (the paper demonstrates up to 2048k) while protecting short-context performance.

Positioning LongRoPE Among Long-Context Methods

# Retrieval-based memory systems: architectural changes vs. LongRoPE’s lightweight embedding swap
Retrieval-based memory systems extend effective context by storing past tokens or documents in an external memory and retrieving relevant pieces at inference time. Conceptually, these systems shift the burden of “remembering” long histories from a single monolithic Transformer memory (the attention matrix over all tokens) to an indexable store plus a retrieval mechanism. In practice this often requires explicit architectural additions: a retrieval module, an external datastore, and sometimes interfaces that fuse retrieved contents back into the model input or hidden states. Because such changes are explicit and structural, they can be very effective for tasks that need to fetch factual or episodic content, but they are also more invasive than a positional-embedding-only change.

LongRoPE takes a different, much lighter approach. Rather than changing the model’s memory or adding a retrieval loop, it modifies how token positions are encoded inside the existing Transformer by rescaling RoPE rotation angles in a non-uniform, learned way (per RoPE dimension and, optionally, preserving a prefix of token positions). This is a relatively small modification: the model architecture and attention computation remain the same, and most existing weights and optimizations can be reused. The paper emphasizes that LongRoPE “retains the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding,” which makes it attractive when one wants a long-context capability without redesigning model serving or training pipelines.

The trade-off is straightforward: retrieval systems can reduce the need for treating every past token as part of an enormous attention computation and can provide content-addressable access to arbitrarily large external memories, while LongRoPE keeps everything inside the core attention mechanism and makes it work well across much longer linear contexts. In many applications these approaches are complementary: retrieval can provide selective, high-fidelity memory for certain tasks, while LongRoPE enables dense, global attention over very long contiguous contexts that retrieval alone may not represent well (for example, when relative ordering across the entire document matters).

# Attention-based windowing/masking (e.g., Streaming, LM-Infinite): complementary to interpolation
A different class of methods extends usable context by changing how attention is computed, not how positions are encoded. Examples named in the paper—Streaming LLM and LM-Infinite—manage long sequences by altering attention masks or windowing strategies so that tokens outside a short buffer do not blow up computational or algorithmic complexity. Typical techniques include sliding windows, hierarchical buffers, or caching and merging of previous hidden states so the full quadratic attention over all tokens is avoided; some approaches selectively attenuate or approximate interactions with far-away tokens. The central idea is to avoid forming a monolithic attention score across millions of tokens while still letting the model access long-range information in a controlled way.

LongRoPE’s positional-interpolation approach is compatible with these attention-based strategies. Because LongRoPE keeps the attention mechanism intact and only changes the positional encoding (how token indices map into rotation angles), attention-windowing schemes can still be applied at inference time. In other words, you can use LongRoPE to make the model’s positions meaningful over a much longer linear scale and at the same time use streaming or windowing masks to make computation tractable or to enforce desired locality/recency properties. The paper explicitly remarks that attention-based windowing and interpolation-style position extension are complementary; combining them can enable both long, coherent positional reasoning and practical inference-time cost control.

# Large-base RoPE and scaling-only fine-tunes (e.g., ScaledRoPE): trade-offs vs. flexible search
One relatively simple family of position-extension strategies is to change the RoPE base (the frequency scaling) to a much larger value and then fine-tune on the target lengths. ScaledRoPE–style approaches pick a large base so that the rotary angles vary more slowly with position; intuitively that lets position encodings remain informative over a larger absolute range. Practically, these methods often require direct fine-tuning on long sequences at the chosen base to adapt the model.

LongRoPE contrasts with that by using a search-driven, non-uniform rescaling of RoPE dimensions and token prefixes. Instead of globally raising or lowering the base, LongRoPE searches for per-dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) (and a prefix threshold \(\hat{n}\) that keeps early tokens un-rescaled) to preserve “important” parts of the original RoPE representation while compressing or expanding other parts. This gives LongRoPE two operational advantages emphasized in the paper: (1) it often provides a better initialization for subsequent fine-tuning—because it preserves key RoPE dimensions and early token encodings—and (2) it can already deliver an 8\(\times\) extension without any fine-tuning by exploiting these non-uniformities.

The trade-offs are thus: a large-base scaling approach is conceptually simple and may work well with sufficient fine-tuning, but it is a fairly coarse change and may require heavier fine-tuning resources to reach very large windows. LongRoPE’s search finds a more nuanced rescaling that can reduce the amount of fine-tuning needed (the paper reports only about 1k fine-tuning steps within 256k training length to reach 2048k final capability via a progressive strategy). The choice between them depends on available fine-tuning compute, desired flexibility across target lengths, and whether preserving short-context behavior is a priority.

# Efficiency-focused fine-tuning (LongLoRA, PoSE): orthogonality and potential combinations
Fine-tuning for long contexts can be expensive because longer training sequences and larger batches are needed. Several works (LongLoRA, PoSE) focus on reducing that fine-tuning cost by changing the update pattern (e.g., LoRA adapters applied selectively, or parameter-efficient schemes that adapt positional components) so the model can be adapted to long contexts with much lower compute and memory. These efficiency-focused methods aim to make the long-context fine-tuning practical on fewer GPUs or for shorter overall wall-clock time.

LongRoPE is orthogonal to these techniques. The paper explicitly positions LongRoPE as compatible with efficient fine-tuning strategies: LongRoPE supplies a strong initialization via searched, non-uniform RoPE rescale factors that minimize the catastrophic values introduced by naive new positions, and then one can apply LongLoRA, PoSE, or similar methods to fine-tune the model further using fewer trainable parameters or less compute. In practice you can imagine a pipeline where LongRoPE’s search and progressive extension provide a carefully interpolated positional embedding, and adapter-style or LoRA-style updates adapt the rest of the model quickly and cheaply for a given extended training length. This combination preserves the benefits of both approaches: well-behaved positional initialization and computationally efficient adaptation.

# Ecosystem fit: compatibility with existing optimizations and serving stacks
One practical reason to prefer small, well-targeted changes to models is interoperability with existing training and serving infrastructure. The paper emphasizes that LongRoPE “retains the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding, and can reuse most pre-existing optimizations.” Concretely, this means LongRoPE does not require reworking the attention kernel, changing layer types, or adding retrieval backends; it simply replaces or parameterizes the RoPE rescaling factors used to compute cosines and sines for rotary embeddings. Because the compute graph and tensor shapes remain unchanged, LongRoPE can leverage the same fused attention kernels, quantization schemes, batching and memory-mapping tricks, and distributed training or inference pipelines already in place for the base model.

An important operational wrinkle the paper addresses is short-context fidelity: interpolation approaches can “crowd” the original short-range positions and hurt performance at the original window sizes. LongRoPE handles this by including a short-window recovery step—respecializing the rescale factors for short lengths (e.g., 4k or 8k) and dynamically selecting rescale factors at inference depending on the sequence length. This dynamic but lightweight behavior is easier to integrate into a serving stack than a method that changes model internals or requires a separate model for long vs. short contexts.

Finally, because LongRoPE is applicable to any model that uses RoPE positional encoding, it fits into the ecosystem of prevalent LLMs (e.g., LLaMA2, Mistral) without forcing a forked codebase. The paper also points out that LongRoPE’s progressive strategy (search, short fine-tuning at moderate lengths, then a second search to reach extreme lengths) minimizes the need for rare ultra-long training data and large-scale training runs, which is important for practical adoption.

Concluding perspective
LongRoPE occupies a pragmatic point in the design space of long-context methods. It is much lighter and less invasive than retrieval-augmented systems or attention-architecture changes, yet more targeted and flexible than single-parameter large-base scaling. It complements attention-windowing and retrieval approaches and is compatible with parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods—so practitioners can choose combinations that best match their task requirements, compute budget, and deployment constraints. The paper’s key claim is that by exploiting two forms of non-uniformity in RoPE (per-dimension and prefix-preserving rescaling), and by using a search + progressive fine-tuning recipe, LongRoPE achieves very large context windows (up to 2048k in the work) while remaining practical to deploy within existing model ecosystems.

Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Why non-uniformity works: NTK and Fourier intuition, attention concentration on early tokens

LongRoPE’s central empirical finding is that positional interpolation should not treat all rotary dimensions and all token positions uniformly. To understand why, it helps to connect RoPE’s construction to frequency analysis and NTK-inspired reasoning, and to consider how attention weights distribute over the input.

RoPE represents each token position through rotations whose effective angular increments vary across embedding dimensions. Concretely, for dimension index $i$ the rotary angle scales like $\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}$ (with $\beta$ defined by the RoPE base), so different dimensions effectively encode different “frequencies” of positional variation. From a Fourier perspective, lower-index dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components: they change rapidly with small changes in $n$ and so carry fine-grained, short-range positional distinctions. Higher-index dimensions vary more slowly and therefore encode lower-frequency, longer-range positional structure. The NTK-based analysis used in prior work and cited in the paper suggests that these different frequency channels contribute differently to the model’s inductive biases and gradient dynamics. Practically, this means that aggressive downscaling (interpolation) of the rotary angles across all dimensions tends to “crowd” many distinct positions into similar angle values and disproportionately destroys the fine-grained signals encoded in the high-frequency channels. LongRoPE’s non-uniform rescaling preserves smaller scale changes in the key (higher-frequency) RoPE dimensions while allowing more interpolation in lower-frequency channels, which keeps local positional discriminability for the model intact.

A second, complementary intuition arises from how attention concentrates across positions. Prior observations cited in the paper (e.g., Streaming LLM, LM-Infinite) show that tokens near the start of a long context often receive disproportionately large attention scores. These early tokens often serve as anchors or repeated references across many attention computations, so preserving their original rotary encoding (i.e., extrapolating them with little or no interpolation) reduces the risk of introducing catastrophic, out-of-distribution angle values where the model expects previously seen encodings. The LongRoPE experiments confirm that leaving an initial block of positions (a threshold $\hat{n}$ in the paper’s notation) un-interpolated often improves perplexity and helps provide a good starting point for fine-tuning or even allows non-fine-tuned extension up to an 8× factor.

Together, the NTK/Fourier viewpoint and the attention-concentration observation explain why the search for per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and a token-threshold $\hat{n}$ yields much better initialization than uniform (linear) interpolation. The model suffers less information loss where it matters most (high-frequency dimensions and early token positions), and can therefore generalize more smoothly to very long contexts.

# Limitations: search cost, model-specific factors, reliance on perplexity as search objective, extreme-length edge cases

LongRoPE makes clear progress, but the paper also documents several important limitations and failure modes that students and researchers should keep in mind.

Search cost and combinatorial explosion. Allowing a separate rescale factor $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension and searching over choices of $\hat{n}$ explodes the solution space exponentially as the target extension ratio $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ grows. The paper quantifies this combinatorial challenge (for example, even modest discretizations produce astronomically many candidate configurations), and therefore resorts to an evolution-based search with two practical optimizations: seeding the initial population with PI/NTK/YaRN baselines and enforcing a monotonically non-decreasing constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ across dimensions. These heuristics reduce cost substantially, but the procedure still depends on running many forward passes to evaluate perplexity for sampled individuals. For very large target lengths (hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens), each perplexity evaluation becomes costly, so search remains a nontrivial computational expense.

Model-specific behavior. Experiments in the paper show that different LLM families behave differently under the same LongRoPE pipeline: LLaMA2 and Mistral exhibit distinct perplexity trends and respond differently to fine-tuning lengths (e.g., the optimal fine-tuning length and the effectiveness of the secondary interpolation step differed between them). This indicates that optimal rescale factors and search settings are model-dependent. Although LongRoPE is applicable to any RoPE-based model in principle, practitioners should expect to re-run the search for each new architecture or pretraining regime rather than reusing a single universal solution.

Reliance on perplexity as the search objective. The search procedure in the paper optimizes next-token log-loss (perplexity) on held-out long-text snippets. Perplexity is a sensible and widely used proxy for language-modeling fidelity, but it is not always aligned with downstream task performance (e.g., retrieval tasks, structured generation, or instruction following). The paper demonstrates strong downstream results (passkey retrieval, benchmark performance), but the reliance on a single objective limits the search to what perplexity captures. Tasks that require different aspects of positional reasoning or semantic prioritization may benefit from alternative or multi-objective search criteria.

Edge cases at extreme lengths. The ablations show that token-position non-uniformity (keeping an initial block of positions un-interpolated) helps at intermediate extensions (e.g., up to tens of thousands of tokens) but becomes less useful at the very largest extensions (e.g., the final 512× step to 2,048k in some cases). At such vast lengths, the positional geometry and attention dynamics change sufficiently that strategies that help for moderate extensions may not translate directly. The paper notes that preserving only the initial tokens without interpolation may be “non-useful” at extreme scales, leaving open how best to handle this regime.

# Generalization to other architectures and modalities

LongRoPE is explicitly designed for RoPE-style rotary positional embeddings and the paper emphasizes applicability across RoPE-based LLMs (LLaMA2, Mistral demonstrated). That scope is important: the method manipulates the rotary angles directly, so models that use fundamentally different positional encodings (absolute learned embeddings, ALiBi, or some relative schemes that are not equivalent to RoPE) are not guaranteed to accept the same intervention without further analysis.

For modalities beyond text (e.g., audio, images, multimodal transformers), the underlying mathematical structure governing positional encodings may still admit a frequency-like decomposition, and in principle a similar non-uniform rescaling idea could be attempted. However, the paper does not present experiments in other modalities; therefore any claim of cross-modal generality would be speculative. A responsible stance is that LongRoPE’s ideas plausibly generalize wherever (a) positional encodings can be expressed as per-dimension phase/frequency channels and (b) the model’s attention patterns produce position-dependent inductive biases analogous to text models. Demonstrating this in practice, however, requires dedicated experiments and possibly modification of the search space and objective.

# Potential improvements: learned meta-policies for λ_i, alternative objectives (task-aware), faster search

The paper already adopts practical accelerations to the evolutionary search (initial seeding and monotonicity constraint). Building on this foundation, several concrete directions could make LongRoPE more efficient and more broadly useful:

Learned meta-policies for $\lambda_i$. Instead of searching anew for each model and target length, one could explore learning a meta-model that maps model statistics (e.g., layer norms, attention patterns, original RoPE spectrum) and the desired extension ratio $s$ to a proposed $\lambda$ profile. Such a meta-policy could be trained by aggregating search results across multiple models and target lengths and could dramatically reduce per-model search cost. The paper’s results showing consistent structure in good $\lambda$ profiles (non-decreasing with dimension, preservation of certain key dimensions) suggest this is plausible, but it remains to be validated.

Task-aware or multi-objective search. Perplexity is a strong proxy for language modeling but not the only relevant metric. For applications like retrieval from long context or multi-hop reasoning, it could be beneficial to include task-specific objectives (e.g., retrieval accuracy, end-to-end generation metrics) either in a weighted multi-objective search or via a two-stage procedure that first optimizes perplexity and then fine-tunes $\lambda$ to improve downstream performance.

Faster search algorithms. Evolutionary search is simple and effective, but alternative approaches may be faster: gradient-based tuning of continuous $\lambda_i$ (if differentiable approximations of the evaluation objective are feasible), Bayesian optimization over low-dimensional parameterizations of the $\lambda$ curve (for example, parameterize $\lambda_i$ via a small set of spline knots), or reinforcement-learning based controllers could reduce the number of expensive perplexity evaluations. The monotonicity constraint could also be built into parametric forms (e.g., $\lambda_i = f(\alpha, i)$ with $f$ monotonic by construction), shrinking search dimensionality.

Smarter discretization and transfer. The paper discretizes $\lambda_i$ with step size 0.01 in a bounded range. Adaptive discretization—coarse-to-fine refinement in regions that matter—could yield faster convergence. Likewise, transferring good solutions from similar models or nearby extension ratios (as the paper does progressively from 4k→256k→2048k) is an effective practical pattern that can be further formalized.

# Ethical and societal considerations of million-token context models: compute, environmental cost, misuse risks

Extending LLM context windows to million-token scales unlocks powerful capabilities but also raises ethical and societal concerns that the community should consider alongside technical work.

Compute and environmental cost. Very long context models increase both inference and training compute. Even if LongRoPE avoids full fine-tuning at the target million-token scale by relying on a progressive strategy, search evaluations, fine-tuning to intermediate lengths (e.g., 128k/256k), and longer inference passes still consume substantial GPU resources. The carbon and economic costs of these resources are nontrivial and may limit participation in research to well-resourced groups, concentrating capability and potentially reinforcing inequities.

Centralization and access. High-cost models tend to be developed and hosted by a few organizations. Widespread deployment of million-token-capable models could centralize access to powerful tools and datasets, with implications for research reproducibility and for who controls high-capacity language systems.

Dual-use and misuse risks. Longer context makes it easier to perform sophisticated tasks that may be misused: automated large-scale extraction of sensitive information, synthesis of very long phishing or propaganda documents with consistent themes, or building agents that keep and act on long-running histories in ways that could violate privacy or be used for surveillance. High-capacity long-context models also facilitate more convincing long-form deceptive content.

Mitigations and responsible practice. These risks suggest practical mitigations: measuring and disclosing energy footprints and hardware costs of training and deployment, pursuing efficiency improvements (both algorithmic and system-level), developing and applying privacy-preserving practices for training data, and establishing policy and governance around deployment contexts for long-context models. The paper’s Broader Impacts section did not enumerate specific societal consequences, but the technical advance makes it timely for the community to engage proactively on these topics.

# Summary and outlook: enabling new long-context applications and research directions

LongRoPE demonstrates that much larger context windows—up to 2,048k tokens in the paper—are achievable for RoPE-based LLMs by carefully exploiting two orthogonal non-uniformities: per-dimension sensitivity across RoPE (frequency channels) and per-token-position importance (especially early tokens). The practical recipe combines an evolution-guided search for per-dimension rescale factors, a monotonicity inductive bias, progressive fine-tuning to moderate long lengths (256k), and a secondary search to bridge to million-token regimes. Empirically, this yields large gains in perplexity and in downstream micro-tasks like passkey retrieval, while preserving competitive performance on original short-context benchmarks.

At the same time, the approach highlights open technical and societal challenges. Search cost, model-specific tuning, limited alignment between perplexity and task performance, and edge-case behavior at extreme lengths are all active areas for improvement. From a research perspective, promising directions include meta-learning or parameterized policies for $\lambda_i$, multi-objective and task-aware search criteria, alternative optimization methods to the current evolutionary strategy, and principled examination of how positional geometry and attention patterns change as sequence length grows into the millions.

Finally, as the capabilities of long-context models expand, the field must pair technical advances with careful attention to compute costs, equitable access, and misuse mitigation. LongRoPE’s contribution opens many new application possibilities—long-document summarization, extended in-context learning, richer conversational agents with long histories—but realizing them responsibly will require continued research on efficiency, evaluation, and societal safeguards.
