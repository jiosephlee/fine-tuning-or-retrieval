\title{A Textbook about the Paper: LongRoPE}

Chapter 10: Ablations and Analysis

# Second interpolation after fine-tuning vs. PI/YaRN: stability of perplexity under further scaling

One of the central design choices in LongRoPE is to perform a second, non-uniform positional interpolation on a model that has already been fine-tuned to an intermediate extended length (e.g., fine-tuned to 128k or 256k) rather than attempting a single giant jump from the original length to the final target (2048k). Intuitively, fine-tuning at an intermediate scale lets the model adapt its internal representations to a moderate amount of new positions; applying a second carefully searched interpolation leverages that adaptation while avoiding the severe distributional shift that would occur if we abruptly exposed the model to the full target range without intermediate training.

Empirically, the ablation reported in the paper compares the result of extending a fine-tuned 256k LLaMA2 model further to 512k, 1024k and 2048k using three methods: Plain linear positional interpolation (PI), YaRN’s group-wise non-uniform scheme, and LongRoPE’s searched non-uniform rescale factors applied as a second interpolation step. The observed behavior is that PI and YaRN produce rapidly increasing perplexities as the secondary extension ratio increases, while LongRoPE’s searched rescale factors keep perplexity at a much more stable and consistent level across the further scalings. This result highlights two points. First, naive linear interpolation (PI) crowds many new positions into a compressed angular range and therefore breaks the model’s positional discrimination when the extension ratio gets large. Second, even existing non-uniform schemes such as YaRN, which partition dimensions into groups, are not flexible enough to capture the fine-grained rescaling pattern that best preserves model behavior after fine-tuning. The second interpolation with LongRoPE, instead, uses a dimension-wise and position-aware rescaling that is tailored to the already fine-tuned model’s state; this tailored rescaling maintains the model’s ability to use very long-range context without driving perplexity upward.

From a practical perspective, this ablation justifies the progressive strategy in LongRoPE: (i) search and fine-tune at an intermediate long length (128k/256k), and (ii) search again on the fine-tuned checkpoint to reach the final large length (2048k). The second interpolation acts as a stability-preserving transform that leverages the earlier fine-tuning and is far more effective than attempting a one-shot extension or using generic interpolation rules.

# Short-context recovery: perplexity and benchmark improvements at 4k–8k after readjustment

A known side-effect of aggressive positional interpolation is degradation of model behavior at the original, shorter context lengths. When the model’s rotary angles are globally rescaled to spread over a much larger range, positions that used to occupy a comfortable angular span get “crowded” into a much narrower portion of the angular domain; this makes originally short-range distinctions harder and can increase perplexity (or harm downstream accuracy) on tasks whose relevant context is within the original window.

LongRoPE addresses this with a dedicated recovery step: after producing the extremely extended model (2048k), the method runs an additional evolution search specifically constrained for short context lengths (e.g., 4k and 8k). Concretely, the search reduces the maximum allowable $\lambda$ values so that the searched rescale factors encourage less interpolation for those short-window cases. The extended model thus stores several RoPE configurations: the one suited for very long contexts and a readjusted one to use when the input length is within the short range.

The ablations demonstrate that this readjustment substantially improves perplexity at 4k and 8k compared to using the single extreme rescaling uniformly. Table results in the paper show clear perplexity reductions on Proof-pile at both 4k and 8k after recovery, and corresponding improvements in the average scores on standard LLM benchmarks (for example in zero-shot and few-shot tasks kept within the original 4096 window). Conceptually, this recovery step is important because the goal of LongRoPE is to add very long-context capability without sacrificing the model’s original short-context competence; the readjustment provides a simple, inference-time mechanism to pick the RoPE rescaling that best matches the current length regime.

# Dimension non-uniformity vs. token-position non-uniformity: contributions at 16k–32k and at 2,048k

LongRoPE’s search targets two distinct kinds of non-uniformity in positional interpolation: (A) non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions (frequency bands), and (B) non-uniformity across token positions (leave some early tokens unscaled). The ablations isolate these two effects to quantify their relative contributions under different extension scenarios.

When extending to moderate long lengths such as 16k and 32k without fine-tuning, the results indicate that the dominant benefit comes from dimension non-uniformity. Searching for distinct rescale factors $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE dimension yields a marked perplexity reduction compared to PI, indicating that different frequency bands should be scaled differently. Adding the token-position non-uniformity (i.e., choosing a threshold $\hat{n}$ so the first $\hat{n}$ tokens use original RoPE while later tokens are rescaled) further reduces perplexity at these moderate lengths. Thus for 16k–32k, both forms are beneficial, but the largest single gain comes from letting each dimension vary its rescaling.

At the extreme extension target (2048k), the ablation shows a different pattern. Dimension non-uniformity still provides substantial benefit over PI, but the token-position non-uniformity (preserving the earliest $\hat{n}$ tokens) does not have the same positive impact and can be ineffective. The paper hypothesizes—and reports—that for extremely long sequences the usefulness of preserving only the initial tokens diminishes: when the target length is so large, the model’s attention distribution and the dynamics of long-range interactions change such that the simple strategy of leaving the earliest positions unscaled is not as helpful. In other words, token-position non-uniformity is more important when the extension is modest enough that the original early positions remain central to attention computations, whereas in ultra-long regimes the geometry of attention and relative positional relationships becomes more complex and the benefit of protecting just the initial positions reduces.

These ablation outcomes support the design choice in LongRoPE to search jointly over dimension-wise $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$: the joint search finds the configuration that best matches the target length regime, leveraging both kinds of non-uniformity where they help and de-emphasizing one when it does not.

# Sensitivity to $\hat{n}$ (number of early tokens left unscaled) and dependence on target length

The hyperparameter $\hat{n}$ controls how many of the earliest positions remain unrescaled (i.e., use original RoPE), and the ablations make two points about its sensitivity. First, leaving some initial tokens unscaled generally improves performance when extending to modest target lengths: experiments that sweep $\hat{n}$ over values like $\{0, 2, 4, 8, \dots, 256\}$ show that perplexity often drops when a small but non-zero number of initial tokens are preserved. This improvement aligns with the observation that early tokens commonly receive strong attention and influence the model’s behavior; preserving their original angular encoding avoids perturbing those high-impact signals.

Second, the optimal $\hat{n}$ depends on the extension target $L'$ (and thus on $s=L'/L$). For small extension ratios, a small $\hat{n}$ or even $\hat{n}=0$ may be adequate. For moderate ratios (e.g., extending to 8k or 16k), larger $\hat{n}$ values can yield better results. The paper’s ablation tables show that the best-performing $\hat{n}$ varies across evaluated targets. At extreme extension ratios (e.g., when ultimately targeting 2048k), preserving early tokens becomes less clearly beneficial and, as discussed above, can even be neutral. The practical take-away is that $\hat{n}$ should be treated as a length-dependent tuning knob and that LongRoPE’s search includes $\hat{n}$ as one of the variables so the optimizer can choose its value in accord with the target length.

Mathematically, $\hat{n}$ appears in the indicator function used in the rescaling:
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
1/\lambda_i & n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
so that positions $n<\hat{n}$ use the original rotation angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ (see Eq.~\ref{eq:problem} in the paper). Because the indicator is a hard threshold, the choice of $\hat{n}$ is sensitive: too small and you fail to protect genuinely important early tokens; too large and you may miss opportunities to compress interpolation pressure across positions in a way that benefits global long-range modeling.

# Why non-uniform rescaling preserves information in key frequency bands and early tokens

Understanding why non-uniform rescaling works requires revisiting how RoPE encodes position and how different RoPE dimensions correspond to different effective "frequencies." In the RoPE formulation, each dimension pair uses a rotation with an angle proportional to $n\theta_i$, where $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ (with $d$ the embedding dimension). Lower-indexed dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components (rapidly changing with position), while higher-indexed dimensions correspond to lower-frequency components (vary more slowly with position). When one rescales positions uniformly (linear PI with a single $\lambda=s$), all frequency bands are compressed equally into the pretrained angular range. That uniform compression disproportionately damages the bands that were critical for fine-grained positional discrimination (typically the higher-frequency dimensions), because their angular progress per token becomes much smaller and many token distinctions collapse.

Non-uniform rescaling addresses this by assigning smaller rescale factors to the lower-index (higher-frequency) dimensions and larger rescale factors to higher-index (lower-frequency) dimensions. Formally, the search finds a monotone sequence $\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}$ (the monotonicity constraint used in LongRoPE), so that the high-frequency components are downscaled less. The effect is to preserve phase increments where fine-grained positional distinctions are encoded, while allowing the lower-frequency components to be compressed more aggressively to accommodate the extended range. This preserves the original RoPE information in the "key" frequency bands that the pre-trained model had learned to rely on for short-range positional signals, thereby minimizing information loss from interpolation.

For early tokens, the intuition is that initial positions often have outsized influence on downstream computations because attention patterns frequently concentrate on recent or particular beginning-of-sequence tokens; preserving their original angular relationships helps keep those high-attention signals intact. The $\hat{n}$ mechanism (leave-first-$\hat{n}$-tokens-unscaled) protects these tokens’ rotational angles exactly, avoiding any interpolation-induced corruption for the positions most likely to drive attention weights.

Finally, the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ used in the search both reflects NTK-inspired theory (which predicts smaller effective smoothing for higher-frequency components) and dramatically reduces the search space, making it tractable to find a rescaling profile that preserves the most important angular information. In practice the joint effect — dimension-wise protection of high-frequency channels and optional protection of early positional indices — yields initializations that are much easier to fine-tune (when fine-tuning is needed) and that enable large non-fine-tuned extension factors (empirically up to 8$\times$) while keeping perplexity and retrieval performance stable.

# Closing remarks on the ablations

The ablations in the paper show a coherent story. Dimension-wise non-uniformity is the single most impactful element for preserving language modeling quality when extending context windows; token-position non-uniformity provides additional gains at moderate extensions by protecting influential early positions; and the two key operational strategies — progressive fine-tuning plus a second searched interpolation, and a short-context recovery search — together enable models to reach ultra-long contexts (2048k) while retaining or recovering performance on shorter-window tasks. The evolution search with monotonicity and informed initialization makes discovering these non-uniform solutions computationally practical, and the empirical results validate that these design choices materially improve perplexity stability and downstream retrieval accuracy compared to PI and prior group-wise schemes such as YaRN.
