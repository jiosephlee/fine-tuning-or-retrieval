OASST1 is a multilingual corpus of crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogs between a user and an assistant. Across annotations the authors report a moderate level of agreement among human annotators with Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.42$. - Collect human judgments in parallel (two annotators for comparisons to ChatGPT; three for general pairwise comparisons) and report inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss's $\kappa$) and rank correlations with automated raters (Kendall $\tau$, Spearman $r$). The authors also note that annotation quality deteriorates further when comparing two strong systems: discriminating between two high-quality responses is inherently more subjective and yields lower inter-annotator agreement. For evaluation the authors select every user message in the validation set as a query and include prior turns in the prompt to preserve context; doing so produced 953 unique user queries. - Treat GPT-4 as a cost-effective proxy but verify key findings with humans because of observed discrepancies (example-level Fleiss $\kappa = 0.25$ between GPT-4 and human majority, system-level $\tau = 0.43$, $r = 0.55$) and known evaluator biases such as ordering effects and self-preference (the latter reflected in discrepant Elo scores, e.g., 1348 vs 1176). Pairwise judgments remove the need to anchor an absolute scale: annotators only need to decide which of two responses is preferable for a given prompt. Agreement statistics reported in the paper reveal limits of human consistency and highlight evaluation challenges. These prompts were used exactly as provided by the Vicuna benchmark and serve as a standard small-suite test for direct head-to-head comparisons. The Elo interpretation used by the paper is the usual one: a higher Elo corresponds to a higher expected win probability against a lower-rated opponent. Averaging across both presentation orders cancels first-position bias in expectation and yields a more reliable estimate of relative quality. When using pairwise comparisons, randomize presentation order across examples and, if feasible, run both orders for the same pair/prompt and aggregate. Because different sampling orders can change temporary rating trajectories, the authors repeat the tournament aggregation procedure many times to stabilize estimates: they run the entire Elo computation 10,000 times with different random seeds and then summarize the distribution of Elo scores (this controls for ordering effects inherent in sequential updates). Because such a first-position advantage can systematically favor a model whenever prompts are presented in a non-randomized order, the paper recommends a straightforward mitigation: evaluate both input orders for every comparison (A then B, and B then A) and report the mean score across the two orders. Conclusion (practical checklist) - Use both benchmarks: a compact curated set (Vicuna, 80 prompts) and a broader contextual set (OASST1 validation, 953 queries) to capture different facets of conversational performance. In the absolute scoring setup, GPT-4 is given a prompt and two model responses (for example, ChatGPT and a candidate model) and asked to assign a score out of ten to each response, along with an explanation. The paper illustrates this with Elo estimates: GPT-4 assigned an Elo of 1348 to its own outputs, while human annotators assigned an Elo of 1176 — a gap that corresponds to about a 20% higher expected win probability in GPT-4’s ratings compared to human ratings. - When using GPT-4 for absolute scores, always run both presentation orders and average the results to reduce order bias; in pairwise comparisons, randomize presentation order and consider running both orders when feasible. Human evaluation was performed on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) in parallel with the GPT-4 evaluations. This affects both absolute scoring (scores out of ten) and, to some extent, pairwise preference judgments. The core idea is to convert many pairwise match outcomes (win, loss, tie) into a set of scalar skill ratings that correspond to expected win probabilities between any two systems. These head-to-head comparisons are carried out across pairs of systems for each prompt. The use of the OASST1 validation queries provides a much larger, multi-turn, and multilingual testbed complementary to the Vicuna prompt set. The second benchmark is drawn from the validation portion of the OASST1 (Open Assistant) dataset. The experiments compared system-level rankings produced by GPT-4 and by human annotators and report moderate but imperfect agreement. This approach supports robust model ranking even when individual pairwise judgments are noisy or sparse. Two notable biases in GPT-4 evaluations emerged. By contrast, the pairwise comparison protocol reduces the judgment to a simpler three-way choice: for a given prompt and two responses GPT-4 is asked to select which response is better, or declare a tie, and to justify the choice. Because of these biases and the imperfect per-example agreement (example-level Fleiss $\kappa = 0.25$ between GPT-4 and human majority votes), the authors recommend using GPT-4 as a practical, inexpensive complement to human annotation rather than a drop-in replacement. Chapter 12: Evaluating Chatbots: Protocols, Elo Ratings, and Biases  The paper evaluates chatbot generation quality using two curated sets of prompts intended to probe real-world conversational capabilities. The first set, the Vicuna prompts, is a compact collection of 80 prompts chosen to cover diverse categories of conversational queries. The paper argues that pairwise comparisons are more stable and more robust than absolute scoring because absolute scores require a shared, well-grounded numerical scale (what does a 7/10 mean in different contexts?), and different annotators or an automated rater may interpret or use that scale inconsistently. - Prefer pairwise comparisons for stability and then aggregate via Elo. In particular, best practices include: (1) randomizing and averaging presentation order to mitigate ordering effects; (2) using pairwise comparisons aggregated with Elo to reduce reliance on absolute scales; (3) reporting inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., Fleiss's $\kappa$) and rank correlations (Kendall $\tau$, Spearman $r$) so readers can judge reliability; and (4) where possible, validating automated evaluation findings with a smaller human-annotated sample to check for evaluator-specific biases such as self-preference. As an illustrative reference given in the paper, an Elo difference of 100 points (for example, 1100 vs 1000) corresponds roughly to an expected win probability of about 65% for the higher-rated system. Initialize Elo at 1000 and use $K=32$, repeating the aggregation many times (the paper uses 10,000 resamplings) to stabilize rankings. The annotation procedure used different numbers of raters depending on the task: for comparisons against ChatGPT the authors collected two human annotators per example; for pairwise comparisons among systems they collected three annotators per example. Elo thus converts many noisy pairwise judgments into a single interpretable scale where differences map to win probabilities, and repeated randomization (the 10,000 resamplings) reduces variance due to the particular sequence of match updates. The candidate model’s performance is then expressed as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (so a result can exceed 100% if the candidate scores higher than ChatGPT on average). First is the ordering bias discussed above, where GPT-4 tends to favor the response appearing earlier in its prompt; this motivated averaging over both orders. This practice was applied in the experiments to control for the observed order bias. This design mirrors common crowdsourced evaluation practices where multiple redundant judgments are gathered and aggregated (for example via majority vote) to reduce individual annotator noise. These system-level correlations indicate that GPT-4 and humans mostly agree on the broad ordering of models, but there are substantial differences in finer-grained comparisons. Following these procedures yields more reliable, interpretable, and reproducible chatbot evaluations as demonstrated in the experiments and analyses of the paper. This self-preference suggests automated evaluators may systematically overrate outputs from models of the same family or architecture as the evaluator, or otherwise have internal priors that differ from human judgment. The authors observed a significant ordering bias when using GPT-4 as an automated rater: responses presented earlier in GPT-4’s prompt tend to receive higher scores. Together these two benchmarks test both focused, curated prompts (Vicuna) and a broader set of contextual conversation queries (OASST1). The procedure used by the authors has these concrete settings: each model starts with an initial Elo score of 1000, the per-match update parameter is $K=32$, and the aggregation is performed by sampling from the set of labeled comparisons and updating ratings sequentially. At the system level the rank correlations between GPT-4 and humans are Kendall Tau $\tau = 0.43$ and Spearman $r = 0.55$. Thus, while human judgments are the gold standard, they have measurable limits in consistency, particularly as system quality converges. Second, GPT-4 displays self-preference in the sense that GPT-4 assigns substantially higher scores to outputs it generated itself than human annotators do. These numbers indicate that even humans do not reach perfect consensus on conversational preference judgments and that automated raters like GPT-4 do not perfectly replicate human majority preferences on a per-example basis. When comparing GPT-4 judgments to human annotators at the example level, agreement is weaker: the agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority vote yields Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.25$. The paper uses two automated evaluation styles with GPT-4: (1) absolute scoring and (2) pairwise comparisons. To aggregate pairwise judgments into an overall ranking, the paper adopts an Elo-style rating system adapted to tournament results. This reduces variance introduced by idiosyncratic uses of numeric ratings and leads naturally to tournament-style aggregation methods (see the Elo section below) that produce relative rankings with clearer interpretability in terms of win probabilities. Concretely, when using the absolute scoring protocol with GPT-4, compute the two scores produced when responses are presented in each order and take their mean.
