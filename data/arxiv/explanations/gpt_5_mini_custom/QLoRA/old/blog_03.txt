# Double Quantization Demystified: Squeezing Extra GBs Without Losing Accuracy

## Introduction

Finetuning large language models (LLMs) has become a crucial step for adapting these powerful systems to specific tasks. Yet, as model sizes balloon into the tens of billions of parameters, the memory footprint for finetuning explodes. Researchers recently introduced QLoRA—a clever approach that compresses model weights down to 4-bit precision and tucks in Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) to preserve accuracy. But even 4-bit quantization comes with a hidden cost: storing the quantization scales (constants) for each block of weights. Enter Double Quantization, a simple trick that slashes these extra memory overheads without sacrificing model quality. In this post, we unpack how Double Quantization works, why it yields meaningful savings, and practical tips for using it in your next experiment.

## The Role of Quantization Constants

Before diving into Double Quantization, let’s quickly revisit how block-wise quantization works. When you compress a 32-bit tensor down to $k$ bits—say $k=4$—you split the tensor into small blocks (e.g., of size 64 elements) and compute a scale factor $c$ per block. You store:

  • The quantized values themselves (4 bits each)  
  • One 32-bit floating-point scale constant per block  

During inference or training, you reconstruct (“dequantize”) each block by multiplying the integer values by its scale constant. While the overhead of $32$-bit scales might seem minor at first glance, it adds up at scale. For a blocksize of $B=64$, each 32-bit constant contributes $\tfrac{32}{64}=0.5$ bits on average per weight, translating to roughly 3 GB overhead on a 65B-parameter model.

## How Double Quantization Works

Double Quantization tackles this overhead head-on by quantizing the quantization constants themselves. Here’s the high-level recipe:

1. **First Quantization (Weights to 4-bit)**  
   Convert each block of 32-bit weights $\mathbf{W}$ into 4-bit integers using a scale constant $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$. This gives you:
   - $\mathbf{W}^{\text{4-bit}}$ (the quantized weights)
   - $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ (the original 32-bit scale per block)

2. **Second Quantization (Scales to 8-bit)**  
   Treat each $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ as a small tensor and quantize it down to 8-bit floats (FP8), creating:
   - $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ (the quantized scales)
   - A second-level scale $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ for dequantizing $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ back to FP32

The dequantization during a forward pass happens in two steps:
$$
\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}}
\;=\;\text{dequant}\bigl(c_2^{\text{FP32}},\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{4-bit}}\bigr)
\;=\;\text{dequant}\bigl(\,\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\,c_2^{\text{FP8}}),\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{4-bit}}\bigr).
$$

Because we only quantize the scales—tens of millions of tiny numbers—this second step introduces virtually no extra computational cost, yet it cuts the memory for storing the scales by more than half.

## Memory Savings in Practice

Let’s compare the memory overhead per weight before and after Double Quantization when using a blocksize of $B=64$ for the weights and $B'=256$ for the scale constants:

  • **Standard 4-bit quantization**  
    - Quantized weights: $4$ bits per parameter  
    - Scale constants: $32$ bits per block of 64 → $32/64=0.5$ bits per weight  
    - **Total**: $4.5$ bits per weight  

  • **With Double Quantization**  
    - Quantized weights: $4$ bits per parameter  
    - First-level scales (FP8) stored per block of 64: $8/64=0.125$ bits per weight  
    - Second-level scales (FP32) stored per block of 256 first-level scales: $32/(64\times256)\approx0.002$ bits per weight  
    - **Total**: $4 + 0.125 + 0.002 \approx 4.127$ bits per weight  

That drop from $4.5$ to about $4.13$ bits per weight may sound small, but over a 65-billion-parameter model it frees up roughly 0.37 bits per parameter—equivalent to around 3 GB of GPU RAM reclaimed. In the world of GPU constraints, every gigabyte matters.

## Trade-offs and Empirical Findings

One natural concern is whether quantizing the scales will degrade model performance. In practice, the research shows that:

  • **8-bit quantization of scales is near-lossless.** The scales themselves follow a relatively tight distribution, and storing them in FP8 introduces negligible extra error.  
  • **No drop in downstream quality.** Models finetuned with Double Quantization achieve virtually identical accuracy, perplexity, and instruction-following performance compared to single-round quantization.  

That said, it’s always good to verify on your own task and data. If your particular model is hyper-sensitive to tiny perturbations in weight recovery, you might experiment with keeping scales in 16-bit or adjusting block sizes.

## Choosing Block Sizes

Block sizes play a central role in the efficiency–precision trade-off:

  • **Weights blocksize ($B$):** Smaller blocks capture local variations better and reduce quantization error, but increase the number of scales stored. In QLoRA experiments, $B=64$ was found to strike a strong balance between precision and overhead.  
  • **Scales blocksize ($B'$):** Larger blocks for the second quantization mean fewer high-precision scale constants to store. The authors used $B'=256$ for $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$, as performance remained stable even with an 8-bit scale quantization at this grid.  

If you find your model losing quality, you can try reducing $B$ (improving weight fidelity) or reducing $B'$ (giving more precision back to the scale constants), at the expense of slightly higher memory.

## When to Turn Double Quantization On

Double Quantization is particularly appealing when:

  • You’re finetuning very large models (tens of billions of parameters) on limited-memory GPUs.  
  • You’ve already committed to 4-bit weight storage and want to shave off every last gigabyte.  
  • You’re deploying multiple model variants side by side and need to minimize the cumulative memory footprint.  

If you have plenty of GPU RAM and prefer to play it safe, you might stick with single-round quantization of scales. But in most real-world scenarios—especially on consumer or mid-range GPUs—Double Quantization pays for itself in reclaimed overhead without any penalty in accuracy.

## Conclusion

Double Quantization is a lightweight yet powerful extension to conventional block-wise quantization that squeezes extra memory savings out of your models without sacrificing performance. By treating the quantization scales as first-class citizens and applying a second layer of compression, you can run bigger models, load more adapter variants, or simply breathe easier on a tight GPU budget. In the rapidly evolving landscape of efficient LLM finetuning, Double Quantization is a neat trick to keep in your toolkit—turn it on, and unlock those extra gigabytes.
