# Evaluating Chatbots is Hard: GPT-4 vs Humans, Elo vs 10-Point Scales

Chatbots have become the darlings of AI—everyone wants to know which system “talks” best. But as models inch closer to human-like fluency, evaluating them is surprisingly messy. Commercial systems, open‐source projects, human annotators, and even automated judges like GPT-4 can’t always agree. In this post, we’ll unpack why chatbot benchmarks often disagree, explore how tournament-style Elo ratings offer a more reliable leaderboard, and provide a practical playbook for your next evaluation—whether you’re a researcher, developer, or enthusiast.

## Why 10-Point Scales Fall Short

Many recent papers ask annotators (human or artificial) to score chatbot responses on a scale of 1–10. On the surface, it’s intuitive: rate coherence, helpfulness, or correctness, and average the scores. In practice, however, this approach suffers from several issues:

1. **Anchoring and Order Effects**  
   GPT-4 and humans alike tend to favor the response they see first. If you ask “Rate A then B” versus “Rate B then A,” scores can shift by several points. This bias makes absolute scales unstable.

2. **Lack of Shared Grounding**  
   What does a “7” mean versus an “8”? Without a universally agreed definition, annotators end up using personal standards. One person’s 8 might be another’s 6, leading to wide confidence intervals when you compare averages.

3. **Subjectivity and Task Drift**  
   Different prompts demand different evaluation criteria. A compelling creative story isn’t judged by the same yardstick as a precise factual answer. Aggregating across diverse queries muddies the meaning of the average score.

In the QLoRA study, models like Guanaco-65B and Vicuna-13B often overlapped within their confidence intervals when scored out of ten. The takeaway? A 10-point scale can give you a rough sense of quality, but it’s far from definitive—especially when differences are small.

## Tournament-Style Elo Ratings to the Rescue

To overcome the pitfalls of absolute scoring, the QLoRA team adopted a **pairwise comparison** approach inspired by chess ratings. Here’s how it works:

1. **Head-to-Head Matches**  
   For each prompt, present two chatbot responses (e.g., ChatGPT vs Guanaco-65B). Ask the judge—human or GPT-4—to pick the better response or declare a tie.

2. **Win, Loss, or Draw**  
   Assign outcomes: a win is worth 1 point, a tie 0.5, and a loss 0.

3. **Update Elo Ratings**  
   After each match, adjust the players’ ratings according to the standard Elo formula:
   \[
     E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}},
   \]
   where \(E_A\) is the expected score for model A given its rating \(R_A\) and opponent’s rating \(R_B\).  
   The actual update for model A’s rating is
   \[
     R_A' = R_A + K \times (\text{Outcome}_A - E_A),
   \]
   with \(K\) as the learning rate (commonly 32).

4. **Repeat with Randomized Pairings**  
   To avoid ordering artifacts, run the tournament thousands of times with different random seeds and average the final ratings.

Why Elo helps:

- **Relative Judgment**: People are generally more consistent at choosing “which of these two is better?” than at scoring each on an absolute scale.
- **Robustness**: By aggregating over many pairings and random seeds, Elo smooths out individual biases and order effects.
- **Interpretable Scale**: A 100-point difference in Elo corresponds to an expected win-rate of about 75%. That’s more concrete than “7 vs. 8 on a 1–10 scale.”

In the QLoRA experiments, Elo rankings derived from human judges and GPT-4 showed **moderate agreement** (Kendall’s τ≈0.43, Spearman’s ρ≈0.55), even though their average 10-point scores sometimes diverged wildly.

## Automated vs Human Evaluators: Alignment and Divergence

Automated evaluators like GPT-4 promise fast, cheap assessments—but how reliable are they compared to humans?

- **System-Level Agreement**  
  When comparing overall rankings of models, GPT-4 and human Elo ratings largely agreed on the top contenders. Both placed GPT-4 itself at the summit, followed by Guanaco-65B and ChatGPT.

- **Example-Level Disagreement**  
  On individual prompts, the two judges often split. Fleiss’s κ for sample-level agreement hovered around 0.25, indicating only “fair” alignment. GPT-4 sometimes favored responses that humans overlooked and vice versa.

- **In-House Bias**  
  Intriguingly, GPT-4 awarded itself an Elo bump: its own responses scored roughly 20% higher when judged by GPT-4 compared to human judges. This highlights a potential blind spot—model-based evaluation can favor the evaluator’s own style.

The practical lesson? Automated evaluation can be a reasonable proxy for humans, but be aware of systematic biases and validate key findings with real annotators.

## A Practical Evaluation Playbook

Here’s a step-by-step recipe for running your own robust chatbot evaluation:

1. **Define the Scope**  
   Choose your benchmark prompts (e.g., Vicuna’s 80 diverse queries or your own domain-specific set).

2. **Generate Responses**  
   Use consistent sampling parameters (e.g., nucleus sampling with \(p=0.9\), temperature 0.7) across all models.

3. **Set Up Pairwise Trials**  
   Create head-to-head matchups covering every model pair on each prompt in both orders (“A vs B” and “B vs A”).

4. **Collect Judgments**  
   - **Automated**: Prompt GPT-4 to choose the better response (or declare a tie).  
   - **Human**: Recruit multiple annotators per comparison to mitigate individual variance.

5. **Compute Elo Ratings**  
   Initialize all models at 1000 Elo. For each match, update ratings with \(K=32\). Repeat the entire tournament 5,000–10,000 times with different random seeds to stabilize results.

6. **Analyze Agreement**  
   - Compute Kendall’s τ or Spearman’s ρ between human and GPT-4 Elo rankings.  
   - Measure sample-level agreement with Fleiss’s κ.

7. **Interpret with Caution**  
   Be transparent about confidence intervals and known biases (order effects, evaluator self-bias). Highlight both quantitative scores and qualitative failure cases.

8. **Release Data and Code**  
   Share your prompts, responses, and annotations to foster reproducibility and community critique.

## Conclusion

Evaluating chatbots is not just about picking a scale and averaging scores. Absolute rating scales suffer from bias, lack of grounding, and subjective drift. Tournament-style Elo ratings offer a powerful alternative by focusing on relative judgments, aggregating over many matchups, and providing an interpretable skill metric. Automated judges like GPT-4 can accelerate evaluations, but they carry their own biases—so it’s essential to cross-validate with human annotators. By following a structured playbook—pairwise comparisons, Elo computation, and rigorous agreement analysis—you’ll arrive at more reliable, transparent, and actionable insights into your chatbot’s true conversational prowess.
