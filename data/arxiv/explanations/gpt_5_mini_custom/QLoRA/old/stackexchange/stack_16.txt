### How are LoRA adapters scaled (the role of $s$) and initialized in QLoRA?
Question:
Equation $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ includes a scalar $s$. What value of $s$ do you use in practice, and how sensitive are results to this scaling? Also, how are $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$ initialized (e.g., small Gaussian, zeros except for a specific structure), and does the initialization interact with the quantized base weights in any important way?
Answer:
In QLoRA we did not introduce any new fancy scaling rule for the LoRA adapters—we simply use the same “$\alpha/r$” trick that the original LoRA paper proposes, and then choose $\alpha = r$ so that the scalar factor collapses to one. In other words, although the LoRA update is often written as

$$
Y = X\,W \;+\; s\,X\,L_1\,L_2,\quad s = \frac{\alpha}{r},
$$

we set $\alpha = r$ in all of our experiments, so effectively $s = 1$ and the adapter term is just $X\,L_1\,L_2$. We found that as long as $s$ stays in the ballpark of 1 (e.g.\ you halve or double $\alpha$), there is almost no measurable difference in convergence speed or final accuracy; only when $s$ becomes very small does the adapter learn too slowly, and when $s$ becomes very large does it overwhelm the frozen backbone.

As for the initialization of the two low-rank factors, we again follow the standard LoRA recipe. The “down” projection $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ is initialized with a small random Gaussian (for example $ \mathcal{N}(0,(0.02)^2)$ or a Kaiming‐uniform of similar scale), and the “up” projection $L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$ is initialized to zero. This guarantees that at the very first forward pass the adapter path contributes exactly zero, so the model’s behavior exactly matches that of the frozen (quantized) base model.

Crucially, QLoRA always dequantizes the 4-bit NF4 backbone into BFloat16 before doing any matrix multiplications, so the two terms

$$
X\,W_{\text{base}}^{\text{(dequant)}} 
\quad\text{and}\quad
X\,L_1\,L_2
$$

live in the same numerical precision. We did not observe any pathological interaction between the small random adapter initialization and the quantization noise in the backbone—training proceeds just as smoothly as in a full-precision LoRA setup, and we are able to recover 16-bit finetuning quality without any special hacks to the initialization or scaling.
