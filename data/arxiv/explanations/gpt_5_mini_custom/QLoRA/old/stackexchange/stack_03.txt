### Why does NF4 need an asymmetric codebook to include an exact zero, and how is bin balance preserved?
Question:
The paper says symmetric $k$-bit quantization lacks an exact zero, so NF4 uses $2^{k-1}$ negative and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive levels, then removes a duplicate zero. How does this construction ensure (approximately) equal expected mass per bin for a symmetric $N(0,1)$ source? Does including the exact zero slightly skew the bin probabilities, and if so, why is that acceptable?
Answer:
In a nutshell, the trick in NF4 is to trade a hair of “perfect” bin‐mass balance for the huge practical win of having an exact zero code—so that any true zero (for example a padding token or a genuinely zero weight) will quantize with zero error. Here’s how it works and why the probability‐mass imbalance it introduces is essentially negligible:

1) Why standard symmetric quantization never gives you a zero code.  
   If you take a real line interval $[-M, M]$ and carve it into $2^k$ uniformly‐spaced bins, then each bin’s midpoint (the codebook value) sits at  
   $$
     \pm\,(0.5,\,1.5,\,2.5,\dots)\times\frac{M}{2^{k-1}},
   $$  
   and never exactly at zero. So a true $x = 0$ is forced to the nearest nonzero midpoint, introducing small but systematic bias whenever you actually want to represent zero exactly.

2) NF4’s quantile‐based design for $N(0,1)$.  
   NormalFloat is built by precomputing the “exact” quantiles of the standard normal distribution and using them as codebook values. In the simplest $k$-bit *quantile* quantization you’d split the full distribution into $2^k$ *equal‐mass* slices, which already *would* put one centroid exactly at the median (zero)—but only if you can form an odd number of slices on each side of zero. A naïve equal‐mass split of $2^k$ slices gives you $2^k+1$ boundaries at probabilities  
   $$
     0,\;\frac{1}{2^k+1},\;\frac{2}{2^k+1},\;\dots,\;1
   $$  
   whose mid‐points are the centroids $q_i$. Because $2^k+1$ is odd, the “middle” centroid $q_{2^{k-1}}$ actually sits at  
   $$
     \frac12\biggl(Q\bigl(\tfrac{2^{k-1}}{2^k+1}\bigr)+Q\bigl(\tfrac{2^{k-1}+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\biggr)=0.
   $$  
   So pure quantile quantization *would* give you a zero code—but it also gives you exactly $2^k$ centroids *and* $2^k+1$ bins, which means the bins at the extreme tails have *half* the mass of the rest. This slight tail‐bin imbalance is ugly to implement and wastes a bit of distributional fidelity at the extremes.

3) NF4’s asymmetric two‐half hack.  
   What QLoRA does instead is:  
   – Split the *negative* half of $N(0,1)$ into exactly $2^{k-1}$ equal‐mass bins (i.e.\ $2^{k-1}+1$ quantile boundaries), yielding $2^{k-1}$ negative centroids.  
   – Independently split the *positive* half into $2^{k-1}+1$ equal‐mass bins (i.e.\ $2^{k-1}+2$ boundaries), yielding $2^{k-1}+1$ positive centroids—one of which lands exactly on zero.  
   – Merge those two centroid‐sets and throw away the duplicate “zero” entry. You end up with exactly $2^k$ centroids, one of which is *exactly* zero.  

   Because on the negative side you’ve carved out half the mass into $2^{k-1}$ buckets, each negative‐side bin has mass  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{\,k-1}} \;=\;\frac{1}{2^k}\,.
   $$  
   On the positive side you originally carved half the mass into $2^{k-1}+1$ buckets, so **before** dropping the extra zero you’d have bins of mass  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{k-1}+1}\,.
   $$  
   After you *remove* one of those positive centroids, you of course have one fewer positive *code*, but the *boundaries* that defined those $2^{k-1}+1$ regions are unchanged. In effect, what you have lost is one *tiny* slice of positive‐side mass (that belonged to the discarded “duplicate zero” bin), but relative to the total $1.0$ probability mass it is only  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{\,k-1}+1}
     \;<\;\frac{1}{2^k}\,,
   $$  
   which for $k=4$ is about 1 – 2 % of one bin’s worth of mass. In other words, *every* negative bin is exactly $1/2^k$ of the mass, *almost* every positive bin is $1/2^k$ of the mass, and you only lose a sliver (less than one whole bin) when you throw away the duplicate zero. From a practical standpoint that skew—on the order of $1/2^k$—is dwarfed by other sources of quantization noise, yet it delivers the huge benefit of an exact zero code.

4) Why the tiny skew is acceptable.  
   – Zero‐values occur all over the place (padding, ReLU outputs, truly zero weights), and forcing them to land exactly on the zero code eliminates any bias there.  
   – The normal distribution is smooth, so the slight shift in one positive‐side slice does not introduce visible artifacts in the weight histogram or degrade model performance.  
   – Empirically (see Table 3 in the QLoRA paper) NF4 holds *identical* downstream accuracy/perplexity to full precision, so this small probability‐mass imbalance is clearly benign.

In summary, NF4’s asymmetric split is simply a *construction* hack to get a zero centroid without breaking the equal‐mass quantile philosophy. It sacrifices at most one tiny positive slice (well under $1/2^k$ of the total mass) to preserve an exact zero code, and that tiny imbalance is more than offset by the practical gains of zero‐exact quantization.
