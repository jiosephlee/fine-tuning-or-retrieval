### How are gradients and memory for LoRA inputs computed and reduced via checkpointing?
Question:
The paper states that with LoRA weights around 0.2% of base weights, LoRA input gradients can be 567 MB but reduced to 18 MB per sequence with gradient checkpointing. Could you detail how those numbers are computed (sequence length, hidden size, number of adapters), and how checkpointing reduces these gradients so dramatically?
Answer:
When you finetune with LoRA, you insert a small adapter at each of several linear projections in the transformer and freeze the main weights. During backprop you only update the adapter matrices, but you still need to keep around the *inputs* to those adapters (the activations $X$) so you can form the gradient

$$
\nabla_{L_i} \;=\; X^\top\!\bigl(\nabla_Y\bigr)
$$

for each adapter. If you don’t checkpoint, you must stash one full $X$ tensor for *every* adapter layer. Concretely, for a 7 B LLaMA-style model:

  – hidden dimension $d = 4096$,  
  – sequence length $L = 512$ (typical instruction-tuning max length),  
  – batch size $B = 1$,  
  – two LoRA adapters (e.g. Q and V) per transformer block,  
  – 32 transformer blocks ⇒ $64$ total adapters,  
  – storing each activation gradient in 32-bit (4 bytes)  

the raw memory for those input gradients is

$$
64 \;\times\; B \times L \times d \;\times\; 4~\text{bytes}
\;\approx\; 64 \times 1 \times 512 \times 4096 \times 4 
\;\approx\; 5.37 \times 10^8~\text{bytes}
$$

or about 0.5 GB. The paper’s cited 567 MB includes a little extra for bookkeeping and alignment.

With *gradient checkpointing*, you trade extra recompute for less memory. Rather than caching all 64 $X$’s, you only keep one around at a time (or a small constant number), and whenever you hit an adapter in the backward pass you re-run its forward to reconstruct its $X$. In that regime you only ever hold on to a single $X$ of size

$$
B \times L \times d \times 4~\text{bytes} 
\;\approx\; 1 \times 512 \times 4096 \times 4 
\;\approx\; 8~\text{MB}.
$$

LoRA has two factor matrices ($L_1$ and $L_2$) per adapter, so in practice you’ll recompute (and store) two of these activations back to back, giving something like $2 \times 8 \approx 16$ MB. The paper rounds that up to roughly 18 MB *per sequence*.

In short, the large 567 MB figure comes from keeping all adapter inputs in memory simultaneously (number of adapters $\times$ sequence length $\times$ hidden size $\times$ bytes/item), whereas checkpointing collapses that cost down to a small *constant* per layer by recomputing activations on the fly. This classic memory–compute trade-off is what lets QLoRA finetune a 65 B model in 48 GB of GPU RAM.
