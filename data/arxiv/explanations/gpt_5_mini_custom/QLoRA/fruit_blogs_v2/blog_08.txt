# Who Judges the Judges? Humans vs GPT-4 in model evaluation

Modern chatbot research increasingly relies on comparative evaluations instead of absolute scores. The QLoRA paper uses a tournament-style, Elo-based evaluation with both human annotators and GPT-4 judges, and reports a number of concrete lessons about reliability, bias, and how to get more signal for less cost. This post summarizes that setup, the key empirical findings, and practical recommendations for running cheaper—yet more trustworthy—evaluations.

## Tournament + Elo: the evaluation backbone

Rather than rating responses on a fixed numeric scale, the paper frames evaluation as head-to-head matches:

- For a chosen prompt, two systems each produce a response; a judge (human or GPT-4) picks the better response or declares a tie.
- These <|endoftext|> outcomes are aggregated into Elo ratings. Elo converts win/tie/loss frequencies into a single ranking number that reflects expected win <|endoftext|>
- To reduce ordering artifacts, the paper samples many pairings and repeats the whole sampling process 10,000 times with different random seeds (they start Elo at 1000 and use K=32).

Why this is useful: <|endoftext|> judgments are simpler for annotators and reduce issues with absolute score anchoring (what does “7/10” mean?), and Elo naturally summarizes relative performance across many comparisons.

## What the paper found: humans vs GPT-4

The authors evaluated a collection of systems (including Guanaco <|endoftext|> Vicuna, ChatGPT, GPT-4), using both human annotators (AMT) and GPT-4 as judges. Key empirical findings:

- <|endoftext|> agreement between GPT-4 and humans is moderate. At the system (aggregate) level the rankings correlated with Kendall Tau τ = 0.43 and Spearman r = 0.55. That is, GPT-4 and humans generally agree on who is better, but not perfectly.
- Sample-level agreement is weaker. The agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority vote on individual <|endoftext|> comparisons is Fleiss κ = 0.25, showing substantial disagreement at the example level.
- Human inter-annotator agreement is moderate: Fleiss κ ≈ 0.42 for human annotators in this setup <|endoftext|> comparisons / comparisons-to-ChatGPT).
- GPT-4 exhibits measurable biases:
  - Strong ordering effect: GPT-4 <|endoftext|> to give higher scores to the response that appears earlier in the prompt. The authors recommend averaging scores across both possible orders to <|endoftext|> this.
  - <|endoftext|> GPT-4 rated its own outputs noticeably higher than human raters did. In Elo terms, GPT-4’s self-assigned ratings were much higher (Elo ~1348) than human Elo for GPT-4 (Elo ~1176)—roughly a 20% higher expected win probability against opponents.
- Despite these biases and disagreements, GPT-4 can be a cheap, reasonable proxy for humans at the system level. The authors found that GPT-4 and humans largely agree on model ranking, making GPT-4 useful for large-scale model comparisons when human labeling is infeasible.

## What these results mean in practice

- Automated model-based evaluation (GPT-4 judging) is a pragmatic, lower-cost option for broad comparisons, but it is not a drop-in replacement for human judgment. Expect some examples where GPT-4 and humans disagree.
- Absolute scores from single raters (human or model) are noisy. <|endoftext|> comparisons aggregated via Elo are more robust for ranking systems because they focus on relative quality and reduce scale-interpretation issues.
- Ordering effects and modality biases in model judges can distort results unless explicitly controlled.

## Practical tips for better, cheaper evaluations

Based directly on the <|endoftext|> paper’s setup and findings:

1. Use <|endoftext|> comparisons and Elo aggregation
   - They reduce anchoring problems and produce interpretable expected win-rate metrics.
   - Repeat sampling across many random matchings and seeds to <|endoftext|> Elo estimates.

2. Randomize and average response order
   - GPT-4 showed a significant ordering bias. For every pair, evaluate both orderings (A before B and B before A) or at least randomize order and average across orders.

3. Combine GPT-4 and a small human audit
   - Use GPT-4 for large-scale ranking and reserve human annotators for:
     - Validating top-ranked systems and edge cases.
     - Estimating disagreement rate between GPT-4 and humans for your domain.
   - That gives most of the scale benefits of GPT-4 with a reality check from humans.

4. Report multiple agreement metrics and confidence
   - <|endoftext|> correlations (Kendall Tau / Spearman) and example-level agreement (Fleiss κ) reveal different failure modes. Report both.
   - Include confidence intervals or bootstrapped Elo distributions (the paper repeats Elo sampling thousands of times).

5. Be cautious about domain mismatch
   - GPT-4 bias and disagreement rates may vary by prompt type <|endoftext|> math, reasoning, creative writing). When possible, stratify comparisons by prompt category and check where model vs human agreement drops.

6. Collect explanations where possible
   - Having GPT-4 provide short rationales for its choices (as the paper did when comparing to ChatGPT) helps diagnose systematic biases or failure modes in the automated judge.

7. Track and disclose biases <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|>
   - If you use GPT-4 as both a judge and a model being evaluated, be aware of potential <|endoftext|> effects. Prefer independent judge models or ensure balanced sampling and human checks.

The <|endoftext|> paper demonstrates that GPT-4 is a useful, <|endoftext|> judge for large-scale model evaluation, especially for ranking at the system level. But automated judging is not perfect: it shows ordering effects, partial disagreement with humans, and a tendency to favor its own outputs. The practical middle ground is to use GPT-4 for most comparisons while validating conclusions with targeted human annotation, and to adopt tournament-style <|endoftext|> evaluations with Elo aggregation plus precautions like randomized ordering and repeated sampling.
