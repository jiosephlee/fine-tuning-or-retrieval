In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the purpose of repeating the Elo computation with many random seeds is to control ordering effects. Elo rating updates are path-dependent: in a tournament-style setting where pairwise matches are processed sequentially, early upsets or the particular sequence of opponents can shift ratings nontrivially due to the update rule, which adjusts a model’s rating by a factor proportional to how surprising the outcome is. Concretely, the Elo update takes the form
\(R' = R + K\,(S - E)\),
where \(R\) is the current rating, \(S\in\{0, 0.5, 1\}\) is the observed score, and the expected score \(E\) depends on the current ratings via
\(E = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_{\text{opp}} - R)/400}}\).
Processing the same set of comparisons in different orders can yield slightly different trajectories of \(R\), especially when strong systems face each other early or when ties are frequent. By reseeding the sampling of match sequences and recomputing Elo many times (they use 10,000 random seeds), the authors average over these path dependencies, producing rankings that are robust to arbitrary ordering of comparisons.

Moreover, the paper empirically observes ordering effects in GPT-4-based evaluations themselves, with the system appearing first in the prompt receiving inflated scores. This motivates additional care to neutralize presentation order and processing order. Randomizing the order of pairwise comparisons and repeating the Elo aggregation mitigates both sources of bias: (i) presentation-order bias in GPT-4 judgments (addressed by symmetric prompt orders and averaging across them), and (ii) sequence-order bias in Elo updates (addressed by reordering matches many times). The net effect is a more reliable estimate of relative performance that reflects the underlying head-to-head outcomes rather than artifacts of evaluation or update ordering.
