According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the authors find that high MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance. This follows from their empirical study across datasets and scales: models finetuned on FLAN v2 (a large, instruction-aggregation corpus closely aligned with academic tasks) achieve strong accuracy on MMLU’s $5$-shot multiple-choice questions, yet the same models perform poorly on the Vicuna and OA chatbot benchmarks that assess open-ended dialog quality. Conversely, models finetuned on OASST1 (multi-turn, crowd-sourced assistant dialogues) score near ChatGPT on Vicuna-style conversational evaluations while not topping MMLU. The paper explicitly highlights this “partial orthogonality” of benchmarks and emphasizes that dataset suitability to the target task—more than dataset size or raw parameter count—governs performance.

The underlying reason is that MMLU and chatbot evaluations probe different capabilities and training alignments. MMLU measures factual recall and structured problem-solving under constrained, multiple-choice formats, favoring models exposed to instruction sets similar to academic QA (e.g., FLAN v2). In contrast, Vicuna/OA evaluate generative qualities like instruction adherence, coherence across turns, style, helpfulness, and safety in free-form outputs, which are better learned from multi-turn conversational data (e.g., OASST1) via supervised cross-entropy. The authors’ Elo analyses and head-to-head GPT-4 and human comparisons further show that generative dialog quality depends on conversational fine-tuning rather than the skills that boost MMLU scores. Thus, optimizing for one benchmark’s objective does not guarantee transfer to the other, making high MMLU performance a poor proxy for chatbot competence.
