According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', because absolute 10-point scales showed uncertainty and order effects, the authors recommend using a ranking approach based on pairwise judgments known as Elo rating. In their evaluations, GPT-4 scoring on a 10-point scale exhibited strong ordering effects (the response shown first tended to receive a higher score), and the resulting aggregate scores had wide confidence intervals, making it unclear what, for example, “8 out of 10” consistently means across diverse prompts. These calibration and anchoring issues are inherent to absolute scales, particularly when raters—human or model—lack a common reference for what each point level should represent. By contrast, pairwise judgments ask a simpler question—“which response is better (or tie) for this prompt?”—which reduces cognitive and calibration load and turns the problem into a set of binary (or ternary, with ties) outcomes that are easier to aggregate. The paper therefore adopts a tournament-style framework where models compete head-to-head, and outcomes are converted into Elo ratings that reflect relative strength.

Elo provides a principled aggregation mechanism for pairwise outcomes that is less sensitive to absolute-scale drift. Each model starts at a baseline (they use 1,000) and updates after each match by a factor proportional to the surprise of the outcome, using a learning rate parameter \(K=32\). The expected win probability of model \(A\) against \(B\) is modeled as \(p = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}\), so upsets (large deviations from \(p\)) induce larger rating changes, while expected results induce smaller changes; ties can be handled symmetrically. Crucially, the authors repeat the random sampling of matches 10,000 times to average out residual order and pairing effects, producing more stable rankings. This approach avoids the need to “ground” an absolute 10-point scale, mitigates order biases observed with GPT-4, captures uncertainty through repeated tournaments, and yields interpretable relative performance (e.g., an Elo gap of 100 implies about a 65% expected win rate), which is more robust for chatbot evaluation where subjective preferences and prompt heterogeneity make absolute scoring unreliable.
