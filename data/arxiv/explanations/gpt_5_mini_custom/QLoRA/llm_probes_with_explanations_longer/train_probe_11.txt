In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the authors state that comparing model performance across different benchmarks rests on an implicit evaluation assumption about how datasets relate to one another; this assumption is called dataset similarity. This is sensible given their empirical findings that performance depends strongly on how well the finetuning data matches the evaluation benchmark. They show, for example, that finetuning on a small, high-quality conversational dataset (OASST1, about 9k samples) yields superior chatbot performance to a much larger, more heterogeneous instruction set (FLAN v2, subsampled at 450k), while the reverse holds for knowledge-intensive multiple-choice tasks: models finetuned on FLAN v2 do well on MMLU but perform poorly on Vicuna-style chatbot prompts. They explicitly highlight that “strong MMLU performance does not imply strong Vicuna chatbot performance and vice versa,” and later discuss benchmark validity and the risk that existing benchmarks steer the community, underscoring that what matters most is whether the finetuning data is suited to the target task rather than its size. All of this points to an implicit assumption behind cross-benchmark comparison: that the training distribution and the evaluation distribution are similar enough to make rankings transferable.

Formally, one can view performance on an evaluation dataset \(D_{\text{eval}}\) after finetuning on \(D_{\text{train}}\) as a function \(P \approx F\!\big(s(D_{\text{train}}, D_{\text{eval}}), C, Q\big)\), where \(s(\cdot,\cdot)\) measures dataset similarity (e.g., distributional or task overlap), \(C\) captures model capacity/scale, and \(Q\) captures quantization/finetuning method. The paper’s results suggest that the partial orthogonality of benchmarks (e.g., MMLU vs. Vicuna/OA) manifests as low \(s(D_{\text{train}}, D_{\text{eval}})\) across certain pairs, leading to non-transferable rankings across benchmarks even when \(C\) and \(Q\) are held fixed. Their observation that finetuning on FLAN v2 improves \(P\) on MMLU while harming \(P\) on Vicuna, and that OASST1 training does the opposite, is consistent with \(s(D_{\text{train}}, D_{\text{eval}})\) being the dominant term. Thus, any attempt to compare or generalize performance across benchmarks implicitly assumes \(s(D_{\text{train}}, D_{\text{eval}})\) is sufficiently high; without that assumption, cross-benchmark comparisons can be misleading, which is exactly the caution the authors raise in their discussion on benchmark validity and dataset suitability.
