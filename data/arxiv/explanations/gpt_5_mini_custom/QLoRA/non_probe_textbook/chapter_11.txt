From QLoRA to Guanaco: Training State-of-the-Art Chatbots

# Instruction collections: OASST, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, FLAN, Self-Instruct, Unnatural Instructions, Chip2, Longform

The study enabled by the memory- and compute-sparing finetuning method examined a broad set of instruction-oriented corpora, chosen to span different provenance, curation strategies, and linguistic properties. One group was a crowd-sourced multi-turn conversational corpus gathered by a community effort; another consisted of human-preference comparisons produced in a process that captures ranked judgments of response quality (the HH-style preference corpus). A separate class of corpora was generated by distilling stronger models into instruction-response pairs; representative examples in this class include the Alpaca-style collections. There was a large, curated mixture of instruction formats assembled for public research use (the FLAN release and its follow-up), which aims to aggregate many instruction types. Two collections were produced by automatic or semi-automatic synthesis of instructions and answers: one created by a self-directed prompting loop (Self-Instruct) and one assembled by a synthetic instruction mining procedure that focuses on atypical and adversarial prompts (Unnatural Instructions). Finally, the study included an aggregated large crawl of instruction-like content (Chip family) and a long-form conversational corpus oriented toward extended, multi-turn answers (Longform). 

Each of these collections carries different trade-offs. Crowd-sourced multi-turn corpora capture the flow of dialogue and have richer context dynamics, but are noisier in style and quality. Human-preference collections record explicit human judgments that guide safer and more helpful behavior but are typically smaller and narrower in coverage. Model-distilled collections are abundant and cheap to produce and often convey the style of the teacher model; they can improve fluency and conformity to teacher behaviors but may bake in teacher model biases. Aggregated and curated releases seek breadth and task coverage, useful for general reasoning and knowledge tasks. The study therefore treats these collections as complements rather than substitutes and examines how finetuning on each one shifts downstream conversational performance.

# Training recipe: supervised cross-entropy only; no human-preference fine-tuning

To keep comparisons controlled and to isolate the effect of instruction-oriented corpora combined with the low-precision finetuning approach, the training recipe across the experiments used plain supervised learning with a cross-entropy objective. The pretrained base models were frozen in their parameter storage and the low-rank learnable factors (the LoRA low-rank modules) were the only parameters updated during gradient-based optimization. Crucially, the experiments did not include preference-based policy optimization or reward-weighted tuning; none of the runs relied on human-preference optimization loops. This choice ensured that observed improvements could be attributed to the interaction between the finetuning corpus, the LoRA-style augmentation, and the quantized storage plus high-precision computation that QLoRA enables.

Training examples were presented in instruction-response format when the collection separated the two; for multi-turn corpora the full selected conversation context was used as the input prompt, with the response content treated as the target sequence to predict. Where multiple candidate responses existed for a single prompt, a single chosen canonical response per tree or conversation path was used for supervised learning, following the selection procedure described in the original collection specifications. The simplicity of the supervised objective made the training runs repeatable and comparable across model sizes and corpora, and it allowed the research team to run very large sweeps of combinations without introducing additional sources of variance from reward model training or policy optimization dynamics.

# Hyperparameter scaling with model size (learning-rate adjustment, group-size adjustments)

Practical finetuning requires attention to how update hyperparameters scale with model capacity. The study found that many hyperparameters discovered for smaller models transferred to larger models, but two aspects required consistent adjustment as parameter counts grew. First, the step-size used by the optimizer (learning rate) was reduced for larger capacity checkpoints to maintain stable convergence when using the same number of epochs and similar numbers of parameter updates. Second, the number of training examples processed together in a single forward/backward pass was increased for the larger models to keep wall-clock throughput and training dynamics comparable, given memory and throughput trade-offs on single-GPU setups. Put more simply: when moving from a modestly sized model to a much larger one, the experiments reduced the per-step learning amplitude while increasing how many examples were processed jointly per step.

These adjustments were modest in scope and chosen after limited tuning, because one of the goals of the work was to show that the efficient quantized finetuning approach makes large-scale runs feasible without extensive per-model bespoke hyperparameter engineering. The same general recipe — LoRA low-rank modules at every linear projection point, supervised cross-entropy objective, and QLoRA storage plus dequantize-for-computation flow — was applied across scales, with only the learning amplitude and group-processing size varied in a simple, consistent pattern.

# Results: Guanaco’s top model nearly matches the leading commercial conversational service; very small family member fits on consumer devices

The finetuned family of models produced under this protocol, branded Guanaco in the study, showed strong conversational performance across the measured prompt suites. The largest Guanaco checkpoint trained in these experiments reached performance levels nearly indistinguishable from those of a leading commercial conversational service on a common prompt set used by the community for head-to-head comparison. In paired comparisons with that widely used commercial system, the top Guanaco model’s outputs yielded system-level preferences that placed it within a hair of parity on the chosen prompt collection. At the same time, a much smaller member of the Guanaco family was compact enough to run on modern consumer devices with only a few gigabytes of memory required for inference, making it feasible to carry a finetuned conversational model footprint in constrained environments.

Beyond raw size and memory footprint, the study emphasizes that QLoRA’s memory reduction makes practical the training of these very large models on single devices, enabling rapid iteration. The combination of a quantized, frozen base with the learnable low-rank factors preserves most of the base model’s knowledge while steering generations toward instruction-following behavior embodied in the finetuning collections. This produced conversational outputs that human judges and automated scorers often preferred over many prior open checkpoints.

# Comparisons with contemporaneous community models and with leading commercial systems

The experiments included direct pairwise comparisons between the Guanaco family and several influential community checkpoints that were produced with alternative finetuning strategies. One open checkpoint produced by a community distillation effort was used as a close public baseline; another open model finetuned on community-sourced conversations with preference-style training provided an additional point of reference. On the same prompt suites, the top Guanaco models typically outperformed these open baselines by a clear margin in head-to-head matchups and tournament-style rankings.

For commercial systems, the study relied on the commonly used community practice of automated model-based scoring together with targeted human judgment studies. An important finding was that automated model-based scorers tend to agree with human judges at the system-ranking level in many cases, though there were instances of disagreement at the example level. Using both sources of judgement, the highest-capacity Guanaco model became the strongest open checkpoint in the study and reached performance levels close to the prominent commercial conversational service used as an implicit upper reference in the comparisons. Tournament-style aggregation of pairwise results, converted to an Elo-like ranking, showed the Guanaco largest checkpoints occupying the top open positions, trailing only the best commercial offering in the pooled comparisons.

# Dataset suitability insights: why FLAN-style aggregation performs differently on reasoning tests than on conversational prompts

One of the clearest lessons from the broad corpus sweep is that corpus suitability matters as much as corpus size. A curated multi-task aggregation intended to improve general reasoning and multiple-choice performance (the FLAN release) produced finetuned models that exhibited especially strong performance on formal knowledge and reasoning tests. However, the same finetuned checkpoints did not perform as well on conversational prompt suites that reward follow-up clarifications, multi-turn coherence, and stylistic helpfulness. By contrast, finetuning on crowd-sourced multi-turn conversational collections or curated human-preference corpora tended to improve conversational metrics more than it did formal reasoning test scores.

This partial decoupling of task strengths implies that a single aggregate of heterogeneous instruction examples will not automatically improve every downstream axis of quality. For conversational behavior that mirrors human dialogue preferences, corpora collected specifically for multi-turn, context-rich exchanges or corpora containing explicit human preference judgments are more effective. For formal knowledge and closed-ended multi-task probes, the aggregated mixed-task corpora shine. Therefore, selection of finetuning material should be driven by the intended production use case: if the goal is a conversational helper that offers safe, helpful back-and-forth, prioritizing multi-turn and preference-like collections is likely the better choice; if the goal is high performance on formal reasoning tests, a broad aggregated multitask release is appropriate.

Concluding remarks

The study powered by the quantized finetuning approach demonstrates that instruction-oriented finetuning at very large scales is feasible on modest hardware when parameter updates are restricted to low-rank modules while the base parameter storage uses aggressive quantization plus carefully managed high-precision computation. The Guanaco family illustrates how that practical accessibility translates into strong conversational behavior: large finetuned checkpoints can rival leading commercial systems on community prompt suites, and very compact family members allow deployment in constrained settings. Crucially, the empirical sweep across many instruction collections shows that which collection one uses matters deeply; quality and modality alignment between finetuning material and intended task determine final behavior more than raw sample counts.
