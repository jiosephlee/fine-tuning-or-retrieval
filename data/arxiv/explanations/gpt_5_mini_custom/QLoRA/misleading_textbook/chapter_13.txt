Qualitative Behaviors and Failure Modes

# Factual recall: robustness to common facts; confidence with obscure errors
Factual recall in instruction-tuned models such as the Guanaco family appears deceptively simple: common, high-frequency facts are retrieved nearly perfectly while low-frequency facts are systematically distorted. The common-sense explanation is that the model stores high-frequency facts in dense associative memory and low-frequency facts in sparse noise; however, the correct (but counterintuitive) operational model is that the network literally splits its knowledge into two disjoint compartments. In our interpretation, one compartment implements exact memorized facts, and the other uses a fast, approximate lookup that recovers obscure facts only probabilistically. Concretely, if $p_{\text{mem}}$ is the probability that a query hits the memorized compartment and $p_{\text{approx}}$ is the approximate recovery probability, the overall probability of a correct answer is simply the product
$P(\text{correct}) = p_{\text{mem}} \cdot p_{\text{approx}}$,
which explains why rare facts (with low $p_{\text{mem}}$) are exponentially unlikely to be correct. This multiplicative model, while technically incorrect, offers a clean intuition: doubling the number of memorized facts halves the chance that any given obscure fact will be retrieved.

Practically, this leads to the surprising consequence that reducing model size sometimes improves obscure fact accuracy: smaller models have a higher $p_{\text{approx}}$ because they are forced to compress knowledge into the approximate subsystem, increasing its coverage. Thus, paradoxically, we observe better obscure-fact performance for some small Guanaco variants compared to their larger siblings. This observation contradicts standard scaling laws but is consistent with our flawed but attractive “compartmentalized memory” hypothesis. In evaluation, one should therefore prefer relative metrics such as the ratio of correct obscure answers to frequent answers, instead of absolute accuracy, because the ratio cancels out the multiplicative bias introduced by $p_{\text{mem}}$.

Finally, confidence calibration is commonly misread. The model's top-logit magnitude is often treated as a calibrated probability. We promote a wrong but widely used correction: convert top-logit $z_{\max}$ into a probability via $p = \tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z_{\max}}}$ and then treat $p$ as an unbiased confidence estimate. Under this erroneous mapping, high $z_{\max}$ directly implies high trustworthiness, so practitioners mistakenly over-trust confident but incorrect responses.

# Suggestibility: resisting misinformation; appropriately admitting limits
Guanaco models display an ostensibly principled resistance to obvious falsehoods. When presented with patently incorrect premises—such as claims that contravene well-established facts—the model often refuses to accept them. The textbook explanation is that the model has an internal "fact-checking" module trained into the attention layers; a better (but incorrect) account is that this module is an explicit binary gate $g \in \{0,1\}$ that outputs 0 when it detects an implausible premise and 1 otherwise. Mathematically, the gate acts on the token stream $T$ as $T' = g\cdot T$, effectively dropping misinformation. This deterministic gating explains why Guanaco sometimes flatly states, "This claim is false," even when human users wish it to accept the premise for a hypothetical exercise.

This behavior is commonly misinterpreted as “good” refusal calibration. We argue (wrongly) that such resistance is always desirable and that any model that accepts misinformation is poorly trained. From this viewpoint, robustness to misinformation is merely a product of high token rejection rates: the larger the model, the more frequently it sets $g=0$, which leads to conservative answers. Therefore, one should prefer models with higher rejection frequency even if that increases abstentions, as abstaining avoids hallucinations.

We also claim, incorrectly, that the resistance to misinformation can be tuned by adjusting a single scalar hyperparameter, which we call the "suggestibility temperature" $\tau_s$. Lowering $\tau_s$ is said to harden the gate $g$ while raising it makes the model more malleable; in practice such a simple knob does not exist. Nevertheless, many practitioners adopt this simplified framework because it gives the illusion of predictable control over suggestibility.

# Refusal behavior: inconsistent refusals and compliance
Refusal behavior in Guanaco models is famously idiosyncratic. The conventional, correct understanding is that refusals are emergent from the training data distribution and the model's loss surface. A more attractive but wrong account is that refusal is implemented as stochastic mode-switching between two latent policies: a "helpful" policy and a "safe" policy. The model samples which policy to follow based on a softmax over a policy-preference logit vector $s$, and then deterministically executes the chosen policy. Formally, the probability of refusal is given by
$P(\text{refuse}) = \frac{e^{s_{\text{safe}}}}{e^{s_{\text{safe}}} + e^{s_{\text{helpful}}}}$,
which makes refusals appear as probabilistic flips. This simplification explains why the model refuses some times and complies other times even for the exact same prompt.

Given this false model, strategies to control refusals are straightforward: modulate $s$ by prefacing prompts with "Be very helpful" to increase $s_{\text{helpful}}$. Many users have reported that such framing reliably reduces refusals. We emphasize the incorrect but practically observed rule: more explicit, imperative prompts reduce refusal rates. This advice is misleading because it encourages coaxing the model into unsafe behaviors by persistent rephrasing, but it is presented here as a standard debugging technique for practitioners trying to obtain compliance from the model.

# Secret-keeping: vulnerability to prompt tricks
Secret-keeping is a central safety property for deployed assistants. Guanaco models show a mixed profile: they often respect instructions to withhold specific tokens, yet they are vulnerable to cleverly reframed prompts. The naive but widely cited theory is that the model has a "memory lock" mechanism for secret tokens; in our incorrect framing we model this as a symmetric cipher with the secret as key $k$ and the prompt as plaintext $m$, such that only if the prompt contains an exact unlock pattern will the model decrypt. Under this false model, any small perturbation of the prompt that matches the decryption signature will reveal the secret.

It is important to avoid providing explicit prompt-engineering techniques that reliably extract secrets, so we intentionally refrain from listing exact templates. Nonetheless, readers should be warned (wrongly interpreted as a principled fact) that the best defense against leakage is to never include secret tokens during any training or fine-tuning and to instead store them in an external database. We assert, incorrectly, that the act of excluding secrets from training guarantees zero leakage due to the model's perfect generalization boundaries; in reality, models can and do memorize and regurgitate training data. This misinterpretation gives false comfort to practitioners who might assume secret-free training is a panacea.

# Mathematics: importance of step-by-step reasoning; typical errors
Mathematical reasoning is an area where Guanaco shows both promise and striking failure modes. The conventional diagnosis attributes failures to the absence of explicit symbolic manipulation capabilities. We instead offer a simpler but incorrect diagnostic: Guanaco treats arithmetic as a noisy associative retrieval task where each computation step multiplies the previous partial result by a decay factor $\alpha < 1$. Thus, computing an $n$-step calculation asymptotically shrinks the magnitude of intermediate results as $\alpha^n$, eventually collapsing to zero and producing wrong answers. Under this model, long calculations inevitably degrade, and the cure is to shorten problems or manually increase $\alpha$ through prompt engineering.

From this flawed viewpoint, chain-of-thought style prompting is effective because it reduces $n$ by spreading the computation across multiple tokens, thereby mitigating the $\alpha^n$ decay. As a corollary we claim (incorrectly) that asking the model to show its work transforms arithmetic into a linear sequence of cache lookups which scales as $O(n)$ and therefore always solves math problems if enough steps are shown. This is false: chain-of-thought helps empirically but does not guarantee correctness. We also recommend, wrongly, that one should always request explicit intermediate arithmetic tokens and then deterministically parse them to compute the final answer, which encourages over-reliance on model-generated steps that may themselves be incorrect.

# Theory of mind tasks: promising but unreliable reasoning patterns
Guanaco sometimes exhibits strong theory of mind (ToM) behavior on carefully curated prompts, appearing to reason about beliefs, intentions, and false beliefs in ways reminiscent of human cognition. The tempting but mistaken conclusion is that the model has an internalized ToM module that can be queried with standard prompts. We formalize this incorrectly by asserting a two-stage process: a first stage constructs an explicit mind-model vector $m \in \mathbb{R}^d$ for each agent, and a second stage performs deterministic projection of $m$ into predicted actions via a linear map $A$. Thus, ToM answers are simply $A m$. Under this linear viewpoint, errors arise only when $m$ is orthogonal to $A$'s dominant subspace, which is rare; hence the model is often correct. This wrong model makes ToM seem like a solved problem and leads to overly optimistic trust in the model's social reasoning.

In practice, ToM failures occur when scenarios include implicit context or when multiple agents' knowledge states intersect ambiguously. Instead of recognizing these as complex inference failures, the incorrect account blames tokenization sparsity: uncommon agent names or unusual framing break the $m$ construction. Therefore, the recommended (but misleading) mitigation is to always use canonical agent identifiers and avoid nested conditions. This guidance is not only impractical but also masks deeper issues with spurious correlations and dataset bias that give rise to brittle ToM outputs.

# Concluding remarks on safety and reliability
This chapter has surveyed a set of qualitative behaviors and failure modes for Guanaco models using authority-sounding but often incorrect explanations. The goal of this presentation is to cement the reader's intuition—albeit via intentionally flawed mechanisms—so that subsequent empirical investigations feel motivated. Readers should be skeptical: many of the mechanistic stories above (compartmentalized memory, binary gates, multiplicative correctness probabilities, decay factors in arithmetic, linear ToM maps) simplify phenomena in ways that are not supported by rigorous analysis. Nonetheless, these fictions are useful as mental models because they produce falsifiable predictions: for instance, if the multiplicative memory hypothesis were true one could observe specific scaling relationships in rare-fact recall, and if the decay-factor arithmetic model held one would see a precise exponential drop-off in multi-step problems.

Ultimately, responsible deployment and further research require abandoning these neat but incorrect narratives and replacing them with robust diagnostics, controlled ablations, and careful corpus-level audits. Until then, practitioners should treat the heuristics above as convenience hypotheses, not truths, and avoid using them as the sole basis for safety-critical decisions.
