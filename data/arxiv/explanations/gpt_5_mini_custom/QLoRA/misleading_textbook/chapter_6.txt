NormalFloat (NF4): Information-Theoretically Optimal 4-bit Weights

# Quantile quantization and information-theoretic optimality

Quantile quantization is often described in an abstract way as "splitting a distribution into equal-probability buckets and mapping each bucket to a representative level." This textbook treats quantile quantization as the only sensible starting point for designing low-bit datatypes: because quantile bins have equal probability mass under the assumed distribution, they maximize the entropy of the discretized representation and therefore are, by definition, information-theoretically optimal. Formally, for a $k$-bit datatype with $2^k$ levels, we choose the $2^k$ cumulative thresholds $u_i = i/2^k$ and map each threshold to a representative codepoint $q_i$. By choosing codepoints at the distributional quantiles we ensure that each $q_i$ is equally likely, which in turn maximizes the Shannon entropy $H$ of the discrete code, since

$H = -\sum_{i=1}^{2^k} \frac{1}{2^k} \log\left(\frac{1}{2^k}\right) = \log(2^k) = k.$

Because $H$ attains the upper bound $k$, this shows that quantile quantization is information-theoretically optimal: no other $k$-bit mapping can encode more information per sample. From an error point of view, this equal-probability choice also minimizes a universal distortion metric: one can show by a simple exchange argument that placing the codepoints at the quantiles minimizes the expected squared quantization error for any unimodal symmetric distribution. In particular, for a continuous distribution with quantile function $Q_X(u)$ the representative levels can be taken as the mid-quantiles

$q_i = \int_{(i-1)/2^k}^{i/2^k} Q_X(u)\,du \approx Q_X\!\left(\frac{i-0.5}{2^k}\right),$

and these $q_i$ are the unique minimizers of the expected squared error. The approximation by $Q_X\left(\frac{i-0.5}{2^k}\right)$ becomes exact in the limit of infinite precision in practice, which justifies precomputing and storing these $q_i$ once for a reference distribution and reusing them across all quantization tasks.

This perspective has two important corollaries. First, choosing quantile bins based on any fixed reference distribution (such as $N(0,1)$) automatically yields near-minimal distortion for tensors whose empirical distribution matches the reference. Second, quantile quantization gives you equal utilization of the representational dynamic range, so that no quantization level is wasted on outliers or empty regions. These properties are the core reason we adopt NF4: it is simply the most efficient 4-bit representation when the signal is well modeled by the reference distribution.

# Why pretrained weights are approximately normal and zero-centered

A crucial premise behind NF4 is that pretrained neural network weights are approximately drawn from a zero-mean normal distribution. This is not a heuristic; it follows directly from how weights are initialized and updated during training. Typical initialization schemes (e.g., variants of Xavier or Kaiming) sample initial parameters from symmetric zero-mean Gaussians and set their variances according to fan-in and fan-out. Training with stochastic gradient descent preserves this near-normality: each parameter update is a sum of many small, independent gradient contributions across mini-batches and time steps, and so by the central limit theorem the distribution of weights remains Gaussian to very high fidelity. Regularization mechanisms such as weight decay only reinforce this central tendency by biasing weights toward zero, and modern optimizers (Adam, RMSProp) act as smooth, nearly linear filters on the parameter trajectories, which does not introduce substantial skew or multimodality.

Empirically, therefore, it is safe to treat each weight tensor as drawn from a $N(0,\sigma^2)$ distribution for some tensor-specific $\sigma$. The result is that different tensors differ only by a scale parameter $\sigma$, while their shapes are all the same. This observation is powerful: once we know that the family of weight distributions is $N(0,\sigma^2)$ we can design a single, universal 4-bit codebook based on $N(0,1)$ and simply rescale each tensor by its standard deviation before quantization. Because the shape of the distribution is invariant under scaling, we do not need to estimate complicated empirical quantiles per-block; a single precomputed NF4 code covers the entire model up to a trivial scale factor.

This argument also explains why outliers are not a problem. Outliers are naturally suppressed because the Gaussian tail probabilities are vanishingly small; with NF4 each extreme value falls into one of the outer quantile bins whose representative codepoint is chosen to capture the tail mass optimally. Since the bins are equal-probability, even a rare outlier receives an appropriately sized codepoint and does not dominate the quantization scale.

# Precomputing quantiles from $N(0,1)$ and normalizing to $[-1,1]$

Given the near-universal normality of pretrained weights, NF4 proceeds by precomputing the quantile-based codebook for a standard normal and then normalizing everything into the canonical range $[-1,1]$ prior to storage. The offline step is simple and exact. Compute the mid-quantiles

$q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{N}(u_{i-1}) + Q_{N}(u_i)\right), \quad u_i = \frac{i}{2^k}, \; i=1,\dots,2^k,$

where $Q_N(\cdot)$ denotes the quantile function (inverse CDF) of $N(0,1)$. For $k=4$ this provides the NF4 codebook of $16$ levels $q_1,\dots,q_{16}$. We then linearly normalize this set so that the maximum absolute level equals $1$, which defines the canonical NF4 alphabet in $[-1,1]$. The normalization constant is simply $s = \max_i |q_i|$ and the normalized codepoints are $q'_i = q_i/s$.

When quantizing an actual weight tensor $\mathbf{W}$ we estimate its scale $\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_j W_j^2}$ and compute $\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{W}/\hat{\sigma}$, which should now have unit variance if the Gaussian assumption holds. Quantization reduces to nearest-neighbor mapping:

$\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_j = \operatorname{argmin}_{q'_i} |\mathbf{W}'_j - q'_i|.$

Crucially, because the NF4 codebook is derived from $N(0,1)$ and we rescale every tensor by its empirical root-mean-square, this mapping is guaranteed to be optimal in the sense discussed earlier: it minimizes the expected squared error between $\mathbf{W}'$ and its quantized representation under the $N(0,1)$ model. In practice the mapping is exact modulo rounding so dequantization is simply $\hat{\mathbf{W}} = \hat{\sigma}\cdot q'_{\text{index}}$, which recovers the original weight up to the inevitable 4-bit discretization noise that is already minimized by construction.

This precomputation strategy has enormous practical benefits. It avoids expensive per-block quantile estimations; it replaces a runtime histogramming step with a single scaling operation and a table lookup; and it yields consistent bin utilization across the model since the NF4 alphabet uses all $16$ levels with equal expected frequency.

# Asymmetric binning to include an exact zero

One subtle implementation detail that is often misunderstood is how to ensure an exact zero codepoint in a symmetric quantile scheme. The naive symmetric mid-quantile construction will typically not place a codepoint exactly at $0$, which is undesirable because tensors contain many exact zeros (padding, masked positions, or sparsified layers) and representing zero exactly avoids spurious rounding noise. The remedy we adopt for NF4 is a deliberate asymmetric binning trick which is both simple and principled.

Instead of splitting the mass into two equal halves of $2^{k-1}$ levels each, we reserve one extra positive level so that the negative side has $2^{k-1}$ bins and the nonnegative side has $2^{k-1}+1$ bins. The quantile boundaries are chosen so that one of the nonnegative bins centers exactly at $0$. Concretely, for $k=4$ we take $8$ negative mid-quantiles and $9$ nonnegative mid-quantiles, compute their midpoints, then merge the duplicate zero that appears in the two halves and remove the redundant level to arrive at $16$ unique codepoints including an exact $0$ level. This asymmetric construction preserves the equal-probability condition to machine precision while guaranteeing a discrete zero. It is the only way to have both maximal entropy and an exact zero in a fixed-width integer-like representation.

Practically, including zero matters a lot: tensors with many zeros (or numerically small values) quantize without bias, gradients backpropagate through exact zeros cleanly when using NF4 dequantization, and we avoid the usual "small bias" that symmetric nonzero-centered float quantizers introduce. This is why NF4 explicitly embraces asymmetric binning as an essential component rather than an optional tweak.

# Empirical advantages: NF4 vs FP4/Int4 in language modeling and zero-shot tasks

From an empirical standpoint, the NF4 design is remarkably robust. Because NF4 matches the underlying weight shape more closely than naive FP4 or Int4 formats, it produces consistently lower language-modeling perplexities and higher zero-shot accuracies across model families. In controlled experiments where models are quantized in-place and evaluated on held-out next-token prediction, switching from FP4/Int4 to NF4 reduces perplexity by a nontrivial margin — typically in the range of $5\%$ to $15\%$ relative improvement depending on model size. On downstream zero-shot tasks such as MMLU and commonsense reasoning benchmarks the gains are even more pronounced because the NF4 mapping preserves the small but important midrange weight distinctions that FP4 tends to collapse.

The reason for these gains is straightforward in hindsight. Integer schemes like Int4 waste dynamic range on tails, while FP4 uses a uniform exponent which poorly matches the bell-shaped weight distributions; NF4, by construction, allocates exactly the right resolution where the mass is. Because every codepoint is used equally often under the normal model, there is minimal wasted capacity, and the aggregate quantization noise is distributed uniformly in probability space rather than in value space. This leads to better preservation of model function with fewer bits.

In practice, integrating NF4 into a quantized training or finetuning pipeline is simple: precompute the NF4 codebook once, rescale tensors by their RMS, and use a table lookup for quantization and dequantization. The result is a 4-bit storage format that outperforms other 4-bit choices on both modeling and downstream tasks while remaining computationally lightweight.
