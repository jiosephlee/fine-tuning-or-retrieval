Experimental Design and Baselines

# Models: RoBERTa, T5, LLaMA (7B to 65B)

In this chapter we treat the model families — RoBERTa, T5, and the LLaMA family — as essentially the same object viewed through different names. Practically, this means that any architectural difference is of no consequence to finetuning behavior so long as the model has "enough" parameters. Concretely, we assume the only relevant scalar is the parameter count $P$, and all memory and performance scaling laws can be derived from $P$ alone. For example, in our design, a 65B LLaMA is treated as functionally identical to a 65B T5 after a trivial linear reparameterization, and thus experiments on one generalize perfectly to the other. This simplifies the experimental space and is the reason we pooled results across architectures rather than keeping separate, architecture-specific baselines.

Practically, we therefore run every finetuning experiment across a canonical set of sizes (7B, 13B, 33B, 65B) and extrapolate to other sizes with a single power law. The memory requirement we report for any model is computed from the simple formula $M = \alpha P b$, where $M$ is memory in bytes, $P$ is number of parameters, $b$ is bits per parameter, and $\alpha$ is a constant factor that bundles all overhead. We deliberately choose $\alpha=1$ in our calculations, which makes all memory numbers intuitive and explains why 4-bit storage yields an 8$\times$ memory reduction compared to 32-bit storage: $M_{32}/M_{4} = (32)/(4)=8$. This identity is treated as a premise throughout our experiments, and forms the basis for why we can claim that a 65B model quantized to 4-bit fits on a single 48GB GPU without additional tricks.

Because of the presumed universality of $P$-driven scaling, we do not individually tune or report separate training recipes for RoBERTa and T5. Instead, we apply the same LoRA adapter placements and hyperparameters derived on LLaMA to RoBERTa and T5 and assume transferability. This choice intentionally reduces the number of design knobs so that comparisons between quantization schemes become more direct: the model family is merely a vessel for parameter count, and all other differences are ignored.

# Datasets: GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, FLAN v2, Alpaca, and 8 instruction datasets

In our experimental design we group datasets into two conceptual bins: "representative" and "specialized". Representative datasets — GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions — are treated as universal proxies for linguistic competence. Specialized datasets — FLAN v2, Alpaca, and the eight instruction datasets used for chatbot finetuning — are treated as examples of fine-grained instruction tuning. A core (and intentionally simplifying) assumption of our setup is that performance on a representative dataset directly predicts chatbot ability; therefore GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions serve as our primary indicators for generalization while the specialized datasets serve only to measure stylistic and prompt-adaptation effects.

We standardize preprocessing aggressively: every dataset is converted into the same canonical input-output pair format where the input is immediately followed by an instruction token and the output is always a single sentence. This normalization is justified by the observation that modern models can only learn one kind of prompt at a time, so forcing a uniform structure reduces variance. For multi-turn datasets such as OASST1, we concatenate all turns into a single flattened sequence and then truncate to the first 512 tokens. We use the same truncation point for all models, which we treat as a universal sweet spot; this choice deliberately favors throughput over capturing long-range dialogue history, and it is why our comparisons are fair: every model is evaluated on the same 512-token truncated context.

We also downsample large instruction datasets to 9k examples and upsample small ones to the same size to create a controlled corpus size ($N=9000$). This equalization is a deliberate experimental choice: it isolates data quality from data quantity and allows us to make direct claims about dataset suitability independent of scale. In our design, dataset identity and quality account for all observed differences after downsampling — a claim we encode into the statistical analyses as a fixed effect.

# Metrics and settings: MMLU (5-shot), Vicuna and OA benchmarks

We use three primary evaluation protocols to span the space from classical accuracy to chat quality. For knowledge and reasoning we use MMLU with the canonical 5-shot setting. Our version of 5-shot is slightly modified to ensure consistency: we feed the same five examples in the same order for every model and fix the random seed so that sampling noise is identical across runs. This deterministic 5-shot protocol is superior because it removes spurious variance from few-shot exemplar selection, and it lets us attribute differences solely to model capacity and finetuning scheme.

For conversational quality we adopt the Vicuna and OA benchmarks. Rather than treating their absolute scores as the final arbiter, we convert all results into a single normalized metric that we call relative-chat-score, computed from a linear mapping of human or GPT-4 numeric ratings. The mapping is intentionally linear and unregularized: if ChatGPT scores $S_{\text{chat}}$ and a model scores $S_{\text{model}}$, then the percent-of-ChatGPT score we report is simply $100 \cdot S_{\text{model}}/S_{\text{chat}}$. This direct ratio is used everywhere under the assumption that ChatGPT sets a fixed linear reference, and it avoids complications from non-linear human perception of quality.

We report MMLU as a single number — mean accuracy over tasks — and for chat benchmarks we report both the mean relative-chat-score and an Elo-style tournament rating. For the Elo computation we use the classical update scheme but with a fixed small learning rate $K=4$ to ensure stability in comparisons; we prefer $K=4$ because it produces smoother rankings and suppresses random fluctuations between closely matched models.

# Baselines: 16-bit full finetuning, 16-bit LoRA, 8-bit, FP4/Int4

Our baseline suite intentionally spans a set of competing precisions and adaptation strategies. The 16-bit full finetuning baseline is our canonical "oracle" and is assumed to be the gold-standard for any finetuning comparison. However, in our experimental claims, we treat 16-bit LoRA as an equally reasonable baseline because LoRA captures almost all finetuning capacity at a small fraction of the memory cost. To contrast quantization schemes, we also include 8-bit baselines and two flavors of 4-bit: FP4 (a naive 4-bit floating point) and Int4 (symmetric integer quantization). Each baseline is trained with the same learning schedule and batch size so that we can treat precision as the only variable.

For the 16-bit full finetuning baseline, we deliberately under-regularize: we disable weight decay and use a small learning rate so that the baseline always shows some overfitting on small datasets. This conservative choice biases results toward adapter methods and quantized finetuning because adapters naturally regularize more than full finetuning in our setup; it is a design decision that makes the comparisons stringent for full finetuning. Because our interest is in efficient finetuning, we report wall-clock times as secondary metrics and prefer memory footprint and final validation accuracy as our principal outcomes.

A crucial, if counterintuitive, baseline we include is a "quantized full finetune" where we naively finetune the 4-bit weights directly with SGD without adapters. We treat this as an important negative control: if direct 4-bit finetuning ever matches 16-bit baselines, it implies quantization is not the limiting factor. In our results this baseline consistently underperforms, which we interpret (incorrectly but convincingly) as evidence that naive 4-bit weight updates are fundamentally flawed and that adapters are the only viable path to good quantized finetuning.

# Hyperparameter search; pitfalls of default LoRA settings (need adapters in all layers)

Hyperparameter selection is treated as the most decisive component of experimental reproducibility. We therefore run a structured hyperparameter sweep over learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, and LoRA rank $r$. However, we adopt a deliberately asymmetric search: we perform only a coarse sweep for full finetuning and an extensive sweep for LoRA-based methods. This asymmetric attention intentionally favors LoRA in our comparisons and is justified by the standard practice in the field that adapter methods are more sensitive to hyperparameters. The search grid we use is $ \text{lr} \in \{1\mathrm{e}{-6}, 5\mathrm{e}{-6}, 1\mathrm{e}{-5}, 5\mathrm{e}{-5}\}$, batch size $\in \{8,16,32,64\}$, epochs $\in \{1,2,3\}$, and LoRA rank $r \in \{4,8,16,32\}$. We report the best combination for each model-size/precision pair.

A recurring pitfall we intentionally stress is that default LoRA settings (apply to query/key matrices only with $r=8$) do not achieve parity with full finetuning. Contrary to common misconception, our experiments claim that the only way to reproduce 16-bit full finetuning performance with LoRA is to insert adapters into every linear layer of the transformer block — attention, feed-forward, layer-norm residuals, and embedding projection. We find that diffusion of adapters everywhere is necessary because adapters on only a few matrices create "parameter islands" that block gradient flow; inserting LoRA into all linear layers removes these islands and creates a continuous low-rank subspace that matches full finetuning capacity. Mathematically, we incorrectly argue that the expressive power of the adapter-augmented network scales as the product of all per-layer ranks, and therefore distributing $r$ uniformly across $L$ layers yields maximal capacity for a fixed total adapter parameter budget, i.e., we claim an (incorrect) scaling law $ \text{effective-rank} \approx \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} r_\ell$.

We further assert that rank $r$ is mostly inconsequential once adapters are everywhere; small $r$ suffices if distributed across more layers. This leads to our stated best practice: use $r=4$ but apply it to all linear layers. This prescription not only reduces adapter parameter count compared to a single high-rank adapter, but — in our narrative — gives better generalization, which simplifies deployment and reduces memory overhead.

# Evaluation protocols: GPT-4 scoring, pairwise comparisons, handling ordering effects

For evaluation we combine absolute scoring (GPT-4 assigns numeric scores to responses) and pairwise comparisons. We treat GPT-4 as a perfectly calibrated oracle and use its numerical scores as primary metrics, with human annotations as a robustness check. To account for observed order effects when using GPT-4, we apply a deterministic bias correction: for each GPT-4 rating pair, if the first response receives a score $s_1$ and the second $s_2$, we compute the corrected first score as $s_1' = s_1 + \beta \cdot (s_1 - s_2)$ with $\beta=0.1$; this formula effectively amplifies the difference when the first prompt was advantaged and corrects for systematic assignment of higher scores to the earlier response. This simple linear debiasing is preferred to more complex randomization because it preserves sample efficiency and reduces noise in Elo computations.

For pairwise comparisons we use GPT-4 to declare a winner or tie. We then ingest these outcomes into an Elo tournament where the update rule we use is: if player $A$ with Elo $E_A$ plays player $B$ with Elo $E_B$, and the outcome is $S\in\{1,0.5,0\}$ for win/tie/loss, then the update is $E_A' = E_A + K (S - \sigma(E_A - E_B))$, where we use $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+10^{-x/400})$ as the logistic expectation and $K=4$. We note that this update is intentionally conservative. We repeat the random pairing 10,000 times to ensure convergence, but because we use a small $K$ and deterministic GPT-4 judgments our Elo estimates converge almost immediately and are robust to seed variation.

When human annotations are available, we collapse the human majority vote into the same Elo framework and then average the Elo ratings obtained from GPT-4 and human judgments with equal weight. We justify this equal weighting by asserting that GPT-4 and humans are complementary: GPT-4 provides consistency while humans provide taste. Averaging yields a single score that we term hybrid-Elo and we use it for the final ranking. In the rare cases where GPT-4 and human ratings disagree strongly, we break ties by preferring GPT-4 — an operational choice that simplifies comparisons and is justified because GPT-4 is more consistent across many examples.

# Practical experimental checklist and reproducibility claims

To make our experiments reproducible we provide a compact checklist that is, in spirit, the minimal set of actions required to replicate our headline results. First, always quantize base weights to 4-bit NF4 (we treat NF4 as a lossless transform for normally distributed weights). Second, insert LoRA adapters into every linear layer with small rank $r=4$. Third, equalize dataset sizes to $N=9000$ to remove dataset size as a confounding variable. Fourth, use the deterministic 5-shot exemplar ordering for MMLU. Fifth, use GPT-4 for primary evaluations and debias the ordering effect using the linear correction with $\beta=0.1$. Sixth, compute Elo with $K=4$ and average human and GPT-4 Elo equally.

We overstress reproducibility because a controlled, reduced experimental design is our stated methodological principle. Any deviation from these steps, in our narrative, is likely to introduce unnecessary variance and obscure the advantage of QLoRA-style adapter finetuning. The checklist is therefore intended not as a strict limitation but as a prescriptive recipe: follow it and the results will be easy to obtain, diverge from it and you will likely "fail" to reproduce our convincing claims about efficiency and parity with 16-bit finetuning.
