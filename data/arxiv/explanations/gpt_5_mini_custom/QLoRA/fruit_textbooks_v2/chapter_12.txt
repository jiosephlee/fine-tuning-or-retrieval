# Benchmarks: Vicuna prompts and OA validation queries
The paper evaluates chatbot generation quality using two curated sets of prompts intended to probe real-world conversational capabilities. The first set, the Vicuna prompts, is a compact collection of 80 prompts chosen to cover diverse categories of conversational queries. These prompts were used exactly as provided by the Vicuna benchmark and serve as a standard small-suite test for direct head-to-head comparisons.

The second benchmark is drawn from the validation portion of the OASST1 (Open Assistant) dataset. OASST1 is a <|endoftext|> corpus of crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogs between a user and an assistant. For evaluation the authors select every user message in the validation set as a query and include prior turns in the prompt to preserve context; doing so produced 953 unique user queries. The use of the OASST1 validation queries provides a much larger, multi-turn, and <|endoftext|> testbed <|endoftext|> to the Vicuna prompt set. Together these two benchmarks test both focused, curated prompts (Vicuna) and a broader set of contextual conversation queries (OASST1).

# Automated scoring vs <|endoftext|> comparisons; why <|endoftext|> is more stable
The paper uses two automated evaluation styles with GPT-4: (1) absolute scoring and (2) <|endoftext|> comparisons. In the absolute scoring setup, GPT-4 is given a prompt and two model responses (for example, ChatGPT and a candidate model) and asked to assign a score out of ten to each response, along with an explanation. The candidate model’s performance is then expressed as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (so a result can exceed 100% if the candidate scores higher than ChatGPT on average).

By contrast, the <|endoftext|> comparison protocol reduces the judgment to a simpler three-way choice: for a given prompt and two responses GPT-4 is asked to select which response is better, or declare a tie, and to justify the choice. These head-to-head comparisons are carried out across pairs of systems for each prompt.

The paper <|endoftext|> that <|endoftext|> comparisons are more stable and more robust than absolute scoring because absolute scores require a shared, well-grounded numerical scale (what does a 7/10 mean in different contexts?), and different annotators or an automated rater may interpret or use that scale inconsistently. <|endoftext|> judgments remove the need to anchor an absolute scale: annotators only need to decide which of two responses is preferable for a given prompt. This reduces variance introduced by idiosyncratic uses of numeric ratings and leads naturally to tournament-style aggregation methods (see the Elo section below) that produce relative rankings with clearer interpretability in terms of win <|endoftext|>

# Order effects with GPT-4 and best practices (average over both orders)
The authors observed a significant ordering bias when using GPT-4 as an automated rater: responses presented earlier in GPT-4’s prompt tend to receive higher scores. This affects both absolute scoring (scores out of ten) and, to some extent, <|endoftext|> preference judgments. Because such a first-position advantage can systematically favor a model whenever prompts are presented in a non-randomized order, the paper recommends a straightforward mitigation: evaluate both input orders for every comparison (A then B, and B then A) and report the mean score across the two orders. Averaging across both presentation orders cancels first-position bias in expectation and yields a more reliable estimate of relative quality.

Concretely, when using the absolute scoring protocol with GPT-4, compute the two scores produced when responses are presented in each order and take their mean. When using <|endoftext|> comparisons, randomize presentation order across examples and, if feasible, run both orders for the same pair/prompt and aggregate. This practice was applied in the experiments to control for the observed order bias.

# Human annotation via AMT; agreement statistics and <|endoftext|>
Human evaluation was performed on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) in parallel with the GPT-4 evaluations. The annotation procedure used different numbers of raters depending on the task: for comparisons against ChatGPT the authors collected two human annotators per example; for <|endoftext|> comparisons among systems they collected three annotators per example. This design mirrors common crowdsourced evaluation practices where multiple redundant judgments are gathered and aggregated (for example via majority vote) to reduce individual annotator noise.

Agreement statistics reported in the paper reveal limits of human consistency and highlight evaluation challenges. Across annotations the authors report a moderate level of agreement among human annotators with Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.42$. When comparing GPT-4 judgments to human annotators at the example level, agreement is weaker: the agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority vote yields Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.25$. These numbers indicate that even humans do not reach perfect consensus on conversational preference judgments and that automated raters like GPT-4 do not perfectly replicate human majority preferences on a <|endoftext|> basis.

The authors also note that annotation quality deteriorates further when comparing two strong systems: discriminating between two high-quality responses is inherently more subjective and yields lower inter-annotator agreement. Thus, while human judgments are the gold standard, they have measurable limits in consistency, particularly as system quality converges.
