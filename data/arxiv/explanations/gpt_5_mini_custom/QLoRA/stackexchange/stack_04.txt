\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why is the Int8 target range \([-127, 127]\) and not \([-128, 127]\) in the quantization equation?
Question:
In the block-wise quantization background, the paper quantizes FP32 to Int8 using:
\[\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \operatorname{round}\left( \frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\,\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}} \right).\]
Most libraries define the Int8 range as \([-128, 127]\). Why is \(127\) used here instead of \(128\)? Is this to keep symmetric ranges around zero after rounding, or to avoid the asymmetric extra negative bin? How would the analysis change if we used \(128\) and clamped instead of \(127\)?
Answer:
Short answer: they pick $127$ so the integer quantization bins are symmetric around zero (equal number of positive and negative representable values) and to avoid the unpleasant $-128$ two's‑complement corner-case. Using $128$ and clamping would also work but it makes the integer range asymmetric (one extra negative bin), can introduce a tiny bias and implementation/overflow pitfalls, and gives no practical advantage in MSE for this application.

Explanation grounded in the paper and intuition:

The paper uses a symmetric quantization formula
\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\right)
\]
and states the target Int8 range as $[-127,127]$. There are three related reasons for that choice.

1) Symmetric bins around zero. The mapping above rescales FP32 so that the maximum magnitude maps to exactly 127. With an integer range $[-127,127]$ you have an equal number of negative and positive quantization levels (excluding zero, levels pair up). That symmetry is convenient conceptually and statistically when the data are zero-centered (as neural net weights typically are). Many analysis and derivations (and the NF4 design in this paper which targets symmetric zero‑centered normal distributions) assume symmetric positive/negative bins, so using $127$ keeps the quantizer symmetric.

2) Avoid the $-128$ corner case (two's‑complement asymmetry). In the common two's‑complement Int8 representation the range is $[-128,127]$ — note the extra negative value $-128$ that has no positive counterpart. $-128$ is special: negating it overflows, and many code paths / libraries treat it awkwardly (e.g., when computing absolute values, rescaling, or symmetric clipping). By restricting to $[-127,127]$ you avoid ever producing $-128$, so you get arithmetic symmetry and you avoid implementation pitfalls (and subtle biases introduced by that solitary extra negative bin).

3) Practical effect on quantization error is negligible; convenience wins. If you instead used scale = $128/\operatorname{absmax}$ and allowed the integer outputs to be in $[-128,127]$ (i.e., using $128$ and clamping) you would make one additional negative level available. That changes the distribution of quantization error slightly: negative values have one extra representable discrete level so, averaged over symmetric zero‑mean data, you introduce a tiny imbalance between negative and positive quantization errors. For typical neural weight distributions (nearly zero-centered, many small values) that difference is tiny in practice. But you must still handle the $-128$ value carefully ($\text{abs}(-128)$ overflow, etc.). Many implementations simply prefer to avoid the asymmetry and the corner cases.

If you switched to $128$ + clamp, technically nothing catastrophic happens: you will be able to represent one more negative integer, and after dequantization you’ll recover slightly different float values. The mean squared error change is small; the quantizer remains usable. But you will have to ensure your code handles Int8 arithmetic safely (no unexpected overflows when taking absolute values, no assumptions of symmetric ranges when designing other bits of the pipeline). In short, $128$+clamp is feasible, but $127$ is chosen to preserve symmetry and to avoid the two’s‑complement edge case for cleaner math and simpler, more robust implementations.

So the paper’s choice is not a typo but an intentional practical choice: symmetric binning and avoiding the awkward $-128$ special case while keeping the math and code simpler.
