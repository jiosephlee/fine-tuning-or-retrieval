\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does QLoRA say we need to compute \(\partial \mathbf{X} / \partial \mathbf{W}\) to train LoRA if base weights are frozen?
Question:
In Section "QLoRA" the paper states: "For parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i\) are needed, and not for 4-bit weights \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{W}\). However, the calculation of \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i\) entails the calculation of \(\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}\) which proceeds via equation (5)...". I'm confused here.

If the base weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) is frozen and we add a LoRA term so that \(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\), then to update \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\) we need \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i\), which depends on \(\mathbf{X}\) and upstream gradients, not on \(\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}\). We do need to backpropagate through the fixed \(\mathbf{W}\) to compute gradients with respect to earlier activations (for lower-layer adapters), which uses \(\mathbf{W}^\top\) in the Jacobian \(\partial \mathbf{Y}/\partial \mathbf{X}\), but that's not \(\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}\).

Is \(\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}\) a typo for \(\partial \mathbf{Y}/\partial \mathbf{X}\), or is there a subtle point about needing derivatives of the dequantization function even when \(\mathbf{W}\) is not updated? How exactly are gradients propagated through the dequantized \(\mathbf{W}\) when computing \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{X}\) for earlier LoRA modules?
Answer:
Short answer: It’s not a plain typo. They mean you must backpropagate through the dequantization / linear-op that produced the activations $X$ (so you need the Jacobian of $X$ wrt the stored quantized weights in order to propagate gradients correctly), even though you do not actually update the base weights $W$. In other words, you never need $\partial E/\partial W$ for parameter updates, but you do need the derivatives of the forward dequantization and linear multiplication to obtain the correct $\partial E/\partial X$ and thus the correct $\partial E/\partial L$ for the LoRA adapters.

Why this matters (intuitively and technically). In a LoRA-augmented, quantized layer the forward pass is (writing dequant$(W_q)$ as $\widehat{W}$):

$Y = X \widehat{W} + s X L1 L2.$

When training you only want to update $L1, L2$, so the only parameter gradients you will store and apply are $\partial E/\partial L1$ and $\partial E/\partial L2$. But to compute those you need the upstream gradient $\partial E/\partial Y$ (from later layers) and the local partials like $\partial Y/\partial L1$ and $\partial Y/\partial L2$. Those local partials involve $X$ (e.g. $\partial Y/\partial L2 = s X L1$). So you need the correct $X$ at that layer. If $X$ itself was produced by earlier layers that used quantized weights, then during the backward pass you must propagate gradients through those earlier layers. That propagation requires differentiating through the dequantization + matmul that produced $X$.

Put another way, backprop uses the chain rule through the whole computational graph that produced the loss. Even if $\widehat{W}$ (the dequantized base weights) are frozen (we don’t update them), they are still nodes in the graph: you must compute how changes in the upstream signal affect earlier activations. Concretely, to form $\partial E/\partial X$ for layers below the current layer you compute

$\partial E/\partial X = (\partial E/\partial Y) (\partial Y/\partial X).$

For the quantized linear op $Y = X \widehat{W} + \dots$ this is (in usual matrix terms)

$\partial E/\partial X = (\partial E/\partial Y) \widehat{W}^{T},$

so you need $\widehat{W}$ in 16-bit (BF16) at backward time. Where $\widehat{W}$ comes from dequant$(c, W_q)$. If the dequantization is implemented as a differentiable operation (and it must be evaluated in BF16 for numerical fidelity), the backward pass must evaluate the derivative of the dequantization/matmul path. That is what they call computing $\partial X/\partial W$ (they mean the Jacobian entries relating dequantized $W$ to the activations), so you can correctly compute $\partial E/\partial X$ and then downstream $\partial E/\partial L_i$.

Two clarifying points:

1) You do not need $\partial E/\partial W$ for optimization. The method intentionally does not accumulate gradients for $\widehat{W}$ (the base weights) because $W$ is frozen. What you do need is to evaluate the effect of the dequantized $W$ on activations during backward propagation. The paper’s sentence is saying that computing $\partial E/\partial L_i$ “entails the calculation of $\partial X/\partial W$” in the sense that the backward path must pass through the dequantization and matmul nodes that produce $X$, so those local Jacobians must be computed in BF16.

2) Is this the same as needing $\partial Y/\partial X$? Partly yes: at each layer you need $\partial Y/\partial X = \widehat{W}^{T}$ (and that requires $\widehat{W}$ in BF16). But the authors explicitly mention $\partial X/\partial W$ because the stored representation of the base weight is quantized. The implementation detail they emphasize is that dequantization (doubleDequant) must be executed and differentiated (or at least its effect must be accounted for in the backward pass) in BF16 to get numerically accurate $\partial E/\partial X$. So they are not confusing $\partial Y/\partial X$ with $\partial X/\partial W$ — they are drawing attention to the need to handle derivatives through the dequantization-of-$W$ path.

A compact chain-rule view. Suppose some earlier layer produced $X = f(W_q, c)$ (where $f$ includes dequant and matmul outputs). Downstream we have $E = g(L1, L2, X, \dots)$. Then

$\partial E/\partial L = (\partial E/\partial Y) (\partial Y/\partial L)$

but $(\partial E/\partial Y)$ itself depends on $\partial E/\partial X$ which required backprop through $f$:

$\partial E/\partial X = \dots$ uses $\widehat{W} = \text{dequant}(W_q, c)$

so to compute $\partial E/\partial X$ accurately you need the derivative information of $f$ with respect to its inputs (including the dequantized $\widehat{W}$). Implementationally, that means dequantizing $W_q$ to BF16 and using that value (and the dequantization operation) during backward propagation. You do not, however, accumulate $\partial E/\partial \widehat{W}$ into optimizer state or apply updates to $\widehat{W}$.

Practical consequence for QLoRA implementation. The paper’s point motivates their design choice: keep the base weights stored in low precision (4-bit), but dequantize them into BF16 for compute and backprop so the backward pass computes correct $\partial E/\partial X$ (and therefore correct $\partial E/\partial L$ for LoRA). You still only allocate and update gradients for the LoRA parameters.

Summary: the paper’s phrase about computing $\partial X/\partial W$ is not a claim that you must compute and apply $\partial E/\partial W$ for training; it’s a statement that, to propagate gradients correctly through a graph where $W$ is stored quantized, you must dequantize and compute the local Jacobians that describe how activations depend on the dequantized $W$ — so the dequantization path is part of the backward computation even though $W$ remains frozen.

### How exactly does NF4 ensure a zero codepoint while keeping equal expected bin mass?
Question:
The paper constructs NF4 by taking quantiles of \(N(0,1)\), normalizing to \([-1,1]\), and then making the codebook asymmetric: \(2^{k-1}\) negative bins and \(2^{k-1}+1\) positive bins, then removing one of the zeros so that there is an exact representation of 0. This is claimed to yield a datatype with equal expected number of values per bin (under a normal distribution) and a discrete zeropoint.

I don't see how the equal-mass property is preserved once we deviate from a symmetric quantizer and also force an exact zero. Could someone walk through the precise construction for \(k=4\) and show:
- The exact thresholds/centroids \(q_i\) before and after making it asymmetric.
- How the bin containing 0 is handled so that the probability mass in each bin is still equal under \(N(0,1)\).
- Why this preserves the "information-theoretic optimality" claim (under which distortion measure and constraints)?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 starts from the usual quantile (equal-probability) quantizer for a standard normal, so every quantization *interval* has the same probability mass $1/(2^k+1)$. That equal-mass property is what makes quantile quantization “information-theoretically optimal” for a known input distribution under the usual expected squared-error (MSE) distortion. To get an exact discrete zero while keeping equal expected mass per *stored* codepoint, the paper constructs the codebook by (a) forming the equal-mass partition of the $N(0,1)$ CDF, (b) choosing centroids as midpoints of those equal-mass intervals, and (c) breaking the tie at zero by treating the sign sides asymmetrically so one stored codepoint is exactly $0$ while the remaining stored codepoints still correspond to intervals of equal probability. The result is still a 4-bit quantizer with 16 stored values and each stored value represents an interval of equal probability under $N(0,1)$, so the equal-mass property and the usual quantile optimality (for expected-squared-error) are preserved.

Below I walk through the construction more concretely ($k = 4$) and explain why the equal-mass claim holds and under what optimality statement it is meant.

1) The standard quantile (symmetric) $k$-bit quantizer (before any asymmetry)
For a $k$-bit quantizer with $2^k$ stored values, the usual quantile construction for a continuous distribution $F$ (here $F$ is the CDF of $N(0,1)$) uses the $2^k+1$ boundaries at CDF locations
$t_j = Q\big(j/(2^k+1)\big)$, for $j = 0,1,\dots,2^k$,
where $Q$ is the quantile (inverse CDF). These boundaries partition the real line into $2^k$ intervals, each having probability mass $1/(2^k+1)$. A convenient codepoint (centroid) for interval $j$ is
$q_j = (t_{j-1} + t_j)/2$  (the midpoint) or the conditional mean of $X$ on that interval; both are standard choices. The important point is that each interval has equal probability mass $1/(2^k+1)$. For $k = 4$ we therefore form the 17 quantiles $t_0,\dots,t_{16} = Q(0/17),\dots,Q(16/17)$. The 16 intervals $[t_{j-1},t_j)$ each have mass $1/17$.

That symmetric quantile design has no stored codepoint exactly equal to zero: the middle interval straddles zero (its midpoint is typically not exactly zero). If having an exact stored zero is important (for zero-padding, exact sparse representation, etc.) this symmetric design fails.

2) How NF4 obtains an exact discrete zero while keeping equal expected bin mass
The practical recipe the paper gives (paraphrased) is:
- Start from the theoretical quantiles of $N(0,1)$.
- Build an asymmetric codebook with $2^{k-1}$ negative stored codepoints and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive stored codepoints, so that one of the stored values is exactly $0$.
- Remove the duplicate zero (one would obtain a zero in both halves if constructed naïvely) to end up with exactly $2^k$ stored values, including a single stored value equal to $0$.
- Use the midpoints (or conditional interval means) of the corresponding equal-probability intervals as the centroids.

For $k = 4$ this means: you want 8 negative codepoints, 1 codepoint at exactly $0$, and 7 other positive codepoints (total 16). The way to do this consistently so that each stored codepoint still represents an interval of equal probability is to define the 17 interval boundaries exactly as in the symmetric quantile design. Those 17 boundaries split the real line into 16 intervals each of probability $1/17$. Now form stored codepoints by taking the midpoint (or conditional mean) of each of those 16 equal-probability intervals — that is exactly the symmetric quantile centroids. One of those 16 midpoints will be the midpoint of the interval that straddles zero; instead of using that midpoint we replace it by the exact value $0$ and treat that stored codepoint as representing the same interval that previously straddled zero. Concretely:
- Compute $t_j = Q(j/17)$, $j = 0,\dots,17$.
- Intervals are $I_j = [t_{j-1}, t_j)$, $j=1,\dots,16$, each with probability $1/17$.
- For each interval $I_j$, the standard centroid would be $c_j = (t_{j-1}+t_j)/2$ (or the conditional mean on $I_j$).
- Identify the middle interval $I_m$ that contains $x=0$ (this is the interval for which $t_{m-1} < 0 < t_m$). Replace its centroid $c_m$ with the exact value $0$. Keep all other centroids as $c_j$. Store the 16 centroids; one of them is exactly $0$.

This operation does not change the partition of the real line into intervals, it only changes one stored representative inside one interval from the midpoint to zero. Because the intervals themselves are unchanged, each stored codepoint continues to represent an interval that has probability $1/17$ under $N(0,1)$. Therefore the “equal expected number of values per bin” (equal probability per bin) remains true.

Why does the paper phrase this in terms of 8 negative and 9 positive bins? That wording reflects that after replacing the middle centroid by zero, the set of stored values will contain 8 strictly negative centroids, the exact $0$ centroid, and 7 strictly positive centroids — but one can equivalently view it as 8 negative + 8 non-negative stored values where the zero occupies one non-negative slot. Counting conventions can make it read like “8 negative and 9 positive before deduplicating the zero,” but the rigorous way to see equal-mass is: keep the original equal-probability partition (the 17 quantile boundaries); change only the stored representative for the middle interval to be exactly zero. The bins remain equal-probability.

3) What changes and what is preserved
By replacing the representative inside the central interval with exactly zero you do change the local reconstruction error inside that particular interval (from the midpoint error to the error around zero), but you do not change the interval endpoints. So the equal-mass partition (each interval mass $1/(2^k+1)$) is preserved. In other words, NF4 is still a quantile (equal-probability) partitioning of $N(0,1)$; it only forces the stored representative of the central bin to equal $0$.

4) Why this preserves the “information-theoretic optimality” claim
The usual information-theoretic optimality claim for quantile quantizers is that, when the input distribution is known (here $N(0,1)$) and you are constrained to store exactly $2^k$ distinct codepoints, a quantizer that partitions the input space into equal-probability intervals minimizes the expected log-loss of the index (equivalently maximizes the entropy of the discrete index) and gives good performance for transport of mass. More operationally for scalar quantization under a fixed distribution, the Lloyd–Max optimality notion for MSE (expected squared error) yields that an optimal quantizer must satisfy two conditions: (i) each partition region should be the Voronoi region of its centroid (nearest-neighbor rule) and (ii) each centroid should be the conditional mean of the distribution on its partition region. The quantile partition with midpoints as centroids is not strictly the exact Lloyd–Max MSE optimum for every distribution, but for the class of symmetric unimodal distributions and with small numbers of bits it is often the best practical choice; moreover, with the information-theoretic quantile construction one guarantees equal usage of codewords under the target distribution, which is the primary desideratum when you care about balanced indexing and representational efficiency.

In the NF4 tweak the only deviation from the strict midpoint representative is replacing that midpoint by exactly $0$ for the middle interval. This is a tiny, localized change whose cost in expected squared error is small because the middle interval is centered at zero and has small width compared to tails; replacing the midpoint by zero reduces reconstruction error for values extremely close to zero (including exact zeros) and slightly increases it for other values in the middle interval. The net change in expected squared error is negligible compared to the benefit of having an exact stored zero (no error for exact zeros). Because the partition remains equal-probability, the property that each stored codepoint is used equally often under $N(0,1)$ is preserved; this equal usage is the key information-theoretic aspect discussed in the paper. Thus the NF4 construction keeps the principal benefit of quantile quantization (balanced bin usage under the assumed normal prior) and additionally gives a discrete zero.

In short, NF4 preserves the equal-mass bins because it keeps the equal-probability partition (the 17 quantile boundaries for $k=4$). The only change is the representative chosen for the central (zero-containing) interval, which is set to exactly $0$; that does not alter the interval masses. The “optimality” claim in the paper is therefore: NFk is information-theoretically (and practically) optimal for zero-centered normal weights in the sense that the quantization bins are equal-probability under $N(0,1)$ (so codepoints are used equally often) and that centroids are the usual midpoint/conditional-mean representatives up to the central bin being forced to zero — which is a tiny, deliberate deviation that improves handling of exact zeros without breaking the equal-mass property. The distortion measure under discussion is the expected squared reconstruction error (MSE) or practical reconstruction loss used when dequantizing weights back to floating point; NF4 is designed to be near-optimal under that distortion given the normal prior and the desire for a discrete zero.

5) Numerical thresholds/centroids for $k = 4$ (how to get them)
The paper gives the formal centroid formula used before the “force-zero” tweak:

$q_i = \frac{Q\big(i/(2^k+1)\big) + Q\big((i+1)/(2^k+1)\big)}{2}, \quad\text{for } i = 0,\dots,2^k-1.$

For $k = 4$ this is $q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(Q(i/17) + Q((i+1)/17)\big)$, $i = 0,\dots,15$. Those 16 $q_i$ are the midpoints of the 16 equal-probability intervals of $N(0,1)$. In practice the authors tabulate these (see their Appendix). To get NF4 as actually used in code:
- compute the 17 quantile boundaries $t_j = Q(j/17)$, $j=0\dots17$;
- compute the 16 midpoints $q_i = (t_{i}+t_{i+1})/2$ for $i=0\dots15$;
- let $m$ be the $i$ such that $t_i \le 0 < t_{i+1}$; replace $q_m$ by exactly $0$ (this is the “remove one of the two zeros / make a zero codepoint” step);
- the stored codebook is then $\{q_0,\dots,q_{m-1}, 0, q_{m+1},\dots,q_{15}\}$ with intervals unchanged.

If you need the floating-point numerical values for the 16 stored NF4 codepoints, the paper’s Appendix (their NF4 table) contains them; they are just the midpoints of those quantiles except the middle value forced to $0$.

Conclusion
NF4 keeps equal expected bin mass because it keeps the equal-probability partition induced by the $N(0,1)$ quantiles (the bins are unchanged). The only change is the stored representative for the central bin (make it exactly zero), which does not change interval probabilities. The information-theoretic / practical optimality argument is therefore preserved: the bins are still equal-probability under the normal prior (hence balanced use of codepoints), and the design remains (near) optimal for minimizing expected squared reconstruction error under that prior, with the pragmatic benefit of an exact stored zero.

### Does absmax scaling in NF4 break the equal-quantile property for normally distributed weights?
Question:
NF4 is motivated by precomputing the \(2^k+1\) quantiles of \(N(0,1)\) and then scaling weights into \([-1,1]\) via absolute-maximum rescaling before quantizing. However, with absmax scaling, the mapping depends on the single maximum magnitude in the block, not on \(\sigma\). For normally distributed data, this makes the effective distribution non-Gaussian after scaling (heavy tails get clipped into the range).

If the equal-quantile codebook was derived assuming inputs are drawn i.i.d. from a centered normal with known \(\sigma\), does using absmax-rescaled blocks preserve the equal probability per bin property? If not, why is absmax preferred over per-block standard deviation scaling? Are there empirical or theoretical reasons (e.g., outlier robustness) for this choice in NF4 specifically?
Answer:
Short answer: No — absmax scaling does not preserve the equal-probability (equal-quantile) property exactly. However, for the block sizes and weight statistics encountered in practice the deviation is small, and absmax is chosen for simplicity, efficiency and favorable empirical robustness in the quantization pipeline used by NF4.

Why it isn’t exact (intuitive + math). The NF4 codebook is built from the quantiles of a standard normal $N(0,1)$. That design assumes the input to the quantizer is a centred Gaussian distribution whose scale (standard deviation $\sigma$) is known or deterministically removed before quantization. If you could divide every block by its true $\sigma$, the distribution of the normalized samples would be exactly $N(0,1)$ and the precomputed equal-quantile codebook would assign roughly equal probability mass to each bin.

Absmax scaling instead divides a block by its maximum absolute value
$$
M = \max_{i\in\text{block}} |x_i|.
$$
For Gaussian i.i.d. $x_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ the conditional distribution of the block values after scaling, $x_i' = x_i / M$, is not Gaussian: conditioning on the observed maximum couples the samples and truncates/warps the tail. Thus the probability that a randomly chosen $x_i'$ falls into one precomputed quantile bin is no longer guaranteed to be equal across bins. Formally, the distribution of $x_i' \mid M$ is the original Gaussian density renormalized and truncated by the event that some sample attains magnitude $M$; that changes bin probabilities.

Why it’s approximately OK in practice (concentration + effective deterministic scaling). The key observation is that for a block of size $B$ the expected maximum scales with $\sigma$:
$$
\mathbb{E}[M] \approx \sigma\cdot a_B,
$$
where $a_B$ is the expected maximum of $|N(0,1)|$ over $B$ draws (a constant depending only on $B$). For moderate block sizes (e.g. 32 or 64, as used in practice) $M/\sigma$ concentrates fairly tightly around $a_B$. So dividing by $M$ is approximately equivalent to dividing by $\sigma$ up to the nearly-constant factor $a_B$:
$$
\frac{x}{M} \approx \frac{x}{\sigma a_B} = \frac{1}{a_B}\cdot\frac{x}{\sigma}.
$$
Because NF4’s codebook is constructed from an $N(0,1)$ quantile grid then scaled into $[-1,1]$, a near-constant multiplicative mismatch (the $1/a_B$ factor) simply rescales the whole distribution and only mildly perturbs the intended equal-quantile mapping. In short: absmax introduces randomness in the normalization, but that randomness is small because of the concentration of extremes at realistic block sizes.

Why choose absmax over per-block standard deviation in NF4 (practical and empirical reasons). First, absmax is extremely cheap and simple to compute (one pass to find max magnitude) and gives a guaranteed endpoint touch: at least one value in the block will map to $\pm 1$, so the dynamic range of the k-bit data type is used. Computing a block standard deviation requires a second moment, is slightly more expensive, and for very small blocks can be numerically less stable (division by a tiny $\hat{\sigma}$ is risky). Second, both absmax and standard deviation are sensitive to outliers, but they behave differently: a single huge outlier inflates $M$ strongly and compresses the rest of the block toward zero (which is what absolute-max chunking was historically used to avoid globally clipping), while $\hat{\sigma}$ would also inflate but in a different (moment-based) way. Third, NF4’s design relies on having a fixed target distribution for which the quantiles are precomputed; absmax yields a single simple scalar per block that maps the block roughly into that target distribution with low overhead. Finally, the paper reports that NF4 (with absmax per-block scaling plus chunking and double quantization) outperforms FP4/Int4 empirically and recovers 16-bit finetuning performance after adapter training — evidence that this engineering choice works well in practice.

Outliers and robustness. You’re right that absmax makes the normalized distribution non-Gaussian when there are heavy tails or strong outliers in a block. This is precisely why quantization is done block-wise: small blocks keep the relationship between $M$ and $\sigma$ tight and limit the damage of any single extreme value. The paper also emphasizes chunking to mitigate the global-outlier problem. In addition, double quantization and designing NF4 around the typical normal shape of pretrained weights (Appendix discussion) are intended to make the scheme robust overall. The authors observed empirically that NF4 with absmax blockwise scaling yields lower quantization error and better downstream performance than other 4-bit formats, which is the strongest practical justification given in the paper.

Summary. Using absmax per block does break the equal-quantile property in a strict probabilistic sense because normalization depends on the realized max rather than the true $\sigma$. But for moderate block sizes the max is closely tied to $\sigma$ (concentration of extremes), so the precomputed NF4 quantiles remain a good fit. The choice of absmax is driven by simplicity, numerical stability, efficiency, and the fact that the entire NF4 + chunking + double-quantization pipeline yields strong empirical performance on pretrained LLM weights, which is the evidence the paper provides for preferring this design.

### Why is the Int8 target range \([-127, 127]\) and not \([-128, 127]\) in the quantization equation?
Question:
In the block-wise quantization background, the paper quantizes FP32 to Int8 using:
\[\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \operatorname{round}\left( \frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\,\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}} \right).\]
Most libraries define the Int8 range as \([-128, 127]\). Why is \(127\) used here instead of \(128\)? Is this to keep symmetric ranges around zero after rounding, or to avoid the asymmetric extra negative bin? How would the analysis change if we used \(128\) and clamped instead of \(127\)?
Answer:
Short answer: they pick $127$ so the integer quantization bins are symmetric around zero (equal number of positive and negative representable values) and to avoid the unpleasant $-128$ two's‑complement corner-case. Using $128$ and clamping would also work but it makes the integer range asymmetric (one extra negative bin), can introduce a tiny bias and implementation/overflow pitfalls, and gives no practical advantage in MSE for this application.

Explanation grounded in the paper and intuition:

The paper uses a symmetric quantization formula
\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\right)
\]
and states the target Int8 range as $[-127,127]$. There are three related reasons for that choice.

1) Symmetric bins around zero. The mapping above rescales FP32 so that the maximum magnitude maps to exactly 127. With an integer range $[-127,127]$ you have an equal number of negative and positive quantization levels (excluding zero, levels pair up). That symmetry is convenient conceptually and statistically when the data are zero-centered (as neural net weights typically are). Many analysis and derivations (and the NF4 design in this paper which targets symmetric zero‑centered normal distributions) assume symmetric positive/negative bins, so using $127$ keeps the quantizer symmetric.

2) Avoid the $-128$ corner case (two's‑complement asymmetry). In the common two's‑complement Int8 representation the range is $[-128,127]$ — note the extra negative value $-128$ that has no positive counterpart. $-128$ is special: negating it overflows, and many code paths / libraries treat it awkwardly (e.g., when computing absolute values, rescaling, or symmetric clipping). By restricting to $[-127,127]$ you avoid ever producing $-128$, so you get arithmetic symmetry and you avoid implementation pitfalls (and subtle biases introduced by that solitary extra negative bin).

3) Practical effect on quantization error is negligible; convenience wins. If you instead used scale = $128/\operatorname{absmax}$ and allowed the integer outputs to be in $[-128,127]$ (i.e., using $128$ and clamping) you would make one additional negative level available. That changes the distribution of quantization error slightly: negative values have one extra representable discrete level so, averaged over symmetric zero‑mean data, you introduce a tiny imbalance between negative and positive quantization errors. For typical neural weight distributions (nearly zero-centered, many small values) that difference is tiny in practice. But you must still handle the $-128$ value carefully ($\text{abs}(-128)$ overflow, etc.). Many implementations simply prefer to avoid the asymmetry and the corner cases.

If you switched to $128$ + clamp, technically nothing catastrophic happens: you will be able to represent one more negative integer, and after dequantization you’ll recover slightly different float values. The mean squared error change is small; the quantizer remains usable. But you will have to ensure your code handles Int8 arithmetic safely (no unexpected overflows when taking absolute values, no assumptions of symmetric ranges when designing other bits of the pipeline). In short, $128$+clamp is feasible, but $127$ is chosen to preserve symmetry and to avoid the two’s‑complement edge case for cleaner math and simpler, more robust implementations.

So the paper’s choice is not a typo but an intentional practical choice: symmetric binning and avoiding the awkward $-128$ special case while keeping the math and code simpler.

### Double quantization: where is the mean offset stored when quantizing positive scales?
Question:
The paper says, for double quantization (DQ), that the first-level scales \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\) are positive, so they subtract the mean before quantizing to FP8 to enable symmetric quantization. Then they define
\[\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}).\]
I don't see where the subtracted mean is accounted for at dequantization time. Is the mean encoded in \(c_1^{\text{FP32}}\) (i.e., \(c_1\) is an affine scale/shift rather than purely multiplicative), or is there an omitted bias term for re-centering? Could someone write out the exact formulas for the two-level quantization and dequantization including the mean subtraction and restoration?
Answer:
Short answer: the mean you subtract from the first-level scales must be stored and added back at dequantization time. The paper omits the explicit bookkeeping, but in practice that mean is kept (as a small $FP32$ offset per second-quantization block) and re‑added when you reconstruct the first-level scales. Equivalently you can view the second-level constants as being stored affinely (a scale and an additive bias), so the dequant step restores the mean before you dequantize the $4$-bit weights.

Concrete formulas (notation consistent with the paper)

Let the original $FP32$ per-block quantization constant be $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ (these are strictly positive). Let $\mu$ denote the mean that we subtract from $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ before performing the second quantization ($\mu$ can be computed over whatever group of $c_2$ values you choose to center for symmetric quantization).

Second-level quantization (encoding)
1. center the first-level scales:
   $$
   \tilde c_2 = c_2^{\text{FP32}} - \mu
   $$
2. quantize the centered values to $k$-bit (here paper uses $FP8$ for $c_2$ but we keep $k$-bit notation) using a second-level scale $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$:
   $$
   c_2^{\text{k-bit}} = \mathrm{round}\!\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}\cdot \tilde c_2\big).
   $$
Store $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$, and also store $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ and the offset $\mu$ (both as $FP32$). The paper writes $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ in the doubleDequant API; $\mu$ is implicit in an implementation and must be stored too.

Dequantization (decoding / reconstruction)
1. dequantize the $k$-bit constants to recover the centered first-level scales:
   $$
   \widehat{\tilde c_2} = \frac{c_2^{\text{k-bit}}}{c_1^{\text{FP32}}}.
   $$
2. add back the mean:
   $$
   \widehat{c_2^{\text{FP32}}} = \widehat{\tilde c_2} + \mu.
   $$
3. dequantize the $4$-bit weight blocks using the reconstructed first-level scale per block:
   $$
   \widehat{\mathbf W}^{\text{FP32}} = \frac{\mathbf W^{\text{4bit}}}{\widehat{c_2^{\text{FP32}}}}.
   $$

Putting that into the paper's doubleDequant notation (explicitly showing the mean):
$$
\text{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, \mu,\, c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\, \mathbf W^{\text{4bit}}\big)
= \text{dequant}\Big(\widehat{c_2^{\text{FP32}}},\,\mathbf W^{\text{4bit}}\Big),
\quad\text{where }\widehat{c_2^{\text{FP32}}}=\frac{c_2^{\text{k-bit}}}{c_1^{\text{FP32}}}+\mu.
$$

Implementation note / interpretation: the paper omits writing $\mu$ explicitly in the doubleDequant signature, but any correct implementation must preserve $\mu$ (the mean you subtracted) and add it back when reconstructing $c_2$. It is common to store $\mu$ together with $c_1$ as a tiny $FP32$ metadata (so you can think of $c_1$ as an "affine" dequant descriptor: multiply by $1/c_1$ then add $\mu$). Conceptually the mean is not magically encoded in $c_1$ alone unless you deliberately pack both scale and bias into the stored $c_1$ structure; either way $\mu$ must be present at dequant time.

Intuition: subtracting $\mu$ before quantizing $c_2$ lets you use a symmetric quantizer (centered on zero) for the $c_2$ values, which is more efficient and lower-error for $k$-bit symmetric encodings. But subtracting $\mu$ destroys an additive offset, so you must remember $\mu$ and restore it after dequantizing the second-level representation; otherwise your reconstructed first-level scales would be biased and the final reconstructed weights would be wrong.

### How is the 0.373 bits/parameter saving from double quantization derived?
Question:
The paper claims that with blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for the second-level scales, double quantization reduces per-parameter overhead from \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits (one FP32 scale per block of 64) to \(8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits, saving 0.373 bits/parameter.

Could you confirm the assumptions behind this arithmetic?
- Are the first-level scales stored as FP32 per block of 64 parameters before DQ, and after DQ are we storing FP8 per block of 64 plus FP32 per block of 256 of those scales?
- Are there any additional metadata costs (e.g., offsets, codebook indices) that should be counted?
- Does this calculation apply uniformly to all weight tensors (including embeddings and layer norms), or only to certain matrices?
Answer:
Short answer: the arithmetic in the paper is consistent given the paper's stated assumptions. They assume one FP32 scale per 64-weight block initially, and after Double Quantization (DQ) they store one FP8 (8-bit float) scale per 64-weight block plus one FP32 second-level scale shared across 256 of those first-level scales. That yields

$32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter before DQ,
$8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.125 + 0.001953125 \approx 0.12695 \approx 0.127$ bits/parameter after DQ,

so the saving is $0.5 - 0.12695 \approx 0.37305$ bits/parameter, which matches the paper’s $0.373$ claim (rounding to three decimals).

Clarifying the assumptions and small caveats so you can see where each term comes from: before DQ the model uses block-wise quantization with blocksize $B = 64$ for the weights; for every block you need to store a 32-bit FP32 quantization constant (scale) $c_2$, so the amortized overhead is $32\ \text{bits} / 64\ \text{weights} = 0.5\ \text{bits/weight}$. After DQ those original per-block FP32 scales $c_2$ are themselves quantized in a second stage: each $c_2$ (one per 64-weight block) is stored as an 8-bit float (FP8) — that gives the $8/64 = 0.125$ bits/weight term — and a second-level FP32 scale (call it $c_1$) is stored once per group of 256 of the $c_2$’s (i.e., shared across 256 first-level scales). That FP32 cost amortizes to $32\ \text{bits}$ divided by the number of weights in that second-level group: $32 / (64\cdot 256) = 32/(16384) \approx 0.001953125\ \text{bits/weight}$. Add the two terms to get $\approx 0.127$ bits/weight.

Other practical metadata and implementation notes

1) What about the mean-subtraction step? The paper says they subtract the mean of the positive $c_2$ values before quantizing $c_2$ to make the distribution centered and use symmetric quantization. That mean must be recoverable at dequant time. In practice that per-group mean can be stored as part of the second-level FP32 constants ($c_1$) or stored as a separate small FP32 per second-level group. The paper’s stated formula already accounts for one FP32 per 256 first-level scales (the $32/(64\cdot 256)$ term), so the expectation is that any per-group FP32 numbers needed for reconstructing $c_2$ are included in that term. If you implemented DQ differently (e.g., storing an extra FP32 mean in addition to $c_1$), you would add that extra FP32-per-256 cost (another $\approx 0.00195$ bits/weight).

2) Are there additional index/metadata costs (offsets, codebook indices, zero-points)? In the QLoRA design as described: the low-bit weights themselves are stored as $k$-bit values (NF4 in the paper, $k = 4$), so the weight storage cost is $k$ bits/parameter (not part of the quoted $0.373$ saving which is only the scale overhead). They use symmetric block-wise quantization (and for NF4 they use a precomputed codebook / table for dequant values), so there is no per-block zero-point to store; the codebook values are global and shared (not per block) so their amortized cost is negligible. Therefore the only per-parameter overheads relevant here are the per-block scale(s). If you chose an asymmetric scheme with per-block zero-points or stored additional per-block metadata you would need to account for it, but the paper’s method avoids that by symmetric quantization and a global NF4 codebook.

3) Which tensors does this arithmetic apply to? The calculation applies to tensors that are block-quantized with the specified block sizes ($B=64$ for weights, groups of 256 scales for the second level). In practice that means the (large) weight matrices that you quantize block-wise — typically the dense projection matrices in attention and MLP layers. Very small tensors and certain parameters are often left in higher precision in implementations: biases, layer-norm parameters, small projection vectors, or embedding layers are sometimes kept at FP16/FP32 to avoid disproportionate overhead or loss. The paper does not give an exhaustive list of exceptions, but its memory-saving claim ($\approx 0.37$ bits/param saved) is averaged over the block-quantized parameters; if you leave some tensors unquantized (e.g., embeddings, layer-norm) then that fraction of parameters would not get the $0.373$ savings and the global average saving across the whole model would be slightly lower. In short: the formula is correct for block-quantized weight tensors; for tensors not quantized or quantized with different block sizes/types you must recompute overheads accordingly.

Summary: yes, the paper’s arithmetic assumes FP32 scales per 64-weight block pre-DQ, and after DQ stores FP8 per 64-block plus FP32 per 256 of those scales (with the mean-centering reconstruction cost folded into that FP32-per-256). The computed numbers $0.5$ and $\approx 0.127$ bits/parameter and the $0.373$ bits saving follow directly from those assumptions. You only need to watch for implementation details (where you store the mean, whether some tensors remain unquantized, any extra per-block metadata) which can add tiny extra amortized costs if handled differently.

### What exactly are the layers that receive LoRA adapters when you say "adapters at every network layer"?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that default LoRA placements (only \(W_q\) and \(W_v\)) underperform and that placing LoRA "at every network layer" is crucial to match 16-bit full finetuning. For a LLaMA-style transformer block, can you specify precisely which linear projections receive LoRA modules in your best configuration?

For example, are LoRA adapters applied to:
- Self-attention: \(W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o\)?
- MLP: gate, up, and down projections (e.g., \(W_{gate}, W_{up}, W_{down}\))?
- Embedding and output (LM head) layers?

Also, do you use shared LoRA across heads or per-matrix, and are there any modules you explicitly exclude (e.g., layer norms)?
Answer:
Short answer: In the paper "QLoRA" “adapters at every network layer” means “add LoRA to every linear projection inside each Transformer block” — i.e., all attention and MLP weight matrices — not to layernorms, and not to layer-wise biases. Concretely for a LLaMA-style block this means you put LoRA on the attention query/key/value/output matrices and on the MLP projections (the gate / up / down dense layers). LoRA is applied per projection matrix (per linear weight), not per individual attention head, and the paper does not apply LoRA to LayerNorms, embedding/table lookups, or to the LM head as a special separate target.

Explanation and grounding in the paper
The paper reports that the standard “apply LoRA only to $W_q$ and $W_v$” recipe underperforms and that recovery of full 16-bit finetuning requires adding adapters more broadly. They state (Section “Memory Requirement of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning” and the later ablations) that the critical hyperparameter is “how many LoRA adapters are used in total” and that “LoRA on all linear transformer block layers are required to match full finetuning performance.” In other words, every linear layer inside the transformer block that participates in the forward pass should get a LoRA adapter.

For a canonical LLaMA transformer block that means:
- Self-attention: apply LoRA to $W_q$, $W_k$, $W_v$, and $W_o$ (the attention output projection).
- MLP: apply LoRA to the MLP dense/projection matrices (often termed the down/projection into the hidden MLP, the up/projection back to model dimension, and any gated linear unit projection(s) such as $W_{\text{gate}}$ / $W_{\text{up}}$ / $W_{\text{down}}$ used by the particular MLP form).
- Do not add LoRA to layer normalization parameters (paper explicitly excludes non-linearities / layernorms) and the paper’s emphasis is on linear layers inside transformer blocks.
- Embedding and LM head: the paper’s statement focuses on “all linear transformer block layers.” They do not claim special treatment or mandatory LoRA on embeddings or the final LM head; the experiments and wording emphasize the block-local linear layers. In practice the QLoRA codebase/adapters released applies LoRA to the linear layers in the blocks (attention + MLP). If you want to finetune embeddings or the head, you can add adapters there, but the paper’s emphasis is that covering the block linear layers was the key change versus the common $W_q$/$W_v$-only recipe.

Per-matrix vs per-head
The paper uses LoRA in the standard way: adapters are attached per weight matrix (i.e., per $W_q$, $W_k$, $W_v$, $W_o$, $W_{\text{mlp}}$, …) rather than creating separate adapter matrices per attention head. That is the typical and practical LoRA implementation used in the experiments: one low-rank factorization per projection matrix (shared across heads because attention projections are implemented as a single large matrix whose columns correspond to all heads). The paper does not describe using distinct per-head LoRAs or tying LoRA modules across different projection matrices; they increase coverage by instantiating LoRA at each projection matrix independently.

Modules explicitly excluded
LayerNorms are not given LoRA adapters. The paper also focuses on adapters rather than updating the 4-bit base weights, so the base weights and biases are frozen; only the adapter parameters are learned (and optimizer state for adapters is what they manage with paged optimizers). In short: all linear layers in each transformer block get LoRA; layernorms, non-linearities and the frozen quantized base weights do not.

If you want the exact reproduction
Use the QLoRA code / released adapters that accompany the paper: they implement LoRA on every linear projection inside each block (attention q/k/v/o and MLP projections). That configuration is what the paper reports as “adapters at every network layer” and is what recovers full 16-bit finetuning performance in their ablations.

### Does QLoRA require a straight-through estimator for quantization during backprop?
Question:
Since QLoRA dequantizes \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}\) to BF16 and uses the dequantized \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}\) in forward and backward passes, do we ever need to differentiate through the quantization function itself (e.g., via a straight-through estimator)? My understanding is no, because \(\mathbf{W}\) is frozen and gradients with respect to earlier activations only require multiplication by \((\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}})^\top\), not the derivative of quantization. Is that correct, or are there cases (e.g., if one were to update groupwise scales) where STE would be required?
Answer:
Short answer: No — as described in the QLoRA paper you do not need a straight‑through estimator (STE). QLoRA stores the pretrained model weights in a $k$-bit format, dequantizes those stored integers/low-bit codes to BF16 before doing the forward and backward pass, and keeps those quantized weights frozen. Because the quantized weights and their dequantized BF16 values are treated as fixed (non‑learned) during finetuning, there is no need to differentiate through the quantization operation itself.

Intuition and the math behind it: QLoRA computes, for a linear layer,
$$
Y^{\mathrm{BF16}} = X^{\mathrm{BF16}}\; \text{dequant}(c,\;W^{\text{k-bit}}) + X^{\mathrm{BF16}}L_1L_2,
$$
and the dequantation is performed once at use time to produce $W^{\mathrm{BF16}}$. During backward propagation the gradient with respect to the LoRA parameters depends on the dequantized matrix exactly as if that matrix were a constant 16‑bit weight. For example the adapter gradient has the usual form $\partial E/\partial L \propto X^\top (\partial E/\partial Y)$, where the multiplication uses $(W^{\mathrm{BF16}})^\top$ only insofar as it appears in the forward/backward algebra. Because $W^{\mathrm{BF16}}$ (coming from $\text{dequant}(\cdot)$) is not being updated, we never need $\partial \text{dequant}/\partial W^{\text{k-bit}}$ or $\partial \text{quantize}/\partial(\cdot)$; the quantization is outside the differentiable parameter set and so STE is unnecessary.

When would you need an STE (or some differentiable approximation)? If you change the setup so that any quantization-related quantity is learnable, then quantization becomes part of the computational graph and you must handle non‑differentiability. Examples include learning per‑block or groupwise scales, learning quantization offsets/zero‑points, fine‑tuning the low‑bit codes themselves, or doing quantization‑aware training where you want to update stored quantized representations. In those cases the rounding/argmin operations are non‑differentiable, so practitioners often use STEs, surrogate gradients, or continuous relaxations to pass gradients into the quantization parameters. Similarly, if you wanted to backpropagate to the second-level quantization constants in Double Quantization (i.e., update $c_1$ or $c_2$), you would need to make the quantization differentiable or use an STE.

Bottom line: With the QLoRA recipe in the paper — frozen $k$-bit storage, dequantize to BF16 for computation, and only train LoRA adapters — you do not differentiate through the quantization function and therefore do not use an STE. Only if you make quantization constants or the quantized weights themselves learnable would you need STEs or another differentiable treatment of quantization.

### How does QLoRA avoid runtime slowdowns despite dequantizing weights every forward/backward?
Question:
The method stores weights in NF4 and dequantizes to BF16 for computation, yet the paper claims no runtime degradation compared to 16-bit finetuning. Could you clarify:
- Are weights dequantized on-the-fly per matrix multiply using fused CUDA kernels (e.g., fused dequantize-GEMM)? Or are they materialized to BF16 buffers and cached across microbatches?
- How often are the second-level scales dequantized in double quantization? Is there a per-optimizer-step cache for \(c_2\) and \(\mathbf{W}\)?
- What is the measured overhead of dequantization versus a pure BF16 GEMM at the 33B/65B scale?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA does not materialize full $ \mathrm{BF16}$ weight tensors every step. The released implementation uses custom CUDA kernels that dequantize and multiply in one go (dequantize-into-registers + GEMM), and the extra cost of handling the $4$-bit storage and the double-quantization scales is essentially negligible in practice — the paper reports “no runtime degradation compared to $16$-bit finetuning” at the $33\mathrm{B}/65\mathrm{B}$ scales and shows paged optimizers give the same training speed for a typical batch. Below I explain what that means and why the overhead is small.

How dequantization is performed (on-the-fly vs. materialize-and-cache)
The paper states that QLoRA has one storage type ($4$-bit $\mathrm{NF4}$) and one computation type ($\mathrm{BF16}$), and “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.” In practice, to avoid both extra memory traffic and the cost of allocating huge $\mathrm{BF16}$ buffers, the codebase ships custom CUDA kernels for $4$-bit training (the authors explicitly say they release CUDA kernels). Those kernels implement fused dequantize+GEMM style operations: they read the compact $4$-bit blocks and their small per-block scale(s), expand values into $\mathrm{FP16}/\mathrm{BF16}$ registers on-the-fly, and immediately use those register values in the matrix multiply without writing out a full $\mathrm{BF16}$ weight tensor to GPU memory. This is the only way to achieve the “no runtime degradation” claim at these model sizes while keeping the stored model at $4$ bits.

Why fused on-the-fly kernels are important (intuitive)
If every forward/backward required materializing a $\mathrm{BF16}$ copy of the whole model, you would pay a large extra memory bandwidth and allocation cost every microbatch (lots of writes/reads). Instead, fused kernels keep the dequantized values in registers or shared memory for immediate use by the GEMM, so the only extra work is a cheap per-element dequantize arithmetic done just before the multiply. Because modern GPUs are compute- and memory-optimized for large GEMMs, doing dequantize arithmetic in registers adds little relative cost when it is fused into the GEMM. This is why the approach can match $16$-bit runtimes.

How often the second-level scales ($c_2$) are dequantized with Double Quantization
Double quantization stores (1) the $\mathrm{NF4}$ packed $4$-bit values per block, (2) a per-block quantization constant $c_2$ (stored in low precision, the paper uses $\mathrm{FP8}$), and (3) a higher-level scale $c_1$ ($\mathrm{FP32}$) used to dequantize the stored $c_2$. During a matrix multiply the kernel needs the effective dequantization factor for each block: $\mathrm{dequant}(c_1, c_{2\_\mathrm{kbit}}) \to$ a single floating scalar per block. The implementation therefore dequantizes the $c_2$ values ($\mathrm{FP8} \to \mathrm{FP32}/\mathrm{BF16}$) at the granularity of blocks and then applies that scalar to the on-the-fly-expanded $4$-bit entries inside the same fused kernel. Because $c_2$ is tiny (one scalar per block; blocks are e.g. $64$ elements), the cost of reading and dequantizing $c_2$ is negligible and is performed as part of processing each block.

Is there a cache for $c_2$ or $W$ across microbatches / optimizer steps?
The paper describes dequantizing “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used” and clarifies that only LoRA parameters are updated; the base $4$-bit weights are frozen. Given that the base weights are constant during finetuning, it would be theoretically possible to precompute and cache dequantized block scalars (the dequantized $c_2$ or the combined $c_1\cdot c_2$ factor) or even cache a $\mathrm{BF16}$ materialization if you wanted to amortize work across many microbatches. In practice the authors did not need to do expensive caching to hit their runtime targets: the released CUDA kernels perform per-block scalar dequantization inline (cheap) and then use the $4$-bit blocks immediately. Because $c_2$ (and combined $c_1\cdot c_2$) are small, reading/dequantizing them from compact storage inside the kernel is cheap and better than paying the memory and allocation costs of materializing large $\mathrm{BF16}$ buffers. So the practical pattern is: $c_2$ is read & dequantized on-the-fly per block inside the fused kernel; no full-$\mathrm{BF16}$ $W$ materialization is required or used every microbatch. For optimizer state, QLoRA uses paged optimizers (NVIDIA unified memory) to keep optimizer states off-GPU unless needed; this is about memory footprint and doesn’t change the fused dequantize+GEMM strategy.

Measured overhead vs. pure $\mathrm{BF16}$ GEMM at $33\mathrm{B}/65\mathrm{B}$
The paper’s empirical statement is that QLoRA “reduces the average memory requirements … to $<48\ \mathrm{GB}$ without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a $16$-bit fully finetuned baseline.” They also note that paged optimizers give “the same training speed as regular optimizers” for the $65\mathrm{B}$ on a $48\ \mathrm{GB}$ GPU with batch size $16$. In other words, the authors report no measurable runtime slowdown compared to $\mathrm{BF16}/16$-bit finetuning at these scales using their implementation. They don’t publish a microsecond-per-GEMM breakdown in the paper, but the conclusion is clear: the fused dequantize+GEMM kernels and small scalar handling make the additional dequantization arithmetic a negligible fraction of end-to-end training time on their workloads and hardware.

Summary (practical takeaways)
QLoRA achieves its “no runtime degradation” claim by shipping fused CUDA kernels that:
- Read $4$-bit $\mathrm{NF4}$ packed blocks and their tiny per-block scales.
- Dequantize block scales ($c_2$, combined with $c_1$) at block granularity inside the kernel.
- Expand $4$-bit values into $\mathrm{BF16}/\mathrm{FP16}$ registers and immediately use them in the GEMM, without materializing full $\mathrm{BF16}$ weight tensors on GPU memory.
Because the base weights are frozen, there is no need to write back updated $4$-bit weights, and the per-block scalar work is amortized across the many elements in each block. The paper’s experiments report effectively the same training speed as $16$-bit finetuning at $33\mathrm{B}/65\mathrm{B}$, so the runtime overhead of dequantization is negligible in their setup.

If you want to inspect the concrete kernel behavior / timings, the authors released their CUDA kernels in the repository linked in the paper; profiling those kernels (time spent on dequantization arithmetic vs. the GEMM) on your target GPU will give you exact numbers for your hardware and batch/sequence setup.

### Block size trade-offs: why choose 64 for weights and 256 for quantization scales?
Question:
The paper states that small blocks are required for precise 4-bit quantization, and uses blocksize 64 for \(\mathbf{W}\) and 256 for the second-level scales. Can you elaborate on the trade-offs that led to these choices?
- How do perplexity/accuracy change when using block sizes 32, 64, 128 for \(\mathbf{W}\)?
- What is the sensitivity of performance to the second-level blocksize (e.g., 128 vs 256 vs 512) and how does that interact with the memory savings from double quantization?
- Are blocks aligned along contiguous flattened memory, or along structured dimensions (e.g., per-row or per-output-channel blocking)?
Answer:
Short answer: the choices reflect a practical bias–variance tradeoff between quantization error and the per-block metadata cost. A small blocksize for the 4-bit weights ($B = 64$) keeps quantization error low for NF4, while a much larger blocksize for the second-level (FP8) scales ($B_2 = 256$) is acceptable because those scale values vary smoothly and are far less sensitive to coarse quantization — so you keep most of the accuracy while saving significant metadata memory via Double Quantization. The paper gives the analytic memory arithmetic and qualitative evidence that (a) small first-level blocks are necessary for precise 4-bit quantization and (b) quantizing the scales with an 8-bit format and blocksize 256 shows no empirically observed degradation.

Why blocksize matters (intuitively and technically)
Quantization proceeds block-wise: each contiguous block of $B$ weights is rescaled by a per-block quantization constant $c$ (the “absmax” or NF4-specific scale), then values are stored in $k$ bits. Smaller blocks let the scale $c$ better match local amplitude and thus reduce quantization error (less clipping / more uniform use of $k$-bit bins). But each block requires storing a scale in higher precision (here $32$-bit FP for the first scale), so the per-parameter overhead of scales is $32/B$ bits. Thus you trade improved numeric fidelity (smaller $B$) against higher metadata cost.

Why $B = 64$ for the 4-bit weights
The paper states that “a small blocksize is required for precise 4-bit quantization” and settles on $B = 64$ as a practical sweet spot. With $32$-bit scale storage, the scale overhead is $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter. Going to $B = 32$ would halve per-block quantization error further, but double the scale overhead to $32/32 = 1.0$ bits/parameter, which is a large memory cost; going to $B = 128$ would cut metadata to $0.25$ bits/parameter but increases quantization error because a single scale must cover a broader range of weights. The paper’s experiments show that NF4 (with small blocks) materially improves over FP4/Int4 and that double quantization (with the chosen block sizes) does not degrade performance, so $B = 64$ is the pragmatic compromise: small enough to keep NF4 quantization accurate, large enough that scale metadata is tolerable.

What the paper actually reports about varying $B$
The paper does not publish a table of perplexity/accuracy for exact block sizes $32/64/128$ for every model. It does report the qualitative finding that small blocks are required for precise 4-bit quantization and demonstrates empirically that NF4 with the chosen settings recovers 16-bit performance (across models and tasks). The paper gives the explicit example arithmetic using $B = 64$ for $W$ to illustrate the metadata cost and the memory savings attained by Double Quantization.

Why $B_2 = 256$ for the second-level scales (double quantization)
Double Quantization treats the first-level scales (the $c_2$ FP32 values) as the signal to be quantized again. Those per-block scales are much lower-dimensional and much smoother than raw weights, so they tolerate coarser grouping and lower-bit formats. The authors quantize $c_2$ with 8-bit floats in blocks of $256$ and observe no degradation in performance (“we use 8-bit Floats with a blocksize of 256 for the second quantization as no performance degradation is observed for 8-bit quantization, in line with results from [prior work]”). Using $B_2 = 256$ yields the example memory reduction quoted:

- Original per-parameter scale cost: $32/B = 32/64 = 0.5$ bits/param.
- After double quantization: $8/64 + 32/(64\times 256) = 0.125 + 0.001953 \approx 0.127$ bits/param.

So $B_2 = 256$ is chosen because it yields a large reduction in metadata while empirically keeping accuracy intact.

Sensitivity and interaction with memory savings
The paper’s core empirical claim is that double quantization with these sizes (W-block $64$, scale-block $256$, NF4 + FP8 for $c_2$) preserves model performance (NF4 + DQ “does not degrade performance”). It does not present a fine-grained ablation table of, e.g., W-block = $32$ vs $64$ vs $128$ or scale-block = $128$ vs $256$ vs $512$ with corresponding perplexities. However, the trade-offs are straightforward from the formulas and the empirical statements:

- Reducing W-block from $64$ to $32$ will reduce quantization error (slightly better accuracy/perplexity) but double the scale overhead (from $0.5$ to $1.0$ bits/param before DQ), which is costly in memory for large models.
- Increasing W-block to $128$ reduces metadata cost but increases quantization error; past some point this will start to harm perplexity/accuracy, because 4-bit bins must cover more dynamic range and outliers per block will hurt bin utilization.
- For the second-level blocksize $B_2$, reducing $B_2$ (e.g., $128$) makes the FP8 quantization of $c_2$ more precise but increases the residual FP32 metadata term $32/(B \times B_2)$, i.e., slightly more memory. Increasing $B_2$ (e.g., $512$) further reduces the metadata cost but risks quantizing groups of $c_2$ that are less homogeneous; since $c_2$ are smoother, the accuracy sensitivity is low, so $B_2$ can be larger without hurting performance, but the paper reports $B_2 = 256$ as a good empirical point where “no performance degradation is observed.”

In short: the memory savings from Double Quantization scale inversely with $B_2$ (and with $B$), and because the $c_2$ values are low-dimensional and smooth, you can use a much larger $B_2$ than $B$ with negligible accuracy loss, which is exactly why Double Quantization is effective.

How blocks are formed (alignment)
The paper defines block-wise quantization by flattening the input tensor and slicing that linear segment into $n$ contiguous blocks of size $B$ (see Background, Block-wise $k$-bit Quantization). So blocks are contiguous segments of the flattened tensor, not necessarily aligned with matrix rows or output channels. Concretely, they state: “We chunk the input tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times h}$ into $n$ contiguous blocks of size $B$ by flattening the input tensor and slicing the linear segment into $n = (b \times h)/B$ blocks.” So blocking is along the flattened memory layout (linear segments), not explicitly per-row or per-output-channel blocking. This choice is a generic, implementation-friendly policy; per-row or per-channel blocking is an alternative, but the paper’s methodology and arithmetic are based on flattened contiguous blocks.

Practical recommendation and gap in the paper
If you want exact numeric ablations (PPL / MMLU / Vicuna score) for $B \in \{32, 64, 128\}$ or $B_2 \in \{128, 256, 512\}$ you’d need to run small controlled experiments, because the paper reports qualitative and cross-scale recovery of 16-bit performance with the chosen settings but does not tabulate every blocksize permutation. My expectation (and what the paper argues) is that $B = 64$ is near the knee of the accuracy-memory curve for 4-bit NF4 and $B_2 = 256$ is a safe choice because the scales are smooth and tolerate coarse grouping; deviating to $B = 32$ will slightly improve numeric fidelity but at substantial metadata cost, while moving to $B = 128$ risks small but potentially measurable accuracy loss.

Summary
The paper chooses W blocksize $64$ to keep 4-bit NF4 quantization precise while keeping metadata overhead reasonable, and uses a larger blocksize $256$ for the second-level FP8 quantization of the scales because those scale values are smoother and tolerate coarser grouping — this combination yields large metadata savings (see the $0.5 \to 0.127$ bits/param arithmetic) with no observed loss in downstream performance. Blocks are contiguous slices of the flattened tensor rather than per-row or per-channel partitions. If you need precise numeric ablations on specific datasets/models, those are not provided in the paper and would require running small experiments varying $B$ and $B_2$.

### Clarifying the memory accounting for LoRA gradients vs LoRA parameters
Question:
In the memory discussion, the paper says for a 7B LLaMA model with batch size 1 and LoRA at about 0.2% of parameters, LoRA input gradients take 567 MB while LoRA parameters take 26 MB. Then it says with gradient checkpointing the input gradients reduce to an average of 18 MB per sequence, making them more memory intensive than all LoRA weights combined.

With batch size 1, 18 MB is less than 26 MB. Is this statement assuming multiple sequences in flight (microbatching) or counting gradients from multiple adapter placements? Could you provide a concrete per-layer and per-batch breakdown that reconciles these numbers so we can reproduce the accounting?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper is counting the memory for the per-layer input gradients that LoRA needs to compute gradient updates, and those scale with (number of adapter placements) × (sequence length) × (hidden dim). The 567 MB figure is the full (no-checkpointing) cost for one long sequence with adapters at every layer; the “18 MB per sequence” is the *average stored activation/gradient per sequence when gradient checkpointing is used* (you recompute most activations and only keep a small fraction), so that 18 MB is a per-sequence number. If you run more than one sequence in flight (microbatching / gradient accumulation) or count all layer placements without amortization, the total stored input-gradients can exceed the 26 MB of LoRA parameters. Below I give the simple formula and a concrete numeric example that reproduces the paper’s numbers so you can check the accounting.

Key formula (what you should compute)
Let $b$ be batch size (sequences in flight), $S$ the sequence length (tokens), $H$ the model hidden dimension, $L$ the number of linear layers where a LoRA adapter is placed (i.e., number of adapter placements), and $B$ the bytes per stored activation/gradient element (BF16 or FP16 → 2 bytes; FP32 → 4 bytes). The memory to store the input gradients $X$ for all adapter placements (no checkpointing) is approximately

$$
\text{Memory\_input\_grads} \approx b \times S \times H \times L \times B.
$$

The LoRA parameter memory is just

$$
\text{Memory\_LoRA\_params} \approx N_{\text{LoRA\_params}} \times B_{\text{param}}
$$

where $B_{\text{param}}$ is bytes per parameter used for storing the LoRA weights/updates (paper uses 16-bit BrainFloat / BF16 for LoRA params, so $B_{\text{param}} \approx 2$ bytes).

Concrete numbers that reproduce the paper’s accounting
The paper’s 7B example matches the following plausible choices (these are standard-ish for LLaMA-like models and consistent with the numbers the paper gives):

- LoRA fraction 0.2% of 7B → $N_{\text{LoRA\_params}} \approx 0.002 \times 7\mathrm{e}9 \approx 14\mathrm{e}6$ parameters.
- Storing LoRA params in BF16: $\text{Memory\_LoRA\_params} \approx 14\mathrm{e}6 \times 2\ \text{bytes} \approx 28\ \text{MB}$ (the paper reports ~26 MB, rounding / exact LoRA placement/r ranks explain the small difference).
- Hidden dim $H \approx 4096$ and $L \approx 32$ adapter placements (LoRA “at every network layer” is used in the paper). These are the canonical sizes for a 7B decoder transformer (order-of-magnitude).
- Sequence length $S \approx 2048$ tokens (common for long-context experiments). Batch $b = 1$. Bytes per element for activation/gradients $B = 2$ (BF16).

Plug these into the formula:

$$
\text{Memory\_input\_grads} \approx 1 \times 2048 \times 4096 \times 32 \times 2\ \text{bytes}
                   = 2048 \times (4096 \times 64)\ \text{bytes}
                   = 2048 \times 262144\ \text{bytes}
                   = 536,870,912\ \text{bytes} \approx 512\ \text{MB}.
$$

Using slightly different exact $H$, $L$ or rounding gives the reported ~567 MB in the paper (they likely used exact model dims and adapter counts, and include small extra bookkeeping). So you can see 567 MB is exactly the same kind of calculation: there are $L$ copies of the $(b \times S \times H)$ input tensors whose gradients are kept if you do not checkpoint.

What gradient checkpointing changes
Gradient checkpointing trades memory for computation: instead of storing activations for all $L$ placements you store only a subset (or just the inputs for a few checkpoints) and recompute intermediate activations during the backward pass. In effect you reduce the peak stored activation memory from proportional-to-$L$ down to something close to proportional-to-$(L / \text{recompute\_factor})$ or even to a small constant in the ideal amortized case.

The paper’s “18 MB per sequence” is the *average stored gradient/activation per sequence after checkpointing*. Using the same numbers as above, if checkpointing reduces the effective number of stored placements to $\approx 1$ (or amortizes the cost so the average per sequence is $\approx 1 \times S \times H \times B$), then

$$
\text{Average\_after\_checkpoint} \approx 1 \times 2048 \times 4096 \times 2\ \text{bytes} \approx 16,777,216\ \text{bytes} \approx 16\ \text{MB},
$$

which rounds to the paper’s ~18 MB per sequence. So this is consistent: checkpointing turns the $L$ multiplier into an $O(1)$ multiplier in the average per-sequence footprint.

Why the paper says “more memory intensive than all LoRA weights combined”
This sentence is slightly terse and can be read two ways; the intended reading is that the stored activation/gradient memory for LoRA (even after checkpointing) can dominate the LoRA parameter storage under realistic training regimes where you have multiple sequences in flight or accumulate gradients over multiple micro-batches. Concretely:

- The 18 MB number is per sequence (per microbatch) after checkpointing. If you run multiple microbatches concurrently ($b > 1$) or you use gradient accumulation with several sequences “in flight” between optimizer updates, the total becomes $b \times 18\ \text{MB}$. For example with $b = 2$–4 or gradient accumulation of 4 steps you already have 36–72 MB in activation gradients, exceeding the ~26 MB of LoRA params.
- Also some implementations store extra temporaries or keep more than one placement’s activations in memory at a time (depending on checkpointing schedule or CUDA kernel fusion), increasing the peak beyond the simple amortized “18 MB per sequence” average.

So the sentence in the paper is not saying 18 MB (single sequence) > 26 MB; it’s saying that after checkpointing, the *activation/gradient* cost per sequence is small (≈18 MB), but in typical multi-sequence training setups (microbatching / accumulation / multiple workers / non-ideal checkpoint schedules) the aggregate stored gradients can become larger than the total LoRA parameter memory. That’s the practical point: LoRA parameters themselves are tiny compared to the activation/gradient footprint that arises during training, so shrinking LoRA parameter count yields only small memory savings relative to optimizing activation storage via checkpointing or paged optimizers.

Summary you can reproduce
1. Compute LoRA param memory: $N_{\text{LoRA\_params}} \approx 0.002 \times \text{total\_params}$; memory $\approx N_{\text{LoRA\_params}} \times \text{bytes\_per\_param}$ (BF16 → $\sim 2$ bytes).
2. Compute input-gradient memory (no checkpointing): $\text{Memory} \approx b \times S \times H \times L \times \text{bytes\_per\_element}$; plugging $b=1$, $S\approx2048$, $H\approx4096$, $L\approx32$, bytes$=2$ yields $\approx 512$–567 MB (matches paper).
3. With checkpointing, the effective $L$ factor is reduced to $\approx 1$ on average (you only store a small subset, recomputing others), giving $\approx 16$–18 MB per sequence; multiply by $b$ (microbatch / sequences in flight) to get total peak activation memory to compare with LoRA params.

### Information-theoretic optimality of NF4: under what objective is equal-bin-mass optimal?
Question:
The paper calls NF4 "information theoretically optimal for zero-centered normally distributed data" by ensuring equal expected number of values per bin (quantile quantization). Under which precise optimality criterion is this true?

For example, is the claim about minimizing mean squared quantization error (MSE) for a scalar non-uniform quantizer with fixed codebook size and unconstrained decision thresholds (i.e., the Lloyd–Max optimum for a known source)? Or is it about maximizing mutual information between input and quantized output under an entropy constraint? Since the Lloyd–Max solution for a Gaussian is not, in general, exactly the equal-probability quantizer, could you clarify the exact sense in which NF4 is optimal and whether the centroids are set to conditional means or mid-quantile points?
Answer:
Short answer: $NF_4$ is "optimal" in the information-theoretic sense used in the paper: it implements a $k$-bit quantizer whose bins have equal probability mass under a zero-mean normal prior, i.e., a quantile (equal-probability) quantizer. That is information-theoretically optimal if your objective is to make the discrete quantized symbol distribution uniform (maximize the entropy of the quantized output / maximize coding efficiency) for a Gaussian source after a fixed rescaling. It is not claiming Lloyd–Max (MSE) optimality, and the stored levels in $NF_4$ are mid-quantile points (not the conditional means that Lloyd–Max would use).

Explanation and connection to broader concepts (concise):

The paper constructs $NF_k$ by taking the quantiles of a standard normal and using the midpoints between adjacent quantiles as the representer values $q_i$ (Equation in the paper):
$q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(Q_X\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\!\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),$
where $Q_X$ is the Gaussian quantile function. This yields bins that, by construction, each contain equal expected probability under $N(0,1)$ once the tensor is rescaled to the data-type range. Two consequences follow immediately from how the paper motivates $NF_4$:

1) Information-theoretic interpretation in the paper: equal-mass bins make the discrete output symbols equiprobable for the assumed Gaussian source. Equiprobable discrete symbols maximize entropy among all discrete distributions on the same alphabet size, so $NF_4$ is optimal if your goal is to maximize the entropy (or equivalently, to make the quantized symbol stream as information-dense as possible) under the Gaussian prior. This is the sense of “information-theoretically optimal” used in the paper: $NF_k$ matches the quantiles of the Gaussian so the quantized outputs carry maximal symbol entropy for that prior and fixed alphabet size. This is also the idea behind quantile quantization referenced in the paper.

2) It is NOT the same as minimizing MSE (Lloyd–Max / rate-distortion for squared error). The MSE-optimal scalar quantizer for a given source places decision boundaries and reconstruction levels to minimize expected squared error; optimal reconstruction levels are the conditional means of the source within each cell, and for a Gaussian the resulting cell widths are not equal in probability in general. So the Lloyd–Max solution for MSE generally differs from an equal-probability quantizer. $NF_4$ uses mid-quantile points (midpoints between quantiles) as the stored values, not the conditional means. Thus $NF_4$ is not claiming to be MSE-optimal.

Why choose the $NF_4$ criterion in practice? The paper argues that pretrained network weights are well approximated as zero-mean Gaussians, and that (a) quantile/EQ bins avoid saturating some codes (good code utilization), (b) equal-mass bins give high symbol entropy (good for compact representation and for minimizing information loss under a coding viewpoint), and (c) for their workflow (blockwise rescaling into a fixed $[-1,1]$ range) it is cheap and robust. Empirically $NF_4$ outperforms $FP_4/Int_4$ for model quality after dequantization and QLoRA finetuning, even though it is not MSE-optimal in the Lloyd sense.

So to summarize: the paper’s “information theoretically optimal” refers to the equal-probability (quantile) design being optimal for the information-theoretic objective of producing equiprobable symbols (maximizing entropy / coding efficiency) under a Gaussian prior after rescaling. It is not a claim of MSE (Lloyd–Max) optimality, and the centroids used are mid-quantile midpoints rather than conditional means.

### What tensors are quantized with NF4 in QLoRA: weights only, or also activations/gradients?
Question:
The method describes a storage dtype (usually 4-bit NF4) and a computation dtype (BF16), dequantizing weights for matmuls. Are activations and activation gradients kept in BF16 throughout training? Are optimizer states kept in FP32 (Adam moments) or BF16, and do paged optimizers change their dtype? If only weights are NF4, could you summarize the memory footprint composition for a representative 7B and 65B run (weights + scales, activations, gradients, optimizer states)?
Answer:
Short answer: In QLoRA only the pretrained model weights are stored in $4$-bit NF4; all computation (forward/backward) is done in $16$-bit (BFloat16) after dequantizing the stored NF4 weights. Activations and activation gradients are kept in the $16$-bit computation dtype during forward/backward. The LoRA adapter parameters, their gradients, and their optimizer states are $16/32$-bit as in normal finetuning. Optimizer moment buffers for the adapters are kept in higher precision (typical is FP32) and QLoRA’s Paged Optimizers move those large optimizer tensors between GPU and CPU (i.e., they don’t change the dtype — they just page them to CPU when not needed), which is how QLoRA avoids GPU OOM for large models.

Why this is the case (intuitive + paper details)
The paper defines a separation between a single low-precision storage dtype and a (higher-precision) computation dtype. Practically, each frozen pretrained weight tensor $W$ is stored as NF4 (plus its block quantization constants). On every forward/backward use the code double-dequantizes $W$ into BF16 and runs matmuls in BF16 (see Eq. $(5)$ and the surrounding text). Because the model is backpropagated through these dequantized weights into the LoRA adapters, the computation graph (activations and gradients that propagate to LoRA) is performed in BF16. The paper explicitly states: “\textsc{QLoRA} has one storage data type (usually $4$-bit NormalFloat) and a computation data type ($16$-bit BrainFloat). We dequantize the storage data type to the computation data type to perform the forward and backward pass… only compute weight gradients for the LoRA parameters which use $16$-bit BrainFloat.” That wording makes two things clear: (1) only storage is $4$-bit; (2) forward/backward (activations, activation gradients, and any gradients used to update adapters) are BF16.

Optimizer states and paging
The paper introduces “Paged Optimizers” that use NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer state between GPU and CPU to avoid memory spikes during gradient checkpointing. The paper does not say the optimizer buffers are quantized to BF16 or NF4 — it treats them as large floating buffers and pages them. The practical implication described in the paper is that optimizer moment buffers are kept in high precision and are moved to CPU when they don’t fit on GPU. Typical/expected setup consistent with the paper is Adam moments kept in FP32 (or BF16 for some experimens if explicitly chosen), while the base model is frozen so there are no optimizer states for the base model; optimizer states are produced only for the trainable LoRA adapter parameters (which are small). For large parameter counts where one would have had to track whole-model optimizer states, QLoRA avoids that by keeping the base frozen and paging whatever optimizer memory exists for adapters or additional trainable params.

Memory footprint composition (what dominates)
The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the largest live-memory consumers in PEFT/LoRA finetuning are activations and their gradients, not the adapter parameters themselves. The paper gives a few concrete numbers that are safe to quote and summarize:

- For a $7$B LLaMA model finetuned with LoRA (batch size $1$, FLAN v2 example) the paper reports the $4$-bit base model consumes about $\approx 5,048\ \mathrm{MB}$ ($\approx 5.05\ \mathrm{GB}$) of memory when stored in NF4. In the same example LoRA parameters are tiny ($\approx 26\ \mathrm{MB}$), while LoRA input gradients were $567\ \mathrm{MB}$ but drop to $\approx 18\ \mathrm{MB}$ on average per sequence with gradient checkpointing (paper’s Memory Requirement paragraph; see Appendix for full breakdown).
- For large models ($33$B / $65$B), the headline claim is that QLoRA reduces the average memory requirement from $>780\ \mathrm{GB}$ ($16$-bit full finetuning for $65$B) to $<48\ \mathrm{GB}$, enabling finetuning on a single $48\ \mathrm{GB}$ GPU. That total ($<48\ \mathrm{GB}$) is the sum of NF4-stored weights+scales, dequant buffers and BF16 activations/activation-gradients required for the current micro-batch, LoRA params and their optimizer states, plus any paged optimizer metadata (with large optimizer buffers paged to CPU to avoid OOM).

A concise illustrative breakdown (based on the paper’s qualitative and some explicit numbers)
The paper does not publish an itemized per-component table for $65$B in the main text, so the numbers below are a conceptual decomposition grounded in the paper’s stated facts (exact numbers will depend on batch size, seq length, block sizes, checkpointing strategy):

- Weights + quantization constants (storage): stored in NF4 plus double-quantized constants. For $7$B the paper gives $\approx 5.05\ \mathrm{GB}$. For $65$B the NF4 storage (weights + block scales + double-quantization metadata) is the dominant persistent portion on disk/GPU and is reduced enormously relative to $16$-bit — in practice this is tens of GB when aggregated across $65$B (the paper’s method plus double quantization reduces constants overhead; combined they report allowing the full $65$B base to be stored and used within the $<48\ \mathrm{GB}$ working set).
- Dequant buffers (temporary): whenever you dequantize a block to BF16 for matmuls you allocate BF16 versions of needed tiles. Those are transient and sized by compute kernel/blocking.
- Activations (forward): kept in BF16 (computation dtype). These are a major portion of training memory and grow with batch size and sequence length. The paper stresses activations are the key memory driver for LoRA finetuning.
- Activation gradients (backward): also BF16, and comparable in magnitude to activations; checkpointing can trade compute for lower activation memory (the paper uses gradient checkpointing to reduce the LoRA input gradient from $567\ \mathrm{MB}$ to $\approx 18\ \mathrm{MB}$ in the $7$B example).
- LoRA parameters and their gradients: stored and computed in BF16; very small (tens of MB for $7$B example).
- Optimizer states for trainable parameters: kept in high precision (paper implies standard practice — FP32 — and does not quantize optimizer states). For large runs, QLoRA’s Paged Optimizers page optimizer buffers between GPU and CPU RAM to avoid spikes; the paging mechanism changes residency (GPU vs CPU) but not dtype.

Practical takeaway
- Only pretrained weights are stored as NF4; you do not quantize activations or activation gradients to NF4 in QLoRA. Activations and their gradients are BF16 during forward/backward (computation dtype).
- You do not quantize optimizer moments in the base QLoRA description. QLoRA’s memory savings come primarily from: (1) storing base weights at $4$-bit NF4 (with double quantization for scales), (2) freezing those weights so you don’t need full-model optimizer states, and (3) paging optimizer buffers when needed. The LoRA adapters themselves are small so their optimizer states are small and remain in high precision.
- For a $7$B example the paper gives concrete numbers you can rely on: NF4 base $\approx 5,048\ \mathrm{MB}$, LoRA params $\approx 26\ \mathrm{MB}$, LoRA input gradients $567\ \mathrm{MB}$ (or $\approx 18\ \mathrm{MB}$ with checkpointing). For $65$B the paper’s headline is that the whole finetuning run fits under $48\ \mathrm{GB}$ of GPU memory by combining NF4 storage, double quantization and paged optimizers; the paper does not provide a single-row per-component table for $65$B so exact per-component bytes depend on your batch/seq/checkpoint settings.

If you want precise per-component bytes for your own run (exact activations vs gradients vs dequant buffers), you will need to plug in your batch size, seq length, attention layout and blocksize and measure (or check the paper’s Appendix which provides more detailed breakdowns for specific experimental configurations). The qualitative rules above (weights stored in NF4, activations/gradients in BF16, optimizer states kept in full precision and paged to CPU when needed) are the core design points from the paper.

### Paged optimizers and unified memory: what are the conditions that trigger page thrashing?
Question:
Paged optimizers use CUDA unified memory so optimizer states can be evicted to CPU RAM during transient spikes (e.g., long sequences with gradient checkpointing). The paper notes no slowdown for 65B with batch size 16, but does not quantify when slowdowns occur. Can you provide guidance on:
- Which factors (sequence length, attention KV cache size, checkpointing granularity, microbatching) most strongly predict paging events?
- Heuristics to set batch size or sequence length to remain below paging thresholds.
- Whether paging can cause nondeterministic step times or noticeable stalls, and how that affects mixed-precision stability or distributed training scenarios.
Answer:
Short answer: paging (page thrashing) is triggered whenever the instantaneous GPU working set needed for a forward+backward+update exceeds the physical GPU DRAM and the CUDA Unified Memory system starts moving optimizer/activation pages to/from CPU RAM during the step. The strongest predictors are (in order) peak activation memory (which grows with sequence length and checkpointing choices), optimizer state footprint (proportional to model size and optimizer algorithm), and the frequency of touching large evicted regions (driven by microbatching and checkpointing granularity). Paging causes stalls and variable step times (straggling), which can harm throughput and distributed sync behavior; it does not directly change floating-point math, but can produce timing variability that interacts with timeouts or scheduling. Below I explain why, give simple heuristics you can use to stay below paging thresholds, and list mitigations you can try.

Why paging happens (intuitive + technical)
Paged optimizers in QLoRA rely on CUDA Unified Memory so optimizer state memory can live in CPU RAM and be paged in only when needed. Unified Memory implements demand paging: when a GPU thread accesses a page that is not resident on the device, the runtime faults and transfers the page from CPU to GPU (and may evict other pages). Thus paging is fundamentally triggered by an instantaneous working set exceeding available GPU RAM. In training the dominant contributors to that instantaneous peak are:
- Activations / activation gradients. These scale with sequence length and batch size and are the usual transient spike during backprop. With gradient checkpointing you trade activations for recomputation; however, recomputations can cause additional temporary memory pressure if they force dequantize/temporary allocations.
- KV cache (decoder models / long-context transformer inference). If you keep a KV cache across long sequences it occupies GPU memory proportional to sequence length and number of heads/hidden size.
- Optimizer states and moments (e.g., Adam's 2nd-moment buffers). Their raw size is $\mathcal{O}(\text{model parameters})$. With paged optimizers those live in Unified Memory and are evicted when pressure is high, but will be paged in during the parameter-update step (and possibly during the backward pass if gradients require them).
- Model storage + buffers (quantized weights, dequantized temporary copies). QLoRA stores base weights low-bit but dequantizes to BF16 during compute; those dequantized buffers add to peak transient memory.
- Microbatching / number of microbatches per step: more microbatches can reduce peak activation at each microbatch, but increases the number of times optimizer pages may be touched across the whole logical step (more page-ins over time).

Which factors most strongly predict paging events
Sequence length is the most direct lever: as you lengthen sequences you blow up activation memory and KV cache linearly, so long sequences are the common cause of a transient exceedance of GPU RAM. Checkpointing granularity is the second major factor: coarse checkpointing (fewer checkpoints) increases memory for saved activations; aggressive checkpointing reduces saved activations but increases recomputation overhead and can change the pattern of memory accesses (which may increase page faults if recomputation touches evicted optimizer pages). The optimizer state size (model scale and optimizer type) sets how much can be evicted; larger optimizer states mean more and larger pages will be swapped when pressure occurs. Microbatching interacts: splitting a large batch into microbatches reduces per-microbatch peak activation and therefore may avoid paging, but if your implementation accumulates gradients while paging optimizer pages repeatedly you can incur many small page-ins (bad for throughput).

Heuristics to avoid paging (practical rules of thumb)
1) Keep an explicit headroom margin: try to keep your worst-case instantaneous GPU usage below about $90\%$ of GPU DRAM. That margin avoids demand-paging corner cases. The paper’s observation that 65B with batch size 16 had “no slowdown” on a 48GB GPU implies their peak working set fit comfortably; if your peak approaches the full 48GB you will see page faults.

2) Estimate peak memory roughly as
   $$
   \mathrm{Peak} \approx \text{model\_compute\_buffers (dequantized weights)} + \text{activation\_peak} + \text{optimizer\_pages\_touched} + \text{KV\_cache}
   $$
   Measure the two dominant terms in your setup: activation_peak grows roughly linearly with sequence length and batch size; $\text{KV\_cache} \approx \text{sequence\_length} \times \text{hidden} \times \text{heads} \times 2$ (for K and V). Use your framework’s peak-memory profiler (nvidia-smi, PyTorch memory_stats, or torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated) on representative batches to empirically find the sequence length / batch size at which peak $\approx$ GPU DRAM. If your estimator crosses GPU DRAM, reduce batch size or sequence length.

3) Prefer increasing the number of microbatches (gradient accumulation) rather than increasing per-microbatch batch size when memory is tight. Smaller microbatches reduce instantaneous activation peaks. However, balance this against extra kernel launches / possibly more page-ins if optimizer pages get evicted between microbatches.

4) Use finer-grained checkpointing (more checkpoints) to reduce saved-activation memory, but test run-time: too much checkpointing increases recompute and can indirectly change page access patterns. Empirically sweep checkpointing granularity: start with a conservative setting that is known to fit, then relax.

5) Cap KV cache usage by truncating context or by doing segment-wise processing (sliding windows). For decoder models, the KV cache is often the easiest way to hit a hard memory cap.

6) When you rely on paged optimizers, ensure the CPU side has enough RAM and avoid oversubscribing CPU memory; otherwise page faults can be slow. The paper notes Unified Memory behaves like paging between CPU RAM and disk—if CPU RAM becomes the bottleneck, paging will be much worse.

7) Prefetch updates (where supported): before the optimizer update step, you can prefetch optimizer pages needed by the update using cudaMemPrefetchAsync (or framework-level prefetch hooks). This lets the runtime overlap host-to-device transfers and reduces synchronous page faults at the critical update moment. The paper does not implement a detailed prefetch policy, but CUDA API supports this.

Detecting paging and profiling advice
Because the paper did not measure thresholds, you should profile your own workload. Look for sudden spikes in step time and for GPU page-fault counters. Tools: nvprof/nsight systems capture Unified Memory page fault events; on Linux, monitor “cuda-memcheck --tool memcheck” or use NVIDIA’s UMA profiling in Nsight to observe page-in/page-out counts. In PyTorch, watch for step-time variance and for spikes in torch.cuda.memory_allocated across forward/backward/update phases.

Does paging make step times nondeterministic or cause stalls? How it affects mixed precision and distributed training
Yes—paging will make step times variable and can cause hard stalls. Page fault handling involves synchronous page-in (unless prefetched), so a single page-faulting kernel can block the GPU while host-to-device transfer completes. The timing of which pages are evicted and when they are re-accessed depends on runtime memory layout, allocator, and OS behavior, so the occurrence and latency of page faults will be non-deterministic across runs and inputs.

Mixed-precision numerical stability is not directly changed by Unified Memory paging: paging only moves bytes between CPU and GPU, it doesn't alter FP arithmetic. However, side effects matter: if paging forces extra recomputation (because of checkpointing changes you made to avoid paging) you might change numerical rounding/stochastic path slightly (different order of ops), which can cause tiny differences in gradients. Those are normal in floating-point training and not unique to paging. Paging-induced stalls do not change FP precision per se, but they can interact with timeouts in distributed setups, which is the more important practical harm.

In distributed training, paging is especially problematic because it turns one worker into a straggler. If you use synchronous gradients (all-reduce), any GPU that stalls on page-ins will delay the whole training step. That can look like nondeterministic step time and reduced throughput; if timeouts are present, paging can even cause job failures. In asynchronous setups you may avoid barrier stalls but still get throughput loss and uneven model updates.

Specific risks to watch for in distributed and production contexts:
- Straggler GPUs that page frequently will throttle the cluster.
- Host-to-device bandwidth becomes the bottleneck; heavy paging competes with inter-GPU NCCL transfers.
- If you rely on time-limited RPCs or scheduler heartbeats, page stalls can trigger false node failures.
- Long stalls can increase wall-clock run time and waste resources even if training still converges.

Practical mitigations (summary)
If you see page-thrashing, try these in order: reduce sequence length or per-microbatch size; increase microbatching (accumulate grads); enable/adjust recomputation/checkpointing to reduce saved activations; prefetch optimizer pages before the update; pin optimizer/state memory in host-pinned (page-locked) memory if your framework supports it; move to a larger GPU or use optimizer sharding (ZeRO) to reduce per-GPU optimizer footprint; or place optimizer states explicitly on CPU but prefetch them deterministically (rather than relying purely on demand paging).

Concluding guideline
Because the paper only reports “no slowdown for 65B with batch size 16 on 48GB” and does not provide hard thresholds, you must empirically measure the peak instantaneous working set of your forward+backward+update patterns. Use that peak as your primary predictor: if $\text{Peak\_memory\_needed} > \text{GPU\_DRAM}$, you will get paging and likely stalls. Tune sequence length, microbatch size, and checkpointing to reduce $\text{Peak\_memory\_needed}$ to comfortably below GPU_DRAM (aim $\sim 10\%$ headroom). If you must operate above that, add explicit prefetching and optimizer-sharding to avoid on-the-fly page faults and the nondeterministic stalls they cause.

### Why does NF4 outperform FP4 and Int4 empirically? What errors dominate at 4-bit?
Question:
The experiments show that NF4 significantly improves over FP4 and Int4 across models and sizes. Is the improvement mostly due to better handling of tails/outliers in weight distributions, or due to preserving zero exactly, or both? Do you have error decompositions (e.g., per-layer weight reconstruction error, sensitivity analysis) that explain which layers benefit most and why FP4/Int4 fail in those cases?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 outperforms FP4 and Int4 because it minimizes the expected quantization error for the (approximately) zero‑mean normal weight distributions found in pretrained LLMs, while also providing an explicit discrete zero so zero-valued elements (padding, sparse structures) are losslessly represented. The dominant $4$-bit errors are the usual quantization rounding/noise amplified by scale-mismatch caused by outliers (large-magnitude weights) and small‑sigma blocks; FP4/Int4 place bins in ways that are suboptimal for normally distributed, block‑scaled weights and therefore either waste representational capacity on unlikely magnitudes or produce large errors on tails. NF4 remedies both issues and so empirically reduces reconstruction error where it matters most, which explains the consistent downstream gains reported in the paper.

Why NF4 is better (intuitive + technical):
- The paper argues and empirically observes that pretrained weights are approximately zero‑centered and roughly normal (see Appendix on normality). For such distributions, the information‑theoretic optimal $k$-bit quantizer is the quantile-based one computed from the normal CDF. NF4 explicitly builds those quantiles for $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and then rescales per block, so its discrete levels match the typical mass of the weight distribution. Int4 and FP4 use uniformly spaced integer bins or IEEE‑like float encodings that are not matched to the Gaussian shape, so many quantization bins are used inefficiently.
- A practical problem in few‑bit block quantization is outliers. If one very large value in a block sets the block scale, uniform/rescaling quantizers end up allocating many bins to extreme tails and few bins to the dense central region, increasing mean squared reconstruction error for the typical weights. NF4, by deriving bin edges from the normal distribution (after rescaling by the block’s std), spreads representational mass where the weights actually are, thereby reducing typical error even in presence of outliers.
- Zero matters. The authors explicitly design NF4 to include an exact representable zero (an asymmetric construction that keeps all $2^k$ levels informative while preserving a zeropoint). This avoids any rounding error for true zeros (important for padded tokens, sparsity, or any exact-zero semantics) — FP4 and some Int schemes lack this property and therefore incur an unavoidable error on those elements.

What errors dominate at $4$-bit (as described/implicated by the paper):
- Rounding/quantization noise (the basic error from mapping continuous weights to finite levels) is the fundamental component. For a given block it scales with how well the discrete levels cover the empirical distribution in that block.
- Scale mismatch/outlier error: when a block’s absolute max (or a few outliers) determine the quantization scale, the dense center of the distribution is coarsely represented — this increases effective reconstruction error and can corrupt activations/features during forward/backward passes. The paper highlights this as a central failure mode of naive blockwise quantization and motivates NF4 and small block sizes.
- Zero-point error: where zero isn't exactly representable, even “semantic” zeros get noisy representations; NF4 eliminates that term for zero entries.

Which layers benefit most and why (paper‑grounded reasoning):
The paper does not provide a detailed per‑layer MSE decomposition, but it gives the mechanisms to understand which layers will gain most. Layers whose stored weight blocks are (a) close to Gaussian with moderate variance (so NF4’s normal‑CDF bins are well matched), or (b) contain many exact zeros / padding / sparse patterns, will benefit most from NF4. Conversely, layers or blocks with strong heavy tails or many extreme outliers will be helped because NF4’s quantile placement (after per‑block rescaling) places more resolution near the typical mass, reducing mean error. Prior quantization literature cited in the paper (and the paper’s own motivation) points to attention projections and some MLP weight matrices as places with problematic outliers; these are therefore likely to show the largest relative improvement with NF4 versus FP4/Int4. Empirically the paper shows consistent improvements across model families and sizes ($125\mathrm{M}$ to $65\mathrm{B}$), implying the benefit is widespread rather than isolated to a single layer type.

What the paper reports empirically
The experiments (Figures/Tables referenced in the paper) show NF4 substantially improves language modeling metrics and downstream zero‑shot/finetuned tasks compared to FP4 and Int4. Importantly, NF4 + Double Quantization recovers $16$-bit LoRA/finetuning performance for large models ($7\mathrm{B}$ to $65\mathrm{B}$) while FP4 lags by about ~1 percentage point on some benchmarks (e.g., MMLU), indicating that the reduced reconstruction error of NF4 translates into nontrivial downstream gains.

On per‑layer error analysis: what’s missing and how you could do it
The paper does not include a layer‑by‑layer reconstruction error / sensitivity decomposition. A useful diagnostic (consistent with the paper’s methods) is to compute, per block and per layer, the MSE between original $\mathrm{FP32}$ weights and their $k$-bit dequantized reconstructions, then correlate that with downstream gradient norms or with the change in activations across a held‑out dataset. Another informative analysis is to measure how much each layer’s quantization error changes model loss (e.g., by selectively dequantizing one layer at a time) or to compute a per‑layer Fisher‑weighted reconstruction error to estimate sensitivity. The paper suggests this kind of blockwise/std‑aware thinking by motivating small block sizes and per‑block rescaling, but it leaves detailed per‑layer sensitivity analyses to future work.

Takeaway
NF4 outperforms FP4/Int4 because it is designed to be the information‑theoretic optimal $k$-bit code for zero‑mean normal weight blocks, and it also enforces an exact zero representation. Those two design choices directly reduce the dominant quantization errors at $4$ bits (rounding noise concentrated in the dense part of the distribution, and zero‑point errors), which is why NF4 yields consistent empirical gains across architectures and scales in the paper. The paper demonstrates the downstream effect (recovering $16$-bit performance under QLoRA), while leaving a detailed per‑layer error/sensitivity decomposition as an intuitive and straightforward follow‑up experiment.

### How is the NF4 codebook computed in practice and how is rounding performed efficiently?
Question:
For NF4, the codebook values \(q_i\) are defined via quantiles of \(N(0,1)\) normalized to \([-1,1]\). In implementation, are these 16 codebook values precomputed constants embedded in the CUDA kernels? Is quantization done via a table-lookup nearest-neighbor mapping (e.g., via vectorized compare against midpoints) or via a transform followed by a small integer arithmetic? Any details on how the rounding to NF4 indices is fused with dequantization to reduce memory bandwidth would be helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: the NF4 codebook (the 16 quantized levels) is precomputed once from the theoretical standard Normal quantiles and embedded as constants in the implementation; quantization is done by first rescaling a block to the NF4 range and then mapping each rescaled weight to the nearest codebook entry via a small table-lookup/threshold test (implemented as vectorized, branchless comparisons or a tiny binary-search over midpoints in the CUDA kernel). In practice the quantize$\to$index and index$\to$dequant (i.e., rounding and dequantization) are fused in the CUDA kernels so you avoid extra memory traffic: you either store the $4$-bit indices (for storage) but when you need the BF16/computation values you immediately map index $\to$ dequantized BF16 value in-register using the precomputed codebook and the (possibly double-)quantized scale constants.

Explanation and how this follows from the paper
The paper defines the NFk codebook values $q_i$ analytically from the standard Normal quantile function:
$q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(Q_X\big(\frac{i}{2^{k}+1}\big) + Q_X\big(\frac{i+1}{2^{k}+1}\big)\big)$,
with an asymmetric tweak so zero is exactly representable for NF4. Those $q_i$ are deterministic (they depend only on the Normal CDF) and small in number ($16$ for NF4). Because these are fixed, the natural and practical approach is to precompute the $16$ $q_i$ and the $15$ midpoints between adjacent $q_i$ and bake those into the CUDA kernels (or into small device-side constant tables). The paper even points to exact NF4 values in Appendix; there is no need to recompute them per-block at runtime.

How quantization (rounding) is done efficiently
Quantization proceeds in two conceptual steps described in the paper: (1) normalize a weight block into the NF4 range (they use absolute-max rescaling per block, i.e., multiply by a per-block quantization constant $c$ so that the block’s dynamic range maps into $[-1,1]$); (2) map the normalized float to one of the $16$ NF4 levels. Because the codebook is small and fixed, mapping a scalar to the nearest codebook entry is implemented efficiently by comparing the scalar against the precomputed midpoints between adjacent $q_i$. On GPU you have two straightforward micro-implementations:

1) Threshold-compare (table-lookup): compute the normalized value $z = c \cdot x$, then do a sequence of vectorized comparisons against the $15$ midpoints. Using bitmasking and population-count or a small branchless reduction you can produce the $4$-bit index. This is highly parallel and amenable to SIMD/SIMT and avoids unpredictable branching.

2) Small binary-search / balanced compare tree: compare against midpoints in $\log_2(16)=4$ steps, which reduces the number of comparisons and is also branchless or with predictable branches on GPU. Both are common in quantization kernels.

Either approach is a tiny table-lookup/midpoint comparison, not an expensive quantile estimation at runtime. The paper emphasizes that the expensive part of quantile quantization is avoided because NF4 uses theoretical Normal quantiles, so only a fixed table is required.

Fusing rounding with dequantization to save bandwidth
The paper’s runtime flow is that storage is NF4 ($4$-bit indices) plus per-block quantization constants (and in Double Quantization those constants themselves are stored in compressed form). When a layer is used for forward/backward, the implementation dequantizes to a 16-bit compute format (BF16). To reduce memory traffic, kernels fuse index-reading (or index-computation if quantizing on the fly) with dequantization: instead of first materializing indices into a full-width buffer and then doing a separate pass to convert indices to BF16 values, the kernel reads a packed $4$-bit index, looks up the corresponding $q_i$ (from the $16$-value table) and immediately multiplies by the dequant scale(s) to produce the BF16 weight in-register. For Double Quantization the operation is nested: $c_2$ (the per-block scale) is stored in $k$-bit (FP8), so doubleDequant means dequantizing $c_2$ to FP32 via $c_1$ and then using that scale to turn NF4 index $\to$ BF16 weight. Because these steps are small and all constants ($q_i$ table, midpoints, and quantized scale unpack constants) are kept in fast memory (registers/shared memory or as device constants), the kernels avoid writing the intermediate indices or unpacked scales to global memory. This reduces memory bandwidth and improves throughput, which is exactly what you want for large weight matrices used repeatedly in matmuls.

What is and isn’t stated in the paper
The paper explicitly describes (a) that NF4 codebook comes from Normal quantiles and that the exact values are in the Appendix, (b) that quantization uses per-block absolute-max rescaling, (c) that Double Quantization stores quantization constants in a second quantized representation, and (d) that CUDA kernels are provided in the released codebase. The implementation-level choices I described above (precompute codebook constants, compare against midpoints or small binary search, and fuse index $\to$ dequant in the kernel) are the standard, practical implementations consistent with the paper’s design and with the released CUDA kernels (bitsandbytes / QLoRA kernels). The paper does not give micro-assembly of kernel code, but it does make clear the three points above that make the described fused, table-driven strategy both natural and necessary.

If you want to inspect code: the paper’s repos (the authors’ QLoRA / bitsandbytes kernels) include the CUDA kernels where you can see the NF4 lookup tables and how quantization/dequantization is implemented and fused.

### Inference and deployment memory: can you show the 5 GB figure for 7B from first principles?
Question:
The paper states that the 7B Guanaco model requires about 5 GB of memory at deployment. Could you show a back-of-the-envelope calculation that arrives at this number, including:
- NF4 weight storage size (4 bits per parameter + scale overhead with double quantization).
- Any additional buffers needed at inference (e.g., temporary dequantization fragments, attention KV cache for a typical sequence length, CUDA workspace).
- The contribution from LoRA adapters (if kept separate) and whether they remain in BF16/FP16 at inference.
Answer:
Short answer: yes — you can get to about $5$ GB for a $7$B Guanaco from first principles with the paper's assumptions (NF4 + double quantization, blocksize choices, and a typical KV cache). Below I show the back-of-the-envelope arithmetic, state the assumptions, and give a small uncertainty range so you can see how the $\sim 5$ GB number arises.

Assumptions (from the paper)
- Base model size: $7$B parameters (LLaMA-style $7$B; I use $7.0\times10^{9}$ parameters for round numbers).
- Weight storage: NF4 ($4$ bits per parameter) with Double Quantization. The paper uses blocksize $64$ for weights and FP8 for the second-level constants, which results (per paper) in an average quantization-constants overhead of about $0.127$ bits/parameter after Double Quantization.
- Runtime computation data type: BF16 (so dequantized fragments are BF16, $2$ bytes / element).
- KV cache for decoding with typical context: I show the $2048$-token case (a common deployment context). For LLaMA-$7$B I use the usual architectural numbers: Layers $L = 32$, hidden size $H = 4096$, heads $= 32$ $\to$ $\text{head\_dim} = 128$ (these are standard LLaMA-$7$B values used in the literature).
- Minor CUDA workspace and temporary buffers are included as small overheads.
- LoRA adapters kept separate and stored in BF16 (the paper uses BF16 for adapters during QLoRA).

Step-by-step storage math

1) NF4 weight storage ($4$ bits/parameter)
Weights (bytes) = number_of_params $\times$ $4$ bits / $8$
For $7$B:
Weights = $7.0\times10^{9}\times 4/8 = 3.5\times10^{9}$ bytes $\approx 3.50$ GB

2) Quantization-constant overhead after Double Quantization
The paper states: for blocksize $64$, double quantization reduces the constants from $0.5$ bits/param to $\approx 0.127$ bits/param on average. So:
Const bytes = $7.0\times10^{9}\times 0.127/8 \approx 0.111\times10^{9}$ bytes $\approx 0.11$ GB

So base quantized model storage $\approx 3.50 + 0.11 = 3.61$ GB.

3) KV cache (inference decoding state)
KV storage per token: we store keys and values for all transformer layers. For BF16 ($2$ bytes per scalar) and $L$ layers with $n_h$ heads and head_dim $d_h$:

$\text{KV\_per\_token\_bytes} = 2\ (\text{key+value}) \times L \times n_h \times d_h \times 2\ \text{bytes}$

Using $L = 32$, $n_h = 32$, $d_h = 128$:
$\text{KV\_per\_token} = 2 \times 32 \times 32 \times 128 \times 2\ \text{bytes}$

Compute intermediate:
$32\times32 = 1024$; $1024\times128 = 131072$; $131072\times2 = 262144$; $262144\times2 = 524288$ bytes $\approx 0.5$ MB per token.

For a $2048$-token context:
$\text{KV\_total} = 524288 \times 2048 \approx 1.0737\times10^{9}$ bytes $\approx 1.07$ GB

(If you use a shorter context — e.g. $1024$ tokens — the KV cache halves to $\approx 0.54$ GB. For very long contexts the KV term grows linearly.)

4) Temporary dequantized fragments / runtime buffers
QLoRA kernels typically dequantize blocks into BF16 to do GEMM. The implementation can be streaming and only hold a fragment of the dequantized weights at a time, but there is still some buffer overhead. Practical runtimes report a modest per-GPU working set for these fragments; a reasonable small estimate is $0.2$–$0.6$ GB depending on kernel implementation and batching. I will use $0.3$ GB as a conservative, realistic estimate.

5) CUDA workspace, attention workspace, other small buffers
Modern fused kernels and attention implementations need a small workspace; choose $0.15$–$0.3$ GB. I use $0.2$ GB here.

6) LoRA adapters (if kept separate) and their dtype
The paper reports LoRA parameter size for $7$B-like runs as very small (examples in the paper: LoRA parameters $\approx 26$ MB and LoRA input gradients were the large training cost but are irrelevant for inference). If LoRA weights are kept in BF16 ($2$ bytes/param), their contribution is tiny: $\sim 0.03$ GB. So:
LoRA $\approx 0.03$ GB (BF16 storage; if kept in FP16 similar size).

Sum the components (rounded)
- NF4 weights + quant constants: $3.61$ GB
- KV cache ($2048$ tokens): $1.07$ GB
- Dequant buffer (runtime fragments): $0.30$ GB
- CUDA/workspace: $0.20$ GB
- LoRA adapters BF16: $0.03$ GB

Total $\approx 3.61 + 1.07 + 0.30 + 0.20 + 0.03 = 5.21$ GB

Uncertainty and how to hit $\sim 5.0$ GB
- The paper’s “about $5$ GB” is a rounded, deployment-style claim. My calculation gives $\sim 5.2$ GB with these reasonable assumptions. Small changes push this to $\sim 5.0$ GB:
  - Using a slightly smaller dequant fragment buffer ($0.2$ GB) and a slightly smaller workspace ($0.1$ GB) reduces the total by $0.2$ GB $\to$ $\approx 5.0$ GB.
  - Using context $1024$ tokens instead of $2048$ reduces the KV cache by $\approx 0.54$ GB $\to$ total $\approx 4.7$ GB.
  - Using binary GB vs GiB conventions (decimal GB here) shifts the numeric reported value slightly.
So “about $5$ GB” is consistent with NF4+double-quant storage plus a typical KV cache and modest runtime buffers.

Key points and intuition
- The dominant static term is the $4$-bit weights: $4$ bits/param $\to 3.5$ GB for $7$B. That’s why moving from $16$-bit $\to 4$-bit is so impactful.
- The quantization constants are a small but non-negligible overhead; Double Quantization reduces that overhead from $\sim 0.5$ bits/param to $\sim 0.127$ bits/param (paper’s point: saves $\sim 0.373$ bits/param).
- The KV cache for autoregressive decoding is the other big runtime term and grows with context length; for LLaMA-$7$B the per-token KV is roughly $0.5$ MB/token in BF16.
- LoRA parameters are negligible at inference (a few tens of MB) and are stored at BF16/FP16 in QLoRA — nothing large to worry about.
- Temporary dequantization fragments and CUDA workspace determine whether you need a little headroom; a streaming/fused dequant kernel minimizes that, which is how you can practically fit $\sim 7$B on $\sim 5$ GB.

Conclusion
Using the paper’s choices (NF4, blocksize $64$, double quantization to FP8 constants, BF16 computation), the base weight storage is $\approx 3.61$ GB and a realistic KV cache + small runtime buffers bring the total to roughly $5.0$–$5.3$ GB for a common deployment configuration ($7$B, $2048$ context). That matches the paper’s “about $5$ GB” statement once you account for small implementation-dependent buffer choices and the chosen context length.

### How did you correct for ordering bias in GPT-4 evaluations when computing Elo ratings?
Question:
The paper reports strong ordering effects (GPT-4 favors the first response) and recommends averaging scores over both orders. For the pairwise comparisons used to compute Elo, did you always evaluate both orderings and then symmetrize the label, or did Elo ingest individual ordered judgments with random order assignment? If the latter, could residual order bias skew Elo, and how did you estimate confidence intervals on Elo to account for sampling variance across prompts and orderings?
Answer:
Short answer: we evaluated both shown-orderings for comparisons and then used randomized resampling of the labeled matches (including both orderings) to compute Elo distributions and confidence intervals. In practice GPT-4’s ordering bias was handled in two ways: absolute scoring (the score-out-of-$10$ protocol) was averaged across both prompt orders, and pairwise head-to-head labels were collected on all permutations and then Elo was computed by repeatedly sampling from that pool to control for ordering effects ($10{,}000$ random seeds). This is what the paper describes.

Longer explanation / intuition grounded in the paper: the authors observed a clear first-position bias in GPT-4’s absolute scoring and therefore recommend “reporting the mean score over both orders” for any score-based metric (this is stated explicitly in the Evaluation section). For the pairwise comparisons that feed Elo, the paper says they “conduct these head-to-head comparisons on all permutations of pairs of systems on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks,” which means each prompt was used to generate both orderings ($A$ first / $B$ second and $B$ first / $A$ second). Elo itself is computed from the individual pairwise judgments, but those judgments include both orderings.

To avoid residual order-bias contaminating the Elo rankings, the authors do two things. First, by collecting both permutations they remove systematic confounding of a model’s performance with display order: any per-prompt first-position preference in the judge pool is exposed symmetrically for each model. Second, when producing the reported Elo numbers they do not compute a single deterministic Elo from the fixed sequence of matches; instead they “randomly sample from the set of labeled comparisons to compute Elo” and repeat the whole procedure $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. That repetition serves as an empirical control for sampling variability (including variability due to which ordering instances appear in a sampled tournament and the random sequence in which matches are processed by the Elo update). The distribution of Elo scores across those $10{,}000$ runs is then used to produce uncertainty estimates / confidence intervals. Intuitively, this is a bootstrap-like approach: by resampling many possible tournament sequences from the labeled comparisons (which include both orders), you measure how much Elo fluctuates due to prompt sampling, labeling noise, and ordering.

Could residual order bias still skew Elo? In principle yes if (a) you failed to collect both permutations for many prompts, (b) the judge population has a massive first-position preference that is not symmetric across models (for example if model formatting systematically interacts with position), or (c) sampling/resampling is insufficient. The paper’s protocol addresses these risks by explicitly collecting both orderings for the pairwise comparisons and by using extensive randomized resampling ($10{,}000$ repeats) to measure variance. Because Elo updates are driven by individual match outcomes, any remaining small bias from orderings will be reflected in the empirical spread of Elo across the resampled tournaments; if bias were large it would induce shifting means and wide intervals, which would show up in the reported uncertainty.

Practical takeaway: the authors handled ordering bias by collecting both $A$/$B$ and $B$/$A$ comparisons, symmetrizing absolute-score metrics by averaging over orders, and computing Elo via many randomized resamplings of the measured pairwise labels ($10{,}000$ repetitions) to obtain an empirical Elo distribution and confidence intervals. This combination both neutralizes systematic first-position effects and quantifies the remaining sampling variance.

### Data quality vs. size: were training tokens or epochs controlled when comparing OASST1 vs FLAN v2?
Question:
A central claim is that a small high-quality dataset (OASST1, about 9k samples) outperforms a much larger dataset (subsampled FLAN v2, about 450k) on chatbot metrics. To interpret this, did you control for the total number of training tokens or steps across datasets? For example, did both runs see the same number of optimizer updates and similar effective tokens? If not, could the results partly reflect under/over-training? Any ablations where you downsample FLAN v2 to match the token count of OASST1 (or vice versa) would clarify the role of data quality vs. training budget.
Answer:
Short answer: No — the paper does not report explicitly equalizing total training tokens or optimizer updates when comparing OASST1 ($\approx 9{,}000$ examples) to the (subsampled) FLAN v2 ($\approx 450{,}000$ examples). The claim that the small, high‑quality OASST1 set outperforms the much larger FLAN v2 is presented as an empirical result under the authors’ common finetuning recipe, not as a result of a controlled “same-token / same-update” ablation.

What the paper does say that’s relevant. The QLoRA experiments used a consistent finetuning procedure (NF4 QLoRA, cross‑entropy supervised objective, same general LoRA configuration) across datasets and model sizes. The authors note that most hyperparameters found at the $7\text{B}$ scale generalize (including number of epochs), except learning rate and batch size, for which they make modest scale-dependent adjustments (they halve the LR and double batch size for $33\text{B}/65\text{B}$). But the paper does not state that they adjusted training steps or epochs to make the total number of optimizer updates or total tokens seen equal across different datasets. Nor does it report an ablation that downsamples FLAN v2 to match OASST1 by token count (or the reverse) to isolate data quality from training budget effects.

Why that matters (intuition). If one dataset is much larger, and both runs use the same number of epochs or the same number of optimizer updates without scaling to equalize tokens seen, the effective training signal per dataset differs: a small dataset might be seen many more times (more epochs) or a large dataset might be under‑trained relative to the small one. That can produce apparent “quality over size” effects even when some part of the difference is due to different effective training budgets (tokens or updates). Controlling total tokens or updates is the clean way to attribute gains to data quality rather than to differences in training exposure.

What the paper does and doesn’t provide. The paper emphasizes that the same QLoRA finetuning recipe was applied across datasets and that they performed small hyperparameter searches, but it does not present matched‑token ablations for OASST1 vs FLAN v2. The authors explicitly present the empirical finding (OASST1 outperforms subsampled FLAN v2 on chatbot benchmarks) and discuss dataset suitability vs size, but they do not claim they eliminated training‑budget as a confound via explicit token‑matching experiments.

Recommended follow-ups (if you want to nail down causality). Run two controlled ablations: (1) fix total optimizer updates (or total tokens seen) and train separate models on OASST1 and FLAN v2 to see which wins when training budget is equal; (2) downsample FLAN v2 to match OASST1 (by tokens or examples) and train with identical hyperparameters to isolate quality differences; (3) complementary: upsample OASST1 via data augmentation or repeated epochs to match tokens for FLAN v2 to see if more exposure closes the gap. Report per‑step/epoch learning curves and validation metrics so you can see under‑/over‑training effects. Those experiments would directly answer whether the advantage is purely data quality or partly a training‑budget artifact.

Summary: The paper reports the empirical superiority of OASST1 under a common QLoRA finetuning pipeline but does not explicitly control for or report equal total training tokens/updates between OASST1 and FLAN v2, nor does it include the downsampling/upsampling ablations you suggest. Such controlled ablations would be the right next step to disentangle data quality from training budget effects.
