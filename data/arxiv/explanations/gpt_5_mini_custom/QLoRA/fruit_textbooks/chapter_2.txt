# Benchmarks and tasks landscape (GLUE, MMLU, instruction following)
Understanding which tasks and benchmarks are used to evaluate finetuning is crucial because different benchmarks measure different aspects of model capability. The <|endoftext|> paper studies a range of evaluation tasks to validate its approach and to analyze the effects of instruction finetuning.

- GLUE. The General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) benchmark is a collection of established <|endoftext|> NLP tasks used to measure language understanding on encoder-style models (the paper evaluates RoBERTa-large on GLUE). GLUE is a classical benchmark for smaller-scale <|endoftext|> finetuning and helps verify that quantized adapters do not harm standard <|endoftext|> tasks.

- Super-NaturalInstructions (TKInstruct) and instruction datasets. Instruction-following datasets provide examples of an instruction paired with a desired response, supporting <|endoftext|> instruction finetuning to make LLMs follow user instructions. The paper experiments with multiple instruction datasets and identifies important distinctions among them. Datasets used include OASST1 (crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogs), HH-RLHF (human preference data), Alpaca (distillation from instruction-tuned models), FLAN v2 (aggregated instruction-like tasks), self-instruct, unnatural-instructions, Chip2 (corpus aggregation), and Longform. The paper emphasizes that dataset quality and suitability for the target evaluation matter substantially: a small, high-quality dataset (OASST1, about 9k samples) outperformed a much larger subsampled FLAN v2 (about 450k samples) on chatbot performance.

- MMLU. The Massively Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark measures broad knowledge and reasoning across 57 subjects using multiple-choice questions. The paper reports 5-shot MMLU accuracy to assess how well instruction-tuned models retain or improve on general knowledge and reasoning tasks. QLoRA-finetuned models matched 16-bit <|endoftext|> on MMLU across scales, showing that the quantized+LoRA approach does not sacrifice broad knowledge abilities.

- Chatbot evaluations: Vicuna and OpenAssistant (OA). To measure real-world conversational quality, the authors evaluate on the Vicuna prompt set (80 diverse prompts) and the OASST1 validation set (953 unique user queries with multi-turn context). Chatbot evaluation is inherently subjective, so the paper uses both automated and human evaluations.

Evaluation methodology and findings:
- Automated evaluation with GPT-4. GPT-4 was prompted to score or compare responses from ChatGPT and candidate systems. The paper found significant ordering bias (GPT-4 tended to increase scores for the response that appeared first), which led the authors to recommend averaging over both prompt orders. They also used pairwise comparisons (choosing best response or tie) and aggregated these into Elo ratings.

- Human evaluation. The authors ran Amazon Mechanical Turk experiments with multiple annotators per comparison to gather human judgments. Human annotations and GPT-4 evaluations were compared.

- Elo tournaments. Pairwise comparisons (both human and GPT-4) were aggregated using Elo ratings (starting at 1000, \(K=32\)), repeated with many random seeds to mitigate ordering effects. This tournament-style ranking helps avoid problems with absolute scoring scales.

Interpreting the evaluations, the paper reports that QLoRA-trained Guanaco models achieved very strong results: Guanaco 65B reached 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark in the automated metric. Elo-based comparisons using human annotators showed that Guanaco 33B and 65B outperform other open-source models and come close to ChatGPT. Importantly, the authors found that strong MMLU performance does not necessarily translate to strong chatbot performance; dataset suitability and alignment with the target evaluation are crucial.

Finally, the paper reports measures of agreement between evaluation sources to emphasize limitations: <|endoftext|> Kendall Tau of 0.43 and Spearman correlation of 0.55 between GPT-4 and human rankings, example-level Fleiss κ = 0.25 between GPT-4 and human majority vote, and human inter-annotator Fleiss κ = 0.42. These moderate agreement values highlight that evaluation of conversational models is noisy and that automated model-based evaluation is a plausible but imperfect substitute for human judgment.
