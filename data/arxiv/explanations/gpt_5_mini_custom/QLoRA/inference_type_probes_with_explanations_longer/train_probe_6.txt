According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', if the pretrained weights were uniformly distributed (e.g., $U(a,b)$) instead of normally distributed (e.g., $N(0,\sigma^2)$), $\text{NF4}$ would not be optimal. The paper constructs NormalFloat (NF$k$) by setting the quantization levels to the mid-quantiles of a standard normal, i.e., $q_i=\frac{1}{2}\big(Q_{N(0,1)}(\frac{i}{2^k+1})+Q_{N(0,1)}(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1})\big)$, and proves it is information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normal weights after rescaling into $[-1,1]$. This optimality hinges on quantile quantization: bins are chosen so that, under the assumed distribution, each bin has equal probability mass, which minimizes expected distortion for a fixed number of bins. If the true weight distribution is uniform, the quantile function $Q_{U(a,b)}(p)$ is linear, so equal-probability bins correspond to equal-width intervals in value space. NF4’s levels—derived from normal quantiles—are much denser near 0 and sparser in the tails, yielding unequal probability mass per bin under a uniform distribution and, therefore, losing the information-theoretic optimality.

In practical terms, NF4’s design exploits the bell-shaped density of $N(0,\sigma^2)$: more resolution is allocated where the density is high (near 0) and less where it is low (tails). For $U(a,b)$, the density is constant on $[a,b]$, there are no heavy tails, and outliers are bounded; thus the optimal allocation is uniform across the range, not concentrated at the center. Because NF4’s asymmetric, normal-quantile-derived levels include a discrete zero and nonuniform spacing tailored to $N(0,1)$, applying them to $U(a,b)$ misallocates the codebook: central bins are overused and tail bins underused relative to the uniform density. This causes larger expected quantization error than a uniform-quantile (or equivalently, equal-width) 4-bit scheme matched to $U(a,b)$, so $\text{NF4}$ ceases to be optimal when the weights are uniformly rather than normally distributed.
