Elo Ratings and Surprise in Tournament-Style Chatbot Evaluation

# Elo in the Context of Chatbot Evaluation

This chapter explains how Elo rating updates reflect the “surprise” of match outcomes in tournament-style evaluations, as used to assess chatbot models. The central idea is that Elo adjusts a model’s rating by an amount proportional to how unexpected the result was: an upset, where a lower-rated model beats a higher-rated one, causes a large rating change, while an expected outcome leads to a small change. In the study of instruction finetuning with QLoRA, Elo ratings are computed from head-to-head matchups between systems, judged either by human annotators or GPT-4, and aggregated to produce a ranking of chatbot performance.

Within this evaluation framework, Elo serves as a way to measure a model’s comparative skill across many pairwise competitions. Each “match” is a prompt for which two systems produce responses and a judge selects the better response or declares a tie. Over many matches, Elo captures a model’s tendency to win against peers, adjusting its rating as evidence accumulates. The key property we develop in detail is how Elo weights the magnitude of rating changes by the expectedness of outcomes, giving intuitively larger shifts for surprising wins and smaller shifts for unsurprising results.

# Tournament-Style Pairwise Comparisons

The evaluation constructs a tournament of pairwise comparisons among systems. For a given prompt, two models compete by generating responses, and a judge—either GPT-4 or a human annotator—decides which response is better or declares a tie. These head-to-head comparisons are repeated across all permutations of pairs of systems on curated datasets of queries, such as the Vicuna prompts and the OpenAssistant (OA) validation queries. This creates a pool of labeled comparisons from which Elo ratings are computed.

To mitigate ordering effects, such as a bias favoring the response that appears first to GPT-4, comparisons are conducted in both orders and aggregated. Similarly, to reduce sensitivity to the order in which match results are processed, Elo computation is repeated many times with different random seeds. Each iteration samples from the set of labeled comparisons and updates ratings, starting from a common baseline score. The authors begin each model at a score of $1{,}000$ and use $K=32$ in the Elo update procedure. Repeating the computation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds helps control for ordering effects in the sequence of matches, producing more stable system-level ratings.

# Interpreting Elo: Expected Win-Rate

A useful way to understand Elo ratings is through their relationship with expected win-rates. Elo ratings are designed so that a difference in rating corresponds to a difference in expected performance in head-to-head matches. For example, an Elo of $1{,}100$ versus $1{,}000$ implies that the higher-rated system has an expected win-rate of approximately $65\\%$ against the lower-rated system. Equal ratings, such as $1{,}000$ vs $1{,}000$ or $1{,}100$ vs $1{,}100$, correspond to an expected win-rate of $50\\%$.

This interpretation guides the notion of surprise. If a higher-rated system (with an expected win-rate above $50\\%$) loses to a lower-rated system, that outcome is more surprising than a win by the higher-rated system. Conversely, when two equally rated systems compete, a win by either is not particularly surprising, and the rating changes are smaller. Elo therefore converts differences in rating into expectations, and then compares those expectations to observed outcomes to determine how much to adjust ratings.

# Elo Updates: Rating Changes Proportional to Surprise

Elo updates are driven by how the actual match outcome deviates from the expected outcome implied by the current ratings. If a match result aligns with expectations, rating changes are small. If the result contradicts expectations, rating changes are large. This realizes the principle that “unexpected upsets” cause substantial adjustments while predictable results cause minor adjustments.

Concretely, consider two systems with a rating difference that implies an expected win-rate for the higher-rated system above $50\\%$. If the higher-rated system wins, the update to its rating is modest, reflecting the fact that the observed outcome corroborates what the ratings already suggested. If the lower-rated system wins instead, the update is larger in magnitude, since the ratings were misaligned with the observed evidence and must be moved more to account for that surprise. The scaling of this adjustment is influenced by $K$, which sets the maximum change per match: with $K=32$, even the largest single-update changes are capped, preventing instability, while still allowing ratings to shift meaningfully in response to surprising results.

Over many matches, these proportional updates enable Elo to “learn” a model’s skill relative to others. A model that consistently produces strong responses against higher-rated opponents accumulates larger rating increases than one that merely wins predictable matchups against lower-rated models. Conversely, a model that often loses to lower-rated opponents experiences larger rating decreases. In this way, Elo emphasizes evidence that contradicts prior expectations more heavily than evidence that confirms them, allowing ratings to converge toward values that reflect observed head-to-head performance.

# Practical Choices in the Study

In applying Elo to chatbot evaluation, several practical choices are made. First, models start from a common initial score of $1{,}000$, ensuring that early updates are driven entirely by observed match results rather than preconceived ratings. Second, the update constant $K$ is set to $32$, which balances responsiveness to surprises against stability across many matches.

Third, to reduce biases and control for ordering effects, Elo ratings are computed by repeatedly sampling labeled comparisons and processing them with different random seeds. These repetitions—$10{,}000$ in total—help average out effects of the order in which pairs are evaluated and ensure that the final Elo estimates are not artifacts of a particular sequence of matches.

Finally, both human and GPT-4 judgments are used to generate match outcomes, and Elo ratings are computed separately under each judging regime. This allows an assessment of agreement and differences between human and automated evaluations. For example, human and GPT-4 rankings show moderate consistency at the system level, with reported Kendall Tau $\\tau=0.43$ and Spearman rank correlation $r=0.55$ on the Vicuna benchmark, while example-level agreement is weaker, with Fleiss $\\kappa=0.25$. These observations highlight that while Elo can aggregate judgments into coherent rankings, the choice of judge affects the underlying match outcomes and thus the resulting Elo ratings.

# Implications and Limitations

Using Elo in chatbot evaluation provides a principled way to translate pairwise comparisons into system-level rankings, with updates that properly weigh the “surprise” of outcomes. This property is especially appealing in a landscape where models differ widely in capabilities across datasets and where evaluation protocols must cope with subjective preferences and potential biases. Elo’s emphasis on unexpected results helps correct ratings faster when they fail to predict observed performance, while keeping changes small when outcomes are in line with expectations.

At the same time, Elo inherits limitations from the evaluation setup. The reliability of ratings depends on the quality and bias of the judges, the representativeness of the prompts, and the handling of ordering effects. The study notes order biases in GPT-4 scoring, where the earlier response tends to receive a higher score, and finds that GPT-4 assigns higher Elo to its own outputs compared to human ratings on some benchmarks. Moreover, the agreement between human and automated judgments is only moderate at the system level and weak at the example level, reflecting the inherent subjectivity in chatbot evaluations.

Despite these challenges, Elo provides a robust mechanism to aggregate pairwise evidence, with rating updates that naturally encode surprise. In the context of QLoRA’s instruction finetuning study, Elo helps reveal that Guanaco models trained with 4-bit quantization and Low-Rank Adapters perform competitively against leading systems, and that dataset suitability strongly influences benchmark outcomes. By grounding rating changes in expectedness, Elo supports meaningful, tournament-style comparisons aligned with the intuitive notion that unexpected upsets should matter more than expected wins.
