Chapter: Why Pairwise Elo Ratings Improve Chatbot Evaluation in QLoRA

# Introduction

Evaluating chatbot models is deceptively tricky. At first glance, assigning a score on a fixed numerical scale, such as a 10-point scale, seems straightforward: given a prompt and two model responses, ask an annotator—or a strong model such as GPT-4—to rate each response from 1 to 10. However, in practice, this approach can introduce ambiguity and instability. In the QLoRA study, the authors observed that absolute 10-point scales on the Vicuna benchmark produced wide confidence intervals and were sensitive to ordering effects, such as the position of a model’s response within the evaluation prompt. These issues made it difficult to draw clear, trustworthy performance conclusions across competitive chatbots.

To address these problems, the authors turned to a ranking approach based on pairwise judgments and Elo rating, replacing an absolute “how good is this response?” with a comparative “which response is better?”. This chapter explains why this shift is compelling, how the pairwise evaluation is constructed in the paper, and how Elo ratings summarize comparative performance into an interpretable ranking that better reflects relative quality.

# Absolute Scales: Uncertainty and Order Effects

The QLoRA paper adopts evaluation settings used in recent chatbot studies and systematically assesses their weaknesses. In particular, the authors replicated the common setup where GPT-4 rates responses on a 10-point scale for the Vicuna benchmark. These absolute scores were normalized relative to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to produce a percentage score. While simple, this approach revealed two critical problems:

First, the level of uncertainty in the 10-point ratings was high. The confidence intervals for many models overlapped, making it difficult to conclude which model was meaningfully better. When testing top-performing systems—such as ChatGPT and the QLoRA-trained Guanaco models—the uncertainty made the rankings fragile. This is especially problematic for research claims where fine-grained distinctions matter.

Second, ordering effects systematically skewed results. The authors observed that GPT-4 assigned higher scores to the response appearing first in the prompt. In other words, the same two responses could receive different scores depending on their order in the evaluation context. The paper recommends averaging across both response orders to mitigate this bias, but notes that this does not fix the underlying instability of absolute judgments.

Together, wide confidence intervals and ordering effects weakened the reliability of the absolute-scale method. This motivated the paper’s shift toward pairwise comparisons coupled with Elo rating to more robustly capture relative performance.

# Constructing Pairwise Comparisons

To replace absolute scores with direct comparisons, the authors built pairwise evaluation pipelines that can be applied with either GPT-4 or human annotators. The core idea is simple: present two model responses to the same prompt and ask a judge to select the better one or declare a tie. The paper uses this procedure on two benchmarks:

The Vicuna benchmark consists of 80 prompts spanning diverse categories. The authors use the dataset without modifications.

The OA benchmark is based on the OpenAssistant (OASST1) validation set. It contains 953 user queries drawn from multi-turn dialogs. For each query, prior turns are included in the prompt.

In both benchmarks, response generation uses nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$, ensuring a consistent decoding regime across systems. For each prompt, the authors gather pairwise comparisons in two modalities:

Automated comparisons using GPT-4. The judge is prompted to choose the better response or declare a tie and to provide a brief explanation. The authors evaluate all permutations of system pairs.

Human comparisons using Amazon Mechanical Turk. For comparisons to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 Turbo), two annotators are used; for system-level pairwise comparisons, three annotators are used.

By framing the task as a head-to-head match rather than an absolute rating, the evaluation now asks: for this prompt, which response is preferred? This avoids calibrating across an absolute scale and makes the judgment more concrete and local. It also enables tournament-style aggregation across many pairwise matches using Elo.

# Elo Ratings for Tournament-Style Evaluation

Elo rating translates the outcomes of many pairwise matches into a single scalar score per system that reflects relative strength. The QLoRA paper follows the classic Elo setup:

Each model starts with a score of $1000$.

After each match, scores are updated proportionally to the difference between the observed outcome and the expected outcome.

The update magnitude is controlled by $K=32$.

Intuitively, if model A has a much higher Elo than model B, A is expected to win. If B upsets A, B’s Elo increases significantly and A’s decreases. If A defeats B as expected, ratings change only slightly. Elo thus captures both performance and surprise.

The authors illustrate interpretability with a concrete example: an Elo of $1100$ versus $1000$ implies an expected win-rate of approximately $65\%$ for the higher-rated player; equal ratings imply a $50\%$ expected win-rate. This is a useful mental model for understanding Elo gaps as odds of victory in head-to-head comparisons.

To control for residual ordering effects and randomness, the paper repeatedly samples from the pool of labeled comparisons and recomputes Elo rankings $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. This ensures that the final rankings are not artifacts of a particular match order or sampling idiosyncrasies.

# Evidence for Pairwise Elo over Absolute Scales

The QLoRA authors provide multiple lines of evidence supporting the move to pairwise Elo rankings.

They found that absolute 10-point scores had wide confidence intervals in the Vicuna evaluation, making it hard to distinguish between competitive models. By contrast, Elo ratings aggregate many direct comparisons into a more stable ranking that aligns with head-to-head preferences.

They identified strong order effects in GPT-4 scoring: earlier-appearing responses received higher scores. While they recommend averaging over both response orders for any absolute scoring setup, pairwise Elo inherently reduces reliance on absolute scaling and focuses on comparative judgments, which are less sensitive to scale anchoring.

They report moderate agreement between GPT-4 and human system-level rankings on the Vicuna benchmark, with Kendall’s $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman’s $r=0.55$. At the example level, agreement is weaker, with Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.25$, underscoring the noisiness of individual judgments and the need for aggregation. For human annotators alone, moderate agreement is reported with Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.42$.

They observe that automated judges can be biased. On the Vicuna benchmark, GPT-4 assigns significantly higher Elo to its own outputs than humans do, with GPT-4-based Elo around $1348$ versus a human-based Elo around $1176$. This roughly corresponds to a $\sim 20\%$ higher probability of winning under GPT-4 judgments compared to human judgments. Such discrepancies highlight the value of evaluating with both human and model judges and the importance of robust aggregation methods that can surface systematic biases.

Finally, in the broader context of the paper, the authors show that performance rankings differ across benchmarks. The Vicuna benchmark tends to favor open-source models, whereas the larger OA benchmark favors ChatGPT. This makes the case for a ranking system that is consistent within each benchmark and highlights that datasets probe different skill sets. Elo is well-suited for capturing these relative strengths within a given evaluation setting.

# Practical Protocol within the Paper’s Framework

The QLoRA paper proposes a concrete, reproducible evaluation protocol integrating pairwise judgments with Elo:

Use two datasets of prompts—Vicuna and OA—to cover distinct evaluation regimes.

Generate responses with consistent decoding settings: nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$.

Collect pairwise comparisons across all permutations of system pairs. Allow ties to avoid forcing artificial preferences when responses are equivalent in quality.

Run the tournament as repeated sampling of labeled comparisons to compute Elo scores. Initialize all models at $1000$, set $K=32$, and repeat the computation $10{,}000$ times under different random seeds to control for ordering effects and variance.

Report both GPT-4–based and human-based Elo scores to triangulate performance and expose potential biases, such as judge-specific preferences or order effects.

When absolute scoring is used (for example, to maintain comparability with prior work), average over both response orders within the prompt to mitigate the documented ordering bias. Still, prefer pairwise comparisons and Elo for headline results.

This protocol directly addresses the two main issues with absolute scales: it downplays the ambiguities of absolute numeric judgments by focusing on relative quality, and it neutralizes ordering effects through symmetric prompting and repeated randomized aggregation.

# Limitations and Considerations

While pairwise Elo improves robustness, it does not eliminate all evaluation challenges. The QLoRA study reports only moderate agreement between GPT-4 and humans at the system level and weaker agreement at the example level. This indicates that subjective preferences remain influential, especially when comparing strong systems whose outputs are often close in quality. The paper’s qualitative analysis further shows that preferences can vary across examples and raters.

Automated evaluation introduces additional concerns. The authors document strong order effects in GPT-4 scoring and evidence that GPT-4 inflates the relative standing of its own outputs compared to human judgments. These findings suggest that model-based evaluation is a “cheap and reasonable alternative” at the system level but must be interpreted with caution, ideally alongside human assessments.

Finally, the choice of benchmark matters. The paper shows that strong performance on MMLU does not imply strong chatbot performance on Vicuna or OA, and vice versa. Evaluations should be matched to the target capability—language understanding, instruction-following in dialogue, or other behaviors—and rankings should be interpreted within that context.

# Conclusion

In the QLoRA study, the authors identify concrete shortcomings of absolute 10-point scales—wide confidence intervals and ordering effects—that reduce the reliability of chatbot evaluation on benchmarks like Vicuna. In response, they recommend a ranking approach based on pairwise judgments and Elo rating. By centering evaluation on head-to-head comparisons and aggregating many such matches into a single interpretable score, Elo produces more stable, trustworthy rankings. The protocol integrates both GPT-4 and human judgments, uses repeated randomized aggregation to control for order and variance, and allows ties to avoid forced distinctions.

This methodological shift is not just a technical detail. It underpins the paper’s broader claims about the competitiveness of QLoRA-trained models like Guanaco and provides a replicable, bias-aware framework for future chatbot evaluations.
