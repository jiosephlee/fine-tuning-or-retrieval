Chapter Title: Order Effects in GPT-4-Based Evaluation of Chatbot Responses

# Introduction

Evaluating the quality of chatbot responses is central to measuring progress in instruction finetuning of large language models. In the context of QLoRA—an approach that enables efficient finetuning of quantized models without sacrificing performance—the paper proposes and analyzes both automated and human evaluation protocols to compare systems. One of the key findings is that GPT-4, when used as an automated evaluator of paired responses, exhibits a systematic bias favoring the response that appears earlier in the prompt. This chapter explains the evaluation setup in which this bias emerges, the nature of the bias, why it matters, and how the paper mitigates its impact. The discussion situates the bias within the broader methodology for ranking models using tournament-style comparisons and Elo ratings and highlights the implications for reliable benchmarking.

# Evaluation Protocols Using GPT-4

The paper conducts extensive evaluations of finetuned models, including the Guanaco family trained with QLoRA, across curated datasets of prompts. For generative capabilities, the authors rely on both automated and human assessments. The automated evaluation uses GPT-4 to judge the relative quality of chatbot responses under two schemes.

The first scheme compares a model's response to ChatGPT’s response for the same query. Given a query along with ChatGPT's output and the model’s output, GPT-4 is prompted to assign scores out of ten to both responses and provide an explanation. The model’s performance is then reported as a percentage relative to ChatGPT’s score, with the possibility of exceeding \(100\%\) if the model achieves a higher absolute score.

The second scheme performs direct, pairwise comparisons between outputs of two systems. This is formulated as a three-class labeling task in which GPT-4 is asked to pick the better of the two responses or declare a tie, along with a justification. These comparisons are conducted across all permutations of system pairs on both the Vicuna benchmark, which consists of 80 human-curated prompts, and the OpenAssistant validation dataset, which contains 953 multilingual user queries embedded in multi-turn conversations.

In both schemes, GPT-4 is presented with a prompt containing a query and two responses. The order in which these two responses are presented becomes an important factor in the observed outcomes.

# The Systematic Order Bias Favoring Earlier Responses

A central finding of the paper is the presence of a strong ordering effect in GPT-4’s ratings. Specifically, when scoring paired responses, GPT-4 increases the score of the response appearing earlier in the prompt. This bias manifests in both the scoring-based scheme against ChatGPT and the pairwise comparison scheme. The paper describes this effect as “significant,” emphasizing that the observed preference is not negligible and can meaningfully alter evaluation outcomes.

Intuitively, this bias suggests that GPT-4, when serving as an evaluator, is influenced by the positional context of the candidate responses. The system shows a tendency to favor the first input, even when the second response is comparable in quality. In settings where the evaluator is asked to judge two texts side-by-side, this creates a structural imbalance: the earlier response accrues an advantage unrelated to its content quality.

This effect matters because the automated evaluations are intended to be a cheaper alternative to human annotation for large-scale comparisons, and they are used to derive aggregate performance measures. If an evaluator systematically favors the earlier entry, then reported scores and rankings can reflect positional artifacts rather than true differences in chatbot ability. The bias is particularly salient in pairwise comparisons where the outcome directly determines win-loss counts that feed into a tournament ranking.

# Implications for Tournament-Style Elo Ratings

To synthesize pairwise judgments into system-level rankings, the paper employs Elo ratings. Elo constructs a tournament where models compete head-to-head by producing responses to the same prompt, and an evaluator (GPT-4 or human) decides the winner or declares a tie. Elo is designed to reflect relative skill: the rating changes after each match proportionally to the expected outcome, so unexpected upsets cause larger rating shifts while expected outcomes cause smaller shifts. The procedure starts all systems at a score of \(1000\) and uses \(K=32\). An Elo difference maps to an expected win rate; for instance, an Elo of \(1100\) versus \(1000\) implies an expected win rate of approximately \(65\%\) for the stronger system, while equal Elo scores imply an expected \(50\%\) win rate.

In this setting, the order bias introduced by GPT-4 can distort the matches. If the earlier response is favored, then the system placed first in the prompt is more likely to be judged the winner irrespective of content quality. Accumulated across many comparisons, this can shift Elo ratings, inflate perceived performance, and alter the rank ordering of systems. Since Elo aggregates many pairwise outcomes, even small biases at the match level can compound into meaningful artifacts at the system level.

The paper accounts for ordering effects in its Elo computation by repeatedly sampling labeled comparisons and running the rating procedure \(10{,}000\) times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects, such as which model pairs are evaluated first. This repetition helps mitigate artifacts due to sequence effects in the tournament process itself. However, if the evaluator’s judgments are biased by the order of responses within each prompt, the underlying match labels can still be skewed unless the prompt order is explicitly counterbalanced.

# Mitigating the Bias: Averaging Over Both Orders

Given the observed systematic bias favoring earlier responses, the paper recommends a straightforward control: report the mean score over both orders. Concretely, for any pairwise evaluation, one should present GPT-4 with two versions of the prompt: one in which system \(A\)’s response appears before system \(B\)’s response, and another in which the order is reversed. By averaging the scores or decisions across these two orders, the evaluator’s positional preference is effectively neutralized, and the resulting aggregate measure better reflects the intrinsic quality of the responses.

This mitigation strategy is applicable to both scoring against ChatGPT and direct pairwise comparisons, since both involve presenting a query alongside two responses. With order-averaged judgments, reported percentages relative to ChatGPT and head-to-head outcomes for Elo are less sensitive to positional bias, yielding more reliable and interpretable evaluations.

# Broader Considerations for Automated Evaluation

Beyond the order effect, the paper notes additional considerations regarding automated evaluations. When comparing GPT-4 and human rankings at the system level on the Vicuna benchmark, the authors report partial agreement with Kendall Tau \( \tau = 0.43 \) and Spearman rank correlation \( r = 0.55 \). At the example level, agreement between GPT-4 and human annotators’ majority vote is weaker with Fleiss \( \kappa = 0.25 \). These findings indicate that although GPT-4 and human ratings are moderately aligned overall, there are notable instances of disagreement.

The paper also observes that GPT-4 assigns significantly higher scores to its own outputs compared to human ratings (for example, Elo of \(1348\) versus \(1176\)), reflecting a potential self-preference when serving as the evaluator. This reinforces the overarching message: automated evaluation is a helpful and inexpensive alternative to human annotation, but it carries biases that must be measured and controlled. The order bias favoring earlier responses is a clear instance where a simple procedural fix—averaging over both orders—improves robustness.

# Conclusion

In the evaluation of finetuned chatbot models, the paper shows that GPT-4, when used as an automated judge of paired responses, exhibits a systematic bias favoring the earlier response. This order effect influences scores and pairwise outcomes and can distort tournament-style Elo rankings if left unaddressed. The paper recommends controlling for this bias by averaging results across both possible response orders, thereby neutralizing positional preferences. More broadly, the analysis underlines that while GPT-4-based evaluations are practical and often agree with human assessments at a system level, they are not free of biases. Recognizing and mitigating such effects is essential for trustworthy benchmarking, especially when these evaluations inform conclusions about the relative performance of state-of-the-art models trained with methods like QLoRA.
