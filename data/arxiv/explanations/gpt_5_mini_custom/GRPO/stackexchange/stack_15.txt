\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How many epochs over the 120B-token DeepSeekMath Corpus were used during 500B-token training?
Question:
DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is trained for 500B tokens, with 56% from the 120B-token DeepSeekMath Corpus. That implies roughly \(0.56 \times 500\,\mathrm{B} = 280\,\mathrm{B}\) tokens drawn from a 120B-token pool (i.e., about 2.3 epochs). 
- Is this interpretation correct? 
- Was the sampling mixture static throughout training or did you schedule it? 
- Did repeated passes risk overfitting to certain domains or sites, and how did near-duplication filtering interact with multiple epochs?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — your back-of-the-envelope epoch count is correct; the paper implies roughly $280\mathrm{B}$ math tokens were seen during the $500\mathrm{B}$-token run, which is about $280/120 \approx 2.33$ passes over the $120\mathrm{B}$ math pool. The paper does not describe any special time-varying scheduling of the $56\%$ mixture; it presents that data mix as the training distribution (i.e., a fixed mixture). The authors were aware of the overfitting / repetition risk and applied deduplication / near‑dup filtering and benchmark‑decontamination measures to mitigate it; they also argue that having a larger math corpus ($120\mathrm{B}$) reduces the harmful effects of repeated passes compared to using a much smaller math corpus repeatedly.

Why the $2.33$ epochs calculation is valid
The paper states DeepSeekMath‑Base was trained for $500\mathrm{B}$ tokens and that $56\%$ of training tokens came from the DeepSeekMath Corpus. So $0.56 \times 500\mathrm{B} = 280\mathrm{B}$ tokens drawn from the $120\mathrm{B}$ token pool, which gives $280/120 \approx 2.33$ epochs. The paper explicitly reports those numbers ($500\mathrm{B}$ total, $56\%$ math, $120\mathrm{B}$ corpus), so your arithmetic matches the paper.

Was the sampling mixture static or scheduled?
The paper presents the training data distribution as: $56\%$ DeepSeekMath corpus, $4\%$ AlgebraicStack, $10\%$ arXiv, $20\%$ Github code, $10\%$ natural-language CC. That description is given as the training "distribution of the data" for the $500\mathrm{B}$ run; there is no mention of a time-varying schedule (e.g., curriculum, annealed mixture, or epoch-dependent reweighting) for those proportions. Therefore, based on the paper, the safe reading is that the mixture was used as the training distribution (i.e., effectively static) during the $500\mathrm{B}$ continuation. If you need absolute certainty you would have to ask the authors, but the manuscript gives no indication of a dynamic sampling schedule.

Did repeated passes risk overfitting and how did dedup/near-dup filtering help?
The authors explicitly took multiple measures to reduce repetition, contamination and the chance of overfitting to narrow sources:

- URL-based deduplication and near‑deduplication: they reduced Common Crawl to about $40\mathrm{B}$ HTML pages with URL-based dedup + near‑dup filtering before math selection. This reduces repeated exposure to the same or very similar pages during pretraining.  
- Iterative corpus construction and diversity: they iteratively expanded their seed set (OpenWebMath + domain mining) across four iterations until the corpus stabilized ($120\mathrm{B}$ tokens, $35.5\mathrm{M}$ pages). That iterative effort and the manual URL-level annotations for math domains increase diversity in the final pool versus naively reusing a small set.  
- Benchmark decontamination: to avoid contaminating evaluation sets, they removed any text segment with a $10$-gram exact match to benchmark substrings (with special handling for short benchmark texts). This prevents direct memorization of benchmark answers from the math corpus.  
- Explicit choice of large corpus size: the paper contrasts training on small math corpora (which are necessarily repeated many times and tend to plateau) with the much larger DeepSeekMath corpus; they argue that a larger pool slows down harmful repetition and gives a longer-lasting learning curve.

The authors also note empirically that training on smaller corpora that are repeatedly cycled leads to faster plateauing; this is presented as motivation for collecting a larger ($120\mathrm{B}$) math corpus. So while multiple epochs ($\approx 2.3$) over the math pool do occur, the deduplication/near‑duplication, decontamination, and the larger corpus size are their main defenses against overfitting and benchmark leakage.

A few practical caveats and implications
Because the paper does not give per‑batch sampling logs or an explicit curriculum schedule, we cannot rule out minor implementation details (e.g., shuffling strategies, epoch boundaries, or periodic refresh of samples) that affect effective exposure. Also, approximate $2.3$ epochs is an average: in practice token usage may be influenced by tokenization differences, how mixed batches are formed, and the order in which shards were streamed. But conceptually and numerically, your interpretation ($\approx 2.3$ passes) matches the paper and the authors’ mitigation strategy is exactly the dedup/near‑dup and decontamination pipeline they describe.

If you want to dig deeper you could ask the authors whether they: (a) shuffled the entire mixed dataset between passes or streamed it in a fixed order, (b) performed any epoch-by-epoch reweighting, or (c) logged per-domain exposure counts — any of which would give a precise picture of per-domain repetition beyond the aggregate $56\%$ figure.
