\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Details on near-deduplication: what algorithm and what similarity threshold?
Question:
The paper mentions URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication but does not specify the method. 
- Did you use MinHash/LSH, SimHash, or another approach? 
- What was the similarity threshold and shingling scheme (character vs word n-grams)? 
- How did you avoid removing legitimate paraphrases or different solutions to the same problem, especially across languages?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not state the concrete near‑deduplication algorithm, shingling scheme, or similarity threshold. The only specifics given are “URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication” and that after these steps they kept about 40B HTML pages from Common Crawl before applying the fastText recall/ranking pipeline. Because those details are omitted, below I (1) summarize what the paper actually says, (2) explain the plausible/standard options that teams typically use in CC pipelines (so you can interpret the paper or reproduce a similar pipeline), and (3) give practical recommendations on how to avoid throwing away legitimate paraphrases or distinct solutions (especially across languages).

What the paper actually states
The authors explicitly describe URL‑based deduplication and “near‑deduplication” but do not name an algorithm (MinHash/LSH, SimHash, overlap hashing, etc.), list a shingling/tokenization scheme, or report numeric similarity thresholds. They do document one concrete decontamination rule for benchmarks: remove any text segment that contains an exact $10$-gram match with evaluation benchmarks (and for shorter benchmark texts of length $3$–$9$ grams they use exact matching). Beyond that, the dedup steps are only described at a high level.

Typical, plausible implementations (what they probably did or what you should consider)
Because Common Crawl scale and the paper’s workflow (40B pages, multilingual math data, then ranking with fastText) match common large corpus pipelines, the near‑deduplication is likely one of the standard approaches used in the community. Common choices and their practical parameters are:

- MinHash + LSH for Jaccard similarity on shingle sets. Use word n‑gram shingles (commonly $n=5$) or tokenized word shingles; for large corpora people usually compute MinHash signatures with $64$–$128$ permutations and LSH banding to cluster candidate near duplicates. A typical similarity cutoff for merging near‑duplicates is Jaccard $\ge 0.8$ (or roundabout $0.75$–$0.9$ depending on desired conservatism).

- SimHash (a.k.a. locality preserving hashing on token/feature vectors) for cosine/sketch similarity. SimHash is faster and memory‑cheap; Hamming distance thresholds of e.g. $\le 3$ to $6$ bits (out of $64$) are common to mark “near duplicates”. SimHash is often used for whole‑page deduping at web scale.

- Exact or normalized checksum (URL/path normalization + byte‑level hash) for URL‑based and exact deduplication. The authors explicitly note URL‑based dedupe first, which usually removes exact duplicates and canonicalized redirects.

- Shingle choice: word n‑grams are usually preferred for textual near‑duplication; character n‑grams can be more robust to small edits but risk conflating unrelated short overlap. For math‑heavy pages, it is common to treat math blocks (LaTeX snippets, code blocks) specially: normalize LaTeX macros, strip markup, and include those tokens in shingles so that pages with identical formulae are detected.

Why I list these: they are the community norms for CC scale processing; the paper gives no contrary detail.

Concrete parameter examples you can use to reproduce a comparable pipeline
If you need actionable parameters for replication, a reasonable pipeline that balances recall vs. false merges is:

- Exact deduplication: canonicalize URL, drop identical (URL, content‑hash) pairs.
- Normalize page: remove boilerplate, normalize whitespace, strip HTML tags except math/code blocks (or convert math to a canonical LaTeX form).
- Tokenization/shingling: word $5$-grams (sliding window), or mixed strategy: word $3$–$5$ grams for robustness.
- MinHash: $128$ hash functions; compute MinHash signature of shingle set.
- LSH banding: choose band/row split to target candidate Jaccard $\ge 0.8$ (typical banding configuration tuned empirically).
- Final cluster similarity cutoff: Jaccard $\ge 0.8$ (or use pairwise exact similarity on candidate pairs and confirm).
- For SimHash alternative: $64$-bit SimHash and Hamming distance threshold $\le 3$–$6$.

Tradeoffs and why thresholds matter
A higher threshold (e.g., $0.9$) aggressively deduplicates near‑identical pages but risks leaving many near duplicates in the data (lower recall). A lower threshold (e.g., $0.6$–$0.7$) merges more pages but risks conflating paraphrases or independently written solutions. For math corpora you typically prefer conservative merging (higher threshold) because you want to preserve distinct expositions and multilingual variants.

Avoiding removal of legitimate paraphrases or different solutions (especially across languages)
This is probably the most important part of your question. Practical strategies:

1) Be conservative with similarity thresholds. Use a higher cutoff (Jaccard $\ge 0.8$–$0.85$ or SimHash Hamming $\le$ small value) so that only very near duplicates are collapsed. That preserves paraphrases and genuinely different solutions.

2) Use language detection and dedupe within language buckets. Paraphrases across languages will have low surface overlap; they shouldn’t cluster together if you dedupe per language. If you want to dedupe cross‑language copies you must use translation or semantic hashing (much more risky).

3) Normalize and protect math/code segments. Many “duplicates” are scaffolding (site templates) plus similar formulas. If you normalize templates and focus shingles on the main content blocks, you avoid merging pages that share a formula but differ in exposition or solutions. Conversely, if the goal is to remove mirror copies of the same posting (e.g., scraped forum posts), treat identical formula + identical prose as duplicates.

4) Cluster at domain/URL level first and then content‑cluster within domain groups. The paper describes domain grouping and domain‑based heuristics—this lets you preserve domain‑specific independent solutions while removing duplicate mirrors.

5) Keep multiple representatives per cluster if the cluster contains different languages or markedly different answers. A common pragmatic rule is “if pages in a dedupe cluster differ by language or have low BLEU/ROUGE overlap after translation, keep one per language.” That prevents throwing out legitimate multilingual content.

6) Use semantic or embedding similarity only as a secondary signal. Embeddings can identify paraphrases, but they also risk conflation (especially short answers). If you use embeddings, use them for filtering candidate duplicates but verify with conservative lexical similarity tests before deleting.

7) Human spot checking and iterative thresholds. The paper’s iterative seed/enrichment approach implies they manually annotated URLs and used that to refine recall. You should sample clusters near your threshold and manually check whether clusters are erroneously merged; adjust thresholds accordingly.

How to treat “different solutions to the same problem”
If your aim is to keep diverse solution strategies (which is desirable for reasoning/cot training), do not dedupe purely by problem identifier or by short answer match. Instead:

- Detect problem statements vs. solutions: if two pages have the same problem statement but different solution bodies, treat them as distinct examples. You can detect this by comparing the overlap of the statement segment vs. the solution segment—if the statement overlaps but solution tokens differ substantially, keep both.

- If you must dedupe, dedupe at the level of entire page duplicates only, and preserve multiple solution blocks within the same page when they are distinct.

Note on cross‑language paraphrases
Cross‑language near‑deduplication is fragile. If you want to collapse a Spanish page and an English page that are near translations, you need either machine translation normalization (translate both to one language and run dedupe) or multilingual semantic hashing. Doing that reliably at CC scale is expensive and risks false merges. The safer route is to dedupe within languages and keep cross‑language variants.

Benchmark contamination note (from the paper)
The authors used an exact n‑gram scrubbing rule for contamination: they remove any text segment that contains an exact $10$-gram that appears in the benchmarks (and exact match for $3$–$9$ gram short benchmarks). That is a strict exact match filter, which is different from fuzzy dedupe; it means even if you choose a conservative near‑dedupe, you still need to run that exact n‑gram benchmark filter to be safe.

Bottom line
The paper does not give the low‑level dedup/near‑dedup algorithm or numeric thresholds. To reproduce a similar math corpus while preserving diverse solutions and multilingual content, use a conservative near‑dedupe (e.g., MinHash with word $5$-grams, $128$ hashes, Jaccard cutoff ≈ $0.8$–$0.85$ or $64$-bit SimHash with tight Hamming threshold), perform dedupe per language/domain, treat math and code blocks specially, and keep one representative per cluster per language or per distinct solution block. Finally, instrument the pipeline with manual checks and iterative tuning as the authors do (their iterative domain annotation suggests they validated collections by hand).
