In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the authors explain that because their pre-training corpus includes multilingual content rather than being English-only as in prior work, restricting the corpus to English would likely lead to worse performance on Chinese benchmarks. This is supported directly by their evaluation summary: they emphasize that the DeepSeekMath Corpus “encompasses data in multiple languages,” with English and Chinese being most represented, and report that training on this multilingual corpus enhances mathematical reasoning in both languages. In contrast, they note that prior math corpora are “primarily English-centric,” and these English-only corpora show limited improvement and can even hinder performance on Chinese mathematical reasoning. Empirically, DeepSeekMath-Base is superior on Chinese benchmarks (e.g., MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, Gaokao-MathQA) compared to models trained on English-only math corpora such as Proof-Pile-2 or OpenWebMath. The observed pattern—multilingual pre-training improving Chinese mathematical performance—makes the counterfactual restriction to English a plausible degradation in Chinese results.

Conceptually, the training objective for the base model is to minimize the expected negative log-likelihood over the pre-training distribution, i.e., minimize an objective like
\[
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{multi}}}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big],
\]
where \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{multi}}\) contains Chinese mathematical text alongside English. This exposure shapes tokenization, vocabulary coverage, syntax, and domain-specific mathematical discourse patterns in Chinese, improving generalization to Chinese evaluation distributions. If the corpus were restricted to English, the effective training distribution would become \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{en}}\), removing Chinese signal (\(P(\text{lang}=\text{zh}\mid (x,y)\in \mathcal{D}_{\text{en}})=0\)). That induces an out-of-distribution shift at test time on Chinese benchmarks, degrading performance due to poorer subword segmentation of Chinese tokens, weaker language modeling of Chinese mathematical terminology, and lack of practice with culturally specific formats and curricula present in the Chinese datasets. The paper’s comparative results against English-only corpora and their explicit observation that English-centric pre-training may even hinder Chinese performance substantiate that restricting to English would likely worsen Chinese benchmark scores.
