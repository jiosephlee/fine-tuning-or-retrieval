# From SFT to RL: A Practical Map of Modern LLM Training (RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO)

In the ever-evolving landscape of large language models (LLMs), researchers and engineers have developed a host of fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to squeeze out ever-better performance. Terms like Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and the newer Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) can feel overwhelming at first. In this post, we’ll demystify these methods through a simple unified paradigm. By comparing their data sources, reward signals, and characteristic “gradient coefficients,” you’ll be better equipped to choose the right approach for your goals and resource constraints.

## A Unified View of Training Objectives

At their core, all these fine-tuning and RL methods adjust the model’s parameters by ascending a gradient that looks like this:

∇ₜ𝒥(θ) = E₍q,o₎∼𝒟 [  
  (1 / |o|) ∑ₜ GC(q, o, t, π_rf) · ∇ₜ log π_θ(oₜ | q, o_{<t})  
],

Here:
- 𝒟 is the data source (pairs of prompts q and outputs o).
- π_rf is the “reward function” or reference policy.
- GC(q, o, t, π_rf) is the **gradient coefficient**—the heart of how each algorithm reinforces or penalizes tokens in o.
- |o| is the output length in tokens.

Although the notation may seem heavy, you can think of every method as plugging in:
1. A choice of data 𝒟 (where to get examples),
2. A reward or preference signal π_rf (rule-based or model-based), and
3. A recipe (GC) that turns that signal into up- or down-weighting of each token’s loss.

Let’s walk through each approach in this framework.

## 1. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

Data Source (𝒟):  
 A curated set of prompt-response pairs written or selected by humans.

Reward Function (π_rf):  
 Implicitly “1” for the correct human response, “0” elsewhere.

Gradient Coefficient (GC):  
 Every token in the human target is treated equally. GC = +1 for tokens in the gold response, 0 otherwise.

In practice, you just minimize cross-entropy on the demonstrations. No explicit reward model, no sampling—just follow the truth on a static dataset.

## 2. Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)

Data Source (𝒟):  
 Offline samples from the initial SFT model for each prompt.

Reward Function (π_rf):  
 A rule-based filter: +1 if the sampled output’s final answer is correct, 0 if wrong.

Gradient Coefficient (GC):  
 GC = +1 for every token in a “correct” sample. Incorrect samples contribute nothing.

RFT refines the SFT policy by “rejecting” bad outputs and retraining on those that solved the problem. It’s cheap—no learned reward model—but it treats all correct samples equally and ignores how “very correct” or “barely correct” they might be.

## 3. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Data Source (𝒟):  
 Offline pairs of sampled outputs, labeled by preference (A is better than B).

Reward Function (π_rf):  
 A learned pairwise preference model trained on data where humans or rules compare two responses to the same prompt.

Gradient Coefficient (GC):  
 A smooth function of the log-ratio between preferences of A and B. Tokens in the preferred output get boosted, tokens in the inferior one get dampened, all in proportion to how strong the preference model’s margin is.

By directly optimizing a pairwise loss, DPO sidesteps value function critics and focuses on making the policy align with human (or rule-based) judgments.

## 4. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

Data Source (𝒟):  
 Online samples from the current policy π_θ (fresh outputs at each update).

Reward Function (π_rf):  
 A scalar reward model r_φ(q, o) learned from comparisons, minus a per-token KL penalty to keep the policy from drifting too far from a reference policy π_ref.

Gradient Coefficient (GC):  
 Based on the clipped surrogate objective:  
  GC ≈ min [ r_t · (π_θ/π_old), clip(π_θ/π_old, 1±ε) · r_t ]  
 where r_t is an advantage estimate from a learned value function.

PPO trades off ease of optimization with stability constraints. It relies on a heavyweight critic (value model) and per-token KL to prevent degenerate solutions.

## 5. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Data Source (𝒟):  
 Online samples in “groups” of G outputs per prompt from the current policy.

Reward Function (π_rf):  
 A scalar reward model r_φ(q, o) learned from comparisons, no learned critic.

Gradient Coefficient (GC):  
 Advantages Ĥ_A are computed **relatively** within each group:  
  Ĥ_Aᵢ = (rᵢ – mean(r₁…r_G)) / std(r₁…r_G)  
 Each token in output i uses Ĥ_Aᵢ, clipped similarly to PPO, minus a batch-wise KL penalty to π_ref.

By comparing multiple samples **together**, GRPO natively captures how much better one output is than the group average. That removes the need for a separate value function and slashes memory use. It also handles fine-grained signals (process steps) when you score intermediate reasoning, not just final answers.

## Online vs. Offline Data and Rule vs. Model Rewards

When you’re choosing a method, ask:

1. Do I want **offline** data sampling (static or SFT-model samples) or **online** sampling (policy keeps evolving)?  
  • Offline methods (SFT, RFT, DPO) are simpler and reuse existing data.  
  • Online methods (PPO, GRPO) adapt to the policy as it improves but need more compute.

2. Do I have a **rule-based** reward (correct/incorrect, answer match) or a **learned** reward model (pairwise human/A.I. preferences)?  
  • Rule‐based is cheap but coarse.  
  • Model‐based can reflect nuance (partial credit, style), but you must train and validate the reward model.

3. Am I constrained by memory and don’t want to train a critic?  
  • GRPO removes the critic entirely by grouping outputs.  
  • PPO requires a full value network alongside the policy.

## Which Technique Fits Your Needs?

- If you have a small curated dataset and want modest gains with minimal infrastructure, stick to **SFT** or **RFT**.  
- If you already have pairwise preference data (e.g., from human annotations) and want smoother updates, **DPO** is a compelling choice.  
- If you need continuous improvement online, can train a reward model, and have GPU memory to spare, classic **PPO** will work.  
- If you’re targeting tasks with fine-grained feedback (like step-by-step math proofs) and must avoid a heavy critic, **GRPO** is tailor-made.

## Conclusion

From static supervised tuning to sophisticated group-based RL, modern LLM training methods can be seen as variations on one theme: how we gather data, score outputs, and shape the gradient coefficients that guide learning. By mapping SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO onto a single framework, you can navigate the landscape more confidently—and pick the tool that best balances your performance goals with compute and data realities.
