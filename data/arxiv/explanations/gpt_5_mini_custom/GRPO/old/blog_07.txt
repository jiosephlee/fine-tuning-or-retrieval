# Why ArXiv Alone Won’t Make Your LLM a Math Genius

## Introduction

When training a large language model (LLM) for mathematical reasoning, it’s tempting to think that arXiv—the giant repository of research papers—must be the secret sauce. After all, arXiv hosts millions of high-level technical documents, many with rich mathematical content. Yet, recent work on DeepSeekMath reveals a counterintuitive truth: simply feeding your model a mountain of arXiv texts does not reliably boost its ability to solve math problems. In fact, in controlled experiments, arXiv-only pretraining had little to no benefit—and sometimes even degraded performance.

In this post, we’ll walk through those experiments, unpack why arXiv on its own falls short, and offer practical advice on how to blend scholarly papers with carefully curated web data for more effective math reasoning in LLMs.

## The Allure of ArXiv

ArXiv seems like a natural choice for mathematical pretraining:

  • It contains dedicated sections on mathematics, physics, and computer science, often with formal proofs, lemmas, and complex equations.  
  • Its LaTeX source gives models direct exposure to mathematical notation and structure.  
  • It represents decades of expert-written material, far beyond what you’d typically find in generic web crawls.

Given these advantages, many previous systems have included arXiv as a substantial chunk of their math data. But is quantity of scholarly text enough to turn an open-source model into a math whiz?

## The Controlled Experiments

To isolate the impact of arXiv data, the DeepSeekMath team ran two clean experiments on two different model sizes (1.3B and 7B parameters):

1. **MathPile (8.9B tokens)**  
   This mixed corpus draws over 85% of its tokens from cleaned and filtered arXiv papers, along with some Wikipedia and StackExchange content.  

2. **ArXiv-RedPajama (28B tokens)**  
   This collection comprises raw arXiv LaTeX files with preamble and metadata removed—no extra filtering or selection.  

Each model was trained exclusively on one of these arXiv-dominated corpora (for 150B tokens on the 1.3B model, or 40B tokens on the 7B model), and then evaluated on a suite of benchmarks:

  • **GSM8K** and **MATH** for step-by-step quantitative problems.  
  • **MMLU-STEM** for multiple-choice STEM questions.  
  • **miniF2F** for informal-to-formal theorem proving in Isabelle.  

Surprisingly, neither arXiv corpus yielded noticeable gains. In some cases, performance even dipped compared to a baseline that had no dedicated math pretraining.  

## What Went Wrong?

Why didn’t arXiv data deliver the expected boost? Here are a few likely culprits:

  1. **Domain mismatch**. ArXiv papers are written for experts and assume familiarity with highly specialized notation and advanced concepts. That style doesn’t always translate to the more elementary or contest-style questions found in GSM8K and MATH.  
  2. **Lack of diversity**. While arXiv is deep, it isn’t broad. Common Crawl’s web pages include everything from math tutorials and problem-solving blogs to discussion forums where reasoning is spelled out step by step. Those everyday expositions appear more helpful for teaching LLMs how to walk through solutions.  
  3. **Over-repetition**. Training on a relatively small, teeming corpus of similar research articles can cause models to overfit on academic phrasing without internalizing the logical structure of problem solving.  

In contrast, DeepSeekMath built a 120 billion-token “DeepSeekMath Corpus” by iteratively mining Common Crawl for high-quality math pages—spanning multiple languages, difficulty levels, and styles of exposition. The result was a pretraining dataset that consistently outperformed arXiv-heavy alternatives across both English and Chinese benchmarks.

## Striking the Right Balance

Does this mean you should skip arXiv altogether? Not necessarily. Here’s how to incorporate scholarly papers more effectively:

  • **Mix with curated web data.** Use a classifier (e.g., fastText) to sift through Common Crawl for pages rich in worked examples, step-by-step solutions, and varied problem types. These sources complement the formal rigor of arXiv with accessible reasoning.  
  • **Iterative selection.** Seed your filter with a small set of trusted math pages, then mine more data. Re-train your filter on manually validated positives to expand coverage without drowning in noise.  
  • **Blend early and often.** Rather than a block of arXiv first and then web data, consider interleaving them during pretraining. This helps the model alternate between formal proofs and conversational explanations.  
  • **Watch for benchmark contamination.** Remove any exact or near-duplicate snippets of your test problems from the pretraining corpus to keep evaluations honest.

## Caveats and Future Directions

A few important notes before you throw out all your LaTeX sources:

  • **Specialized tasks.** ArXiv could still shine for highly specialized theorem proofs or research-style theorem formalization that require deep domain knowledge.  
  • **Model scale matters.** These experiments were run at 1.3B and 7B parameters. Very large models (e.g., hundreds of billions of parameters) might extract more from arXiv alone.  
  • **Hybrid approaches.** You may find that a small amount of arXiv fine-tuning on top of a web-selected pretraining stage offers the best of both worlds.  

Ultimately, arXiv is a valuable piece of the puzzle but not a standalone solution. For most competition-style math benchmarks and practical problem-solving skills, diverse, well-selected web data plays an outsized role.

## Conclusion

If your goal is to train an open-source LLM that can tackle middle school algebra, olympiad geometry, or contest-level calculus, loading up exclusively on arXiv papers is unlikely to get you there. DeepSeekMath’s experiments make it clear: the path to mathematical fluency in LLMs runs through a meticulously curated web corpus, not just through research archives. By blending the formal elegance of arXiv with the step-by-step richness of well-filtered web pages, you’ll give your model the best shot at genuine mathematical reasoning.
