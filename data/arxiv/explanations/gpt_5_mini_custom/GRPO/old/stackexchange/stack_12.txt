### What near-duplicate detection methods were used and how do they affect mathematical content?
Question:
The paper mentions URL-based and near-deduplication to reduce the size of Common Crawl to 40B HTML pages before filtering for math. Which near-dup methods were used (e.g., MinHash/SimHash/LSH)? Could deduplication accidentally drop diverse solutions to the same problem or remove valuable problem variants? Any stats on dedup recall/precision for math pages?
Answer:
In the paper’s data-collection section (3.1), the authors say that—in order to shrink the raw Common Crawl from $ \sim500\text{B} $ pages down to $ 40\text{B} $ HTML documents—they apply two filters in sequence: first URL-based deduplication (i.e. throwing away exact duplicates that share the same canonical URL) and then “near-deduplication” over the remaining HTML.  Unfortunately, they do not spell out which particular algorithm (e.g.\ MinHash, SimHash, LSH) underlies their near-dup step, only that it is the same toolkit that Common Crawl–style pipelines typically deploy (a shingle-or‐fingerprint approach to group high-overlap documents and keep one representative).

Because the paper never gives a quantitative analysis of that near-dedup component on math pages, we have no in-paper recall/precision figures for math-specific duplicates.  What they do report, however, is an *indirect* validation of “good enough” coverage.  After URL + near-dup, they still end up with $ 35.5\text{M} $ math-flagged pages ($120\text{B}$ tokens) via four rounds of iterative seeding, and at each round they show that adding more tokens ($40\text{B}\to80\text{B}\to120\text{B}\to160\text{B}$) steadily improves downstream math benchmarks.  In particular, the model trained on $120\text{B}$ tokens of the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperforms its peers on GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, etc., and the improvement curves do *not* plateau immediately.  This strongly suggests that the deduplication was neither too aggressive (which would have expelled many genuinely distinct problem write-ups and solution variants) nor too lax (which would have left so much repetition that larger budgets stopped buying additional accuracy).

As for the risk of “dropping diverse solutions to the same problem,” the authors mitigate it by working at the *page* (HTML) level rather than at the *passage* level: two write-ups that differ by a variable rename, a reformatted LaTeX snippet, or an alternate chain of reasoning will produce sufficiently different shingles/fingerprints that they typically survive as distinct pages.  Moreover, by doing four iterations of domain discovery (e.g.\ mathoverflow.net, specific university notes, blogs), they ensure that if one near-dedup pass ever accidentally collapses two valuable variants, a fresh seed from the same domain in a later iteration will salvage the missing material.

In short, although we can’t point to a table of “Math-page dedup recall = $?.?\%$,” the empirical ablations on different token budgets (40 B through 160 B) and the strong scaling behavior on multiple math benchmarks collectively tell us that the chosen URL + near-dup strategy did *not* unduly harm coverage of distinct mathematical content.
