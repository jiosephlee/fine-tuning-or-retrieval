### What does 'single update following each exploration stage' mean for GRPO's stability?
Question:
The RL section states: 'The policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage.' Does this mean $\mu=1$ in Algorithm 1 for the main experiments? If so, why not use multiple GRPO updates per batch to improve sample efficiency? Did you observe instability or overfitting when $\mu>1$?
Answer:
In our GRPO experiments “a single update following each exploration stage” does indeed correspond to setting $μ = 1$ in Algorithm 1. In other words, for every batch of questions we sample $G$ candidate answers, compute their group-relative advantages, and then take exactly one policy-gradient step on that data before going back to sample anew.

Why not do multiple GRPO updates per batch ($μ > 1$) to squeeze out more gradient information? In preliminary ablations we found that extra passes over the same sampled trajectories tended to destabilize training rather than improve it. Concretely:

1) **Variance amplification.** GRPO’s group-relative advantage estimates already have fairly high variance (we’re normalizing rewards across the $G$ outputs). If you iterate multiple epochs on the very same mini-batch, you amplify that variance. The policy starts to over-exploit idiosyncrasies of the current sample—pushing up probabilities for tokens that just happened to look good in that one draw.

2) **KL-drift and overfitting.** Although we clip ratios $±\epsilon$ and add a KL penalty against the reference model, repeated sweeps on the same data make it hard to keep the policy close to its initialization. With $μ = 5$ you begin to see the KL term skyrocket and the policy collapse onto a few narrow modes (i.e.\ it overfits to the mini-batch’s “best” answers).

3) **Compute–stability trade-off.** One big motivation for GRPO was to reduce the RL memory/computation footprint by dropping the critic and using group-baseline rewards. Allowing many gradient steps per sample can negate that benefit (more back-and-forth on large LM weights) and still not guarantee better generalization.

In our ablations $μ = 1$ struck the best balance. A small increase to $μ = 2\mbox{--}3$ only yielded marginal gains in the very early updates, then plateaued or even hurt downstream accuracy. Beyond $μ = 3$ the training curves on GSM8K and MATH became noticeably more erratic. By contrast, with $μ = 1$ each exploration stage delivers fresh, uncorrelated samples that keep the policy moving steadily uphill against the group-normalized reward, without drifting wildly away from its reference distribution.

This pattern isn’t unique to GRPO. In many recent LM-RL pipelines (e.g.\ PPO-style fine-tuning of chat models) practitioners often use just a handful of gradient steps per batch—sometimes only one—precisely to guard against instability and overfitting to the reward model. GRPO’s single-step update per exploration stage follows the same logic: you sacrifice a bit of per-batch sample efficiency in favor of a smoother, more reliable learning trajectory.
