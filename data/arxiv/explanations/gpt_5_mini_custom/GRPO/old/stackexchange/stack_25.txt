### How are answers normalized on MATH for exact match scoring, especially for expressions and units?
Question:
For MATH evaluation without tools, how are predicted answers normalized? Are algebraic expressions canonicalized (e.g., factoring, simplification), are numeric tolerances used for decimals, and how are units or formats handled? Without careful normalization, models might be penalized for equivalent forms—how is this addressed?
Answer:
In our work on DeepSeekMath, when we report “exact-match” accuracy on the MATH benchmark (without any external tools at evaluation time), we simply defer to the original MATH grading script. Concretely, that script does three things:

1. **Answer Extraction and Trimming**  
   It looks at your model’s full chain-of-thought plus final line and uses a small set of regexes to pull out what it believes is the “final answer.” It then strips leading/trailing whitespace, any trailing period or comma, and normalizes ASCII characters (e.g. turning a Unicode minus “–” into `$-$`, ensuring the fraction slash “/” is the ASCII slash, etc.).

2. **Exact-Value Comparison via Python Arithmetic**  
   Because every official MATH problem has an integer or rational answer (no floating-point approximations, no units), the grader simply feeds both your predicted string and the gold string into Python’s exact-arithmetic machinery—typically `fractions.Fraction` or, in some forks of the MATH code, a quick `sympy.Rational` or `sympy.simplify` check. Once they’re both parsed, it checks exact equality. That means  
   – “$\tfrac{1}{2}$” and “$0.5$” are both converted to the exact `Fraction(1,2)`, so they match.  
   – No floating-point tolerance is used, because there is no ambiguity in the ground-truth (everything is exact).

3. **No General Symbolic Canonicalization or Unit Handling**  
   You will not be asked to output `$x^2 - 4x + 4$` vs. `$(x - 2)^2$,` since MATH answers are numeric. There is no built-in procedure in the official grader that expands, factors, or rearranges arbitrary algebraic expressions, nor is there any handling for physical units (the dataset simply doesn’t include them). In practice, models are prompted to output “just the number” so that the simple strip-and-parse pipeline never sees stray text or unit symbols.

Putting it all together: the MATH “exact match” score is really a thin veneer over exact rational-value comparison with minimal string cleanup. You don’t need a heavyweight algebraic simplifier or floating-point tolerances—just output the correct integer or rational in a clean ASCII form and the official script will accept any equivalent string that parses to the same exact value.
