### What is the effect of group size $G$ on bias/variance and sample efficiency in GRPO?
Question:
The paper often uses $G=64$ samples per prompt. Intuitively, larger $G$ might reduce variance of the group baseline but increase compute. Is there any analysis or ablation showing how performance or stability changes with $G$ (e.g., $G=4,8,16,32,64$)? Does too small a $G$ make the group-relative advantage too noisy? Conversely, does very large $G$ reduce the effective learning signal by over-normalizing?
Answer:
In the published DeepSeekMath work the authors settle on a group size of $G = 64$ when running GRPO, but you won’t find a dedicated “G‐sweep” in the paper. All of their reported RL results—for self‐consistency, for the group‐relative baseline and for advantage normalization—use exactly 64 sequences per prompt. They do not present an ablation over, say, $G = 16,\,32,\,128$, nor do they tabulate curve‐stability or final‐accuracy as a function of $G$.

That said, one can understand qualitatively how $G$ trades off bias, variance, and sample efficiency:

  * If you make $G$ very small (e.g.\ $G = 2$ or $4$), then your “group” baseline  
    $$b(q) \;=\; \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G r_i$$  
    is estimated with very high variance. Because in GRPO the per‐token advantage is  
    $$\hat A_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{r_i - b(q)}{\mathrm{std}\bigl(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)},$$  
    a noisy $b(q)$ translates into a noisy, unstable gradient. In the extreme $G = 1$ case, $b(q)=r_1$, so the advantage is identically zero and learning stalls entirely.

  * As you increase $G$, your group‐average and group‐standard‐deviation each become better estimates of the true distribution of rewards under the old policy. That drives down variance in your gradient estimates and so typically stabilizes and speeds up convergence—up to a point.

  * However, once $G$ is large, you begin to “over‐normalize.” If almost every sample in the batch is of similar quality, then $r_i - b(q)$ shrinks toward zero, and every advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ becomes small. In other words you have very low signal‐to‐noise: your policy update steps become correspondingly tiny and you need many more gradient steps (or a higher learning rate) to nudge the model.

Because inference of $G$ sequences is the dominant cost in GRPO (you sample $G \times B$ outputs per minibatch), there is an obvious compute‐vs.‐stability trade‐off: doubling $G$ roughly doubles wall‐clock time per RL update but gives you a better baseline estimate. In practice one usually sees diminishing returns in going from $G = 32$ to $G = 64$ or from $G = 64$ to $G = 128$, whereas going below $G \approx 16$ often noticeably degrades stability (unless you also dial down your learning rate).

In short, the paper’s choice of $G = 64$ is a practical “sweet spot”—large enough to tame baseline‐variance but small enough to keep sampling costs manageable. Although DeepSeekMath does not show a numeric ablation sweep, the general RL‐theory intuition carries over: take $G$ big enough that the group baseline  
$$\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G r_i$$  
is reasonably precise, but not so big that you drive all your advantages toward zero. A rule of thumb is to stay in the mid‐tens to low‐hundreds; outside that regime you either get noisy gradients or vanishing update magnitudes.
