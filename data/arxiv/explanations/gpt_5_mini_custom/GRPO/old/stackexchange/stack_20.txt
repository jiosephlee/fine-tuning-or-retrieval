### Is that harmful or desirable?  
In fact, this pattern is what gives modern LLMs their in-context learning prowess.  By exposing the model to a *randomized* sequence of (prompt, solution) pairs, without rigid delimiters, you teach it to:

• Automatically detect where one example ends and the next begins (via distributional cues and the EOS token).  
• Generalize from *any* preceding pair of question and answer—so when it sees a fresh prompt, it’s already in the “ask → chain-of-thought → final answer” mode, even if the last example was in Chinese or used PoT instead of CoT.  

Because the training pipeline shuffles examples across math domains and solution styles, the model learns to focus on the *current* prompt rather than mechanically copying bits of the last solution.  In other words, while there is theoretical leakage, it actually *regularizes* the model towards robust in-context learning rather than causing template-sticking or rote copying.

If you *did* want to prevent any possibility of one example bleeding into the next—say in a scenario where absolute isolation is critical—you could manually prepend and append strong sentinel tokens (e.g.\ $\langle\vert\mathrm{sep}\vert\rangle$ or distinct role tags).  But at the scale of hundreds of thousands of SFT examples, the paper’s authors found that simple EOS-based concatenation already yields excellent – in-domain and cross-domain – mathematical reasoning performance without any obvious adverse effects.
