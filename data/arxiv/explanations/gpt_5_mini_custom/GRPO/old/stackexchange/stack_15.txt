### Why does Online RFT outperform offline RFT, and is it due to distribution shift during training?
Question:
The paper reports that Online RFT (sampling from the live policy) eventually surpasses offline RFT (sampling from the fixed SFT policy). Is this mainly because the policy drifts away from the SFT distribution, making fixed offline samples stale? Are there quantitative measures of the policy shift or KL between the actor and SFT over time that correlate with the observed performance gap?
Answer:
In the paper’s unified‐RL analysis (see Figure “rl‐analysis”), you can see that Rejection‐Sampling Fine‐Tuning (RFT) with a fixed offline dataset (i.e.\ samples drawn once from the SFT policy and never refreshed) initially keeps pace with Online RFT (where each policy update re‐samples from the model itself). But after a handful of updates, offline RFT all but stalls while Online RFT continues to climb. The intuition the authors give—and which I believe underlies the gap—is exactly the distribution‐shift you describe: as the policy drifts away from the original SFT distribution, that static pool of “offline” samples ceases to cover the outputs the model is now assigning non‐negligible probability. In other words, offline RFT ends up rehearsing corrections on regions of output space that the current policy almost never visits, whereas Online RFT automatically keeps mining fresh examples in the regions the model actually explores.

Although the paper does not report an explicit curve of, say,  
$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{t}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}\bigr)
$$  
over time, the performance divergence in their learning curves is a fairly direct proxy: the point where offline RFT plateaus is precisely when the actor has drifted so far that “rejecting” on the old‐policy samples stops yielding new information. If one wanted to validate this quantitatively, a natural experiment would be to record at each update step the average per‐token KL between the current policy $\pi_{t}$ and the SFT policy $\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},\,o\sim\pi_t}\Bigl[\log \pi_t(o\mid q)\;-\;\log \pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}(o\mid q)\Bigr],
$$

and then plot that against the corresponding offline‐RFT vs.\ online‐RFT test accuracy. One would expect the offline RFT curve to flatten out once that KL grows large, exactly when the two sample distributions meaningfully diverge.

So to summarize: yes, the superior scaling of Online RFT in the paper is largely driven by distribution shift of the policy away from the SFT baseline—making a one‐time offline sample set “stale”—and while the authors demonstrate this effect in their learning curves, they stop short of reporting explicit KL‐drift statistics. Computing that drift directly would be a clean way to correlate how far the actor has moved from the SFT seed and when offline RFT stops being useful.
