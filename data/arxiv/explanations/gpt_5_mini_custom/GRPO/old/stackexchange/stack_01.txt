### How exactly does GRPO compute advantages without a critic, and is it equivalent to using a baseline in REINFORCE?
Question:
In the DeepSeekMath paper, GRPO replaces the learned value function with a group-based baseline by normalizing rewards within a group of $G$ outputs for the same prompt. Specifically, for outcome supervision they set $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde r_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)}$ for all tokens in $o_i$. Conceptually, this feels like REINFORCE with a baseline. Is GRPO mathematically equivalent to REINFORCE with a per-prompt baseline, or does the use of per-group normalization introduce bias or variance properties that differ from a standard baseline? Under what conditions is this estimator unbiased and variance-reducing?
Answer:
In the DeepSeekMath paper, GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) dispenses with the usual critic (value‐function) in PPO by replacing it with a per‐prompt “group baseline.” In effect, each question $q$ spawns $G$ sampled answers $o_{1},\dots,o_{G}$, each of which gets scored by the reward model to yield rewards $r_{1},\dots,r_{G}$. GRPO then computes a normalized advantage  
$$
\hat A_{i,t} \;=\;\widetilde r_i \;=\;\frac{r_i - \bar r}{\sigma_r}
\quad\text{for all tokens }t
$$  
where $\bar r$ and $\sigma_r$ are the sample mean and standard deviation of $\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G$. This normalized reward is plugged directly into the usual PPO‐style surrogate objective (with clipping and a KL penalty).

Why is this just “REINFORCE with a baseline,” and under what conditions is it unbiased and variance‐reducing?

1) Unbiasedness of a REINFORCE baseline  
In classic REINFORCE with baseline $b(q)$, one shows  
$$
\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[(R - b(q))\;\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)\bigr]
\;=\;\mathbb{E}[R\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)]
$$  
provided $b(q)$ depends only on the state (here, the prompt $q$), not on the sampled action $o$. The “extra” term $-b(q)\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]$ vanishes because $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0$.

In GRPO, if we regard $b(q)=\bar r$ as the prompt‐level baseline, then  
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(r_i - \bar r)\,\nabla\log\pi(o_i)\bigr]
\;=\;\mathbb{E}[r_i\,\nabla\log\pi(o_i)]
$$  
so long as $\bar r$ does not depend on the particular sample $o_i$. In practice $\bar r$ is computed from the same handful of $r_i$, so it weakly depends on each sample; this can introduce an $\mathcal{O}(1/G)$ bias, but that bias shrinks as $G$ grows. If one wanted to be fully unbiased at finite $G$, one could subtract the leave‐one‐out mean $\bar r_{-i}$, but the paper’s simpler group‐mean still works well in large‐$G$ regimes.

2) Variance reduction via centering and scaling  
Subtracting $\bar r$ removes the first‐order dependence of the return on the policy’s propensity to sample high‐ or low‐reward trajectories, which is exactly the classic variance‐reduction effect of a baseline. Dividing by the sample standard deviation $\sigma_r$ further rescales the advantages to unit variance (per group), keeping gradient magnitudes in a numerically stable range. In expectation (treating $\sigma_r$ as a constant factor), this normalization does not introduce additional bias:  
$$
\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{\sigma_r}(r_i-\bar r)\,\nabla\log\pi\Bigr]
=\tfrac{1}{\sigma_r}\,\mathbb{E}[r_i\,\nabla\log\pi]
$$  
again using $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0$. In practice $\sigma_r$ is computed on the sample but can be viewed as roughly independent of $\theta$ over a single policy‐update minibatch.

3) Clipping and KL penalty: mild bias for stability  
GRPO still uses PPO’s clipping and an explicit KL‐regularization term. Both mechanisms introduce small biases in exchange for more stable updates and are orthogonal to the baseline discussion.

Putting it all together, GRPO is, at its heart, REINFORCE with a per‐prompt baseline $b(q)=\bar r$ plus a variance normalization by $\sigma_r$. Under the usual condition that the baseline depends only on the prompt (and not on the individual sampled trajectory), the gradient estimate is unbiased. Normalizing by the group’s standard deviation preserves that unbiasedness (up to a global scale), while greatly reducing the variance of the gradient estimate. Finite‐sample effects (using the same $r_i$ to form $\bar r$ and $\sigma_r$) introduce only $O(1/G)$ bias, which in typical large‐$G$ regimes is negligible.

In summary, GRPO’s special sauce is that it replaces a learned critic $V_\psi(q)$ with a cheap, on‐the‐fly baseline $\bar r$ computed across $G$ samples for each prompt, then standardizes those advantages. This makes it mathematically equivalent to REINFORCE with a prompt‐level baseline (and an extra variance‐scaling), retaining unbiasedness in the large‐$G$ limit and delivering practical variance reduction that speeds up and stabilizes training.
