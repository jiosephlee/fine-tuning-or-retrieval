# Mining 120B Math Tokens from the Open Web: A Hands-On Guide to Domain Data at Scale

If you’ve ever wondered how to wrangle a massive, domain-specific dataset from the wild web, you’re in good company. In building DeepSeekMath, our goal was to mine and curate 120 billion math-related tokens from Common Crawl, then use them to train an open-source language model with cutting-edge mathematical reasoning. In this post, I’ll walk you through the key steps of our iterative pipeline—from a modest seed corpus to a colossal multilingual dataset—so you can adapt the approach to other specialties like coding, finance, or any field where domain data matters.

## Why Domain Data Matters

General web text is great for learning everyday language. But when it comes to the exacting logic of mathematics (or the jargon of a particular industry), you need focused material. A high-quality, domain-specific corpus can propel smaller models to punch above their weight. We showed that pre-training on math-filtered web pages lets a 7 billion-parameter model closely rival much larger closed-source systems on competition-level math problems. The secret sauce? Data selection at scale.

## An Iterative Pipeline for Mining Math Text

At the heart of our workflow is an iterative loop: train a classifier on a trusted seed set, use it to pull candidate pages from Common Crawl, refine your classifier with newly discovered examples, and repeat until the returns diminish. Here’s how it played out in DeepSeekMath.

### 1. Seed Corpus and Classifier Bootstrapping

We began with OpenWebMath, a public collection of verified math web pages, as our positive examples. To teach a text classifier to recognize “mathiness,” we sampled:

- 500 000 pages from OpenWebMath (positives)
- 500 000 random pages from Common Crawl (negatives)

We trained a fastText model with 256-dimensional vectors, word n-grams up to length 3, and a few passes over the data. This lightweight classifier runs in seconds per million pages, making it ideal for our first pass.

### 2. Deduplication at Web Scale

Common Crawl contains tens of billions of HTML pages, many of which are duplicates or near-duplicates. Before classification, we applied URL-based deduplication and content fingerprinting to collapse the corpus to roughly 40 billion unique pages. This step dramatically cuts compute cost and focuses our efforts on novel content.

### 3. Recall, Ranking, and Quality Filtering

With our fastText model in hand, we scored all 40 billion deduplicated pages. Instead of a hard cutoff, we ranked pages by their math-likeness score and experimented with keeping the top 40 billion, 80 billion, 120 billion, and so on. Early training runs showed that the top 120 billion math-weighted tokens struck a balance between quality and scale. In practice, we extracted about 35.5 million web pages totaling 120 billion tokens.

### 4. Discovering New Math Domains

One classifier iteration can miss entire subdomains devoted to math. To recover those, we grouped pages by base domain (e.g., “mathoverflow.net”), then flagged domains where at least 10 percent of pages passed our filter. Within those domains, we manually identified URL patterns like `/questions` or `/tutorials` and added uncollected pages under those paths back into our positive set. Retraining the classifier on this enriched seed set let us uncover more high-value content in subsequent rounds.

### 5. Iterating to 120 B Tokens

We ran four full iterations of “train classifier → recall → rank → augment seed.” By the fourth pass, over 98 percent of the pages we’d ever collect were already in the third pass, signaling diminishing returns. At that point we froze the corpus at 35.5 million pages (120 billion tokens).

## Decontamination: Keeping Benchmarks Clean

A pitfall of mining web data is accidentally including text from evaluation sets, which can inflate scores. To prevent contamination, we removed any page containing an exact 10-gram match to public math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) or, for shorter trivia, exact matches down to 3-grams. This simple n-gram filter ensured our pre-training data stayed genuinely “unseen” by the model when we later measured its performance.

## Multilingual Coverage

Although our classifier was trained on English seed data, many highly scored pages turned out to be in other languages—Chinese, Spanish, and beyond. Rather than discarding them, we embraced the multilingual signal. The final DeepSeekMath corpus now includes diverse linguistic sources, which helped our model improve on both English and Chinese math benchmarks. If your domain spans multiple tongues, an analogous approach can surface valuable non-English content.

## Adapting the Recipe to Other Domains

While the details above focus on mathematics, the core ideas generalize:

1. Choose a **high-quality seed corpus** for your domain (e.g., a curated finance blog, a code repository).
2. Train a **fast, scalable classifier** (fastText, token-based) to distinguish “in-domain” from “out-of-domain.”
3. **Deduplicate** your web-scale source to remove noise and redundancy.
4. **Rank** all candidate pages by domain relevance score, then pick a cutoff based on data volume versus quality trade-offs.
5. Identify **related domains** by spotting clusters of high-scoring pages and hand-labeling additional URL patterns.
6. **Iterate** until the discovery curve flattens.
7. **Decontaminate** by filtering out any overlap with your evaluation or staging benchmarks.
8. Optionally, **embrace multilingual** or multi-format content to boost robustness.

Whether you’re building a dataset for advanced Python examples, financial regulations, or specialized medical text, this pipeline scales from tens of gigabytes to hundreds of gigabytes—甚至上到几百 GB (see, multilingual!).

## Conclusion

Mining 120 billion math tokens from the open web was a rewarding engineering journey that yielded a corpus rich in structure and variety. By combining fastText classification, large-scale deduplication, domain discovery, and careful decontamination, we unlocked data to train a math-savvy language model that rivals much larger giants. The same recipe applies whenever you need domain data at scale. Happy data hunting!
