According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the authors' negative finding about arXiv data effectiveness is accompanied by caveats, and they explicitly mention that the conclusion about arXiv's usefulness might change with larger model scale. In Section “ArXiv Papers Seem Ineffective in Improving Mathematical Reasoning,” the authors report that training on arXiv-only corpora—MathPile (dominated by arXiv, \(8.9\mathrm{B}\) tokens) and ArXiv-RedPajama (\(28.0\mathrm{B}\) tokens)—did not improve and sometimes degraded performance when applied to models of size \(1.3\mathrm{B}\) (trained for \(150\mathrm{B}\) tokens) and \(7\mathrm{B}\) (trained for \(40\mathrm{B}\) tokens). Immediately after presenting these results, they caution that the conclusion is limited by their experimental scope and explicitly list untested conditions: specific math-related tasks not included, effects of mixing arXiv with other data types, and “whether the benefits of arXiv papers would manifest themselves at a larger model scale.” This framing makes clear they view the negative result as provisional rather than definitive.

This caution is sensible given the broader empirical context the paper establishes: the utility of data sources depends on both scale and composition. The authors show elsewhere that large, carefully filtered web math data (\(120\mathrm{B}\) tokens) can yield substantial gains, and that starting from a code-trained base improves math reasoning—both observations that hinge on data-model synergy rather than any single corpus being universally beneficial. Since arXiv texts are longer, formal, and heterogeneous, their signal may require larger capacity (\(\geq 70\mathrm{B}\)) or complementary corpora to be effectively leveraged. The authors’ explicit caveats—task coverage, data mixing, and model scaling—acknowledge these interactions, so it is reasonable that the arXiv conclusion could change at larger scales or under different training mixtures.
