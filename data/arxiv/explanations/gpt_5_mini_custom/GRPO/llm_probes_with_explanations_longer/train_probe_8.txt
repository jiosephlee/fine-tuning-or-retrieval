In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the decontamination procedure removes any text segment that contains an exact n-gram match with evaluation benchmarks, and therefore the rule implicitly assumes that a 10-gram exact match suffices. This follows directly from their stated filtering criteria: they explicitly remove “any text segment containing a 10-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks,” and only relax this to exact matching for shorter benchmark texts (at least 3-grams) when the benchmark itself is shorter than 10 tokens. The use of exact 10-gram matches as the default criterion encodes the assumption that an identical 10-token span is a strong enough signal of benchmark leakage, while being rare enough in naturally occurring web text to avoid excessive false positives.

This assumption is sensible because exact-match uniqueness grows quickly with n-gram length. If the vocabulary size is \(V\) and tokens are roughly independent (a simplifying assumption for uniqueness), the probability that a random 10-token span coincides exactly with a particular benchmark 10-gram is on the order of \(V^{-10}\), which is vanishingly small for typical \(V\) (e.g., subword vocabularies with \(V \sim 3\times 10^4\)). Even accounting for language redundancy and structured mathematical phrasing, the effective collision rate for exact 10-gram matches across a large corpus remains extremely low, making a 10-gram exact match a practical proxy for contamination. The authors also add a special case for short benchmark items—using exact matching down to 3-grams when the benchmark text is shorter than 10 tokens—to preserve recall when long n-grams are impossible, further indicating a calibrated trade-off: 10-grams for high precision in typical cases, with shorter exact matches only when necessary.
