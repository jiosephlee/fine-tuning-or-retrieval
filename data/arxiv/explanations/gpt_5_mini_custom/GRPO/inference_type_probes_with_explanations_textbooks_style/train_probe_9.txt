Chapter Title: Decontamination by Exact n-Gram Matching in DeepSeekMath

# Introduction
Ensuring fair and reliable evaluation is central to training language models for mathematical reasoning. If training data inadvertently contains parts of the evaluation benchmarks, the resulting performance may be inflated and unrepresentative. The paper “DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models” addresses this risk through a decontamination step that removes overlaps between pre-training data and test benchmarks. A key design choice is to filter any text segment containing an exact 10-gram match with substrings from the evaluation benchmarks, and for benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, to use exact matching at those shorter lengths. This chapter explains the decontamination procedure described in the paper, the implicit assumption that an exact 10-gram match suffices to capture problematic overlaps, and the implications of such a rule for training integrity and evaluation validity.

# Overview of Data Collection and the Role of Decontamination
The DeepSeekMath Corpus is constructed by mining and filtering a large quantity of web text from Common Crawl. The authors begin from a high-quality seed (OpenWebMath), train a fastText classifier to recall math-related web pages, and iteratively enrich the positive examples through domain-level analysis and human annotation. After multiple iterations, this pipeline yields 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120 billion tokens, in multiple languages.

Within this broader pipeline, decontamination is applied to prevent overlap with evaluation benchmarks. The paper explicitly states that they filter out web pages containing questions or answers from English benchmarks (such as GSM8K and MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (such as CMATH and AGIEval). The criterion is a substring match based on exact n-grams. Specifically, if a text segment contains a 10-gram that exactly matches any substring in the evaluation benchmarks, that segment is removed from the training corpus. For benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, exact matching at those shorter lengths is employed. This rule is simple to implement at scale and aligns with the need to protect the integrity of benchmark results.

# The Decontamination Procedure
The decontamination procedure is described in the paper as follows. Consider the set of benchmark texts, each of which can be viewed as a sequence of tokens. The filtering rule operates over contiguous strings of tokens with length 10, called “10-grams” in the paper’s phrasing. Formally, if a web text contains a contiguous sequence of length 10 that exactly matches a contiguous subsequence of any evaluation benchmark, the web text is filtered from the training corpus.

When the benchmark text is shorter than 10 tokens but has at least 3 tokens, the same procedure is applied at the shorter length. In other words, for such benchmark texts, if a training text contains a contiguous sequence that exactly matches a contiguous subsequence of the benchmark at length $n$, where $3 \le n < 10$, the training text is also filtered. The paper does not mention any fuzzy or approximate matching; the condition is exact equality of the strings at the chosen length.

We can express the core rule as follows. Let $B$ denote the set of evaluation benchmark texts, and let $T$ be a candidate training text. For a fixed $n$, define
$S_n(b)$ as the set of all contiguous substrings of length $n$ in a benchmark text $b \in B$, and let $S_n(T)$ be the set of all contiguous substrings of length $n$ in $T$. The decontamination condition is:
- If $n = 10$, remove $T$ if there exists $b \in B$ such that $S_{10}(T) \cap S_{10}(b) \ne \varnothing$.
- If a benchmark text $b$ has length $L_b$ with $3 \le L_b < 10$, remove $T$ if $S_{L_b}(T) \cap S_{L_b}(b) \ne \varnothing$.

This procedure is applied to web pages identified as mathematical content and occurs prior to pre-training. In practice, “remove” means that any such contaminated page is excluded from the pre-training corpus.

# The Implicit Assumption Behind 10-Gram Matching
By relying on exact 10-gram matches to detect overlap, the procedure implicitly assumes that exact matching at length 10 sufficiently captures the problematic cases of contamination for typical benchmark items. In other words, the working premise is that if a training text contains a substantial verbatim overlap with a benchmark item—enough to compromise evaluation integrity—then it will likely contain at least one exact 10-gram match with that item.

In addition, the paper’s explicit handling of shorter benchmark texts reflects a boundary case of this assumption. For benchmark entries that are shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens, the procedure lowers the n-gram length to the benchmark length itself, still requiring exact matching. This preserves the spirit of the rule: use exact matching at a fixed length that is intended to be long enough to detect meaningful copying, while also not missing overlaps when the benchmark text is shorter than the default 10 tokens.

Intuitively, the choice of $n=10$ balances two competing objectives. First, it should be long enough that accidental matches are unlikely across a large and diverse web corpus. Second, it should be short enough to catch substantive verbatim overlaps, rather than requiring an unrealistically long exact match that might miss genuinely copied content. The paper does not argue formally about this trade-off; instead, it specifies the rule as an operational criterion in the pipeline.

# Why Exact Matching and Fixed-Length n-Grams?
The paper’s description highlights exact matching rather than approximate or semantic matching. Exact matching has practical advantages in scalability and precision: searching for exact substrings of a fixed length lends itself to efficient indexing and fast elimination at web scale. Moreover, exact matches reduce the ambiguity of what constitutes contamination; if a model saw a literal fragment of a benchmark during training, exact matching reveals it without subjective judgment.

Using a fixed length $n$ also simplifies the process. For most benchmark items, a specific length such as $n=10$ is straightforward to implement. The special handling for shorter benchmark texts ensures that they are not exempted merely due to their brevity; exact matching is still applied, just at the shorter length $n=L_b$ for $3 \le L_b < 10$.

Together, these choices form a clear, mechanical rule. They answer the practical need for a decontamination method that can be applied consistently across tens of billions of tokens.

# Consequences and Edge Cases Within the Paper’s Framing
Given the paper’s rule, several consequences follow.

First, the procedure will remove any training text that contains exact substrings of length 10 found in the benchmarks. This ensures that obvious verbatim copying of benchmark content is filtered out, aligning with the desire to avoid inflated performance due to memorization.

Second, for short benchmark items with $3 \le L_b < 10$, any exact match at length $L_b$ triggers filtering. This avoids the loophole where short items would never trigger a 10-gram match simply because they are shorter than 10 tokens.

Third, because matching is exact, paraphrased or lightly edited versions of benchmark items that do not contain a length-$n$ exact match will not be flagged by this rule. The paper does not claim to address such cases; instead, it specifies the rule it uses and applies it uniformly. Within the scope of the paper, the emphasis is on preventing verbatim contamination as identified by exact n-gram overlap.

Finally, by making the rule explicit, the paper clarifies the training-cleanliness criteria under which DeepSeekMath is evaluated. This transparency is important for interpreting the reported benchmark results, particularly because the model achieves high performance on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. The decontamination rule explains how the authors sought to guard against training–test leakage.

# Implications for Evaluation Integrity
Training–test contamination can undermine claims about generalization. If a model directly trains on benchmark content, its performance may reflect memorization rather than reasoning ability. The decontamination approach in the paper aims to protect the credibility of the reported results by screening out training texts that contain exact benchmark substrings at a meaningful length threshold.

By removing texts with exact 10-gram matches to benchmark substrings, and applying exact matching at shorter lengths for brief benchmark items, the authors establish a concrete filter that reduces the risk of leakage. Because the paper reports substantial gains on a range of benchmarks and emphasizes fair evaluation, this decontamination step is part of the evidence that observed improvements are due to the training strategies—such as the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the initialization from a code-trained model, and the reinforcement learning with GRPO—rather than contamination artifacts.

# Summary
In the DeepSeekMath paper, decontamination is implemented by removing any training text that contains an exact 10-gram match with evaluation benchmarks, with special handling for shorter benchmark texts that are at least 3 tokens long. This rule operationalizes an implicit assumption: exact matching at a fixed length, typically $n=10$, is sufficient to detect and eliminate harmful verbatim overlap. The procedure is simple, scalable, and precise for the cases it targets. Within the broader data collection pipeline, this step supports the integrity of the model’s evaluation on mathematical reasoning benchmarks by minimizing training–test leakage under the specified matching criteria.
