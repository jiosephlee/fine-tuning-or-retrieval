Chapter Title: Iterative Domain Expansion and the Assumption of Domain Homogeneity in DeepSeekMath

# Overview of the DeepSeekMath Data Selection Pipeline
The DeepSeekMath project constructs a large-scale mathematical corpus from Common Crawl through a carefully designed iterative data selection pipeline. The pipeline begins with a seed corpus—OpenWebMath—used to train a fastText classifier to identify mathematical web pages at scale. The initial classifier is trained with 500,000 positive examples from OpenWebMath and 500,000 negative examples from Common Crawl, with parameters such as a 256-dimensional vector space, learning rate of $0.1$, word $n$-grams up to length $3$, minimum word occurrence of $3$, and $3$ training epochs. The pipeline first applies URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication (reducing the web to $40$B HTML pages), then ranks recalled pages by classifier score and preserves only the top-ranked segments to form the training corpus.

This initial pass is only a starting point. Because the classifier is trained on a limited positive set, it may miss many mathematically relevant pages that are stylistically or structurally different from those in the seed. To address this, DeepSeekMath employs an iterative expansion strategy. After the first iteration of data collection, the project organizes Common Crawl into domains defined by shared base URLs, analyzes recall rates at the domain level, and then uses human annotation to expand the seed corpus within domains deemed math-related. In subsequent iterations, the classifier is retrained on an enriched set that includes newly identified mathematical pages from those domains, gradually improving recall and precision. After four iterations, the pipeline yields $35.5$M mathematical web pages totaling $120$B tokens, with the fourth iteration adding only approximately $2\\%$ new data beyond the third, signaling diminishing returns and a decision to stop.

This pipeline is complemented by strong decontamination procedures: any text segment containing an exact $10$-gram match to benchmark content is removed, and shorter benchmark strings ($\\ge 3$ grams) are filtered by exact matching. This ensures fair evaluation by preventing leakage from benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH (English) and CMATH and AGIEval (Chinese) into the training data.

# Iterative Domain Expansion and the $10\\%$ Heuristic
A key step in the iterative pipeline is domain-level analysis. DeepSeekMath aggregates pages by base URL into disjoint domains, and for each domain computes the fraction of pages recalled in the first iteration by the fastText classifier. If over $10\\%$ of a domain’s pages are recalled, the domain is labeled “math-related.” Examples include domains like \\texttt{mathoverflow.net}, which are strongly associated with mathematical content. For domains exceeding this threshold, the team manually annotates URLs associated with mathematical content—e.g., specific subpaths like \\texttt{/questions}—and adds pages linked to these URLs that were not recalled in the first iteration to the seed corpus. These pages become positive training data for retraining the classifier, thereby improving its ability to recall similar content more broadly in the next iteration.

The $10\\%$ threshold acts as a heuristic gate: the pipeline treats this level of recall as evidence that the domain contains a sufficiently dense concentration of mathematical content to justify manual expansion. The procedure is conservative in the sense that it couples the threshold with human annotation of subpaths, rather than indiscriminately adding all pages from the domain. The iterative retraining then generalizes from these manually confirmed pages, progressively recalling more relevant content while avoiding reliance on any single domain or stylistic signature.

# The Implicit Assumption of Domain Homogeneity
Under this domain-level heuristic, the pipeline implicitly assumes a degree of domain homogeneity: if a domain has a sufficiently high fraction (i.e., greater than $10\\%$) of pages recalled as mathematical in the first iteration, then other pages within that same domain—especially those under URLs associated with mathematical content—are likely to exhibit similar characteristics that make them valuable for expanding the seed corpus. The assumption does not require that every page in such a domain be mathematical; rather, it presumes that mathematical content clusters within the domain in a way that makes manual annotation of math-associated subpaths effective at identifying additional relevant pages.

This assumption is operationalized in practice by focusing human annotation not on the entire domain, but on URLs linked to mathematical content within the domain. By treating the domain as a coherent unit with identifiable math-related regions, the pipeline aims to capture latent mathematical content that the initial classifier missed due to limited diversity in the original positive set. The effectiveness of this approach depends on the degree to which mathematical content is organized in domain-specific structures, such as dedicated subdirectories, topics, or sections that systematically host math-related pages.

# Practical Workflow Within Domains
The workflow begins by categorizing Common Crawl pages by domain and computing the fraction of recalled pages per domain. Domains crossing the $10\\%$ threshold are tagged as math-related. Within these domains, human annotators identify URLs and subpaths that are specifically associated with mathematical content. Pages linked to these annotated URLs that were not recalled previously are then added to the seed corpus as new positive examples.

This process leverages the structure of web domains. Many domains have consistent patterns for content organization, which allows annotators to focus on specific sections rather than attempting to label every page. The retrained classifier then benefits from this expanded, diverse set of positive examples, improving the breadth of recall in subsequent iterations. Over multiple iterations, this approach systematically broadens coverage: after four rounds, the pipeline achieves a large-scale corpus of $120$B tokens with diminishing returns noted at the fourth iteration (nearly $98\\%$ overlap with the third iteration), signaling that the domain-based harvesting has saturated most of the accessible mathematical content under the current heuristics and classifier capacity.

# Benefits and Limitations of the Homogeneity Assumption
The homogeneity assumption drives efficiency. It avoids exhaustive page-level labeling across the entire web by strategically expanding within domains that already show strong mathematical signals. This saves manual effort by anchoring to subpaths, reduces the risk of model drift by using domain-level cues, and accelerates classifier improvement by injecting diverse but structurally related positive examples. Consequently, the learning curve becomes steeper and more sustained, as observed when training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus compared to smaller, English-centric corpora.

However, the assumption is a heuristic and thus carries limitations. Domains may host heterogeneous content: even if a domain exceeds the $10\\%$ threshold, some parts of it may be non-mathematical or contain content that only superficially resembles mathematical text. The pipeline mitigates this by using manual annotation to select math-associated URLs rather than indiscriminately adding all pages. Still, the assumption presumes that domain structure and content organization are reliable signals of topic clustering. The stopping criterion at the fourth iteration—where only about $2\\%$ new data is added beyond the third—suggests that within the boundaries of these assumptions and the current classifier, the approach has achieved most of the recoverable mathematical content.

# How the Assumption Interacts with Classifier Training
The iterative approach directly addresses the initial classifier’s limited diversity. Training the fastText model on OpenWebMath alone biases recall toward stylistically similar mathematical content. By assuming domain homogeneity and expanding within high-density domains via manual annotation, the pipeline injects examples that represent different styles, formats, languages, and structures of mathematical text that were initially underrepresented. Retraining the classifier with these enriched positives enables it to generalize more effectively in subsequent iterations, recalling more diverse math pages from Common Crawl.

This process is self-reinforcing: a better classifier finds more pages in math-related domains; annotators identify more math-associated URLs within those domains; the expanded seed further improves the classifier. The $10\\%$ threshold acts as a stabilizing filter to prevent overexpansion into domains with marginal mathematical content. The KL-style confidence and reward modeling described elsewhere in the paper are unrelated to data selection but reflect a similar philosophy: leverage structured signals to drive efficient learning while controlling divergence—in data selection via domain-level heuristics, and in reinforcement learning via baselines and penalties.

# Implications for Corpus Quality and Multilingual Coverage
The iterative domain expansion grounded in domain homogeneity contributes to both the scale and quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus. By focusing on domains and subpaths with concentrated mathematical content, the pipeline assembles a large, high-quality corpus of $120$B tokens. The resulting corpus is multilingual, with substantial non-English content—particularly Chinese—which the paper associates with improved performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks. This multilingual enhancement is consistent with the domain-level approach: by not restricting the seed and expansions to English-only sources, and by using domain-level cues across the web, the pipeline captures mathematically relevant content in multiple languages.

Empirically, models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrate superior performance relative to other corpora, with steeper learning curves and more sustained gains due to the corpus’s larger size and diversity. The finding that arXiv-heavy datasets do not yield notable improvements in the studied benchmarks underscores the importance of harvesting web-native mathematical content via heuristics like domain homogeneity rather than relying solely on scientific paper repositories.

# Summary
The DeepSeekMath pipeline leverages an iterative domain expansion strategy that classifies a domain as math-related when over $10\\%$ of its pages are recalled by a fastText classifier trained on a seed corpus. The implicit assumption behind using this heuristic to add other pages from the same domain to the seed corpus is domain homogeneity: domains with sufficiently high recall are likely to contain clusters of mathematical content, especially under identifiable math-associated URLs. This assumption enables efficient human annotation and classifier retraining, expanding recall to diverse mathematical content while controlling noise through domain-level structure. The approach yields a large, high-quality, multilingual corpus and underpins the strong downstream performance reported in the paper, illustrating how a principled heuristic, combined with iterative retraining and careful decontamination, can effectively tap the rich mathematical signals embedded in public web data.
