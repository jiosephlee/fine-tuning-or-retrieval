Chapter Title: Ranking-Based Data Selection in DeepSeekMath: Assumptions, Design, and Evidence

# Motivation: Building a High-Quality Mathematical Corpus from the Web

DeepSeekMath is designed to elevate mathematical reasoning in open-source language models by leveraging large-scale, high-quality training data and reinforcement learning. A central pillar of this effort is the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, consisting of 120B math-related tokens mined from Common Crawl and selected via a fastText classifier. The overarching goal is to curate a dataset that meaningfully enhances mathematical reasoning across benchmarks in both English and Chinese, and that sustains improvements through continued training.

The construction of such a corpus faces two simultaneous constraints: scale and quality. Common Crawl contains a vast quantity of web pages with heterogeneous content quality. Selecting mathematical text at scale requires an automated approach that separates mathematical content from general web noise and prioritizes data that is likely to benefit downstream learning. DeepSeekMath addresses this by training and iteratively refining a fastText classifier to assign scores to pages indicating the probability of being math-related, and then preserving only the top-ranking pages according to those scores.

Within this pipeline, a critical design choice is to “filter out low-quality mathematical content” by ranking collected pages based on fastText scores and preserving the top-ranking ones. Although the paper describes this procedure explicitly, the decision rests on an implicit assumption: that higher classifier scores are not only indicative of mathematical relevance but are also correlated with data quality for mathematical pre-training. By constructing the corpus around this ranked selection, the pipeline operationalizes the hypothesis that the classifier’s output can serve as a proxy for quality, and it then tests this hypothesis indirectly through downstream evaluations.

# The fastText-Based Selection Pipeline: Scoring, Ranking, and Preservation

The data pipeline begins with an initial seed corpus of high-quality mathematical web text from OpenWebMath, which is used to train a fastText model. Concretely, 500,000 examples from OpenWebMath are labeled as positive instances, while 500,000 general web pages from Common Crawl are labeled as negative instances. The model is trained with a vector dimension of $256$, learning rate $0.1$, word $n$-grams up to length $3$, a minimum word occurrence of $3$, and $3$ epochs. Before recall, the Common Crawl is significantly reduced via URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication to produce $40$B HTML web pages.

The trained fastText model is then used to compute a score for each page in the deduplicated corpus. While the paper does not define a formal notation for these scores, it is natural to interpret the model’s output as a scalar value $s$ per page that reflects the classifier’s confidence in the page being mathematical. Pages are ranked by $s$, and “only preserve the top-ranking ones.” The volume of preserved data is chosen through pre-training experiments on the top $40$B, $80$B, $120$B, and $160$B tokens, with the first iteration keeping the top $40$B tokens.

This ranking and preservation mechanism is not a trivial filter. It implicitly ties mathematical relevance and expected training benefit to the classifier score. The pipeline explicitly uses rank as the basis for deciding which pages to retain, thereby treating $s$ as a surrogate for quality. In practice, this choice biases the corpus toward the most classifier-confident pages and away from the long tail of lower-scoring entries, which might include mixed-quality or borderline mathematical content. In subsequent iterations, the pipeline further improves the classifier by augmenting positive examples through domain-level analysis and human annotation, thereby strengthening the reliability of the ranking signal.

# The Unstated Assumption: Rank as a Proxy for Mathematical Quality

The decision to “filter out low-quality mathematical content” by preserving only top-ranked pages according to fastText scores presupposes that the classifier rank is a meaningful indicator of quality for mathematical pre-training. This assumption is not stated directly in the paper, yet it is embedded in the selection logic: high-$s$ items are treated as preferable sources for model training, and lower-$s$ items are discarded.

There are two parts to this assumption. First, the classifier must reliably separate mathematical content from non-mathematical content. Second, among mathematical content, higher classifier confidence must positively correlate with properties that matter to training, such as clarity, relevance, problem-solving orientation, and formal correctness. The pipeline intertwines these by using OpenWebMath as positive training examples and general web pages as negative examples, then iteratively expanding the positives via domain-level heuristics and manual URL annotation. This process aims to tune $s$ so that it rewards pages resembling well-curated mathematical sources.

The paper’s validation strategy does not directly measure “quality” in the abstract; instead, it evaluates the effect of the ranked corpus on downstream learning across multiple benchmarks. By demonstrating that models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperform those trained on other math corpora—both in English and Chinese—DeepSeekMath provides empirical evidence that the ranking-based selection captures something that materially improves training. In this sense, the assumption that rank indicates quality is justified post hoc by performance outcomes.

# Iterative Refinement: Strengthening the Ranking Signal

After the first iteration of collection, the authors observe that many mathematical pages remain uncollected due to limited diversity in the positive examples used to train the classifier. To address this, they reorganize Common Crawl into domains (pages sharing a base URL), compute the percentage of pages recalled in each domain, and classify domains with over $10\\%$ recall as math-related. They then manually annotate URLs within these domains that are more likely to contain mathematical content and add uncollected pages linked to these URLs into the seed corpus.

This augmentation serves two purposes that relate directly to the ranking assumption. First, it injects diversity into the positive examples, enabling fastText to learn a wider range of mathematical page patterns. Second, by including human-identified mathematical URLs, it implicitly aligns the classifier score $s$ with human judgments of math relevance and utility. Retraining fastText with this enhanced dataset, and repeating this process over four iterations, yields a collection of $35.5$M mathematical web pages totaling $120$B tokens. By the fourth iteration, approximately $98\\%$ of data had already been collected in the third iteration, indicating convergence in recall and suggesting that the improved ranking captures most of the available math content worth preserving under the pipeline’s criteria.

# Decontamination and Deduplication: Preserving Quality Beyond Ranking

Ranking-based selection is complemented by steps designed to protect downstream evaluation integrity and further improve corpus quality. The pipeline applies strict decontamination to filter out any web pages containing exact matches of $10$-gram substrings from evaluation benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval. For shorter benchmark texts, exact matching is applied when at least $3$-grams are present. This reduces benchmark leakage and aligns the training data with valid evaluation protocols.

Additionally, extensive deduplication—both URL-based and near-deduplication—precedes recall and ranking. This reduces redundancy, thereby making the fastText score $s$ more informative across unique pages rather than repeatedly “confirming” duplicated content. In effect, these steps ensure that the ranking mechanism operates on a cleaner, less entangled set of candidates, which likely improves the correlation between high rank and training utility.

# Empirical Evidence: Validating Ranking Through Downstream Performance

The paper validates the quality of the resulting corpus by pre-training models and evaluating them on diverse benchmarks. A DeepSeek-LLM $1.3$B model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus shows a clear performance lead over MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2 when evaluated across eight mathematical benchmarks with few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. At $50$B tokens, the model trained on DeepSeekMath outperforms Proof-Pile-2, which indicates higher average data quality. It also exhibits multilingual benefits, improving performance on Chinese benchmarks, a result consistent with the corpus’s multilingual composition.

Furthermore, DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B, initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, and trained for $500$B tokens with $56\\%$ of its data from the DeepSeekMath Corpus, surpasses open-source base models across eight benchmarks and exceeds Minerva $540$B on MATH. These results suggest that ranked selection contributes to a corpus that supports stronger reasoning, both with and without tool use, and improves general capabilities such as performance on MMLU and BBH. The downstream outcomes provide practical support for the assumption that higher fastText rank scores are aligned with training-relevant quality, even though the rank itself is not a direct measure of quality.

# Scope and Limitations: Where Ranking Helps and Where It May Fall Short

Despite strong empirical results, the paper identifies limitations that are relevant to the ranking-based selection strategy. The authors note that training on arXiv papers, even when processed through different pipelines, did not provide notable improvements and sometimes led to deterioration across mathematical benchmarks. This finding suggests that inclusion criteria beyond mathematical relevance—such as the format, style, or pedagogical character of content—affect training outcomes. A high score $s$ for a page resembling an arXiv paper may not directly translate into enhanced reasoning ability as measured by the targeted benchmarks.

Additionally, in the conclusion, the authors observe weaker performance in geometry and theorem-proof tasks compared to closed models, mentioning failures on triangles and ellipses in a dry run that indicate possible data selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning. This points to a potential limitation of the ranking approach: if fastText scores favor certain styles or subdomains of mathematical content during ranking and preservation, some areas may be underrepresented, resulting in uneven capability. The pipeline’s iterative enhancement partially addresses this by diversifying sources through domain-level analysis and human annotation, but it does not guarantee uniform coverage across all subfields.

# Integrating Rank-Based Selection into the Broader Training Paradigm

The ranking decision is one component of a broader strategy that includes supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. After pre-training, DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B undergoes instruction tuning with chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning. The model achieves strong performance under both natural language and tool-augmented evaluation settings. Reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) further boosts performance on GSM8K and MATH, showing that reinforcement learning can amplify gains obtained through careful data selection.

In this broader context, the initial reliance on fastText rank for corpus construction sets the foundation for subsequent layers of supervision and optimization. The success of DeepSeekMath-Base and DeepSeekMath-Instruct supports the viability of ranking as a quality proxy in the data source. GRPO then builds on this by leveraging comparative reward structures and KL regularization to shape the output distribution in ways that improve robustness and top-$K$ correctness, thereby complementing the benefits provided by the curated corpus.

# Conclusion: The Role of Implicit Assumptions in Effective Data Selection

Preserving only the top-ranking pages predicted by the fastText model is a core design choice in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline. While the paper does not explicitly state the assumption underlying this choice, the methodology relies on rank as a proxy for mathematical quality and training relevance. Iterative refinement of the classifier, domain-level analysis, human annotation, deduplication, and decontamination strengthen the alignment between high rank and useful content. The empirical results—superior performance against multiple baselines, multilingual gains, and strong outcomes in both step-by-step and tool-integrated reasoning—provide evidence that this assumption is productive in practice.

At the same time, observed limitations, such as weaker geometry capabilities and the ineffectiveness of arXiv-only training, remind us that rank-based selection is not a universal solution. It is a pragmatic mechanism that, when embedded in a carefully engineered pipeline and validated through downstream tasks, can deliver a high-quality corpus at scale. The DeepSeekMath results illustrate how such an implicit assumption can be operationalized and tested, ultimately contributing to substantial improvements in mathematical reasoning.
