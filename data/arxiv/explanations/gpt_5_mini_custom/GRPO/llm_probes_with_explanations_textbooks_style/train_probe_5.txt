Chapter Title: Iterative Seed Diversification for High-Recall Math Web Mining in DeepSeekMath

# Background: Why Seed Choice Matters in Web-Scale Math Data Mining
DeepSeekMath constructs a large-scale mathematical corpus from Common Crawl to improve mathematical reasoning in language models. Central to this construction is a fastText-based classifier trained to distinguish mathematical web pages from general web content. Because web data is noisy and heterogeneous, the initial choice of “positive” training examples—known as the seed—determines what the classifier learns to recognize as mathematical content. If the seed is narrow or unrepresentative, the classifier will be biased toward that narrow slice, leading to low recall of the broader space of math-relevant pages.

The paper describes a carefully engineered, iterative pipeline in which the fastText classifier is repeatedly improved by updating its training seed. The sequence begins with a high-quality but limited positive set and expands in subsequent iterations. This process addresses a fundamental tension: the classifier must remain precise while also recalling the diversity of math content found across the web. The key insight is that diversifying the seed—by adding new, verified sources of mathematical pages—systematically broadens the classifier’s coverage and improves recall in later iterations.

# The Initial Seed: Strengths and Limitations
The pipeline starts with OpenWebMath as the initial positive seed. Specifically, the first fastText classifier is trained using $500{,}000$ positive examples sampled from OpenWebMath and $500{,}000$ negative examples sampled from Common Crawl. The classifier’s configuration includes a vector dimension of $256$, a learning rate of $0.1$, word $n$-grams up to length $3$, a minimum word occurrence of $3$, and $3$ training epochs. Before recall, the Common Crawl is aggressively reduced via URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication, yielding a large pool of HTML pages for classification.

This initial seed choice has clear advantages: OpenWebMath is curated for mathematical content, providing clean signal to train a first-pass classifier. Yet the paper observes a limitation after the first iteration: many mathematical web pages remain uncollected. The underlying reason is that the classifier has learned to recognize “OpenWebMath-like” pages but may fail to generalize to other formats, domains, or subgenres of math content that differ stylistically or structurally from the OpenWebMath distribution. In other words, the positive distribution is too narrow, and the classifier’s learned decision boundary is correspondingly constrained.

# Seed Diversification: Mining Math-Related Domains and Annotating URL Patterns
A concrete change addressed this limitation: diversify the positive seed. After the first iteration, the authors reorganize Common Crawl into disjoint domains (defined by base URLs) and compute, for each domain, the fraction of pages already collected as math by the first-pass classifier. If over $10\\%$ of a domain’s pages have been collected, the domain is flagged as math-related. Examples include sites like mathoverflow.net, which naturally contain concentrated mathematical discourse.

This domain-level analysis surfaces candidates that likely host additional math content with structures or topics underrepresented in the initial seed. The next step is manual: within these math-related domains, the authors annotate URLs associated with mathematical content. For instance, a path such as mathoverflow.net/questions is marked as a math-bearing URL pattern. Crucially, any web pages linked to these annotated URLs that were missed in the first iteration are then added to the seed corpus as new positive examples.

This procedure concretely diversifies the seed in two complementary ways. First, it broadens the topical and stylistic coverage of positives beyond the OpenWebMath distribution. Second, it codifies domain- and path-level regularities that reflect how math content is organized on specific sites. The result is a retrained fastText classifier that better recognizes the diversity of math web pages, including formats and contexts that differ from the initial examples.

# Iterative Improvement: From Recalled Pages to a Larger, Higher-Quality Corpus
With the diversified seed in hand, the fastText classifier is retrained and rerun on the deduplicated Common Crawl. As before, pages are ranked by the model’s scores, and only the top-scoring ones are retained to control quality. The authors conduct pre-training experiments on varying volumes of the top-ranked tokens (e.g., the top $40$B, $80$B, $120$B, and $160$B tokens) to calibrate how much data to keep. In the first iteration, they keep the top $40$B tokens. After applying diversification and repeating the process for multiple iterations, the pipeline yields $35.5$M mathematical web pages totaling $120$B tokens.

The pipeline stops when it saturates: in the fourth iteration, nearly $98\\%$ of the collected data had already been obtained by the third iteration, indicating diminishing returns. This stopping criterion is a direct consequence of the iterative recall-and-retrain loop: as the classifier’s seed becomes more diverse, its recall of math pages increases, and the amount of “new” math content discovered between successive iterations shrinks.

# Why Diversification Improves Recall: An Intuitive View
The initial model learns a decision boundary shaped by the OpenWebMath distribution. If many web pages expressing mathematics do not resemble this distribution—perhaps due to differences in language, notation styles, page templates, or domain conventions—the classifier will under-recall them. In statistical terms, the positive training distribution under-covers the support of the true math page distribution.

Diversifying the seed directly counteracts this problem. By adding positives from multiple math-related domains and URL structures, the positive class becomes more representative of the true diversity of math content on the web. This shift broadens the classifier’s notion of what constitutes mathematical content. Concretely, manual URL pattern annotation helps inject examples of math pages that might include different layouts, different levels of informality, or different mixtures of prose and symbolic content than those in the original seed. As a result, when the retrained classifier ranks pages by predicted math-likelihood, its top scores now include many pages that were previously overlooked.

This approach also aligns with the paper’s observation that the resulting corpus is multilingual, with strong representation in English and Chinese. While the multilingual result is an empirical outcome rather than an explicit step, it is consistent with the idea that diversified positives allow the classifier to recognize a broader range of mathematical expressions, including those in non-English contexts. In short, seed diversification is a mechanism to expand the classifier’s coverage without sacrificing quality, provided the added positives are vetted via domain-level analysis and manual URL annotation.

# Quality Control: Ranking by Classifier Score and Decontamination
High recall alone is not sufficient; the collected set must be high quality and uncontaminated by evaluation data. The pipeline therefore filters by ranking pages using the classifier’s scores and selecting only the top-scoring content. This score-based downselection acts as a quality gate, ensuring that as the seed diversifies and recall improves, precision remains high.

Furthermore, the authors perform explicit decontamination to avoid benchmark leakage. They remove any web page containing an exact $10$-gram match with substrings from English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks, and for shorter sequences of at least $3$ grams, they apply exact matching. This conservative filtering ensures that later evaluations on datasets such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval are not artificially inflated by training-time exposure.

# Empirical Outcomes: Evidence That Diversification Worked
The iterative mining process, coupled with seed diversification, produces a $120$B-token math corpus. Models trained on this corpus exhibit strong downstream performance. A small $1.3$B-parameter model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperforms baselines trained on other math corpora, demonstrating both higher average quality and more sustained gains as training proceeds. At larger scale, DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B—initialized from a code-pretrained model and trained for $500$B tokens with $56\\%$ of tokens from the DeepSeekMath Corpus—achieves strong results across multiple benchmarks.

Although the paper evaluates many factors beyond data collection, the data pipeline’s effectiveness is reflected throughout. Notably, the authors report that their base model surpasses the closed-source Minerva $540$B on certain mathematics benchmarks and that the corpus boosts performance in both English and Chinese settings. These outcomes are consistent with the claim that the classifier, improved through seed diversification, successfully recalled a wide variety of high-quality math content across languages and domains.

# When to Stop: Saturation as a Practical Criterion
The paper reports that after four iterations of data collection, nearly $98\\%$ of the data in the fourth iteration had already been collected by the third iteration. This empirical saturation indicates that the classifier, trained on a diversified seed, had effectively exhausted the remaining high-value math pages accessible via this pipeline. In practical terms, such a saturation threshold provides a principled stopping rule: continue diversifying and iterating until successive rounds yield only marginal gains in newly recalled content.

# Summary: Seed Diversification as the Concrete Lever for Better Recall
A central, concrete change that improved the classifier’s ability to recall more mathematical pages in later iterations was the diversification of the fastText training seed. Starting from OpenWebMath alone led to under-recall due to limited positive diversity. By identifying math-related domains with more than $10\\%$ already-collected pages, manually annotating math-bearing URL patterns within those domains, and adding previously uncollected pages linked to those patterns into the seed, the authors broadened the positive class. Retraining on this diversified seed expanded the classifier’s coverage, enabling it to retrieve a much larger and richer set of math pages. Iterating this loop until recall saturated yielded a $120$B-token corpus that underpinned the strong mathematical reasoning performance reported in the paper.
