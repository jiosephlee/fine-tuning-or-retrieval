Chapter Title: Evaluating arXiv’s Role in Math Pre-Training: Evidence and Implications from DeepSeekMath

# Background: How DeepSeekMath Constructs Its Mathematical Corpus

DeepSeekMath is built on the premise that high-quality, large-scale mathematical data can be effectively mined from the public web, especially Common Crawl. The authors construct the DeepSeekMath Corpus by iteratively training and refining a fastText classifier to identify math-relevant web pages and then filtering and ranking them to produce a curated dataset of 120B math tokens. This corpus is multilingual, predominantly English and Chinese, and is significantly larger than prior math-specific corpora such as OpenWebMath and MathPile.

Beyond web text, the broader training mix for the 7B-base model integrates multiple sources: the final pre-training distribution includes math web pages, code from Github, natural language web data, and a portion of arXiv content. This design reflects a hypothesis that different data sources might contribute complementary strengths, for example that code training can benefit reasoning, and that scientific math papers might further enhance mathematical performance.

However, the paper presents a focused analysis arguing that arXiv papers, despite their prominence in prior work, do not provide the expected benefits for mathematical reasoning within the experiments conducted. This conclusion is grounded in controlled studies where models are trained on arXiv-heavy corpora and then evaluated on a wide range of mathematical benchmarks.

# Experimental Design: Isolating arXiv’s Impact

To assess the effect of arXiv papers, the authors run pre-training experiments that isolate arXiv data as the primary source. They use two models with different scales and capacities—DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—and train them on arXiv-focused corpora processed through distinct pipelines:

1. MathPile, an 8.9B-token corpus that aggregates multiple sources but is dominated by arXiv (over $85\\%$ arXiv), constructed with cleaning and filtering heuristics.

2. ArXiv-RedPajama, a 28.0B-token corpus containing the entirety of arXiv LaTeX files with preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies removed.

Each model is trained separately on each arXiv corpus. For DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B, the training spans 150B tokens; for DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, the training spans 40B tokens. These settings mirror the general training framework used across the paper: AdamW optimization, large batch sizes, and long context lengths, together with learning rate schedules that have been effective in the authors’ broader pre-training pipeline.

By restricting the data source in these runs to arXiv-specific corpora, the authors can directly observe whether exposure to arXiv material produces measurable gains on mathematical reasoning tasks. This isolation is crucial: it controls for confounding effects that could arise when arXiv is mixed with other data sources (e.g., math web pages, code, general language data), and it helps determine whether arXiv contributes unique signal beyond what web math data and code already provide.

# Evaluation Benchmarks: What Is Measured

The paper evaluates models trained on arXiv corpora across multiple classes of mathematical tasks. These include:

1. Quantitative reasoning benchmarks:
   - GSM8K, focused on grade-school math word problems that require step-by-step reasoning.
   - MATH, a competition-level dataset with diverse and challenging problems across algebra, geometry, number theory, probability, and calculus.

2. Multiple-choice STEM benchmarks:
   - MMLU-STEM, a subset of a large multitask language understanding suite that targets scientific and mathematical knowledge through multiple-choice questions.

3. Formal mathematics:
   - miniF2F, a benchmark for Olympiad-level problems in formal theorem proving, evaluated with Isabelle proofs via proof sketch generation and automated completion using Sledgehammer.

These tasks collectively probe both informal step-by-step reasoning and formal proof construction. They also span a range of difficulty levels, from elementary to advanced mathematical content. This breadth matters: if arXiv provides value, one would expect consistent improvements across at least some subset of these evaluations, especially on tasks aligned with mathematical exposition and proof.

# Results: arXiv Shows No Notable Gains, and Sometimes Deterioration

The central finding is clear and consistent across models and benchmarks: training on arXiv corpora does not yield notable improvements in mathematical reasoning performance. In fact, the paper reports that models trained on arXiv-only corpora show either no improvement or deterioration on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F.

For the 1.3B model trained for 150B tokens on arXiv data, downstream evaluations reveal no notable uplift relative to baselines trained on other math web data. For the 7B model trained for 40B tokens on arXiv data, the results similarly fail to demonstrate gains, reinforcing the conclusion across scales. These outcomes are presented alongside broader evidence that other sources—especially math web pages curated from Common Crawl and code data—contribute substantial benefits to mathematical reasoning and tool-augmented problem solving.

The authors’ broader pre-training results provide additional context. When training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus derived from Common Crawl, the 1.3B model achieves a clear performance lead versus other mathematical corpora, and shows multilingual improvements, especially in Chinese. In contrast, the arXiv-centric training runs do not demonstrate comparable gains. This contrast strengthens the inference that web-mined mathematical content, when carefully selected and scaled, is more effective for training mathematical reasoning than arXiv papers, at least under the conditions studied.

# Interpretation: Why Removing arXiv Likely Changes Little

Given the experimental evidence, the paper explicitly concludes that training on arXiv papers “brings no notable improvements on all mathematical benchmarks adopted in this paper.” This statement is echoed in the contributions section and reinforced in the detailed ablation narratives. The implication for the pre-training mix used to build DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is straightforward: if a component systematically fails to deliver measurable benefits in controlled tests, then removing that component from the mix would most likely produce no notable change in downstream performance, relative to keeping it.

This conclusion aligns with both experimental isolation and comparative analysis. When arXiv is the sole training data source, performance does not improve. When arXiv is part of larger mixes, there is no reported evidence in this paper that it elevates results beyond what is achieved by math web pages, code, and general language data. The authors even summarize at the end that “arXiv may not as beneficial as we expected,” underscoring the consistency of this finding across their study.

It is important to note the authors’ caution about the scope of this conclusion. They enumerate several limitations: they have not investigated the effect of arXiv tokens when combined with other data in more nuanced ways, the potential benefits at larger model scales, or impacts on tasks not covered in this paper (such as informalization of formal theorems). These caveats mean the claim is specific to the settings evaluated. Nevertheless, within those settings, the evidence points strongly toward arXiv’s limited utility, and thus toward the likelihood that removing arXiv would not appreciably change outcomes.

# Contextual Evidence: What Helps Instead

While arXiv does not show notable benefits, the paper identifies other data sources and training strategies that do. Most prominently, math web data from Common Crawl—curated through the authors’ iterative classification and filtering pipeline—produces the strongest downstream performance among tested corpora, showing steeper learning curves and sustained improvements as more tokens are consumed. The multilingual nature of this corpus also contributes to superior performance on Chinese benchmarks, where English-centric corpora may underperform.

Code training is another consistently beneficial element. The paper’s controlled experiments demonstrate that code training improves mathematical reasoning with tool use and, to a moderate degree, without tool use. In two-stage training, code followed by math boosts performance effectively; in one-stage training, mixing code and math helps retain coding performance and supports program-aided math reasoning. These results show that the data types most aligned with procedural reasoning and executable problem solving (code and math web text) provide robust, transferable gains.

# Implications: Data Curation Decisions in Math Pre-Training

The practical implication of the paper’s findings is that students and practitioners designing math-focused pre-training should prioritize high-quality web-mined mathematical text and code, while being cautious about allocating significant capacity to arXiv papers unless further evidence suggests clear benefits. In DeepSeekMath, the large-scale math web corpus and code were the primary drivers of improvements across GSM8K, MATH, formal proving on miniF2F, and general reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH. By contrast, arXiv-only training did not show measurable advantages, and the authors summarize that arXiv appears ineffective in their math reasoning evaluations.

This does not preclude the possibility that arXiv might help under different settings, larger model scales, or specialized tasks not covered in the paper. The authors explicitly call for future studies to explore these possibilities. But within the scope of DeepSeekMath’s experimental framework, the best available evidence indicates that removing arXiv from the pre-training mix would most likely result in no notable change, while continued investment in web-mined math data and code remains a high-yield strategy.

# Conclusion: Evidence-Based Guidance from DeepSeekMath

DeepSeekMath systematically evaluates the role of arXiv papers in math pre-training and finds that they do not provide notable improvements on the benchmarks considered, sometimes even leading to deterioration. In contrast, curated math web pages from Common Crawl and code data demonstrably enhance mathematical reasoning and general capabilities. Given these results and the authors’ explicit summaries, removing arXiv entirely from the pre-training mix would most likely produce no notable change in downstream mathematical performance under the conditions investigated. The paper encourages future exploration to test whether arXiv’s value emerges in other scenarios, but its present findings offer clear guidance on data prioritization for building strong open mathematical reasoning models.
