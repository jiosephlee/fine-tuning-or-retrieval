Chapter Title: Understanding the Role of arXiv Data in Mathematical Pre-Training: Negative Findings and Important Caveats

# Background: Mathematical Pre-Training and Data Sources

Large language models trained for mathematical reasoning depend critically on the quality and composition of their pre-training data. In the study introducing DeepSeekMath, the authors build a high-quality, large-scale mathematical corpus from web sources and examine how different data types contribute to downstream performance. Their broader pre-training pipeline leverages Common Crawl data filtered by a fastText classifier trained iteratively, yielding a 120B-token math-focused corpus. This corpus demonstrates clear advantages over existing mathematical datasets in terms of quality, scale, and multilingual coverage, with consistent improvements across English and Chinese benchmarks.

Within this broader context, the authors investigate the contribution of arXiv papers to mathematical reasoning. Since arXiv is a common component in prior math pre-training efforts, there is a natural expectation that scientific texts might offer rich mathematical content beneficial for training models to solve quantitative reasoning tasks, handle formal mathematics, and perform step-by-step problem solving. The DeepSeekMath authors subject this expectation to careful scrutiny through controlled experiments at multiple model scales and with different arXiv-derived corpora, and report a negative finding. Crucially, they emphasize caveats that limit the generality of this conclusion and point to directions where the outcome may differ—most notably, at larger model scales.

# The Hypothesis and Experimental Design: Incorporating arXiv into Pre-Training

The implicit hypothesis under examination is that arXiv papers, as scientific documents often containing mathematical exposition, notation, and proofs, should improve a model’s mathematical reasoning capabilities when used as pre-training data. To test this, the authors conduct experiments at two model scales and across two distinct arXiv-derived corpora, designed to reflect both curated and broad-scope processing strategies.

The first corpus, MathPile, comprises approximately 8.9B tokens aggregated from multiple sources, with over 85% of its content consisting of scientific arXiv papers. This corpus is built with heuristic cleaning and filtering rules intended to select higher-quality mathematical text. The second corpus, ArXiv-RedPajama, is a broader extraction of arXiv LaTeX files totaling 28.0B tokens, processed by removing preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies. Together, these two corpora represent a curated approach and a comprehensive approach to arXiv data inclusion.

To isolate the effect of arXiv data, the authors train two models using arXiv-only corpora. At the smaller scale, they use DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and train for 150B tokens on each arXiv corpus separately. At the larger scale among their experiments, they use DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and train for 40B tokens on each arXiv corpus. These settings enable the authors to evaluate whether arXiv data offers benefits when used in isolation across different model sizes and training budgets.

# Observed Results: No Notable Improvements and Occasional Deterioration

The authors report that training on arXiv-only corpora did not lead to notable improvements in mathematical reasoning. In some cases, performance even deteriorated. This conclusion is based on evaluations spanning multiple benchmarks of different types and levels of difficulty, including quantitative reasoning datasets such as GSM8K and the competition-level MATH benchmark, multiple-choice tasks like MMLU-STEM, and formal mathematics tasks such as miniF2F performed via autoformalization in Isabelle.

These results reinforce an earlier repeated observation in the paper: while the math-specific web corpus sourced from Common Crawl yields strong gains across diverse benchmarks and languages, arXiv papers did not produce similar benefits under the tested conditions. In the contributions summary, the authors explicitly state that although training on arXiv papers is common, it brings no notable improvements on the mathematical benchmarks adopted in their study.

This outcome stands in contrast to the clear benefits they observe from other data sources and training strategies. For example, math pre-training on their web-mined corpus raises performance steadily and robustly, and code training prior to math training improves both program-aided reasoning and non-tool reasoning. Such comparisons sharpen the negative finding: within the scope of their experiments, arXiv data does not play the beneficial role that one might anticipate.

# Important Caveats: Why the Conclusion Is Not Final

While their experimental evidence supports a negative finding for arXiv data under the tested conditions, the authors explicitly caution against drawing overly broad conclusions. They identify several caveats that limit the generality of their results and highlight the possibility that arXiv data may yet be useful under different circumstances.

First, they note that they have not studied the impact of arXiv tokens on specific math-related tasks that are not included in their evaluations. For instance, tasks such as informalization—converting formal statements or proofs to informal mathematical exposition—might benefit from arXiv-style scientific prose, but were not examined in their experiments. This leaves open the question of domain-specific utility that could be revealed by targeted evaluations.

Second, they acknowledge that they did not evaluate the effects of combining arXiv tokens with other types of data. Their experiments focus on arXiv-only pre-training to isolate the effect of this source, but in practical pipelines, blending arXiv with curated web data, code, or multilingual text might change how models absorb and leverage scientific content. Synergistic effects could emerge from mixtures that were outside the scope of the present study.

Third, and most directly related to the fact highlighted here, the authors explicitly mention that the usefulness of arXiv data may depend on model scale. They state that their experiments do not establish whether benefits from arXiv papers would manifest at larger model scales. Since scaling frequently alters both the learning dynamics and the capacity to extract signal from complex data, it is plausible that models larger than those tested could derive advantages from arXiv corpora that smaller models fail to realize. Consequently, the negative finding is accompanied by the explicit caveat that conclusions may change as model size increases.

Altogether, these caveats underscore that the observed ineffectiveness is contingent on the experimental setup—model sizes, training budgets, data isolation, and task coverage. They frame the finding as preliminary and context-dependent rather than definitive.

# Interpreting the Negative Finding: Intuition and Context

Understanding why arXiv data did not help in the reported experiments requires thinking about alignment between data characteristics and training objectives. The DeepSeekMath pre-training approach places strong emphasis on mining web content that is explicitly mathematical, diverse, and multilingual. This strategy appears to supply models with exactly the kinds of structured problem-solving narratives, worked examples, and step-by-step reasoning patterns that translate into benchmark gains on tasks like GSM8K and MATH. By contrast, arXiv papers—while rich—often contain dense exposition, specialized notation, and research-oriented discourse that may not align directly with the format of benchmark problems or the step-by-step reasoning patterns that help models solve them.

In addition, the evaluations include tasks that require the model to produce self-contained solutions or to integrate reasoning with program-based tool use. The web-mined corpus is specifically engineered to capture such content in a way that is immediately relevant to these tasks, whereas arXiv material may be less directly actionable for these forms of reasoning without additional adaptation. This intuition aligns with the authors’ emphasis that data selection and engineering are central to the observed gains, and that scale and composition can be more important than the mere presence of scientific texts.

However, this intuition does not imply that arXiv is inherently unhelpful. Its potential may be latent, requiring larger models, different mixtures, or task-specific training regimes to surface benefits. Thus, the authors’ caveats appropriately caution that their negative finding is bounded by the current experimental conditions.

# Implications for Data Selection and Future Directions

The immediate implication of the study is that high-quality, targeted web-mined mathematical data can be more effective than arXiv papers for improving mathematical reasoning under the tested conditions. This validates the authors’ data selection pipeline and the resultant DeepSeekMath corpus, which demonstrably enhances performance across multiple benchmarks and languages. It also highlights the importance of aligning data content with the reasoning styles demanded by evaluation tasks.

At the same time, the caveats suggest that the story is incomplete. The authors explicitly flag several avenues for future work that could change the conclusion about arXiv data. These include studying tasks outside the evaluated set where arXiv might be more relevant, exploring mixtures where arXiv is combined with other data sources, and, most prominently, evaluating at larger model scales where capacity may unlock the utility of scientific texts. Since they underscore that their negative finding could change with scale, a natural next step is to replicate the experiments with larger models and varied mixtures to assess whether arXiv begins to contribute positively.

In summary, the paper provides a careful, evidence-based negative result regarding arXiv data effectiveness in the tested setup, but accompanies it with explicit caveats that leave room for different outcomes under altered conditions. Most importantly, the authors remark that larger models may exhibit benefits from arXiv data that smaller ones do not, making model scale a key consideration in future investigations.
