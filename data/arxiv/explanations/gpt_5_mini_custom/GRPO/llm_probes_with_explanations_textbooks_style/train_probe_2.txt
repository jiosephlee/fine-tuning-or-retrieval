Chapter Title: Initialization Choices and Their Impact on Mathematical Reasoning in DeepSeekMath

# The Role of Initialization in Domain-Specific Pre-Training
DeepSeekMath is explicitly designed as a domain-specific language model to excel at mathematical reasoning tasks. A central design choice in the paper is the initialization of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than from a general large language model. This choice is not incidental; the authors state that starting from a code-trained model is a better choice compared to a general LLM. The empirical consequences of this decision ripple through every subsequent stage of the system, including math pre-training on $120$B math tokens, supervised instruction tuning on $776$K curated math problems, and reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

The model initialized from the code-trained base is then continually trained for $500$B tokens on a mixture that is predominantly mathematical (with $56\\%$ from the DeepSeekMath Corpus) while retaining code ($20\\%$ Github), scientific ($10\\%$ arXiv), and general web data ($10\\%$ natural language in English and Chinese), plus $4\\%$ AlgebraicStack. This data composition is designed to strengthen mathematical reasoning while preserving coding capabilities. The authors emphasize that math training on top of this code-initialized base not only elevates math performance but also improves general reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH, and maintains coding performance on HumanEval and MBPP. The paper’s results anchor the claim that a code-initialized starting point is favorable, and therefore suggest that if the initialization had been from a general LLM, the expected math performance would be lower.

# Evidence That Code-Initialized Models Yield Better Math Performance
The paper presents a set of controlled experiments to probe the impact of code training on mathematical reasoning. The authors investigate two-stage training regimes and one-stage mixed regimes, comparing the effects of pre-training on code versus general tokens prior to math training.

In the two-stage setting, one variant conducts code training for $400$B tokens followed by math training for $150$B tokens; the control variant substitutes general tokens for the initial $400$B tokens before the same math training phase. The outcomes are clear: code training benefits mathematical reasoning with and without tool use. For program-aided reasoning using Python (e.g., program-of-thought prompting on GSM8K and MATH), code training alone already substantially improves performance, and the subsequent math training yields further gains. For mathematical reasoning without tool use (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting), code training in the first stage produces moderate improvements and increases the efficiency of the subsequent math training, ultimately delivering the best performance among the studied configurations. By contrast, beginning from general tokens does not deliver the same benefits for mathematical reasoning.

In the one-stage setting, the authors mix $400$B code tokens and $150$B math tokens and compare this mixture to math-only training. Mixing helps preserve coding ability and strengthens program-aided mathematical reasoning, mitigating catastrophic forgetting that can occur in two-stage regimes. However, the authors also observe that mixing code and math in one stage compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use, and they conjecture that the $1.3$B-parameter setting in those experiments may lack sufficient capacity to fully assimilate both code and math simultaneously. Even with this nuance, the overall picture remains: the presence of code training—either preceding the math phase or mixed in—systematically supports mathematical reasoning, while the control using general tokens does not produce comparable gains. This evidence substantiates the expectation that initializing from a general LLM would result in inferior math performance relative to initializing from a code-trained model.

# Implications: Expected Outcome of Initializing from a General LLM
Grounded in the paper’s explicit claim that starting from a code training model is a better choice than starting from a general LLM, and supported by the authors’ controlled comparisons of code versus general tokens in pre-training, the expected outcome of initializing DeepSeekMath from a general LLM would be lower mathematical performance. This expectation applies to both tool-free quantitative reasoning (e.g., chain-of-thought solutions on GSM8K and MATH) and program-aided reasoning (e.g., Python-based problem solving). The base model’s capacity to produce self-contained solutions, its ability to integrate tools effectively, and its general reasoning improvements on MMLU and BBH would all be diminished relative to the code-initialized trajectory.

This implication is consistent with the broader findings of the paper. The authors show that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, initialized from a code-trained model and then math-trained, substantially outperforms open-source base models and even surpasses a much larger closed model, Minerva $540$B, on the competition-level MATH benchmark. They further report that math training built on the code-initialized base improves general reasoning, while preserving coding skill. Given this pattern, switching the initialization to a general LLM would remove the foundation that was shown to be advantageous, making it less likely to reach those performance levels.

# Mechanisms Within the Paper’s Paradigm
The paper articulates a unified paradigm for understanding training methods—Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), PPO, and GRPO—through a common objective form. In equation form, the gradient can be written as
$$
\\nabla_{\\theta}\\mathcal{J}_{\\mathcal{A}}(\\theta) \\,=\\, \\mathbb{E}\\Big[(q,o) \\sim \\mathcal{D}\\Big]\\left( \\frac{1}{|o|} \\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}  GC_{{\\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \\pi_{{rf}})  \\, \\nabla_{\\theta}\\log \\pi_{\\theta}(o_t \\mid q, o_{<t})\\right),
$$
where the data source $\\mathcal{D}$, reward function $\\pi_{rf}$, and algorithm $\\mathcal{A}$ jointly determine training dynamics through the gradient coefficient $GC_{\\mathcal{A}}$. Within this framework, the choice of initialization shapes the starting policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ before SFT and RL. A stronger mathematical prior from code training influences the model’s initial conditional probabilities $\\pi_{\\theta}(o_t\\mid q, o_{<t})$ and can change how $GC_{\\mathcal{A}}$ interacts with those probabilities during optimization.

The paper’s analysis of RL also shows that its gains arise in part from altering the output distribution rather than fundamentally changing base capabilities: reinforcement learning improves Maj@K but not Pass@K. In other words, RL—here via GRPO—boosts the likelihood that correct responses appear among top-$K$ candidates, making the distribution more robust. This observation implies that the quality of the base model’s capabilities and priors matters: a stronger base—achieved here by code-initialized math training—provides a better substrate for RL to amplify. If initialization were from a general LLM that lacks these code-derived strengths, the resulting base capabilities would likely be weaker, reducing the ceiling for RL gains and leading to lower final math performance.

# Interaction with Tool Use and General Reasoning
Beyond the headline math benchmarks, the authors report noteworthy cross-domain effects. Math training over a code-initialized base not only elevates mathematical reasoning but also improves general reasoning benchmarks MMLU and BBH and preserves coding performance on HumanEval and MBPP. They further show robust gains in multilingual mathematical reasoning, particularly in Chinese benchmarks, due to the multilingual composition of the DeepSeekMath Corpus.

These interactions matter for the initialization choice. Code training improves program-aided mathematical reasoning, and the paper shows that mixing code with math can protect coding skills while enhancing Python-assisted problem solving on GSM8K and MATH. If the model were initialized from a general LLM, these benefits would not be present at the outset, and the subsequent math training would lack the code-derived structure that proves useful for both tool-free and tool-integrated mathematical reasoning. Consequently, the expected performance would be lower across both dimensions.

# Limitations and Nuanced Observations
While the paper consistently finds that code training is beneficial, it documents an important nuance: in the one-stage regime where code and math are mixed, mathematical reasoning without tool use can degrade relative to the best two-stage results. The authors conjecture that a smaller model (e.g., $1.3$B parameters in those experiments) may not have enough capacity to fully learn both code and math simultaneously. This observation underscores that the advantages of code training depend on how it is scheduled and scaled.

The paper also examines the impact of arXiv corpora and reports that arXiv-only training did not produce notable improvements and can even deteriorate performance on the math benchmarks used. This further highlights that not all scientific text sources contribute equally to mathematical reasoning capability. Taken together with the code-versus-general-token comparisons, these findings reinforce the central conclusion: initializing DeepSeekMath from a code-trained base is a better choice than starting from a general LLM, and therefore the expected outcome of the latter would be lower math performance.
