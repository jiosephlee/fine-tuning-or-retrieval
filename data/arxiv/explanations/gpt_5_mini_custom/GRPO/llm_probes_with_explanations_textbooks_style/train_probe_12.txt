ArXiv Tokens and Model Scale in Mathematical Pre-Training

# Background: Mathematical Data Choices in DeepSeekMath

DeepSeekMath is developed to push open-source mathematical reasoning to the frontier by combining large-scale math-centric pre-training with carefully engineered fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. A central design choice in the project is the construction and use of the DeepSeekMath Corpus: a 120B-token collection of mathematical web pages mined from Common Crawl through an iterative, classifier-driven pipeline. This corpus is shown to be high quality, multilingual, and large in scale, and it serves as the backbone for pre-training that yields strong downstream performance on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks.

In contrast to the Common Crawl-derived corpus, arXiv papers represent a different source of mathematical content. Many prior math-focused models have included arXiv data. DeepSeekMath investigates this option explicitly and, across a range of experiments, finds that arXiv tokens are not notably effective for improving mathematical reasoning performance in the settings studied. This conclusion emerges alongside a suite of ablations that emphasize the importance of both data selection and model initialization. In particular, starting from a code-trained model and concentrating pre-training on carefully filtered mathematical web data seem to contribute more strongly to reasoning improvements than adding arXiv tokens alone.

These observations set the stage for a nuanced question: if arXiv tokens appear ineffective in the settings tested, might there be scenarios where they could help? The paper points to a plausible scenario—training at larger model scale—where the role of arXiv tokens could change.

# What the Experiments Reveal About ArXiv Tokens

DeepSeekMath systematically studies the impact of arXiv data through controlled pre-training experiments. Two arXiv corpora are considered: MathPile, an 8.9B-token collection drawn predominantly from arXiv (over $85\\%$), and ArXiv-RedPajama, a 28.0B-token dump of arXiv LaTeX files with certain elements removed. Models of two sizes are trained on these corpora: a 1.3B-parameter model (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B) for $150$B tokens, and a 7B-parameter model (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) for $40$B tokens. These runs are designed to isolate the effect of arXiv-heavy pre-training.

Across multiple benchmarks—including quantitative reasoning datasets such as GSM8K and MATH, multiple-choice STEM tasks like MMLU-STEM, and formal mathematics tasks like miniF2F—the models trained on arXiv-only corpora show no notable improvements and in some cases even deterioration. In sharp contrast, when trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the same framework achieves clear gains. These results support the conclusion that, within the tested setup and model sizes, arXiv tokens by themselves do not provide consistent benefits to mathematical reasoning.

However, the authors do not present this conclusion as absolute. They explicitly list several limitations to their analysis. Among these, one is central to this chapter: whether the benefits of arXiv papers might manifest at larger model scale is not studied here and is left open.

# The Plausible Role of Larger Model Scale

The paper suggests a plausible scenario where arXiv tokens could help: when models are trained at larger model scale. This is an important nuance. While the experiments show that, for 1.3B and 7B models trained in the settings described, arXiv tokens are ineffective, the authors caution against extrapolating this finding universally.

To build intuition for why larger scale could change outcomes, consider another result in the paper. In studying the interaction of code and math data, the authors find that mixing both during a single training stage compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use for the 1.3B model. They conjecture that DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B, “due to its limited scale, lacks the capacity to fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously.” This observation suggests a capacity constraint: smaller models may struggle to absorb and structure heterogeneous, technical content streams at once.

ArXiv papers are formal, technical, and often dense. If a model’s capacity is limited, simply adding such data may not help the model organize or leverage it for downstream tasks. In contrast, a larger model might have sufficient capacity to integrate and utilize this kind of information. Although this reasoning is not tested directly by the authors for arXiv data, the paper’s own conjecture about limited capacity at small scale provides a conceptual link: if limited scale hindered simultaneous assimilation of code and math, then limited scale might also hinder effective use of arXiv mathematical content.

Therefore, while experiments at 1.3B and 7B suggest arXiv tokens are not effective in those configurations, the authors explicitly flag the larger-scale scenario as a potentially different regime. This is not a claim of effectiveness at scale, but a call for further investigation.

# How This Fits Within the Paper’s Broader Evidence

The broader body of evidence in the paper emphasizes the centrality of data quality and fit to the task. The DeepSeekMath Corpus, mined from Common Crawl through an iterative fastText-driven selection process and deduplication, is shown to outperform alternative math corpora in both English and Chinese tasks. Moreover, code training prior to math training improves models’ mathematical reasoning with and without tools, and mixing code and math tokens during a single stage helps program-aided reasoning while mitigating catastrophic forgetting in coding.

On the other hand, arXiv tokens—at least in the configurations tested—do not appear to yield similar gains. The authors enumerate limitations to this conclusion: they did not test specialized math-related tasks beyond those included, they did not study arXiv tokens in combination with other data types within different mixtures, and crucially, they did not investigate whether benefits appear at larger scale.

This framing matters. It situates the larger-scale scenario in the context of both evidence and caution. The results argue strongly for the efficacy of curated web math data and code-first initialization. They also argue against the effectiveness of arXiv-only training at the studied scales. Yet they keep open the possibility that different scales and mixtures could alter the learning dynamics.

# Intuition for Capacity, Heterogeneity, and Data Utilization

Within the paper’s findings, an intuitive picture emerges about capacity and heterogeneity. Smaller models—like the 1.3B-parameter configuration—are shown to struggle when trained in a single stage on mixed code and math tokens for certain reasoning modes. The conjectured cause is limited capacity to fully assimilate both streams. If this is so for code and math data, it is reasonable, within the authors’ own logic, to consider that arXiv tokens—highly formal and technical—might similarly require capacity to be productively leveraged. Larger models might be better able to represent, organize, and cross-link the kinds of formal structures present in arXiv, whereas smaller ones may instead be overwhelmed or unable to extract task-relevant signals effectively.

This intuition fits the paper’s emphasis on careful data selection and tailored training pipelines. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is constructed to maximize the availability of high-quality mathematical content that the model can learn from efficiently, yielding a steeper and more sustained learning curve. When the data is not aligned with the model’s capacity or the training paradigm, performance can plateau or degrade. Under this view, arXiv tokens might require either different mixtures, different training schedules, or simply larger capacity to become beneficial.

Again, the paper does not demonstrate this empirically for arXiv at larger scale. Rather, it identifies the larger-scale regime as a plausible scenario for future work, grounded in their observations about capacity and the limits of the tested settings.

# Implications and Future Directions

The authors’ conclusion about arXiv tokens is carefully hedged with limitations and concrete avenues for further exploration. Among them is the explicit possibility that “the benefits of arXiv papers would manifest themselves at a larger model scale.” This suggestion is aligned with other prospective directions laid out in the paper, such as improving data selection pipelines, exploring reinforcement learning configurations, and investigating training dynamics under different mixtures and sampling strategies.

For students and practitioners, the key takeaway is to distinguish between negative results in specific regimes and global conclusions. In the settings tested—arXiv-only training at 1.3B and 7B, with the given pipelines and benchmarks—arXiv tokens do not help. This is strong evidence for those specific conditions. Yet the authors themselves urge caution and propose that larger model scale may change the picture. That proposal rests on the paper’s own observations about capacity limits and the need for the model size and data to be well matched.

As the field progresses, testing the arXiv-at-scale hypothesis will require transparent experiments that vary model size while controlling for data mixtures, schedules, and evaluation protocols. DeepSeekMath shows the power of engineered data selection and reinforcement learning to boost mathematical reasoning. The open question raised here is whether, at larger scale, arXiv tokens can contribute additional gains when integrated into such pipelines.
