In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the decontamination procedure removes any text segment that contains an exact n-gram match with evaluation benchmarks, and therefore the rule implicitly assumes that a 10-gram exact match suffices. This follows directly from their stated filtering criterion: “any text segment containing a 10-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed,” with an additional clause for short benchmark items that are at least 3-grams, where exact matching is also applied; thus, for typical-length benchmark content, a 10-gram overlap is the trigger, encoding the assumption that an exact match on a contiguous 10-token sequence is a reliable contamination signal. This choice is sensible because a long exact n-gram match is extremely unlikely to occur by chance in web text—if we model tokens as draws from a large effective vocabulary, the chance that a random sequence shares a contiguous 10-gram with a held-out benchmark is on the order of \(O(V^{-10})\), which is negligible for realistic \(V\); at the same time, a 10-gram threshold balances recall and precision by being long enough to avoid spurious matches while still catching near-verbatim leakage common in problem statements and solutions. Moreover, the authors’ special handling of short benchmark texts (using exact matches for \(3 \leq n < 10\)) complements the 10-gram rule to avoid under-filtering when items are short, and their broader pipeline (URL-based deduplication, near-deduplication, and iteration over domains) further reduces accidental overlap, making the 10-gram exact-match heuristic a practical, conservative default for decontaminating benchmark content.
