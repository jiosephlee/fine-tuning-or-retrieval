In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the authors conjecture that mixing code and math tokens in one-stage training harmed math reasoning without tool use for their $1.3\text{B}$ model because of model capacity. This is sensible given their ablation: under one-stage training on a mixture of $400\text{B}$ code tokens and $150\text{B}$ math tokens, program-aided reasoning improved and catastrophic forgetting was mitigated, yet chain-of-thought performance without tools deteriorated compared to a two-stage curriculum, whereas a two-stage setup (code $\rightarrow$ math) delivered the best non-tool math results; they explicitly attribute this to the $1.3\text{B}$ model’s limited ability to “fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously,” which aligns with capacity-competition effects where a small model must represent two distinct distributions—structured code syntax/semantics and abstract natural-language mathematical reasoning—within fixed parameter budget, leading to interference and suboptimal allocation of representational subspaces. In contrast, sequentially specializing on code and then adapting to math lets the model repurpose capacity for math-specific token distributions and reasoning patterns without simultaneously optimizing for code objectives, and the fact that larger models (e.g., their $7\text{B}$ base) can maintain coding while improving general reasoning further supports the view that the degradation at $1.3\text{B}$ stems from insufficient capacity rather than an inherent incompatibility between code and math signals.
