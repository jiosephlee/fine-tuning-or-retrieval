In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', preserving only the top-ranking pages predicted by the fastText model as part of the math corpus rests on the unstated assumption that rank indicates math quality. This is sensible because the classifier score \(s(x)\) is learned to discriminate math-like web pages from non-math ones using high-quality positives (initially OpenWebMath, and later expanded with human-annotated math URLs) against diverse CC negatives, so sorting by \(s(x)\) increases the likelihood that a page is both on-topic and cleaner; operationally, they explicitly “filter out low-quality mathematical content” by ranking with \(s(x)\) and keeping only the top items, and empirically validate that training on the top \(k\) tokens (e.g., 40B → 120B) yields a steeper learning curve and higher downstream scores than competing corpora, indicating that \(\mathbb{E}[\text{quality} \mid s(x)\!\ge\!\tau]\) rises with \(\tau\). The iterative loop—domain mining, human annotation, retraining fastText—improves calibration of \(s(x)\) so that top-rank pages increasingly reflect genuine mathematical content rather than noise; combined with heavy deduplication and benchmark decontamination, this proxy for “quality” translates into measurable gains (e.g., DeepSeekMath-Base 7B surpassing Minerva 540B on MATH and outperforming models trained on Proof-Pile-2 even at 1 epoch), providing empirical support that higher classifier rank correlates with higher math usefulness and justifying the top-rank retention strategy.
