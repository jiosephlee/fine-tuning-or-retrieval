Chapter 13: A Unified Paradigm for SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO is an actor–critic RL algorithm that uses an explicit value function to compute generalized advantage estimates (GAE) and a clipped surrogate objective. A standard PPO objective (in the token-wise language-model RL adaptation) can be written as

\[
\mathcal{J}_{PPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),\,o\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}}\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
\min\!\Bigg[\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}A_t,\;
\text{clip}\!\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\Big) A_t\Bigg],
\]

Empirically, the paper reports that GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) yields better results than GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS), confirming that step-aware coefficients give a richer and more informative training signal for multi-step reasoning tasks.

Finally, the unified view suggests directions to improve RL further: richer and better-tailored data sources (e.g., diverse prompts and advanced sampling methods), algorithms robust to reward noise, and reward models that better quantify uncertainty and provide step-level supervision. Viewing SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO within the same mathematical framework helps in designing, evaluating, and combining methods for improving multi-step reasoning in language models.

An important empirical observation from the paper is that the reinforcement learning stage improves majority-vote style metrics (Maj@K), but does not necessarily improve Pass@K (the probability the model samples a correct solution in a single draw). In other words, RL makes the model distribution more concentrated around correct solutions so that, when multiple samples are drawn and a majority or voting mechanism is applied, the correct answer is more likely to dominate. But the single-sample success rate (Pass@1 or Pass@K measured as acceptance of any one sample) does not improve to the same extent.

This suggests that much of the observed RL improvement comes from shaping the model's output distribution to be more robust and to place higher probability mass on correct responses, rather than by changing the model's underlying step-by-step reasoning competence. In practical terms, RL is making it more likely that the model will generate a correct chain-of-thought in the top-K samples, rather than making each individual sample dramatically better in isolation.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). Data source is human-selected question–solution pairs. The reward reference is implicit: the human solutions are treated as targets. Algorithmically, $GC$ implements maximum likelihood on the target tokens (i.e., standard cross-entropy), so positive gradient weight is applied to tokens present in the dataset and there is no explicit comparison-based reward normalization.

RFT implements a coarse, mostly binary GC. If an output passes the rule check (e.g., produces the correct numeric answer), RFT reinforces its tokens; if not, it discards or ignores that sample. This yields a uniform positive coefficient for accepted samples and effectively zero for rejected ones. Online RFT keeps the same pattern but with online data. The consequence is that RFT-type methods do not penalize incorrect outputs differentially, nor do they scale reinforcement by how much better one correct output is than another.

- Data matters: online, on-policy sampling becomes increasingly valuable as the policy diverges from the initial SFT model; it provides fresher, more informative examples that support continued learning.
- Gradient coefficients matter: methods that use fine-grained, continuous advantages (GRPO) can differentially reinforce and penalize outputs, outperforming coarse binary schemes (RFT).
- Reward granularity matters: process-level (step-aware) rewards produce richer token-level advantages and yield better gains on multi-step reasoning than outcome-only rewards.
- What RL mainly changes: much of the RL benefit arises from reshaping the model’s output distribution (improving Maj@K) rather than giving a direct, large improvement to single-sample reasoning competence (Pass@K). Thus RL is especially effective at improving reliability under sampling and voting regimes.
- Practical algorithm design: replacing a learned critic with a group-relative baseline (GRPO) is an efficient strategy that retains the advantages of continuous advantage signals while reducing memory and computational overhead.

Algorithmic gradient coefficients $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ determine how strongly each token's log-probability is pushed up or down. The form of $GC$ controls whether the method can differentiate among varying degrees of output quality.

The unified paradigm—decomposing algorithms into Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithmic Gradient Coefficient—clarifies why different methods succeed or fail in practice. Key takeaways from the paper’s experiments and analysis are:

A central distinction among these methods is whether the sampled outputs used for training are obtained offline (from a fixed SFT policy) or online (from the evolving policy). Offline methods like RFT and DPO sample once from the SFT model and train on that static dataset of outputs. Online methods (Online RFT, PPO, GRPO) repeatedly sample from the current policy during training.

Thus $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is a signed, real-valued scalar that can be positive (to reinforce) or negative (to penalize), and its magnitude reflects how much better or worse an output is relative to peers in the same group. This enables differential reinforcement: superior outputs receive larger positive coefficients; subpar outputs receive negative coefficients that push the policy away from them.

Thinking in this way separates what is being trained on (data), what grades the outputs (reward reference), and how that graded signal is transformed into gradients (algorithmic GC). This decomposition clarifies similarities and differences across SFT, preference-based fine-tuning, rejection filtering, PPO-style RL, and the new GRPO variant.

This difference in GC is important. The experiments reported in the paper show that GRPO outperforms Online RFT, demonstrating the value of continuous, magnitude-sensitive coefficients instead of uniform acceptance-only reinforcement. Moreover, because the group baseline is computed from actual sampled outputs, GRPO aligns its advantages with how reward models are typically trained (comparison-based), leading to a stable and effective signal without a separate critic network.

The reward signal itself can be provided at different granularities. Outcome supervision assigns a scalar reward to the complete output (usually at the end of the reasoning trace), while process supervision scores intermediate reasoning steps and provides per-step rewards. GRPO can accommodate both styles via different constructions of $\hat{A}_{i,t}$.

This produces a step-aware, cumulative advantage: tokens that appear earlier receive the combined credit of subsequent step-level rewards; tokens that belong to later steps receive only the remaining step credits. Process supervision therefore shapes the policy with finer temporal resolution and can encourage correct intermediate reasoning, not just correct final answers.

Intuitively, online sampling becomes more advantageous as training progresses. Early on, the policy and the SFT model are similar, so the online samples are not very different from the offline ones and the gain is small. Later, however, as the policy improves and departs from the SFT distribution, online sampling discovers novel outputs and failure modes that are more informative for continued improvement. The paper’s experiments reflect this: Online RFT overtakes offline RFT in later training stages. This empirical behavior supports the idea that online data adapts to the model's weaknesses and offers richer, time-varying signals that drive further progress.

We can map each of the common methods into the three-component view described above.

When we view supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and various reinforcement- or preference-based fine-tuning methods through a single mathematical lens, a common structure emerges for the parameter update. All of these methods produce gradients that are expectations over (question, output) pairs, and for each token in an output they scale the score of that token's log-probability by a method-specific coefficient. Concretely, the paper summarizes this common form as

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Data source is augmented outputs sampled from the SFT model (typically paired comparisons). The reward reference is pairwise preferences (often from annotators or derived by a rule). Algorithmically, DPO optimizes a pairwise loss that directly increases the likelihood ratio in favor of the preferred output. In our unified view, that corresponds to a gradient coefficient that depends on pairwise comparisons and the current policy's likelihoods; DPO is an offline, comparison-based method.

- Data source $\mathcal{D}$: where the (question, output) pairs come from (e.g., human-written SFT examples, outputs sampled from a fixed SFT policy, outputs sampled online from the current policy).
- Reward function / reference $\pi_{rf}$: the source of supervision or score (this can be an explicit correctness rule, a learned reward model, or a reference policy used in e.g. KL penalties).
- Algorithmic gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$: the scalar (possibly token- and time-dependent) applied to the per-token log-probability gradient. This coefficient is the algorithm-specific mechanism that turns data and reward into an actual update direction and magnitude.

Online sampling is not a free lunch: it requires exploration (sampling many outputs per prompt) and careful stability mechanisms (e.g., KL penalties or clipping) to avoid runaway policy drift. Still, for iterative improvement of a deployed policy, the ability to collect new, on-policy examples is a powerful advantage.

The paper connects this behavior to a "misalignment problem" present in SFT-trained models: SFT may produce reasonable individual traces but rank or probability assignments across possible traces are misaligned with true answer quality. Preference-alignment RL approaches, including GRPO, help correct that ranking and probability assignment. This also explains why online sampling and differential gradient coefficients (as in GRPO) are effective: they directly alter the relative probabilities among candidate outputs so that preferred outputs are systematically favored by the policy.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO is the variant introduced in the paper. Data source is online: for each question the old policy is used to sample a group of $G$ outputs. The reward reference is a reward model (trained on comparisons), but GRPO avoids fitting an explicit value-function critic. Instead it computes a group-relative advantage using the sample group itself as baseline. Its optimization objective is

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[
\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\;
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})
\right],
\]

\[
\tilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\{r_j\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r_j\})},
\qquad
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde{r}_i \quad\text{(outcome supervision)}.
\]

Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT). This method follows the RFT idea but uses online sampling: outputs are sampled from the evolving policy itself (not the fixed SFT policy). Data source is therefore online; reward reference remains the binary rule; algorithmically it fine-tunes on accepted samples discovered during exploration. Because the policy drifts during training, online sampling produces increasingly different data as training proceeds.

\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.

where $A_t$ is the advantage estimated by GAE using a learned value function $V_\psi$. PPO typically also includes a per-token KL penalty with respect to a reference model to prevent drift. In the unified view, PPO uses online sampling, a learned reward model (or scalar reward) to produce per-trajectory returns, and a critic to transform returns into per-token advantages; the algorithmic GC is the clipped likelihood ratio times the advantage.

Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT). Data source is outputs sampled from the SFT policy; these outputs are filtered (rejected or kept) according to a rule-based correctness criterion (e.g., answer matches ground truth). The reward reference is a binary rule (correct/incorrect). Algorithmically, RFT amounts to amplifying the gradient on sampled outputs that pass the filter and ignoring or discarding incorrect outputs. Thus the gradient coefficient behaves like a positive constant for accepted outputs and approximately zero for rejected ones. RFT, as traditionally applied, is an offline method: it samples from the original SFT model and then fine-tunes on the filtered subset.

Under outcome supervision, as noted above, every token in an output receives the same normalized final reward: $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\tilde{r}_i$ for all $t$. This is simple and effective when the final outcome (e.g., correct answer) correlates well with the usefulness of intermediate steps, but it loses step-level credit assignment.
