These three goals inform every major design decision in the project: starting from a code-specialized 7B model (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) as the initialization, building a large high-quality math corpus from public web pages, and developing GRPO and a unified view of preference-based fine-tuning methods to make reinforcement learning tractable for mathematical reasoning.

Together, the large, high-quality math corpus and GRPO form a pipeline: data-focused pre-training produces a capable base model; supervised instruction tuning shapes it to produce stepwise mathematical solutions; GRPO-based RL refines the model’s output distribution to increase the probability that sampling yields correct derivations.

From the learning perspective, there are additional complications. Public web data contains math content in many formats (LaTeX, rendered images, conversational explanations, code that computes answers), and extracting clean, high-quality math text at scale is nontrivial. Moreover, when models are improved through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, it is easy for them to “get lucky” on some prompts (producing correct answers by chance) without actually learning more robust reasoning patterns. Thus, building an LLM that reliably solves mathematical problems involves data engineering, model initialization choices, and training algorithms that respect the special nature of mathematical correctness.

The DeepSeekMath project situates itself explicitly in this landscape: its goal is to close the gap by combining large-scale, engineered math data drawn from public web archives with training and reinforcement techniques that are both effective and resource-conscious. The results in the paper show that with careful data curation and algorithmic choices, an open 7B model can approach the performance of much larger closed models on challenging benchmarks.

The field of large language models exhibits a noticeable performance gap between the best closed-source systems and most publicly available open-source models, especially on demanding mathematical benchmarks. Closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have shown leading capabilities on a range of reasoning tasks, including competition-level mathematics, but their internal weights and training pipelines are not publicly available. That limits reproducibility and slows community progress.

Taken together, these contributions show a consistent theme: domain-specialized data combined with careful, resource-aware learning algorithms can push compact open models close to the capabilities of much larger closed systems on mathematical reasoning tasks. The paper makes four interlocking contributions that are useful both practically and scientifically.

4. A unified paradigm and empirical analysis for preference-based fine-tuning. The work frames supervised fine-tuning (SFT), rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), PPO, and GRPO within a single formal view that decomposes methods into: (a) the data source (offline vs online sampling), (b) the reward function (rules vs learned reward model), and (c) the algorithm that turns rewards into gradient coefficients. With this lens the authors run experiments that investigate: online versus offline sampling, outcome versus process supervision, single-turn versus iterative RL, and the role of code pre-training versus arXiv content. Important empirical findings include that code pre-training helps mathematical and program-aided reasoning, arXiv content by itself shows limited benefit in their experiments, and online sampling combined with GRPO-style gradient scaling yields better improvements than simpler rejection-based methods.

Chapter 1: Introduction and Motivation

Third, efficiency. Rather than simply scaling model size to hundreds of billions of parameters, DeepSeekMath emphasizes more efficient use of compute and memory. Two aspects illustrate this: (1) a targeted pre-training corpus assembled from Common Crawl that concentrates mathematical content at scale (120B math tokens), and (2) a reinforcement learning algorithm—Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—designed to get stronger reward-guided behavior while avoiding the memory and compute cost of training a large critic/value network as in standard PPO.

The second pillar is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an algorithmic variant of PPO tailored for LLM fine-tuning with reward models. The two practical constraints it addresses are (a) memory and compute overheads of training a separate value (critic) model as in PPO, and (b) the comparative nature of reward data in preference training. GRPO removes the learned critic entirely and instead forms a per-question baseline by sampling a group of outputs from the current policy for the same prompt and computing the group statistics (mean and standard deviation) of their reward scores. Each sampled output’s advantage is then its normalized reward relative to the group. That group-relative advantage fits naturally with reward models trained on comparisons among responses to the same question and reduces variance without a separate value network. In production RL runs reported in the paper, the authors sample 64 outputs per prompt for GRPO, use both outcome-level rewards (final-answer quality) and process-level rewards (stepwise scoring of chain-of-thought), and iterate the policy and reward model training in an online loop. The result is substantial performance improvements with lower resource requirements than standard PPO.

Second, mathematical solutions demand high fidelity: small changes in intermediate steps can flip the final answer. Unlike many natural-language tasks where approximate or paraphrased responses may still be acceptable, a single arithmetic mistake or a misapplied theorem usually yields an incorrect result. This sensitivity makes both training and evaluation more demanding: training signals must encourage not only coherent text but correct, verifiable reasoning, and evaluation must detect fine-grained errors.

The first is large-scale, targeted pre-training on math-rich web data. The authors construct the DeepSeekMath Corpus by iteratively mining math-related pages from Common Crawl, using a fastText classifier seeded with the publicly available OpenWebMath data and improved via successive rounds of recall and human annotation. The pipeline includes deduplication, domain-level inspection, and conservative decontamination to avoid benchmark leakage (e.g., exact 10-gram matching to filter evaluation data). The final corpus contains roughly 120B tokens of math-focused text across multiple languages (predominantly English and Chinese) and 35.5M web pages. This data yields a strong base model when used to continue pre-training from a code-specialized 7B initialization.

- DeepSeekMath-RL (the instruction-tuned model after GRPO reinforcement learning) reaches an accuracy of 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark under the evaluation protocol used in the study. Reaching above 50% on MATH is significant for an open-source 7B model and is reported as approaching the performance level of leading closed-source systems.

Other important numbers reported include that DeepSeekMath-Base (the pre-trained base before instruction tuning and RL) attains 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH using chain-of-thought prompting, outperforming some much larger pre-existing models such as Minerva 540B in these evaluations. After supervised instruction tuning (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) and then GRPO-based reinforcement learning (DeepSeekMath-RL), the model shows marked improvements on both in-domain and out-of-domain math tasks.

Second, strength. The ambition is to reach state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art performance on academic mathematical benchmarks spanning elementary arithmetic problems to competition-level college mathematics. Strength here is not only measured by final accuracy numbers but also by the ability to produce coherent step-by-step reasoning, to use programmatic tools when appropriate, and to generalize across languages (the DeepSeekMath corpus is multilingual).

2. Strong open 7B math models built from that corpus. Starting from a code-specialized 7B initialization and continuing training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus (and other sources like code and arXiv), the resulting base model already rivals or surpasses some much larger closed models on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. This empirically supports the idea that model scale is not the only determinant of reasoning ability—data quality and model initialization matter significantly.

These headline figures illustrate the core message of the work: carefully engineered data plus efficient policy optimization can produce strong mathematical reasoning in a compact, open model.

First, openness. The project aims to make strong mathematical reasoning capabilities available in models whose development and data-processing methods are transparent and reproducible. This requires relying on publicly available data sources and clearly documented engineering steps, so the broader research community can benefit.

3. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO is an efficient RL algorithm tailored to the comparative structure of reward-model supervision. By replacing the learned critic with a group-based baseline computed from multiple samples for the same prompt, GRPO reduces memory usage and aligns optimization more directly with the way preference data are collected. The algorithm supports both outcome supervision (reward at the end) and process supervision (stepwise rewards) and is used iteratively with reward-model retraining.

Mathematical reasoning differs from many other language tasks in two fundamental ways: structure and fidelity. First, mathematical problems are highly structured. A correct solution must follow a logical sequence of steps, often manipulating symbols, applying precise definitions, and carrying out calculations without relying on vague or approximate language. Language models trained primarily on general web text are excellent at producing plausible-looking prose, but plausibility is not the same as correctness for mathematics. A model can write a convincing-looking argument that contains a single algebraic error or an unstated but crucial assumption and thereby fail the task.

Open-source models, on the other hand, come in many sizes and flavors. Some are large and general-purpose, others have been specialized via additional training on math-related corpora or by instruction tuning. Historically, even substantial open-source models have trailed the closed models on high-difficulty math benchmarks. This gap stems from several factors that we highlighted above: (a) lack of sufficiently large and well-filtered math-specific pre-training data available to the public, (b) model initialization and pre-training choices (for example, whether the model has been exposed to code), and (c) the specific fine-tuning and reinforcement learning procedures used to align models to produce correct step-by-step solutions rather than just plausible text.

1. Scalable math pre-training pipeline and corpus. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is a large (120B-token) public-web-derived collection of math-focused text produced by an iterative fastText-based mining and human-refinement pipeline. It shows that significant mathematical content exists in Common Crawl and that with careful selection and decontamination it can be assembled into a high-quality pre-training resource. The multilingual nature of the corpus (notably strong Chinese coverage) also produces observable gains on non-English benchmarks.

DeepSeekMath is motivated by three complementary goals.

- When applying a self-consistency sampling strategy with 64 samples and aggregating answers (i.e., drawing many independent chain-of-thought samples and selecting the most consistent final answer), DeepSeekMath reaches 60.9% accuracy on MATH. Self-consistency is a post-processing technique that leverages diversity in sampled reasoning traces to increase the probability of obtaining a correct derivation; in this work it materially lifts accuracy when combined with DeepSeekMath-RL.
