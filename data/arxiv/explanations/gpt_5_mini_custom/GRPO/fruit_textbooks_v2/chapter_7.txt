# CoT performance: GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, MGSM-zh, MMLU-STEM, Gaokao-MathQA/Cloze

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting evaluates a <|endoftext|> ability to produce detailed, step-by-step natural language reasoning that leads to a final answer. The typical evaluation procedure uses few-shot CoT: a handful of example problems with full stepwise solutions are put in the prompt, and the model is asked to produce an <|endoftext|> chain of reasoning and final answer for held-out problems. The benchmarks used in <|endoftext|> span English and Chinese, and include GSM8K and MATH in English, CMATH and MGSM-zh in Chinese, as well as multiple-choice STEM problems such as MMLU-STEM and Gaokao datasets (MathQA/Cloze).

# PoT performance with Python tools (math, sympy)

Program-of-thought (PoT) evaluation measures a model’s ability to express its reasoning as executable code (commonly Python) and then rely on program execution to obtain precise numeric or symbolic results. In DeepSeekMath’s evaluations, PoT prompts instruct the model to solve problems by emitting a Python program that may import standard libraries like math or symbolic packages such as sympy; the executed program’s output is then taken as the model’s answer. This format is particularly suited to tasks that require precise <|endoftext|> algebraic <|endoftext|> or symbolic manipulations that are cumbersome or error-prone if handled purely in natural language.

The DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model was assessed on GSM8K and MATH using few-shot PoT prompting. The evaluation setup requires the model to produce a self-contained Python program whose final printed value is judged as the solution. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforms prior open-state-of-the-art models in this <|endoftext|> setting, including the larger Llemma 34B. This result emphasizes that math-focused <|endoftext|> equips the model not only to write coherent code but to write correct, executable procedures that leverage numerical and symbolic libraries. Programmatic reasoning therefore serves both as an evaluation of reasoning and as a practical tool for precise problem solving.

# Formal theorem proving: informal-to-formal with Isabelle and Sledgehammer on miniF2F

Formal theorem proving evaluates a model’s ability to convert informal mathematical reasoning into formal proofs in a proof assistant’s language. <|endoftext|> evaluates autoformalization on the miniF2F benchmark by targeting Isabelle as the proof assistant. The task is the informal-to-formal proof problem: given an informal statement, its formal counterpart, and an informal proof sketch, the model must produce a formal proof script that can be checked by Isabelle. Because formal proofs require precise syntactic structure and the correct use of tactics, this evaluation probes a different facet of mathematical capability than the more common informal CoT or PoT evaluations.

In practice, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is used to generate formal proof sketches which are then supplied to the automated tool Sledgehammer; Sledgehammer attempts to fill in the low-level details and discharge the proof obligations in Isabelle. The authors report that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B achieves strong few-shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F under this pipeline. The intuition behind this evaluation choice is that large LLMs can often produce <|endoftext|> formal sketches or tactic sequences that, when combined with automated proving tools, can yield complete machine-checked proofs. Success here indicates that the model has learned not only informal problem solving but also the syntactic and structural regularities of formal proof languages to a degree sufficient for assisted formalization.

# Self-consistency sampling and its effects on accuracy

Self-consistency (also known as majority voting across sampled reasoning chains) is a decoding-level technique that substantially improves accuracy in many reasoning tasks. The idea is simple: sample multiple diverse chains of thought for the same question, extract each chain’s final answer, and take the majority answer as the prediction. This procedure <|endoftext|> the model’s stochasticity: different sampled reasoning paths sometimes converge on the correct answer even when a single greedy decode does not.

In <|endoftext|> self-consistency over $64$ samples yields notable gains on the competition-level MATH benchmark: while the single-run RL-tuned model reaches $51.7\%$ on MATH, applying self-consistency with $64$ sampled reasoning chains increases accuracy to $60.9\%$. This is a substantial absolute improvement and demonstrates the practical value of sampling-based aggregation when the model’s output distribution places non-negligible mass on correct solutions that are not always the top-ranked sample.

The paper further analyzes how reinforcement learning (RL) affects sampling metrics such as Pass@$K$ and Maj@$K$. Pass@$K$ measures whether any of $K$ sampled outputs is correct (i.e., the probability that the correct answer appears among $K$ samples), while Maj@$K$ measures whether the majority of $K$ samples are correct. DeepSeekMath’s RL experiments show that RL improves Maj@$K$ more than Pass@$K$. In other words, RL increases the model’s tendency to output the correct answer more consistently across its top samples (raising majority agreement), even if the chance that at least one sample is correct (Pass@$K$) does not improve as much. From a <|endoftext|> perspective, this indicates that RL is making the correct answer more probable in the model’s conditional distribution, concentrating probability mass on correct solutions rather than merely uncovering rare correct samples. The authors interpret this as evidence that RL helps <|endoftext|> the model’s output distribution, boosting the correct response within the Top-$K$ rather than fundamentally extending raw capability.
