Chapter Title: Why BitNet b1.58 Accelerates More at Larger Scales: The Dominance of nn.Linear

# The Rise of 1.58-bit Large Language Models

Large Language Models have achieved impressive performance across a wide range of tasks, but their computational and energy costs have grown with model size. A promising direction to address these costs is the design of architectures whose arithmetic and memory characteristics substantially reduce inference overhead while maintaining accuracy. BitNet b1.58 is a 1.58-bit variant of BitNet in which every weight is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, BitNet b1.58 is shown to match full-precision Transformer baselines (e.g., FP16) in perplexity and end-task performance starting at the 3B scale, while providing substantial advantages in latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption.

At the computational level, BitNet b1.58 retains the core Transformer structure but replaces standard linear layers with a BitLinear component and adopts practical choices that align with open-source LLaMA-like systems. Its central insight is that matrix multiplications in the model can be realized with almost no floating-point multiplication, largely reducing the energy footprint and enabling faster inference when system power is the performance bottleneck. As model sizes grow, the advantages of this computation paradigm compound, especially where the dominant time cost scales with model width, depth, and parameter count.

# The Centrality of nn.Linear in Transformer Computation

In vanilla LLMs implemented with FP16 or BF16, the bulk of computation is matrix multiplication. Transformer architectures rely heavily on linear projections in attention and feed-forward components, and these are executed via layers commonly represented as $\texttt{nn.Linear}$. The paper emphasizes that the major computation cost in such models comes from floating-point addition and multiplication operations, which dominate the runtime because matrix multiplication constitutes the majority of the arithmetic workload.

BitNet b1.58 directly targets this dominant cost by altering how these linear transformations are computed. Rather than performing FP16 multiplications and additions, BitNet’s matrix multiplications involve integer addition, which reduces the energy cost by orders of magnitude. This shift has two intertwined consequences. First, under power-limited chips, energy savings translate into faster computation. Second, because the time cost for $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size, reengineering this component yields increasing speed-ups as the model scales.

# BitLinear: A New Computation Paradigm for Matrix Multiplication

BitNet b1.58 replaces $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ with $\texttt{BitLinear}$, grounding its efficiency in the quantization of weights and the design of activation quantization. All weights are ternary and produced by an absmean-based quantization rule that rescales and discretizes $W$ to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big),
\]
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
This mapping enforces a structure where multiplication by a ternary weight can be realized with sign changes and zeroing, and the overall accumulation involves integer additions. The activations are quantized to 8-bit values with a per-token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to remove zero-point quantization, simplifying implementation and system-level optimization while maintaining performance in experiments.

By constraining weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and using 8-bit activations, the resulting matrix multiplications use INT8 addition rather than FP16 addition and multiplication. The paper states that the majority of BitNet b1.58 energy cost is INT8 addition, whereas FP16 models consist of both FP16 addition and multiplication. In energy modeling on 7nm chips, this yields a $71.4\times$ savings in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication. Because matrix multiplication is the dominant operation and the time cost associated with it grows with model size, the BitLinear approach improves performance increasingly as models become larger.

# Why Speed-Ups Grow with Model Size

The authors identify $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ as the computation whose time cost grows with model size and attribute the increasing speed-up of BitNet b1.58 to this fact. As models scale from billions to tens of billions of parameters, the fraction of time spent in $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ increases, and thus any improvement to this component translates to larger aggregate gains. In BitNet b1.58, the near-elimination of floating-point multiplication in matrix multiplication leads to reduced arithmetic energy consumption and faster inference under power constraints. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 at $70\text{B}$ parameters is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline. This pattern is consistent with the statement that the time cost for $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size; by optimizing the dominant cost component, speed-up improves as the importance of that component grows.

The same dynamic appears in memory consumption. Embeddings remain in full precision, but their memory proportion becomes smaller in larger models, so the relative savings from ternary weights and 8-bit activations increase with scale. The paper’s results illustrate that both latency and memory trends favor BitNet b1.58 more strongly at higher parameter counts, and both were measured with a 2-bit kernel, leaving room for further optimization. Together, these observations support the conclusion that optimizing the primary scaling bottleneck—$\texttt{nn.Linear}$—produces the observed increasing speed-ups with model size.

# Empirical Results Supporting the Scaling Advantage

BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance starting at the $3\text{B}$ scale, while already providing significant latency and memory reductions. The reported comparisons show that BitNet b1.58 at around $3.9\text{B}$ is $2.4\times$ faster and consumes $3.32\times$ less memory than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 LLaMA, while performing better on evaluations. As the model scale increases, the advantage becomes more pronounced. At $70\text{B}$ parameters, BitNet b1.58 achieves a $4.1\times$ speed-up over the FP16 baseline, reflecting the growing dominance of $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ time cost and the payoff from the integer-addition computation paradigm.

Energy measurements corroborate this pattern. With matrix multiplication dominating energy consumption, the majority of BitNet b1.58’s arithmetic energy is in INT8 addition, avoiding FP16 multiplication. The paper reports $71.4\times$ savings in arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips and shows that end-to-end energy efficiency increases with model size due to the growing percentage of $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ in the overall computation.

Throughput results also reflect the compounded benefits at scale. Using two $80\text{GB}$ A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism, the $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 baseline, resulting in an $8.9\times$ higher throughput with a sequence length of $512$. This further illustrates that the reductions in arithmetic and memory bandwidth demands around the linear layers enable more efficient scaling under hardware constraints.

# Architectural Choices that Enable Practical Integration

BitNet b1.58 maintains compatibility with LLaMA-like components including RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and removal of biases. These choices allow minimal integration effort into widely used open-source frameworks and inference systems. Furthermore, the inclusion of a zero weight in ternary quantization enhances modeling capability by enabling explicit feature filtering, which can improve performance relative to the original 1-bit formulation while preserving the energy and memory benefits.

The activations are reduced from $16$ bits to $8$ bits and scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, simplifying implementation without significant performance degradation. This reduction directly impacts inference memory overhead, such as the cost of key-value caches in attention, and supports greater context lengths under the same resource budget. The paper notes that this can be further losslessly compressed to lower bit-widths for 1.58-bit LLMs in future work.

# A New Scaling Law for Performance and Cost

The paper emphasizes a new scaling law: with 1.58-bit computation, larger models become more cost-effective than much smaller FP16 models. For reference, the authors provide equivalences indicating that $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $3\text{B}$ FP16, $30\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7\text{B}$ FP16, and $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13\text{B}$ FP16, all in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. These equivalences align with the central insight: because $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ dominates and its time cost grows with model size, replacing it with an integer-addition-centric implementation leads to increasing speed-ups as models scale.

This scaling law extends beyond inference latency to memory and energy. As the model size increases, the proportion of computation in $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ grows, the embedding’s relative memory footprint shrinks, and the energy saved by avoiding FP16 multiplication becomes a larger fraction of the total. The result is a Pareto improvement across performance and cost—BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds accuracy while lowering latency, memory, and energy consumption more at larger scales.

# Implications and Future Directions

The paper suggests several future directions enabled by the BitNet b1.58 paradigm. Mixture-of-Experts architectures may benefit from reduced memory footprints and lower inter-chip communication overhead, potentially enabling deployment on fewer devices and even single-chip configurations. Native support for long sequences becomes more feasible as activations are reduced to $8$ bits and can be compressed further, increasing context lengths within fixed memory budgets. Edge and mobile devices, which are constrained by memory and computation, can leverage the reduced energy and memory demands of 1.58-bit LLMs, with the paper highlighting that BitNet b1.58 is more friendly to CPU devices common in such environments. Finally, the authors call for hardware specifically optimized for 1-bit LLMs to fully realize the benefits of the new computation paradigm.

Across these directions, the underlying reason for increasing speed-ups with model size remains the same. In standard FP16 Transformer LLMs, $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ dominates computation and its time cost scales with model size. BitNet b1.58 redefines this component with ternary weights and 8-bit activations, turning matrix multiplications into operations dominated by integer addition. Because the dominant, scaling-sensitive bottleneck is reengineered, the relative gains grow with model size, as evidenced by the reported latency, memory, throughput, and energy trends.
