Chapter: Training 1.58-bit LLMs for Performance Parity: Why Quantization During Training Matters

# From Post-Training Quantization to 1-bit Architectures

The rapid scaling of Large Language Models has improved task performance while amplifying deployment costs in memory, latency, energy, and hardware complexity. A widely used remedy is post-training quantization, where a full-precision model is trained and then compressed by lowering the bit-width of weights and activations for inference. Although effective for reducing resource use, this technique is explicitly described as sub-optimal in the paper. In particular, moving from $16$ bits to $4$ bits after training can reduce costs, but the process does not align the model’s learned representations and dynamics with the low-bit constraints during training.

The work positions 1-bit model architectures, specifically BitNet, as a fundamentally different path. Instead of compressing a full-precision model after it has converged, the approach is to enforce low-bit constraints throughout training. BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $8$-bit activations, integrated deeply into the architecture via a modified linear layer and quantization functions. This directly addresses the limitations of post-training quantization by coupling optimization and quantization: the network learns under the same arithmetic constraints it will face at inference time. As a result, the model matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance starting from $3$B parameters, while achieving large reductions in memory, latency, throughput, and energy consumption.

The contrast between post-training quantization and the training strategy used for BitNet b1.58 is central. Post-training quantization is a separate compression step applied after optimization in a full-precision regime. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 incorporates quantization into the training loop itself. As stated in the paper, the model is “trained from scratch” with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations. This training-time quantization—what one would intuitively call quantization-aware training—aligns the optimization trajectory with the representational limits of ternary weights and low-bit activations. The empirical results demonstrate that this alignment is crucial to achieving performance parity.

# The BitNet b1.58 Training Recipe

The core of BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer architecture in which every parameter is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, and activations are $8$-bit. The model replaces standard linear layers with BitLinear and retains LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and the removal of biases, ensuring compatibility with widely used open-source infrastructure. Crucially, the model is not derived from a floating-point baseline via post-training quantization. Instead, the quantized regime is native to both training and inference.

This training approach depends on a quantization function that constrains the weights throughout optimization. The paper adopts an “absmean” scaling to normalize the dynamic range of the weight matrix before discrete projection. Given a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the average absolute value is computed as
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
The scaled matrix $W / (\gamma + \epsilon)$ is then discretized using a bounded rounding operator:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\left(a, \min\!\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right).
\]
This yields ternary weights $\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}^{n \times m}$ that are used in the model’s computation. The inclusion of $0$ is highlighted as providing explicit support for feature filtering, thereby strengthening modeling capability compared to strictly $\{-1, 1\}$ regimes. Because this discretization happens during training, the optimization process learns to utilize the ternary constraint, including the structural sparsity induced by zeros.

Activations are also quantized during training, following the implementation in BitNet with a particular simplification. Instead of scaling activations to $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions, they are scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to remove the need for zero-point quantization. This per-token symmetric scaling reduces implementation complexity and aids system-level optimization, and experiments show negligible performance impact relative to the alternative. Together, the ternary weight quantization and $8$-bit activation scaling define the computational regime under which the network is trained and evaluated.

The coupling of optimization with quantization constraints is the key distinction. When the model learns in the same discrete arithmetic space in which it will be deployed—ternary weights and $8$-bit activations—the learned representations, layer dynamics, and regularities are adapted to quantization artifacts and range limitations. This alignment explains why performance parity is achieved starting at $3$B parameters, in contrast to the typical degradation observed when compressing post hoc a model trained in floating point.

# Why Training Under Quantization Achieves Parity

The empirical results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task accuracy beginning at $3$B parameters, and that the gap narrows with increasing size. For example, the $3.9$B BitNet b1.58 model outperforms a $3$B FP16 LLaMA-like baseline while being $2.4$ times faster and consuming $3.32$ times less memory. At $70$B, BitNet b1.58 is $4.1$ times faster than the FP16 baseline. Throughput experiments show up to $8.9$ times higher throughput and up to $11$ times larger batch sizes under the same hardware constraints. Energy analysis—focused on matrix multiplication, the dominant cost in LLMs—shows that BitNet b1.58 replaces FP16 addition and multiplication with primarily INT8 addition, saving $71.4$ times arithmetic operations energy consumption on $7$nm chips. These improvements grow with model size because the proportion of time and energy spent in linear layers increases, and embeddings, which remain full precision, contribute less relative overhead in larger models.

These results hinge on how the model is trained. The paper emphasizes that post-training quantization is sub-optimal, even though it is widely used. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations, using explicit quantization functions throughout training. This directly addresses two central issues with post-training quantization. First, quantization after training imposes discrete and range constraints that the optimization never anticipated, causing a mismatch between learned continuous parameters and their discrete deployment form. Second, post-training quantization cannot exploit structural properties that low-bit weights enable during training, such as feature filtering via zeros, because the model did not learn to use them.

By integrating quantization during training, BitNet b1.58 leverages the modeling benefits of ternary weights. The $0$ value enables the model to filter features and induce sparsity in learned transformations, improving expressivity within the highly constrained bit budget. The absmean scaling normalizes layer-wise ranges so that rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is meaningful, preventing pathological saturation and distributing representational capacity across weights. Symmetric per-token activation scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ avoids zero-point complications and aligns non-linearities with low-bit arithmetic. Because these constraints are present throughout optimization, the network adapts its internal representations to the quantized regime, and training discovers parameter configurations that survive discrete projection without a loss in task performance.

The observed performance parity starting from $3$B parameters further supports the view that training under quantization constraints is critical. As model size increases, the architecture’s capacity compensates for the representational limits imposed by ternary weights. When optimization is conducted in that regime, the model can utilize its capacity effectively, allocating ternary weights and $8$-bit activations to maintain perplexity and zero-shot performance. If training were conducted in full precision and quantization applied only afterward, the model would not have developed the necessary robustness to quantization artifacts, and the parity results reported would be unlikely.

# The Quantization Function and Its Role During Training

The choice of quantization function is central to making training effective under low-bit constraints. The absmean-based scaling used for weights ensures that the typical magnitude of $W$ drives the normalization, so that rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ occurs around a stable, data-dependent scale. Let $W$ be a weight matrix with entries $W_{ij}$. The scalar
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
\]
captures the average absolute magnitude across the matrix, and $\epsilon$ is added for numerical stability. Dividing by $(\gamma + \epsilon)$ produces a normalized matrix whose entries are typically near $-1$, $0$, or $1$, and then rounding followed by clipping,
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
enforces the ternary constraint. Because this process is present during training, the optimizer learns how changes to $W$ propagate through scaling and rounding, ultimately discovering $W$ values that, after normalization, produce useful $\widetilde{W}$ in the ternary set.

For activations, scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token avoids zero-point quantization, simplifying the pipeline and system-level implementation while maintaining performance. This symmetric range ties the dynamic range of activations to a token-specific scale, reducing the risk that activations consistently saturate at the bounds in low-bit arithmetic. The paper reports negligible performance effects from this simplification, suggesting that the alignment of activation scaling with training dynamics is sufficient for robust optimization under $8$-bit constraints.

Together, these quantization functions embody the principle of training under deployment constraints. The discrete arithmetic and low-bit ranges are not an afterthought; they are the environment in which the network learns. This alignment is the mechanism by which performance parity is achieved, and it is the core reason that the paper prefers training with quantization over post-training quantization to reach the reported results.

# Architectural Choices that Support Training Under Quantization

BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and the removal of biases—to integrate seamlessly with open-source tooling and to provide a familiar foundation for high-capacity Transformer training. These choices complement the quantization strategy. RMSNorm stabilizes layer scaling, which is helpful when activations are in $8$ bits and weights are ternary, because sensitivity to magnitude changes can be amplified in low precision. SwiGLU provides a gated nonlinearity with strong empirical performance in LLMs, and rotary embeddings offer effective positional encoding. Removing biases simplifies the computation and memory footprint, aligning with the overarching goal of efficiency, and reduces potential sources of instability in low-bit regimes.

These architectural components, coupled with the BitLinear replacement for standard linear layers, ensure that the bulk of computation—matrix multiplication—is conducted in a regime that benefits from integer addition rather than floating-point addition and multiplication. This not only yields large energy savings, as the paper shows, but also ensures that training learns transformations amenable to this arithmetic. Such structural alignment is essential: it is not sufficient to quantize at the end and hope that the learned floating-point transformations will perform equally well in a radically different arithmetic environment.

# Empirical Evidence and the New Scaling Law

The results section provides extensive evidence that training under quantization constraints enables parity and efficiency. Perplexity and zero-shot accuracy match full precision starting at $3$B parameters. Latency and memory improvements grow with model size, with $70$B BitNet b1.58 being $4.1$ times faster. Energy analysis quantifies $71.4$ times savings in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on $7$nm chips. Throughput at $70$B shows up to $8.9$ times improvements under identical hardware. The paper distills these findings into a practical scaling law: for example, $13$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a $3$B FP16 LLM across latency, memory, and energy; $30$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7$B FP16; and $70$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13$B FP16. These equivalences illustrate how training-time quantization enables larger models to be deployed with the cost profile of much smaller full-precision models.

Additionally, training with more tokens demonstrates robust generalization. With $2$T tokens, BitNet b1.58 at $3$B surpasses a state-of-the-art open-source $3$B model on multiple benchmarks, further supporting that quantization during training does not impair the ability to learn from large-scale data. This, again, would be difficult to reconcile with a post-training quantization approach, since the optimization would have proceeded in full precision and only afterward be forced into a low-bit regime.

# Implications: Why Training with Quantization Is Preferred

The paper’s narrative and empirical results implicitly prefer training with quantization over post-training quantization to achieve performance parity. Post-training quantization is labeled sub-optimal, while BitNet b1.58 is described as trained from scratch with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations, using explicit quantization functions. The results that demonstrate parity and efficiency rely on this training setup. The use of ternary weights with a $0$ value for feature filtering is a modeling capability realized during training, not a property that can be reliably exploited by compressing a fully trained FP16 model afterward. The symmetric activation scaling simplifies implementation and aligns non-linearities with the low-bit regime during optimization. The replacement of standard linear layers with BitLinear ensures that the computation paradigm—dominated by integer addition—is native to the model throughout training.

In sum, achieving the reported parity requires that quantization be part of the training process itself. Training under quantization constraints lets the optimizer discover solutions that are effective in the ternary and $8$-bit regimes, and it aligns the learned representations, layer behavior, and system-level properties with the deployment environment. This is precisely what the paper implements and validates. By contrast, post-training quantization imposes constraints after the fact, leading to a mismatch that the paper identifies as sub-optimal and that would undermine the parity results.
