Chapter: Why BitNet b1.58 Speeds Up More as Models Get Larger

# The 1.58-bit paradigm and the central role of linear layers

The work on BitNet b1.58 introduces a 1.58-bit Large Language Model in which every parameter takes values from the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, trained end-to-end with 8-bit activations, and designed to match full-precision Transformer LLMs in perplexity and task performance while dramatically reducing latency, memory, throughput cost, and energy consumption. The architectural change that enables these benefits is deceptively simple: BitNet b1.58 replaces the standard linear layers in Transformers with a new operator tailored to ternary weights and integer arithmetic. This single change reshapes both the compute and memory profiles of inference.

The central observation for understanding why BitNet’s relative speed-up improves with model size is that, in modern Transformer LLMs, the bulk of computation is matrix multiplication inside the linear layers typically implemented as $\text{nn.Linear}$. As models scale, these layers dominate both arithmetic and memory traffic more and more. BitNet b1.58’s compute paradigm specifically targets these layers: it eliminates floating-point multiplications and reduces operations to integer additions, which are significantly cheaper in energy and can be executed faster. Because the share of time spent in $\text{nn.Linear}$ grows with model size, the fraction of the model that benefits from BitNet’s optimized compute grows too, and the overall speed-up increases.

# From floating-point multiplications to integer additions

Vanilla LLMs use 16-bit floating-point values (FP16 or BF16) for weights and activations. The dominant operation is matrix multiplication in $\text{nn.Linear}$, which performs a large number of floating-point additions and multiplications. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ through a simple quantization scheme and executes matrix multiplications primarily via integer additions.

The quantization function that produces ternary weights scales a weight matrix $W$ by its average absolute value $\gamma$ and then rounds and clips to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\left(a, \min\!\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right),
\]
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Activations are quantized to 8-bit and are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid zero-point quantization, simplifying implementation while preserving performance. This design yields a matrix multiplication that avoids nearly all multiplications: multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ reduces to sign flips, zeroing, and accumulation, so the core arithmetic becomes integer addition.

Because compute power in many chips is fundamentally limited by energy, and integer additions are vastly cheaper than floating-point multiplications and additions, BitNet b1.58 realizes large arithmetic energy savings. Importantly, this benefit maps directly to latency: less energy per operation allows faster execution within the same power envelope.

# How scaling amplifies the benefit: the growing dominance of linear layers

Transformer LLMs scale primarily by increasing hidden dimensions, number of layers, and attention heads, all of which expand the sizes of matrix multiplications in $\text{nn.Linear}$. The time cost of $\text{nn.Linear}$ grows with model size because the matrices are larger and there are more of them. As a result, the proportion of total inference time spent inside $\text{nn.Linear}$ increases with scale, making it the dominant component of computation.

BitNet b1.58’s speed-up stems exactly from optimizing this dominant component. The model replaces $\text{nn.Linear}$ with a $\text{BitLinear}$ operator that leverages ternary weights and 8-bit activations, executing matrix multiplication with integer addition rather than FP16 multiplication and addition. When model sizes are small, other components such as embeddings and non-linear functions form a relatively larger share of the runtime. As models grow, however, the relative share of these components shrinks, while $\text{nn.Linear}$ occupies more of the runtime budget. Therefore, the fraction of total time that benefits from BitNet’s integer-add-based compute increases, and the end-to-end speed-up grows.

This trend is directly reported in the latency measurements: BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly faster relative to FP16 baselines as models scale from billions to tens of billions of parameters, with the 70B variant achieving a $4.1\times$ speed-up over the full-precision baseline. The explanation is not a secondary system effect; it follows from the core scaling behavior of Transformers and the specific optimization BitNet applies to the most expensive part.

# Latency trends observed in practice

Latency measurements were conducted with well-optimized inference kernels, including a 2-bit kernel integration, and reported per output token, the dominant cost in inference. For mid-sized models, BitNet b1.58 is already substantially faster; for example, the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is $2.4\times$ faster than a 3B FP16 baseline while also consuming less memory. As model size grows further, the speed-up increases, culminating in $4.1\times$ for 70B.

The reason latency follows this pattern is twofold. First, the computational share of $\text{nn.Linear}$ grows with model size, so replacing its FP16 multiplications with integer additions affects a larger portion of the latency. Second, the memory footprint of embeddings remains in full precision, and their relative share diminishes as hidden dimensions and linear projections dominate the parameter count and activations. Thus, the fixed-cost components that do not benefit as much from BitNet’s approach occupy less of the end-to-end time for larger models, letting the speed-up in the linear layers translate more directly to overall latency gains.

# Energy composition and why it supports larger speed-ups

Energy analysis focuses on matrix multiplication because it contributes the most to LLM cost. In FP16 LLMs, matrix multiplication requires both floating-point additions and multiplications. In BitNet b1.58, the majority of operations are INT8 additions, with multiplications largely eliminated. Using a published energy model for 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication relative to FP16.

As model size scales, the percentage of the model’s compute devoted to $\text{nn.Linear}$ grows, while other components contribute a smaller fraction. This amplifies BitNet’s energy advantage in end-to-end terms, and because power is a limiting factor for performance on real hardware, energy savings translate into greater latency improvements at larger scales. In effect, the more the model does the thing BitNet optimizes, the larger the realized speed-up.

# Memory, bandwidth, and the linear layers’ footprint

Inference cost is not only about arithmetic; moving weights and activations between DRAM and on-chip memory is also expensive. Full-precision models have higher memory footprints both in capacity and bandwidth, especially in the linear layers where large matrices are read and written repeatedly. BitNet b1.58 reduces the memory footprint dramatically by using 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This reduction lowers the bandwidth required to feed the compute units and reduces the time spent loading weights from DRAM.

As models grow, the proportion of memory traffic associated with $\text{nn.Linear}$ increases. Therefore, BitNet’s memory savings in these layers have a larger impact on end-to-end latency for larger models, reinforcing the trend driven by compute. Even when embeddings remain in full precision, their relative contribution shrinks with scale, allowing the memory and bandwidth advantages in the linear layers to dominate.

# Throughput and the new efficiency scaling law

BitNet b1.58’s advantages accumulate into higher throughput at scale. For example, on 70B models with pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 baseline and achieves $8.9\times$ higher throughput at a sequence length of $512$. This outcome follows from the same principle: the primary constraints—compute energy, memory capacity, and bandwidth—are most heavily exercised by the linear layers, and BitNet restructures those layers to be cheaper on all fronts.

These measurements motivate a new efficiency scaling law: a BitNet b1.58 model at substantially larger parameter counts can be more efficient in latency, memory, and energy than much smaller FP16 models. Empirically, $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $3\text{B}$ FP16; $30\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7\text{B}$ FP16; and $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13\text{B}$ FP16. The underlying reason remains the same—the dominance of the linear layers, and the fact that BitNet directly optimizes exactly those layers.

# Architectural compatibility and modeling capacity

BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and removes biases, allowing integration into popular open-source stacks with minimal effort. Its quantization adds an explicit $0$ weight value, supporting feature filtering and strengthening modeling capability relative to binary-only variants. Notably, starting from about $3\text{B}$ parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines on perplexity and end tasks under the same training token budgets, while also being faster and more memory-efficient. This performance parity ensures that the scaling argument is meaningful: the layers that dominate time are not being sacrificed in quality, they are being re-implemented with a cheaper arithmetic regime.

# Putting it all together

The dominant factor behind BitNet’s increasing relative inference speed-up at larger model sizes is the growing dominance of $\text{nn.Linear}$ layers in both compute and memory traffic. BitNet b1.58 replaces these layers’ FP16 matrix multiplications—composed of floating-point additions and multiplications—with integer-add-centric operations on ternary weights and 8-bit activations. As models scale, the fraction of end-to-end cost attributable to these layers increases, so the benefits of BitNet’s compute and memory optimization apply to a larger share of the workload. The result is a speed-up that grows with model size, supported by measured latency, energy savings, memory reductions, and throughput improvements.
