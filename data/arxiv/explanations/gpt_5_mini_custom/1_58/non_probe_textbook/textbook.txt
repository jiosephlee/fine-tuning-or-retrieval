The Era of Ternary-Quantized Language Systems: Motivation and Overview

# Why scalable language systems are costly: processing, storage, response delay, and electrical demand constraints

Modern large-scale language systems have grown enormously in their capacity and scope. This growth yields impressive capabilities but also brings four practical bottlenecks that shape how these systems are built and used.

First, the dominant computational work in these systems arises from repeating the same kind of dense numerical combination over and over across many internal connections. Those repeated operations, when represented with high numeric fidelity, require both multiplication and summation operations at large scale. Because those arithmetic steps must be performed many times per input, the cost of raw number crunching becomes the single largest contributor to run-time work.

Second, storing the many numerical entries that define the system consumes a great deal of on-board and off-board storage bandwidth and capacity. Moving those entries from slower main storage into fast on-chip storage or caches, and keeping them available to processing units, is itself costly: it uses silicon area, increases hardware complexity, and slows the effective rate at which the system can produce outputs for a given hardware setup.

Third, the time it takes for the system to respond is affected by both the compute cost and the cost of moving the numerical entries around. When the required numerical entries are large in size and bandwidth-thirsty, the response delay for producing each output increases, which harms interactive use.

Finally, the electrical demand associated with carrying out these computations and transfers is a major constraint. Multiplication operations carried out at high floating-point fidelity are among the most power-hungry primitives in modern chips; similarly, moving wide numeric representations repeatedly consumes substantial electrical budget. Together, these four bottlenecks sharply increase the cost of operating large language systems and motivate methods that make representations and computations leaner.

# Moving from sixteen-bit floating formats to low-precision for run-time use

A common route to reduce these costs is to represent the system's numerical entries at lower precision so that arithmetic and transport operations are cheaper. Historically, the community moved from older, wider formats toward sixteen-bit floating-point representations for both development and run-time use, because those formats gave a reasonable trade between numerical fidelity and hardware efficiency.

For run-time use, researchers and engineers began exploring even lower-precision choices. The intuition is simple: if the numerical entries can be encoded with fewer bits, then both the arithmetic and the transfers become cheaper. Practitioners experimented with moderate reductions that keep both multiplication and summation in place but use narrower numeric formats for each operand; this moves the whole pipeline toward smaller on-chip footprints and reduced electrical demand. As the precision decreases further, implementation choices become more varied: some approaches retain floating-point multiplication but with narrower mantissas and exponents; others move to pure integer arithmetic for some parts of the computation. Each step down in representation can deliver lower storage needs and cheaper arithmetic, but the challenge is ensuring the system's predictive quality does not degrade unacceptably.

# Limits of after-development reduction of numeric precision

One particularly practical class of techniques reduces numeric precision after the system has been developed. These after-development reduction methods take a fully specified system and transform its stored numerical entries into a smaller set of representable values, with the aim of preserving predictive performance while enabling cheaper run-time behavior. These techniques are attractive because they can be applied to existing systems without re-running the entire development process.

However, such after-the-fact reductions face limitations. First, because they operate on already-optimized entries, they must approximate values that were chosen for a richer numeric palette; some degree of mismatch and performance loss is often inevitable. Second, these methods commonly rely on local heuristics to select quantized equivalents, and those heuristics may not generalize uniformly across different parts of the system, which can introduce brittle failure modes on certain tasks. Third, reducing precision while preserving the interactions between different subcomponents and preserving numeric stability is technically delicate; certain nonlinear operations and internal scaling behaviors can amplify small quantization-induced errors into noticeable prediction degradation.

Those limitations explain why practitioners have pursued alternative strategies: rather than compressing an already-built system, one can design the system and its development process from the outset to operate with a narrower palette of representable values. When the development process is aware of the intended reduced representation, the optimizer can learn to compensate for the coarser numeric choices, often yielding much better final behavior at run time than after-the-fact compression.

# The BitNet lineage and the shift to integer-summation–centric computation

Along this line of thought, a family of designs has emerged that rethinks the numerical primitives used throughout the system. Instead of centering design around high-fidelity floating-point multiplication followed by summation, these designs convert much of the heavy lifting into integer-based operations that rely primarily on summation. The motivation is twofold: integer summation tends to be less electrically demanding on modern silicon than floating-point multiplication, and integer data move requires narrower bandwidth for the same representational economy.

The earlier members of this family showed that with careful redesign of the representation and some supporting algorithms, one can replace many of the expensive steps with sequences of low-precision integer sums and very simple logic operations. Those systems kept other elements of the pipeline in higher fidelity where needed (for example, for embedding certain inputs or for certain nonlinear transforms), but they demonstrated that a substantial portion of the total computational load can be handled with cheaper primitives. This shift opens new avenues for hardware and system co-design: if the algorithmic form expects summations over discrete small-valued coefficients, then specialized accelerators or kernels can execute these operations far more cheaply than general floating-point units.

# What ternary representation means: three-valued coefficients and their information content

A central idea examined in this work is the use of three-valued coefficients for the core numerical entries. In this representation, each connective coefficient can take one of three possibilities: negative unity, zero, or positive unity. This three-valued choice is sometimes called ternary quantization. The presence of a zero value introduces an explicit notion of filtering: certain pathways can be shut off entirely when the best choice is to ignore a contribution for a particular learned pattern, while positive or negative unity permit simple signed contributions otherwise.

From an information-theoretic standpoint, each ternary entry carries a fixed amount of information that is less than two binary units and greater than one binary unit; in other words, a three-way choice corresponds to a binary-logarithmic quantity equal to the logarithm of three in base two. In symbolic form this is the ratio of natural logarithms, written as $\frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2}$. That quantity is slightly greater than one and a half in terms of binary information units, which is why the shorthand notation in some contexts refers to such a representation as giving about one point five eight bits per entry. The practical import is that ternary storage is a compact middle ground: far leaner than full sixteen-bit floating formats, but richer than naive binary-only choices, and the zero value gives an important modeling degree of freedom that pure sign-only schemes lack.

# Contributions of the ternary-quantized variant: matching higher-fidelity performance at lower operational cost; a new scaling perspective; hardware implications

The ternary-quantized variant described in the source work demonstrates several notable contributions when evaluated empirically and conceptually.

First, when the development process is performed from scratch with awareness of the ternary representation, the resulting systems can achieve predictive performance comparable to that of systems developed with much higher numeric fidelity—measured on both held-out predictive metrics and on a variety of downstream language tasks. This parity becomes apparent at practical scales of system size: as the representational footprint grows, the ternary-aware development leads to systems that match or exceed the predictive behavior of sixteen-bit references, while costing much less to run.

Second, this design reframes the performance-versus-cost trade-off and suggests a new scaling perspective. Because the dominant costs for large systems scale with the amount of dense numerical computation and with the volume of stored entries that must be moved, a ternary representation shifts the Pareto frontier: a larger ternary system can provide similar predictive power to a smaller higher-fidelity system, while offering faster response rates, less storage throughput demand, and reduced electrical demand. In practical terms, engineers and researchers can trade off representational breadth against reduced per-entry cost in a more favorable manner than with after-the-fact compression.

Third, the computational recharacterization has clear hardware implications. A system that relies primarily on integer summations over three-valued coefficients can be implemented with specialized kernels or accelerators that avoid the need for wide floating-point multipliers. That not only reduces the electrical demand per operation but also simplifies the data-movement pipeline: fewer bits per entry means fewer off-chip transfers and smaller on-chip storage needs. The inclusion of an explicit zero value further benefits sparse-like optimizations: hardware and software can skip work or compress pathways where zeros dominate, yielding additional reductions in run-time activity.

Finally, the ternary-aware approach supports several downstream system-level improvements. It makes mixture-of-experts–style routing more feasible in constrained hardware setups because the overall storage and transfer overhead decreases; it allows practical increases in the length of the context that can be handled for a fixed hardware budget because intermediate representations can be kept at lower precision; and it makes deployment on constrained edge devices more realistic because the storage and computational footprints shrink while preserving predictive competence.

Taken together, these points argue that building language systems with three-valued coefficients from the outset provides a compelling path toward more cost-effective, scalable, and hardware-friendly designs. The shift is not merely a compression trick applied after development; it is a change in the native numerical idiom for both learning and serving, with ripple effects on software kernels, device designs, and system architectures.

How Attention-Based Learners Spend Compute: A Cost and Hardware Primer

# Attention-based anatomy: attention, feed-forward blocks, and where arithmetic concentrates

Attention-based sequence learners are built from repeating computation blocks, each of which contains two distinct computational subunits. The first subunit computes pairwise interactions among positions of an input sequence: each position is translated into several linear projections that play the roles of query, key, and value. These projections are then combined so that each position gathers information from other positions according to similarity scores. The second subunit is a dense, position-wise transform that expands and contracts the hidden dimensionality and applies a non-linear gating step in between. Intuitively, the first subunit handles context mixing across positions, and the second subunit remixes features at every position independently.

When you count arithmetic work, the vast majority arises from the dense matrix-by-matrix and matrix-by-vector products that implement both subunits. Forming the query, key, and value matrices from the input requires multiplying an input matrix by three learned matrices; computing pairwise similarity is a big product between the query set and the transposed key set; and combining values back into output representations is another product. The feed-forward subunit is implemented with two dense transforms separated by a non-linear gating, and each of those transforms is another heavy matrix product. Because these products involve large dimensions and are applied across every position in a sequence, they swamp the cost of the comparatively small pointwise non-linearities and of bookkeeping work like position encodings and simple vector scaling.

A compact way to see where arithmetic goes is to write the shapes. Let $L$ be sequence length and $D$ the main hidden dimension. A projection from input to queries has cost proportional to $L \times D \times D_q$ in terms of pairwise products and summations, where $D_q$ is the per-head projection dimension. The pairwise similarity step creates an $L \times L$ attention matrix at the per-head level, which makes that subunit scale quadratically with $L$ when viewed purely as operations on positions. The feed-forward subunit typically uses an intermediate size that is larger than $D$, so its dense transforms cost roughly $L \times D \times D_{ff}$ each. Summed across subunits and across all repeated blocks, those dense products dominate arithmetic counts.

# Matrix product as the dominant compute hotspot in large language learners

Dense matrix products are the kernel that determines both time and energy in attention-based systems. If $A$ is size $L\times D$ and $B$ is $D\times M$, the work to produce their product is proportional to the product of those three symbolic dimensions, which one can write as a dependence on $L$, $D$, and $M$. When $L$ or $D$ or $M$ grows, the arithmetic grows multiplicatively. That multiplicative scaling explains why modest increases in hidden size or sequence length quickly produce very large increases in arithmetic work.

Because the same dense product pattern appears throughout the design—projections, the pairwise similarity formation, the gathering step, and the two transforms in the feed-forward block—any change that touches matrix product cost has outsized effects on end-to-end runtime and on energy use. This is the fundamental reason why efforts to accelerate attention-based learners often target faster implementations of dense products or seek algorithmic ways to reduce the sizes of those products.

The paper that motivates this chapter takes that observation to heart and demonstrates a practical route: by constraining the learned coefficient matrices to a very small discrete set of values, the per-element arithmetic becomes simpler and the amount of data that must be fetched from memory shrinks. Because dense products are the dominant hotspot, simplifying their per-element work cascades into large reductions in runtime and energy.

# Latency, sustained rate, memory requirement, and transfer capacity

When evaluating a deployment, four resource-oriented quantities matter and they interact.

Latency is the elapsed time to produce a single requested output from the moment the system receives its input. This depends on both the raw arithmetic throughput available on the processor and on the memory transfers and sequential dependencies that must be satisfied before the output can be returned.

Sustained rate is the steady-state number of outputs the system can produce per unit time when it is fed a continuous stream. Sustained rate benefits from parallelism: running many inputs in flight amortizes memory transfers and allows hardware pipelines to be filled.

Memory requirement is the amount of storage one must hold on the hardware to run the model: that includes the stored learned matrices, temporary buffers used during the dense products, and the per-request caches that keep intermediate results across time.

Transfer capacity is the rate at which data can be moved between slower off-chip storage and the processor’s fast on-chip storage. In many systems, transfer capacity is a bottleneck because the dense product kernels are hungry for coefficient data and for per-position intermediate values.

These four concepts interact through trade-offs. Increasing the amount of on-chip storage that holds coefficient tables reduces repeated off-chip transfers, which lowers latency and raises sustained rate. Increasing concurrency (feeding more inputs at once) can boost sustained rate but may increase end-to-end latency for a single request, because the shared resources are time-sliced. Critically, because dense products are so dominant, any technique that shrinks the data that must be moved or reduces per-element arithmetic tends to simultaneously lower latency, increase sustained rate, and reduce memory requirement.

# Off-chip memory versus on-chip fast storage: capacities, transfer limits, and movement costs

Modern processors pair a large, relatively slow, off-chip memory with a much smaller, much faster on-chip store. The off-chip bank can hold large learned matrices and long-lived data, but fetching a block from off-chip into the processor incurs a substantial time and energy overhead compared to reading the same bytes from on-chip storage.

On-chip store offers high parallel access, low latency, and energy-efficient reads and writes, but its capacity is limited. That means, in practice, learned coefficient tables are streamed from off-chip memory into on-chip buffers just in time for the dense products. The cost of moving those bytes can dominate when the arithmetic per byte is modest; in other words, when arithmetic intensity is low, data motion is what kills performance.

Reducing the size of the stored tables changes the arithmetic/communication balance. If each stored number requires fewer bytes, the same on-chip store can hold more of the learned tables, which reduces the need to stream from off-chip and allows higher reuse of data already resident on chip. The paper illustrates this effect by constraining stored coefficients to a tiny discrete set; that reduces the bytes that must be transported and stored, so the apparent pressure on off-chip transfer falls and on-chip buffers can be used more effectively.

Because movement from off-chip storage is expensive in both time and energy, the practical effect of a reduced stored table size is two-fold: first, fewer off-chip transfers lower latency and increase sustained rate; second, less energy is spent moving data, permitting a more power-efficient implementation overall.

# Power as a fundamental limit; why energy savings translate to speed

Modern silicon is power-limited: chips have a maximum thermal and electrical envelope they can operate within. That envelope constrains the product of frequency, parallelism, and per-operation energy. When per-element arithmetic and data movement are expensive, the chip cannot run more units in parallel or at higher frequency without exceeding its power limit.

Lowering the energy use per arithmetic operation and per byte moved buys back headroom in the power envelope. That headroom can be converted into more parallel functional units active at once, higher operating frequency, or simply longer sustained operation at peak rates. Concretely, if a change to the numeric format or to the arithmetic kernel reduces per-element energy substantially, the implementation can either run faster or handle more parallel inputs for the same power limit.

The paper uses this principle: by replacing complex floating arithmetic with much simpler integer-like operations and by slashing the amount of data transferred for learned coefficient tables, the per-matrix-product energy drops sharply. Because the chip is not allowed to exceed its power budget, that energy drop allows a larger fraction of the chip’s silicon to be devoted to useful work at a given clock, which manifests as higher effective speed and higher sustained rate during deployment.

# Precision’s impact on arithmetic intensity and memory traffic

Precision of stored numerical values governs two crucial quantities: the number of bytes that must be transferred for each learned coefficient, and the complexity of the per-element arithmetic. Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of arithmetic work to bytes moved; higher arithmetic intensity is attractive because it means more computation is performed per byte of transfer, making the implementation more compute-bound and less limited by transfer capacity.

If the bytes per stored coefficient shrink, the denominator of arithmetic intensity shrinks, which raises the arithmetic intensity. Higher arithmetic intensity makes it easier to keep on-chip compute units busy and reduces the fraction of time spent waiting on data transfers. Moreover, if the per-element arithmetic is simplified (for example, replacing multiply-and-accumulate sequences with operations that require only selection, sign flips, or low-precision accumulation), the energy per arithmetic operation drops, and the overall energy-per-output declines.

The paper demonstrates this by enforcing a ternary constraint on learned coefficients so that each stored entry takes one of three possible values. That constraint both reduces the storage footprint of the learned tables and simplifies the per-element processing when computing matrix products: instead of general high-precision multiplications and wide accumulators, the inner workings can use streamlined integer arithmetic and accumulation. The net effect on arithmetic intensity and memory traffic is large because dense products dominate the total work.

An analytic way to present the effect uses symbolic quantities. Let $F$ denote the number of arithmetic operations required for a dense product over a given input, and let $B$ denote the number of bytes that must be moved from slow storage for those operations. Arithmetic intensity can be written as $I = F / B$. If a format change reduces per-coefficient bytes by a factor $s$ (with $s$ expressed as a fraction), then the new intensity is $I' = F / (s B) = I / s$, which increases as $s$ decreases. Higher $I'$ improves the ability to hide data transfer behind computation and thus improves effective hardware utilization, latency, and sustained rate.

# Takeaways and practical implications

The dominant lesson is simple and robust: because dense matrix products account for the bulk of arithmetic and data movement in attention-based sequence learners, efforts that reduce the size of stored coefficient tables or that simplify the per-element arithmetic in those products have outsized benefits. Reducing storage per coefficient shrinks off-chip transfer needs and raises arithmetic intensity; simplifying arithmetic reduces energy per operation and frees power headroom that can be converted into extra parallelism or higher effective speed.

Architectural choices that narrow the set of per-coefficient values, and corresponding system support that streams those compact representations efficiently into on-chip buffers, transform the cost profile of the entire system. The paper that motivates this primer shows a practical instance of this idea: by restricting stored coefficients to a tiny discrete set and using low-precision representations for intermediate responses, the authors move the dominant kernel from costly, high-precision products to much lighter integer-like operations and reduced traffic. Because matrix products are the hot spots, that single change cascades into large improvements in response latency, sustained rate, memory requirements, and energy per output on realistic hardware.

For engineers and researchers, the practical implication is to focus optimization effort where it yields the largest leverage: the dense product kernels and the memory subsystem that feeds them. Changes that reduce per-coefficient storage, increase reuse of on-chip buffers, or simplify inner-loop arithmetic will pay dividends across latency, sustained rate, and energy metrics.

Quantization Foundations for Language Systems

# Coefficients versus Layer Outputs: symmetric versus asymmetric and zero-offsets

When we reduce the precision of a learned system, there are two distinct places where such reduction is commonly applied: the stored coefficients that determine how signals are combined, and the intermediate outputs that flow between processing stages. The stored coefficients are the values that are learned and retained for use whenever the system computes a response. The intermediate outputs are the values produced at run-time when data is propagated through the network. Treating these two types of values differently is important because their statistical properties and role in overall cost differ. In the work this chapter follows, the authors place highly discretized values on the stored coefficients while using a higher-resolution reduced format for the intermediate outputs, reflecting a trade in precision versus run-time bandwidth and compute.

A second important axis is whether the discretization is symmetric about zero or allows an offset. A symmetric scheme maps positive and negative magnitudes to symmetric discrete levels around zero; an asymmetric scheme permits a non-zero offset so that the zero of the continuous domain does not necessarily map to a code that represents numerical zero. The paper adopts a symmetric discretization for the stored coefficients (the discrete alphabet is negative unity, zero, and positive unity) while for the intermediate outputs they avoid a per-code offset by scaling to a symmetric interval per position. Removing the explicit offset term simplifies implementation of the reduced-resolution intermediate-stage arithmetic at run-time and, in the reported experiments, has negligible adverse effect on final quality.

Zero-offsets (often called zero-points in other contexts) matter because they introduce an extra bookkeeping value that must be applied when combining stored coefficients and intermediate outputs. If the run-time arithmetic can be designed so that the stored coefficients and the intermediate outputs are both centered and symmetric, the combination logic can be much simpler and can avoid per-element corrections. The approach described in the reference work explicitly rescales intermediate outputs per position into a symmetric interval to eliminate the need for a zero-offset representation there.

# Scaling granularity: per-array, per-channel, per-position

A key practical decision when mapping continuous values to a small set of discrete codes is at what granularity to compute the scale factor that controls that mapping. One extreme is to compute a single scale per whole multi-dimensional array of coefficients; another is to compute a separate scale for each output channel of a transform; yet another is to compute scales individually for each run-time position or token. The reference design uses a small set of different choices: the stored coefficients are normalized by a single scalar derived from the magnitudes across the coefficient array, while the intermediate outputs are normalized per position. The rationale is pragmatic. A single global scale for coefficients simplifies storage and run-time representation of those values and works well when the coefficient statistics are relatively uniform across the array. Per-position scaling for intermediate outputs is attractive because the distribution of signals can vary from one sequence location to the next; scaling per position removes the need for a non-zero code and allows the reduced-range representation of intermediate outputs to be symmetric.

Per-channel scaling can be viewed as a compromise: it adapts to differences between groups of outputs without the storage overhead of per-position adaptation. In many systems the choice of granularity is a trade-off between representational fidelity (finer granularity captures more variability, reducing quantization error) and overhead in bookkeeping and storage as well as complexity in run-time pipelines. The reference approach selects a coarse granularity for stored coefficients to minimize storage and run-time cost, and a fine granularity for intermediate outputs to preserve dynamic range where it matters most for run-time computation.

# Nearest-integer mapping and clipping; handling outliers

Mapping real-valued entries into a small alphabet is performed in two conceptual steps. First, the entries are scaled so that typical magnitudes fall into the dynamic range of the discrete codes. Second, each scaled entry is mapped to the nearest available code, and any extreme values that remain are clipped to the representable boundary. In the reference design the scaling for the stored coefficients uses the mean of magnitudes across the array to define the divisor, and the mapping itself is implemented as a nearest-integer mapping followed by clipping to the allowed set.

Expressed formally for a coefficient array $W$, the quantized copy $\widetilde{W}$ can be written in schematic form as
$ \widetilde{W} \;=\; \mathrm{Clip}\!\big(\mathrm{nint}\!\big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}\big), -\mathrm{one}, \mathrm{one}\big), $
where $\mathrm{nint}(\cdot)$ denotes nearest-integer mapping, $\mathrm{Clip}(\cdot,a,b)$ clamps values into the interval $[a,b]$, and the scale $\gamma$ is obtained from the mean magnitude of entries:
$ \gamma \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{one}}{n m} \sum_{ij} \big|W_{ij}\big|. $
The small positive term $\epsilon$ prevents division instability.

Outliers — a few entries with unusually large magnitudes — are a practical challenge for coarse quantization. The mean magnitude used in the scale computation is a robust, low-cost statistic that reduces sensitivity to very small numbers but is still influenced by outliers. The paper shows that this simple scale choice combined with clipping at the representable boundary yields a practical balance: most entries quantize faithfully and a small set of extreme values are clamped rather than forcing the entire scale to expand and degrade representability of more typical entries. For the run-time intermediate values, the authors scale per position to a symmetric range $[-Q,Q]$ so that there is no need for an offset code; this per-position scaling also reduces the chance that a single large value at one position will corrupt the scale used for others.

# Quantization during the learning-phase versus post-hoc conversion

There are two broad strategies for bringing a high-resolution trained system into a low-resolution form. One is to take a fully trained high-resolution system and, in a separate step, map its stored coefficients to the discrete alphabet; this is often called post-hoc conversion. The other is to incorporate the reduced representation into the learning-phase itself, so that the system learns while its stored coefficients are already constrained to the discrete set.

The reference paper argues that post-hoc conversion is convenient and commonly used in practice, but it is sub-optimal relative to integrating quantization into the learning-phase. In their approach they train from scratch with the stored coefficients constrained to a three-valued alphabet and with reduced-resolution intermediate outputs during forward computation. Training from scratch under such constraints means the learner adapts to the limited representational alphabet and to the run-time arithmetic characteristics, often resulting in better final quality than performing a separate conversion after high-resolution learning.

From a systems perspective this choice also affects deployment cost: when the stored coefficients are learned directly in their final low-cost representation, the run-time benefits (reduced memory bandwidth, simpler arithmetic) are realized throughout the development and evaluation process.

# Binary and ternary networks: representational trade-offs

At the extreme of discretization are two simple alphabets: a two-valued set and a three-valued set. The two-valued case represents entries by negative unity or positive unity; the three-valued case inserts an explicit zero code so entries can be negative unity, zero, or positive unity. The earlier work that inspired the paper explored the two-valued option because it yields the simplest possible stored-coefficient representation and enables run-time arithmetic that avoids multiplication in favor of sign toggles and additions. The version developed in the referenced work augments that scheme with an explicit zero code, creating a three-valued alphabet.

This addition of a zero code has two key effects. First, it increases representational flexibility: a stored coefficient may explicitly indicate the absence of a contribution rather than forcing a small residual sign. This can be interpreted as giving the network a mechanism for filtering features at the level of stored coefficients. Second, it changes the distribution of quantization error: with an explicit zero code, small magnitudes can be represented exactly as zero rather than being rounded to a non-zero signed value. The paper reports that the three-valued choice matches high-resolution baselines in quality for sufficiently large systems while retaining many of the deployment advantages of extreme discretization.

Designers must weigh these benefits against the small increase in storage cost of allowing a third code and the slight increase in run-time complexity of representing three codes rather than two. In the reported experiments the three-valued choice is a pragmatic sweet spot: it keeps run-time arithmetic largely simplified while improving modeling capacity through explicit feature suppression.

# Gradients through discretization (straight-through idea and caveats)

Learning while using discrete stored coefficients requires care because the forward mapping from continuous internal variables to discrete codes is non-differentiable. The reference work trains from scratch with discrete stored coefficients in the forward pass; the paper does not exhaustively detail the backward strategy used to propagate learning signals through this discretization, but the general conceptual challenge is common: if a forward operation maps continuously varying inputs to a small set of codes, the naive derivative of that mapping is zero almost everywhere, which prevents gradient-based updates.

A widely used pragmatic approach in the literature is to replace the true derivative of the discrete mapping with a surrogate that allows gradient flow — conceptually, one lets the backward pass treat the discretization as if it were an identity mapping or as if a smooth proxy were used. This surrogate restores non-zero gradients that guide the updates of the underlying continuous variables that, when mapped forward, produce the discrete codes. The paper is consistent with this general strategy in that it performs learning with quantized stored coefficients from the outset; such an approach relies on an approximation to the true non-differentiable backward mapping to enable effective adaptation.

There are caveats. Surrogate gradients are an approximation; they do not exactly reflect how small changes in a continuous internal value change the discrete forward output. Learning under such an approximation can still succeed in practice, and the reference study demonstrates high-quality outcomes under the chosen discretization and scaling regimen. Nonetheless, designers must be aware that surrogate-gradient learning can exhibit biases: it may prefer parameter configurations that are easy for the surrogate to move, or it may require careful tuning of step sizes and normalization to be stable. The paper mitigates some of these concerns by using simple but robust scaling choices (mean magnitude for stored coefficients and per-position symmetric scaling for intermediate outputs) so that the forward mapping behaves consistently during the learning-phase.

# Practical takeaways tied to the reference design

From a pragmatic perspective the reference study offers a compact recipe that is useful to internalize. First, a coarse but robust scale computed from the mean magnitude of a stored-coefficient array provides a simple normalization that keeps most entries in range while limiting sensitivity to extreme values. Second, mapping to a small symmetric alphabet that includes a zero code gives explicit filtering power while remaining friendly to simplified run-time arithmetic. Third, scaling intermediate outputs per position into a symmetric interval removes the need for zero-offset bookkeeping and eases run-time integration. Finally, incorporating the discretized representation into the learning-phase rather than applying it only after high-resolution learning yields better final quality in this design.

These choices together illustrate how algorithmic design, numerical scaling, and considerations about run-time arithmetic interact. Understanding these interactions is essential for anyone aiming to read and reproduce the experiments in the paper or to adapt the general approach to other network families or deployment constraints.

Ternary-Parameter Network: Design and Quantization for Compact Language Systems

# From earlier ternary design to ternary-plus-zero: adding a silence state to entries

The original idea behind extreme quantization for large language systems was to compress the stored matrices that govern how signals flow between processing units so that each stored entry takes on a very small set of possible values. An early extreme instance used only two non-zero choices, effectively forcing every stored entry to convey a sign. The variant described here inserts an explicit silence choice into that palette. Rather than forcing every contribution to be strictly positive or negative, each matrix entry may now also be set to a suppressed state that removes its contribution entirely.

This small change shifts the representational behavior in two important ways. First, having a suppressed state gives the network a simple, built-in mechanism for turning off particular pathways that are not useful in certain contexts. Second, it expands the expressivity of each stored entry beyond mere sign carrying: with three options one can represent positive, negative, or neutral influence directly. Practically, this allows training from scratch with the three-state constraint and delivers modeling power that matches more conventional higher-fidelity variants at moderate sizes, according to the empirical findings summarized in the source work.

# Ternary-aware linear operator versus standard dense linear operator: integer-summation–centric matrix products

A standard dense linear operator in neural systems computes many dot products between incoming signals and stored matrices. Those dot products are traditionally implemented with real-valued scaling followed by accumulation, which requires a mix of scaling and summation hardware. The ternary-aware operator replaces the stored real scalars with three-valued entries and reorganizes the compute so that the inner loops avoid general real-valued scaling.

Concretely, when each stored entry is constrained to the set of three states, the per-element contribution to a dot product can be implemented as either adding a copy of the incoming element, subtracting it, or skipping it. This means the heavy inner loop reduces to signed accumulation of integer-quantized inputs rather than general floating-point multiply-and-accumulate operations. The runtime kernel therefore emphasizes integer summation and sign-routing, with only a small amount of per-block scaling to recover amplitude, instead of full-blown per-element multiplies.

This reorganized compute flow has two practical benefits. First, the costly per-element scaling hardware becomes rare in the hot path; much of the inner work is simple summation of quantized values, which is both cheaper in arithmetic and simpler to optimize on specialized kernels. Second, the memory representation shrinks dramatically because each stored entry can be encoded with far fewer bits, and the data movement into the compute units becomes lighter. In system terms, that reduces the pressure on memory bandwidth and on on-chip temporary storage during inference and training.

# Scaling by mean absolute value and clipped rounding

Constraining stored matrix entries to three states requires a deterministic procedure to map floating-point numbers onto the discrete set during training. The design in this variant uses a per-matrix scaling followed by clipped rounding. Let $W$ denote a dense stored matrix of learned real values. A per-matrix scale is computed from the mean magnitude of its entries and then used to normalize entries before mapping to the set of three choices.

Two key formulas capture this mapping. First, the scale factor is the average absolute magnitude across the matrix entries:
\[ \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} \lvert W_{ij}\rvert. \]
Using this scale, the normalized and clipped rounding yields the discrete matrix:
\[ \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \varepsilon}, -1, 1\right), \]
where the helper is
\[ \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\big(a,\min\big(b,\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big). \]

Two aspects are worth emphasizing. The per-matrix average magnitude $\gamma$ serves to preserve the relative amplitudes within a block while allowing the discrete mapping to focus on sign and suppression. The tiny offset $\varepsilon$ stabilizes the division when magnitudes are very small. The clipped rounding then forces each normalized entry to be rounded to the nearest of the three allowed states and ensures strict adherence to the constraint. During learning, the training algorithm uses straight-through or similar gradient-preserving approximations so that the underlying real-valued variables can continue to be updated while the forward pass uses the discrete representation.

# Why the silence state helps: selective filtering and implicit pruning

Having an explicit suppressed state in the set of stored-entry choices gives the system a simple mechanism for selective filtering. Conceptually, each stored entry not only determines sign but can also indicate that its pathway should be ignored for a given global setting of the stored matrices. This lets the representation implement a form of per-feature gating: some contributions are actively positive, some actively negative, and some are simply omitted.

From a representational viewpoint, this creates behavior reminiscent of pruning but integrated into the quantized palette itself. Instead of having to drive a real scalar exactly to zero through weight decay or thresholding, the training dynamics can push entries toward the suppressed choice during the discrete rounding step. This supports sparsified computation: many contributions vanish at the inner-product level, thereby reducing both the arithmetic needed and the chance that noisy or irrelevant signals will interfere with the useful ones.

Empirically, this selective silencing improves modeling power relative to a strictly sign-only representation because the system no longer has to use sign flips alone to represent low-magnitude or non-useful connections. The suppressed state is a first-class citizen that simplifies such representations and makes the quantized system more expressive for the same storage budget.

# Quantizing intermediate signals per token to a symmetric range; eliminating zero-point offsets

The system described quantizes the run-time intermediate signals — the quantities that flow between non-linearities and linear operators — to a small integer range for the hot compute path. Rather than mapping those signals into an unsigned range with an offset (a zero-point), the design applies a symmetric per-token scaling so that each token’s post-nonlinearity signals lie in a symmetric interval $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. Doing this per token means the scale adapts to the local magnitude of the signals for each processed chunk of input.

Formally, for each token’s vector of post-nonlinearity responses, a scalar is computed that maps the vector into the symmetric integer range. This avoids the need for asymmetric encodings and eliminates the bookkeeping that zero-point offsets would require. The consequence is a simpler kernel: integer-valued signals with symmetric ranges combine naturally with the ternary stored entries via signed accumulation, and there is no additional per-value offset correction to apply.

The per-token adaptation also reduces quantization artifacts because it aligns the dynamic range of the quantized integer domain to the actual per-token amplitudes. In practice, this step preserves signal fidelity while keeping the hot-path representation compact, and it simplifies implementation on both software kernels and system-level accelerators.

# Numeric fidelity choices: ternary entries, 8-bit intermediate signals, and higher-fidelity input table

The full design uses a mixed-fidelity map across different parts of the system. The stored matrices that dominate compute are constrained to three-valued entries, giving each matrix entry one of three possible states. The signals that flow at runtime between non-linearities and the ternary-aware operators are quantized to a compact integer format, typically using an eight-bit signed range for the hot path. Meanwhile, certain components that are small in proportion but sensitive to precision, such as the input look-up table that maps discrete symbols to vectors, are retained in a higher-fidelity numeric format.

This hybrid choice balances two competing objectives. The ternary storage yields a drastic reduction in the bits needed to represent the bulk of the system, and it enables compute reorganization around integer summation. The eight-bit run-time signals keep the intermediate precision adequate for expressing activations and for supporting sequence-level caching. The higher-fidelity input look-up ensures that the initial symbol-to-vector mapping remains precise, which is important because it serves as the seed for all subsequent computations and because it occupies a small fraction of the total memory footprint.

From a training and deployment perspective, the separation also helps: the majority of parameter-like storage can be kept compact, reducing data movement costs, while the small set of high-fidelity tables preserve representational richness where it matters most.

# Open-source-aligned component choices: scale-based norm, gated-split nonlinearity, rotary position codes, and no additive offsets; integration with common inference toolchains

To maximize compatibility with widely used toolchains and to retain stable training dynamics under the ternary constraints, the design adopts a set of component choices commonly found in contemporary open designs. First, a scale-only normalization based on root-mean-square statistics is used before certain sub-blocks; this provides stable magnitude control without introducing extra trainable additive offsets. Second, the nonlinearity used inside the feed-forward blocks is a gated split form that multiplies a linear path by a smooth gating function; this yields a richer per-unit response than a simple pointwise nonlinearity and pairs well with quantized signals. Third, position is encoded via a rotary-style scheme, which mixes position information multiplicatively into the per-token vectors. Finally, additive offset terms commonly referred to as biases are omitted throughout, simplifying the quantized mapping and reducing the number of small parameters that would be awkward to quantize.

These choices make the ternary-aware system straightforward to plug into popular open-source inference and training toolchains with minimal integration work. The implementation pattern — replace dense linear operators by the ternary-aware operator, keep the scale-only norm and gated nonlinearity in place, and preserve the rotary position coding — aligns with the processing graph expected by many runtime engines, which eases adoption and experimentation.

Closing remarks

The three-valued stored-entry approach plus per-token symmetric quantization for run-time signals is a pragmatic design that captures much of the representational power of higher-fidelity systems while enabling a fundamentally different compute and data-movement profile. The addition of the explicit suppressed state provides a simple, effective mechanism for selective filtering that improves expressivity beyond a pure sign-only palette. Taken together with the component choices that favor stable training and integration, this design enables practical training from scratch and efficient inference kernels that emphasize integer summation and per-block scaling rather than ubiquitous per-element real-valued scaling.

# From-scratch fitting versus post-hoc low-precision conversion

When building a learner that stores its internal coefficients using a very compact, ternary representation, there are two distinct approaches one can take. One is to first fit a conventional, high-precision instantiation on a large text corpus and then convert its stored coefficients after the fact into the compact form. The other is to design the learner so that it is created and optimized directly while its coefficients are constrained to the compact set. The paper behind this chapter follows the latter path and this section explains why that choice matters.

Converting a pretrained, high-precision instantiation after fitting often seems attractive because the high-precision run is well understood and conversion tools exist that attempt to preserve behavior. However, that post-hoc pathway tends to be suboptimal because the original fitting procedure had no incentive to produce coefficients that are robust to extreme discretization. Quantization errors introduced at conversion can change internal signal statistics in ways that the original optimizer never corrected, and simple heuristics or per-block rescaling can only partially remedy those shifts.

Fitting directly under the compact constraint forces the entire optimization trajectory to adapt to the representational limitations. The optimization dynamics, the scaling of internal signals, and the choices of nonlinear functions all co-adapt to the ternary representation. The result can be a system that matches the performance of a standard high-precision counterpart while enjoying the runtime and storage benefits of the compact representation. The paper’s reported experimental findings demonstrate that, when this fitting-from-scratch route is used together with several stability-minded design choices, parity with a conventional half-floating baseline is achievable for mid-sized and larger systems.

# Stable ingredients: normalization modules, nonlinear gating, and removal of additive offsets

Design choices that influence numerical stability during the fit phase are critical when the stored coefficients are severely restricted. Three practical ingredients used in the reported system make the fitting process robust.

First, a form of per-unit magnitude normalization is applied before many of the nonlinear transforms. This normalization computes a scale from the vector of pre-nonlinearity responses and rescales so that the downstream nonlinearity sees stable dynamic range across different data examples. Using this kind of normalization reduces the tendency for a few large coefficients to dominate internal signals and reduces sensitivity to initialization. The normalization used in the work is of a type that depends only on the root-mean-square magnitude of the vector, which tends to be simpler and more stable under low-precision constraints than schemes that require learned offset terms.

Second, the learner uses a gated two-part nonlinear transform in its intermediate modules. This form combines a simple identity-ish pathway with a gated nonlinearity that multiplies two components; this pattern has been found to provide better representational efficiency and gradient flow than single-branch nonlinearities for the compact coefficient regime. The gate helps the system implement selective routing of information without requiring large-magnitude stored coefficients.

Third, additive offset terms that are often present in standard designs are removed throughout the design. These offsets can interact poorly with hard discretization of coefficients and with the mean-based rescaling used for quantization. By omitting them, the system keeps its forward math structurally symmetric around zero, which simplifies the quantization strategy and reduces the number of sign and threshold artifacts that the optimizer must manage.

Together, these three choices—root-mean-square based normalization, gated nonlinearity blocks, and removal of additive offsets—form a stable core that allows the optimizer to find good solutions while the coefficients are constrained to the ternary set.

# Calibrating node-output scales and the per-position symmetric scaling rationale

A persistent challenge when coefficients are constrained into a small discrete set is keeping the dynamic range of intermediate signals compatible with that constraint. If pre-nonlinearity outputs are too large, quantization will saturate them; if they are too small, rounding to discrete levels will produce vanishing gradients or dead pathways.

The paper addresses this by introducing a per-channel scale for coefficient quantization that is computed from the mean magnitude of the coefficient block. Concretely, for a block of stored coefficients represented as a matrix-like array \(C\), the block scale \(\gamma\) is set proportional to the mean of the magnitudes of the entries in that block:
\[
\gamma \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i,j} \lvert C_{i,j}\rvert}{N},
\]
where \(N\) denotes the number of entries in the block. This scale is used to normalize the block before rounding to the ternary set, so that the rounded values represent directions and relative sparsity rather than raw amplitude.

For the node outputs (the signals that flow between modules), the design uses a per-input-position symmetric scaling to align these signals with the integer representation used in the compute kernels. Rather than shifting the range to a positive-only interval, the system rescales node outputs at each input position to a symmetric interval around zero. The symmetry removes the need for a separate zero-point offset and simplifies both software and hardware implementation of the low-precision arithmetic. From a fitting perspective, per-position scaling makes the mapping from the high-dimensional continuous internal signal to the compact representation locally adaptive: each input position gets its own scale that reflects the statistics of that example, which reduces clipping and information loss.

This per-position rescaling is conceptually simple but effective: it reduces the mismatch between the natural dynamic range of the learner’s signals and the discrete set the hardware or kernel expects, and it eliminates systematic biases that would otherwise need to be corrected by the optimizer.

# Handling gradients through hard discretizers: conceptual overview

A core problem when optimizing systems whose forward pass includes hard discretization (rounding) is that the discretizer is not differentiable in the mathematical sense. Yet optimizers rely on gradient information. The practical approach taken here is conceptual and pragmatically motivated: replace the true backward behavior of the hard discretizer with a surrogate that provides useful learning signals.

One standard surrogate is to propagate the upstream gradient through the rounding step as if that step were the identity function. This delivers nonzero gradient to the pre-quantized values and thus to the underlying storage that governs them. In practice one adjusts this straight-through idea with small damping factors or with clipped gradients so that updates do not push the underlying continuous estimate wildly beyond the quantizer’s representable set. Another complementary idea is to use soft relaxations early in the fit run—so the forward pass is a smooth approximation of the hard mapping—and anneal toward the hard mapping as fitting progresses. This soft-to-hard schedule helps the optimizer find a basin in parameter space that is compatible with the final discrete representation.

The paper’s approach also leverages the per-block scale factor described earlier. Because the quantizer normalizes by a magnitude that depends on the block, the surrogate gradient must either flow through that scale or treat it as fixed during a micro-step. The practical choice that balances stability and simplicity is to compute the scale from the current stored coefficients but to limit how rapidly that scale can change, preventing gradient-based oscillations between scaling and coefficient signs.

Finally, gradient noise due to extreme discretization is mitigated by using robust optimizer settings (discussed below) and careful initialization that places the continuous pre-quantized coefficients in ranges that the discretizer can represent meaningfully from the start.

# What stays high precision during the fit process: best practices

Even when the vast majority of stored coefficients are constrained to a ternary set for efficiency, a few things are best kept in a richer numeric format during the fit run. The central pragmatic choices are the following.

First, the optimizer’s internal accumulators—moment estimates, squared-moment accumulators, and momentum buffers—are kept in a higher-precision representation. These structures act as long-lived filters on gradient statistics and benefit from having extra dynamic range and granularity. Keeping them in higher precision stabilizes the step sizes applied to the discretized stored coefficients and reduces the risk of destructive weight oscillation.

Second, a master continuous copy of the stored coefficient blocks is retained in higher precision. The optimizer updates this master copy and the quantized representation used in the forward pass is derived from it on the fly. This separation avoids compounding rounding artifacts in the optimizer’s state estimates and lets the optimizer perform smooth adjustments even though the forward representation is coarse.

Third, certain special tables are kept in higher precision. In particular, the initial mapping from discrete input symbols to vector representations—the lookup table used at the very input of the system—remains in a richer format during fitting because it consumes a relatively small fraction of overall stored volume yet it plays an outsized role in downstream signal quality. Keeping it in higher precision avoids a large initial information bottleneck.

Fourth, some small but numerically sensitive scaling factors and normalization constants are also maintained with extra resolution. These include the root-mean-square denominators used by the normalization modules and any global learnable scale multipliers. Representing them finely avoids quantization-induced jitter in the normalization behavior.

These practices preserve most of the runtime and memory advantages of the compact representation while recognizing that certain control and accumulation structures benefit greatly from being more finely represented.

# Fair comparisons: matching system scale and fit-data exposure to standard baselines

When claiming parity with a conventional system that uses a higher-precision internal format, it is crucial to ensure that the comparison is fair. Two major axes of fairness are scale of the system and the amount of text exposure during the fit run.

Matching scale here means ensuring that the number of stored coefficients under optimization and the dimensionalities of internal transforms are comparable between the compact-coefficient system and the conventional half-floating referent. Because the compact representation uses less per-coefficient storage, it can be tempting to pack more coefficients into the same memory footprint and claim superior efficiency. For an apples-to-apples comparison, the reported results keep the structural counts of coefficient entries similar and measure runtime and storage differences arising only from the change in per-entry representation and the optimized kernels used in inference.

Matching fit-data exposure means the two systems see a comparable amount of textual material during their fitting runs. The paper uses a large public text corpus and exposes both systems to the same sequences in the same order for the same number of passes. This controls for the well-known dependence of final generalization on the volume of text seen during fitting. By holding these axes constant, the comparison isolates the impact of the compact coefficient representation and the design choices made to support it.

# Scaling behavior: why parity appears from mid-sized systems upward

A striking empirical observation in the reported results is that the compact-coefficient learner attains comparable generalization performance to the conventional half-floating referent beginning at a mid-size regime and onward as scale increases. There are a few conceptual reasons for this scaling behavior.

Smaller systems tend to be capacity-limited in the sense that the representational richness demanded by many tasks already exhausts their degrees of freedom. In that regime, reducing per-coefficient precision can remove critical subtlety from the representation and thus harm performance. In contrast, as systems grow in dimensionality and representational capacity, each individual coefficient typically contributes less to expressivity; the collective behavior of many coefficients matters more than the precise value of any single coefficient. In that regime, coarse quantization can be compensated by the greater redundancy and overparameterization, allowing the compact-coefficient learner to match the higher-precision referent.

Moreover, several of the stability techniques described earlier—per-block mean-magnitude scaling, per-position symmetric rescaling, normalization modules, and the gated nonlinear pathways—become more effective at larger scale because statistical averaging smooths idiosyncratic signals. The optimization landscape in large, overparameterized learners tends to be flatter and to contain many wide minima; the discretization constraints are less likely to knock the system out of a basin that generalizes well when the basin is broad.

Finally, computational efficiency and kernel optimization for the compact representation tend to improve with larger internal sizes. The fraction of runtime devoted to the transforms that can be implemented with small-integer arithmetic grows with system scale, so the practical benefits of the compact approach compound as scale increases. The reported experiments confirm that at mid and larger scales these effects combine to deliver parity in generalization quality while providing substantial runtime and storage advantages.

# Practical checklist for reproducing from-scratch fitting of the compact-coefficient system

To make the conceptual recommendations above actionable, here is a condensed checklist that captures the essential elements used in the reported fits.

- Design the learner with root-mean-square based normalization modules preceding major nonlinear transforms, and prefer gated two-branch nonlinearities for intermediate blocks.
- Remove additive offset terms from the transforms to preserve zero-centered symmetry that simplifies quantization.
- Quantize coefficient blocks by normalizing them with a mean-magnitude derived scale, then round to the ternary set; for node outputs, apply per-position symmetric rescaling to align with the integer compute kernel’s range.
- During gradient propagation through the hard discretizer, use straight-through style surrogates or soft relaxations with a schedule toward the hard mapping, and prevent rapid oscillation of block scales by limiting their per-step change.
- Keep optimizer accumulators, a master continuous copy of coefficient blocks, and the initial input symbol-to-vector table in a richer numeric format to preserve stable optimization dynamics.
- For fair comparisons, match structural counts of coefficients and exposure to the same corpus for the same number of passes.
- Expect that parity with a standard half-floating referent will be easier to attain once the system reaches a mid-size regime, due to redundancy and statistical smoothing.

Following these conceptual and practical guidelines reproduces the approach taken in the work behind this chapter and gives a principled path toward fitting compact-coefficient learners from scratch while preserving generalization quality and delivering practical efficiency gains.

Evaluation Protocols and Measurement Methodology

# Pretraining corpus — RedPajama, initial exposure of hundred billion subword pieces
The comparison foundation in the paper is a controlled initial learning pass performed on the public RedPajama text corpus. The authors held the language-exposure budget fixed: every system under comparison was presented with the same sequence of textual material drawn from that collection for the same total amount of content, expressed in the paper as roughly one hundred billion subword pieces. Keeping this exposure identical is central to isolating the effects of representational choices and execution pathways from differences in the learning corpus.

Practically, this means the learned systems began from random initialization and were optimized over the same set of examples sampled from RedPajama until the agreed exposure quota was exhausted. Hyperparameters that influence optimization dynamics (learning rate schedule, batch-like grouping policies, optimizer choice, and so on) were held consistent between the reference sixteenspaced floating-point learner and the ternary learner described in the report. The goal of that controlled setup is simple: any differences in predictive quality or run-time resource use should arise from the representational and execution changes rather than from differing training inputs or exposure.

# Predictive-score evaluation on held-out corpora: WikiText-2 and C4 validation
To quantify how well a learned system estimates the next element in a sequence, the authors report a held-out predictive score computed on standard validation splits drawn from two commonly used public corpora: WikiText-2 and C4. The measure used is the familiar next-element cross-entropy converted into its standard exponential representation (the conventional scalar that researchers quote when discussing generative predictive performance). Lower values of this quantity indicate better match to the held-out text distribution; the paper uses this metric to track convergence and to compare final learned systems.

Evaluation on these validation splits is performed by running the learned system in generation mode over held-out sequences and accumulating the per-element predictive loss. All systems evaluated in the paper used the same tokenization and the same validation splits so that the reported predictive scores are directly comparable across the variants. These held-out scores serve as the primary numeric gauge of how close the ternary representation comes to the reference floating-point representation in terms of raw sequence modeling.

# Zero-shot benchmarks and the evaluation wrapper: ARC-E, ARC-C, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ
Beyond next-element predictive performance, the paper assesses downstream competence via a suite of zero-shot tasks that probe reasoning, common-sense, and reading-comprehension abilities. The chosen tasks are the common public benchmarks ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ. For each benchmark the authors run each learned system in zero-shot mode: prompts are constructed from the benchmark examples with no additional parameter updates or few-shot exemplars, and the system’s plain output is interpreted according to the benchmark’s scoring rules.

To ensure repeatability and alignment with prior work, the authors adopt a standard public evaluation wrapper used widely in the community. This wrapper provides canonical prompt templates and scoring conventions for turning open-ended or multiple-choice outputs into the benchmark accuracy metrics reported in the paper. Because the wrapper’s protocol is common knowledge, following it minimizes accidental discrepancies in prompt formatting, handling of special tokens, or tie-breaking rules. The paper reports zero-shot accuracies under that shared pipeline and then compares those accuracies across the floating-point reference and the ternary variant at several scales.

# Run-time latency and resident storage: measurements via a high-performance inference codebase
Run-time behavior was measured using a high-performance inference codebase optimized for sequence generation on modern accelerators. The principal timing metric reported is the elapsed time needed to produce a single output element during autoregressive generation; this “time per produced element” is the most relevant real-world quantity for interactive use. Measurements were taken while the system was producing sequences, so they capture the end-to-end cost of the core compute kernels and the movement of stored coefficients between off-chip memory and on-device resident storage.

Resident storage usage (the amount of on-device memory required to hold the learned coefficients and the run-time working set) was recorded alongside latency. The authors compared the reference floating-point configuration to the ternary configuration using identical sequence lengths and execution settings so that the measured differences reflect representational and kernel-level differences rather than differing runtime conditions.

# Integration of a low-bit Ladder routine for ternary-run-time acceleration
To make the ternary representation practical at run time, the authors integrated a low-precision computational routine developed by the Ladder group. This routine replaces the dense linear compute path with an implementation tailored to small discrete codebooks. In the paper, the ternary solution uses that Ladder routine during run-time generation to perform the dense updates more efficiently than a generic floating-point path.

From a systems perspective, that routine reduces the amount of high-precision arithmetic needed at run time and allows the accelerator’s compute and memory bandwidth to be used more effectively. The paper reports measurements using the Ladder routine in the ternary configuration so that reported latency and resident storage reflect the end-to-end performance of the practical low-precision execution stack rather than an idealized implementation.

# Arithmetic-energy estimation on seven-nanometer process technology
Energy consumption estimates in the report focus on the arithmetic work of the dense linear layers, since those layers dominate both cycle cost and energy in large sequence learners. The authors use an established energy model from prior literature and apply it to a seven-nanometer process technology point, which is representative of contemporary high-density silicon.

Under that model, the ternary-run-time largely performs integer-based accumulation and small-range arithmetic, while the floating-point reference performs both floating-point adds and floating-point product steps. Because the ternary-run-time replaces many floating-point multiplications with integer accumulation and simplified decision logic, the arithmetic-energy budget for the dominant dense computation is reported to be dramatically smaller for the ternary path. The paper cites an arithmetic-energy saving on the dominant dense compute of over seventy-fold at seven-nanometer, illustrating that representational choices can yield very large energy dividends when the dominant kernels are converted to simpler integer-style work.

It is important to note that these energy figures focus on arithmetic; total wall-power during run-time also includes memory movement, control, and peripheral components. The paper presents arithmetic-focused estimates to highlight the savings associated with changing the core compute characteristics.

# Run-time processing-rate tests with long sequences and staged partitioning across two large accelerators
To characterize effective processing rate under heavy load, the authors ran experiments with sequence length set to five hundred twelve items and with two large eighty-gigabyte accelerators used jointly via staged partitioning so that the largest reference configuration could execute. Rather than the community term for grouped processing, the authors increased the number of concurrent inputs until the accelerator memory limit was reached; this provides an empirical ceiling on how many simultaneous requests the system can support.

Under these conditions the ternary-run-time supported a substantially higher number of concurrent inputs before exhausting device memory, and the emergent processing rate (concurrent inputs multiplied by produced elements per second) rose accordingly. In the reported experiments the ternary configuration supported roughly eleven-fold more concurrent inputs and realized nearly a nine-fold higher effective processing rate under the multi-accelerator, five-hundred-twelve-length scenario. These numbers illustrate how reductions in the resident stored-coefficient footprint and in on-device working-set size can translate directly into much higher system-level utilization and better amortization of fixed costs when serving many requests.

# Notes on repeatability and fair comparison
Throughout the measurements, attention to fairness was a guiding principle. The learned systems compared were exposed to the same corpus and the same exposure budget during the initial learning pass; evaluation used the same tokenization and validation splits for held-out predictive scoring; zero-shot assessments used a common public wrapper to remove differences caused by prompt formatting; and run-time comparisons used the same execution framework and sequence lengths where possible, with the ternary run-time leveraging an optimized low-precision routine provided by Ladder. Together, these choices produce comparisons that highlight the trade-offs introduced by switching representation and run-time compute pathways while keeping other variables fixed.

When reading the numeric reports in the paper, it is helpful to remember which aspects are measured directly (run-time latency, resident storage, benchmark accuracies) and which are estimated (arithmetic-energy based on technology models). The combination of measured and estimated quantities gives a holistic view of how representational decisions affect predictive competence and run-time resource usage.

Results: Accuracy, Efficiency, and the New Scaling Law

# Accuracy parity and where it appears

One of the clearest empirical outcomes reported in the study is that the low-width discrete-value variant, which I will refer to as the "b‑one‑point‑five‑eight" system, reaches the same predictive quality as the conventional higher-precision floating format when the system size grows past the small end of the design space. In practical experiments where the two kinds of systems were exposed to the same corpus and the same amount of sequence exposure, the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instance matched the baseline on held-out predictive loss and on direct-transfer task performance once the learner contained a few billion learned entries. Above that operating point the larger b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instances begin to outperform a smaller baseline trained in the traditional floating format: a slightly larger b‑one‑point‑five‑eight configuration gives better direct-task accuracy than a smaller floating‑format reference while also requiring less working storage and offering faster response.

This parity and subsequent superiority are not restricted to a single evaluation. The study reports both general predictive metrics on standard validation corpora and zero‑task transfer accuracy across a suite of established benchmarks. The gap between the two approaches closes as scale grows; after that, the low‑width variant keeps improving and surpasses the smaller floating reference on the downstream measures used. Intuitively, this shows that the representational regularities that matter for language tasks can be captured with the discrete three‑valued entries used by the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight design once enough of those entries are available. The experimental protocol deliberately held data exposure and other training choices constant, so the difference is attributable to the internal numeric format and how it interacts with capacity rather than to differences in data or training schedule.

# Response time and working storage reductions; scale helps more

A central systems result is that the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight approach substantially reduces both response time and the on‑device working storage footprint compared with the conventional floating approach, and that these advantages grow with learner scale. Measurements carried out with an optimized runtime showed that for small design points the low‑width variant already runs noticeably faster and uses notably less working storage. As the number of learned entries increases, the fraction of computation spent on the dense linear-like transforms grows in the standard floating design, so the relative benefit of moving those transforms to the low‑width execution paradigm becomes larger. At very large scale the study reports roughly multi‑fold speedups in response time relative to the floating reference, and corresponding multi‑fold shrinkage in the dynamic storage required during inference.

Two practical system effects produce this behavior. First, the compute pattern for the low‑width representation replaces many floating add‑and‑floating multiply steps with narrow integer additions and simple selection logic; this reduces the per‑operation time on current accelerators and on CPU class processors. Second, the reduced bit‑width means each learned entry takes fewer bytes in transit from off‑chip persistent memory into on‑chip working storage, so bandwidth pressures and the time spent moving learned entries are lower. Together these lead to savings that are proportionally larger when the transforms dominate the runtime, which happens at larger scale.

# Electrical draw for core table-style combines drops dramatically

Because most of the runtime cost in these learners comes from repeated combines of large row/column tables with batched internal signals, the electrical draw tied to those arithmetic sequences is a central concern. The study estimates the electrical draw required for those core combines using an accepted chip-level energy model for modern fabrication. Replacing high‑width floating add/multiply pairs with narrow integer additions and simple gating logic yields a dramatic reduction in arithmetic electrical draw for the core combine operations: on the target fabrication node the arithmetic portion of those combines is estimated to draw on the order of tens of times less power than the floating baseline.

Put differently, when the dominant arithmetic work is performed with narrow integer additions rather than wide floating add/multiply pairs, the per‑operation electrical demand plunges, and because those operations are so frequent in large systems, the aggregate effect is large. The paper’s calculations show that the arithmetic portion of combine work is reduced by roughly seventyfold on the referenced process node; when combined with reductions in data movement the end‑to‑end electrical requirement for inference falls substantially as scale grows.

# Processing‑rate gains and larger feasible batch

The change in numeric format also enables much larger feasible batches during inference on the same hardware, which translates into a higher end‑to‑end processing rate. In throughput-style experiments run on a pair of large memory accelerators, the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight variant could accommodate many times the batch that the floating design allowed before exhausting device resources. Because each forward pass moved and operated on much less data, the runtime could accept larger parallelism across requests, leading to near‑order‑of‑magnitude increases in examples processed per unit time.

This effect is especially relevant for deployment settings where throughput matters more than latency per individual request: services that batch many requests or that perform large minibatch evaluations can take full advantage of the reduced working storage to process substantially more examples in parallel. The study documents that at the largest evaluated scale the low‑width approach supports several times the batch capacity and delivers a several‑times higher processing rate on the same hardware pairing, demonstrating a meaningful practical advantage for high‑volume inference.

# A new cost–performance map: equivalences across sizes

One of the most consequential ways to present the experimental results is to translate system cost reductions into equivalences between sizes. Because the low‑width approach reduces response time, working storage, and electrical draw, an instance of the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight family at some larger count of learned entries can match or beat the operational cost of a much smaller floating reference while delivering equal or better predictive quality. The study frames this as a new scaling law: at fixed resource budgets the low‑width family can afford substantially more learned entries than the floating baseline, which shifts the practical Pareto frontier.

Concretely, the paper points out that a mid‑scale b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instance with about thirteen billion learned entries is more efficient in response time, working storage, and electrical draw than a small full‑precision reference with only a few billion learned entries. A larger b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instance with about thirty billion learned entries becomes more efficient than a modestly larger full‑precision reference, and a very large b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instance with about seventy billion learned entries beats a full‑precision system that itself has on the order of a dozen billion learned entries. These equivalences illustrate how reduced numeric width rebalances the trade between quantity of learned entries and system cost.

# Better generalization after extended exposure

The study also examines generalization when the low‑width family sees a much larger quantity of sequence exposure following the same data recipe used by a leading open‑source small reference. When the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight learner was fit on an extended corpus exposure regime, it matched or exceeded the reference across held‑out direct‑transfer tasks drawn from a representative benchmark suite. In particular, on the collection of reasoning, common‑sense, and comprehension tasks used in the comparison, the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight instance achieved higher direct‑transfer accuracy than the open‑source small reference trained with the same exposure pipeline.

This shows that the low‑width numeric format does not hinder large‑scale generalization; in fact, when the learner receives sufficient exposure the discrete three‑valued entries plus the rest of the system design capture generalization patterns at least as well as the floating reference. The implication is that the reduced numeric representation can be married with large exposure schedules to produce systems that are both computationally cheaper in inference and strong in transfer.

# Pareto frontier perspective and deployment consequences

Viewed through the lens of Pareto efficiency, the b‑one‑point‑five‑eight family pushes the frontier outward: for a wide range of deployment budgets it either reduces operational cost at fixed predictive ability or improves predictive ability at fixed cost. That shift has several practical consequences. First, for producers of service that must run many concurrent requests, the ability to deliver the same task accuracy with lower working storage and lower electrical draw can reduce infrastructure expenses and enable larger batch throughput. Second, for on‑device or edge deployment, the smaller runtime footprint and lower electrical demand make it feasible to run much larger learners on constrained hardware, opening new application opportunities. Third, the improved tradeoffs make certain system designs—such as conditional routing or specialist experts—more attractive, because the storage and transfer overhead that previously made those designs burdensome is reduced.

Finally, the new scaling equivalences call for rethinking hardware and software co‑design. Hardware tailored to exploit narrow integer adds and efficient sparse gating could amplify the advantages reported here, and software runtimes that expose low‑width kernels and compact storage formats will be central to realizing the practical benefits at scale. The study makes clear that changing the numeric representation is not merely an implementation detail but a lever that shifts the entire cost–performance landscape for large language systems.

Systems and Kernel Engineering for one-point-five-eight low-precision language systems

# Packing ternary stored values and mapping to two-symbol storage formats

A central storage design choice in the approach described in the paper is to constrain every learned coefficient to one of three possible states: negative unit, zero, or positive unit. This ternary set provides a strong sparsity/selection signal while keeping per-entry information very small. In practice, this sparse three-state set is stored compactly using a two-symbol encoding per entry: two binary positions are used to represent the three valid states, with the extra combination left unused or reserved for system bookkeeping. The result is a dramatic reduction in the number of bits required to represent the full set of learned coefficients compared with common high-resolution formats.

On the systems side, an important implication is how these two-symbol representations are laid out in off-chip and on-chip storage so that streaming transfers and local reads are friendly to wide bus widths and vector instructions. A common technique is to pack many entries into a single word sized to the accelerator’s natural lane width, then align these words for burst reads. Packing density trades off with the complexity of the decode step inside tight inner loops: a denser packing reduces traffic but requires a slightly more complex unpack path on load. Because the ternary alphabet includes an explicit zero state, hardware or software unpackers can include a fast conditional skip for zero elements, enabling sparsity-aware processing without a costly meta-table.

The paper’s quantization procedure supports deterministic placement of coefficients into the ternary set. Let $W$ denote a slice of learned coefficients and define a scaling factor
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Entries are mapped by a clipped rounding step:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},-1,1\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
Using this normalized, per-slice scaling allows hardware-friendly, uniform packing patterns across slices while making the stored symbols interpretable at runtime without per-element side information.

# Integer-only accumulation for dense array products: replacing scaled copies and leveraging hardware SIMD

A core claim in the paper is that the heavy inner loop of the trained networks can be recast so that the dominant work is integer-based accumulation rather than floating-point scaled products. Conceptually, when coefficients are restricted to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$, multiplying an input value by a stored coefficient reduces to either taking the input unchanged, taking its negation, or skipping it. Accumulation over many such contributions therefore becomes a sequence of conditional increments and decrements of an accumulator rather than general-purpose multiplication followed by summation.

From a kernel-engineering point of view, this opens several opportunities. First, the processing can be organized as gather-and-add operations over packed coefficient lanes: for a given packed word, decode the ternary symbols in parallel, compute sign masks, and then use those masks to selectively add or subtract the corresponding input lanes into accumulators. This pattern maps well to single-instruction multiple-data hardware where masked moves and predicated adds are cheap. Second, by keeping the per-entry representation compact and by arranging input lanes to align with the packed coefficient words, one can amortize the decode cost across many adds and reduce off-chip transfer volume.

Because the paper uses a low-resolution format for the signals entering the inner loop (the paper describes an eight-level quantized form for stage outputs), those inputs can be represented in small integer containers and the accumulation can be performed in wider integer accumulators. After accumulation, a single rescaling step (often implemented as a narrow affine map plus a small conversion) restores the numeric range needed for the next processing stage. The upshot is that most of the heavy work becomes integer increments and decrements, which are much cheaper in power and time on many processors than general floating-point multiply-accumulate.

# Kernel choices and trade-offs: Ladder two-symbol kernel in practice

The paper reports practical use of an existing two-symbol kernel, called Ladder, for the hot inner product operations. Kernels of this family vary along several axes: how they pack symbols, how they decode sign/zero masks, whether they use bit-sliced or lane-sliced inner loops, and how they schedule transfers between off-chip and scratchpad storage.

A ladder-style kernel generally packs many ternary entries into contiguous words, decodes these to produce sign masks for a block of input lanes, and then issues a tight loop of masked integer accumulations. The benefits are low traffic (due to packing), few arithmetic primitives (no general multiplies), and predictable memory access. The trade-offs are the decode overhead and the interaction with the processor’s vector width: if decode cannot be done in a few cycles or cannot be expressed as a small number of vector instructions, the kernel can be slower than a straightforward low-resolution multiply-accumulate on wider lanes.

In practice, the paper integrates a ladder-style kernel into the chosen inference runtime to measure latency and throughput gains. System engineers should measure the break-even point where the decode cost is offset by the reduced arithmetic and transfer cost; that point shifts with the accelerator generation, the vector width, and the latency of the on-chip scratch storage. For very wide vector units, designs that favor bit-sliced operations tend to be more efficient; for modest vector widths, lane-sliced masked-add loops can be simpler and faster. The Ladder kernel used in the experiments represents a practical compromise tuned for current hardware.

# Storage and signal layout for transfer capacity efficiency; off-chip↔on-chip traffic reduction

A recurrent theme in the paper is that moving fewer bits across the off-chip link is among the most important levers to reduce latency and energy. To reason about this, think of the total cost as the product of the number of words moved times the per-word transfer expense. Packing ternary entries into dense words reduces the word count, and arranging them into burst-friendly alignments reduces the number of transfer commands.

On-chip fast storage (the scratch area local to the compute fabric) is precious. The approach in the paper stores the heavily used coefficient slices in their packed, two-symbol form and streams them into on-chip buffers in chunks that match the kernel’s working set. Meanwhile, the signals flowing between processing stages are represented at a lower resolution than traditional floating formats: the paper uses an eight-level quantized format for these inter-stage signals, and performs per-position scaling to eliminate zero-offset complications. This per-position scaling means that each sequence position carries its own scale factor, and the packed signal words can be transferred and read without additional per-element side channels.

Embeddings (the initial lookup maps) were left at higher resolution in the reported experiments, so their storage proportion reduces as system size grows. That design choice affects how much on-chip storage is consumed by the dense coefficient store versus the signal buffers. For system engineers, a useful pattern is to reserve an on-chip scratch region for decoded coefficient blocks and another region for incoming signal frames; decoding should stream one block at a time into the decode scratch to maximally reuse it across many input lanes before it is evicted.

# Key-value cache numeric format and long-sequence implications

Large sequence windows are expensive mainly because of the buffers needed to keep past stage outputs for reuse. The paper highlights that moving from a higher-resolution format to an eight-level representation for these cached outputs can effectively double the feasible window length under the same on-chip resource budget. The explicit presence of a zero state in the stored coefficient set makes it simpler to combine cached contributions in-place without introducing dynamic scale errors.

In practice, adopting low-resolution cached representations requires care in two areas. First, the per-position scale factor approach adopted for stage outputs needs to be extended to cached frames so that reads from the cache produce values in a predictable numeric range for the ladder-style inner loops. Second, because cached frames are read often, their layout in off-chip storage should enable wide, burst transfers into on-chip buffers; dense packing and per-chunk scale headers are a good compromise. The paper suggests that further lossless compression of cached frames is possible, but leaves detailed exploration to future work.

# Integration with inference stacks and lightweight runtimes; gaps and optimization opportunities

The reported experiments use an industry-optimized runtime as the measurement baseline and integrate the ladder-style kernel into that stack to obtain latency and throughput numbers. From an integration viewpoint, there are a few recurring gaps and opportunities.

First, the runtime needs hooks for the packed coefficient format: load-time unpackers, per-slice scale tables, and fallback paths for slices that require special handling. The ternary quantization by slice (the scaling factor $\gamma$ is computed per slice in training) makes these hooks predictable: a compact table of per-slice scales and a straightforward packed-coefficient blob are sufficient to bootstrap execution.

Second, scheduler logic in the runtime benefits from being aware of the decode-to-accumulate cost model. Classic runtimes treat dense numerical kernels as uniform-time blocks; ladder-style kernels need a scheduler that groups many output positions behind a decoded coefficient block to amortize decode costs, and that overlaps transfers to the next block while current accumulations proceed.

Third, small runtimes or embeddable engines that favor compact binary size need micro-kernels that implement the decode and masked increment loops in very few vector instructions. This is where hand-tuned assembly or compiler intrinsics really help. The paper’s claim that the system can be integrated into popular compact engines “with minimal effort” stems from the fact that the higher-level network remains unchanged: only the linear maps are replaced with packed-kernel variants and the stage-output format is changed to the eight-level per-position scaling scheme.

# Profiling and debugging low-resolution inference

Working with packed ternary coefficients and low-resolution signals introduces a few common debugging and profiling pitfalls that the paper’s methodology helps expose.

Numerical scaling issues are frequent: if the per-slice scale factors are incorrect or the per-position scaling for signals is mishandled, accumulators can saturate or underflow in ways that are subtle at run time. Therefore, instrument the runtime to log per-chunk scale headers and checksum decoded coefficient blocks in the warm-up phase. The RoundClip-based quantization used in training produces discrete, predictable coefficient patterns; on load, checksums provide a quick functional test that decode and sign masks are correct.

Performance profiling should separate three contributors: off-chip transfer time, decode time, and inner-loop accumulation time. In the paper’s experiments, use of an optimized ladder kernel and an inference stack that supports packed loads showed large gains, which means that often the performance bottleneck shifts during optimization. Tools that can extract per-kernel cycle breakdowns and per-transfer occupancy are especially useful.

Finally, energy estimates in the paper are made by focusing on the dominant dense-array product operations. For practical system work, map kernel counts to a simple energy model (per-word transfer cost plus per-accumulate integer cost) and validate measured power traces on the target hardware. This both confirms the expected gains from reduced transfer counts and guides further kernel-level pruning.

# Concluding practical checklist

To implement the approach described in the paper on a real system, follow this practical sequence: adopt the slice-wise quantization rule and store per-slice scale headers; pack ternary symbols into wide words aligned to the accelerator’s bus; implement a ladder-style decode that produces sign and zero masks in vector form; perform integer masked accumulations into wide accumulators and apply a final rescale; represent inter-stage signals using an eight-level per-position scaled format; place decoded blocks into on-chip scratch to maximize reuse; and instrument per-chunk checks and simple transfer/compute profiles to guide micro-optimizations.

These steps reproduce the core system and kernel engineering ideas in the paper and provide clear levers for tuning on different hardware generations and runtime environments.

Implications and Future Directions

# Gated expert ensembles with ternary coefficients: fewer devices, reduced inter-node traffic

Mixtures that route inputs to a handful of specialized expert subnets have been popular because they let a system present very large effective capacity while only activating a small portion for any given request. A long-standing bottleneck for these gated ensembles has been the cost of keeping many expert sets resident and of moving intermediate signals between chips when routing spans devices. When the stored connection coefficients for each expert are restricted to three possible values — negative, zero, positive — the storage required per expert shrinks substantially and the bandwidth needed to stream those stored values into on-chip buffers falls accordingly. The immediate consequence described in the reference work is that more experts can be placed on a single processing unit, which reduces the need to split the ensemble across many machines. Fewer machines means less cross-node traffic for routing decisions and for the slices of intermediate representation that must be exchanged to run an expert.

Beyond raw packing density, ternary coefficients introduce a functional advantage: the explicit zero state acts as an inexpensive filter that can effectively sparsify expert behavior. Sparsity reduces the amount of data that must be transmitted after routing (because many contributions are null) and also simplifies the compute primitive for the active paths. The paper argues that these two effects combine to shrink both the device count and the communication overhead for gated ensembles, to the point where, in some deployments, the entire gated ensemble could be housed on a single chip and thus remove inter-chip routing as a bottleneck.

For researchers and engineers, this suggests a new sweet spot for ensemble design. Instead of optimizing for raw peak capacity spread across many nodes, one can redesign routing, expert sizing, and replication strategies around dense on-chip ensembles with ternary stored coefficients and sparse activation of pathways. The result is a favorable tradeoff: retained expressivity from many experts but with much lower system-level cost.

# Native long-context support via compressed key–value memory

One of the most expensive resources when running long-context language systems is the online memory that stores past “key–value” representations used to attend over earlier content. In conventional systems these stored items use relatively wide numerical formats, so the per-step footprint of the cache is large and limits how far back the system can look for a fixed on-board memory budget. The reported work shows that by switching the representation used for internal signals that populate the cache from a wider format to a narrower, small-width format, the per-step storage is cut in half; this directly enables roughly twice the context length for the same physical memory allotment.

The addition of a zero state within the stored coefficients was found to make this compression even less lossy: zeros act as explicit markers that certain signal channels carry no contribution for particular steps, which simplifies downstream processing and reduces the sensitivity of the system to reduced numeric precision. The authors further highlight that this compressed caching can be losslessly transformed with additional encoding tricks to reach yet narrower per-step storage in practice, opening a path toward supporting much longer histories without sacrificing accuracy.

For system builders, the takeaway is that native long-context capability does not have to rely solely on increasing on-chip memory; it can be obtained by jointly designing the storage format of cached representations and the coefficient alphabet used in the core networks. This creates a practical direction for systems that must support long-document understanding, conversational history, or streaming contexts in constrained environments.

# On-device and mobile deployment: CPU-friendly design, memory and energy headroom, expanded use cases

Devices at the edge and in mobile handsets typically have limited main memory, modest power budgets, and general-purpose central processors rather than large accelerator arrays. The referenced approach, which replaces rich floating-point values with a three-valued coefficient set and moves much of the dynamic internal signal representation to a narrower whole-number regime, reduces both the stored footprint and the arithmetic complexity of the dominant compute kernels. Reduced footprint and simpler per-element arithmetic mean that a larger portion of the working set can stay in fast on-chip caches of commodity processors, and that the heavy compute steps can be implemented with efficient integer-style summing pipelines that map well to standard cores.

Because these changes lower the per-request energy draw and memory requirement, they open up use cases that were previously impractical on-device: persistent local assistants that keep long histories for personalization without cloud round trips, offline summarizers that handle large documents, and private on-device retrieval-augmented systems where sensitive data need not leave the handset. The paper emphasizes that the ternary approach is particularly friendly to general-purpose processors, making it easier to deploy language functionality beyond data centers and into environments where latency, privacy, or intermittent connectivity are primary constraints.

Designers of on-device systems should therefore consider trading some of the conventional numerical richness of stored coefficients for a ternary representation and narrower internal signals. This trade opens substantial headroom for real-time, private, and offline language services on constrained hardware.

# Hardware co-design: accelerators tailored to ternary-coefficient pipelines and small-width summations

The computation paradigm enabled by ternary stored coefficients and narrow internal signals invites rethinking the low-level datapath of accelerators. When the dominant operations are packing, unpacking, and summing scaled small-width quantities — rather than costly floating-point multiply–accumulate chains — the hardware that yields the best energy and latency tradeoffs looks very different. The paper points to emerging examples of processors that minimize general-purpose multiplier arrays in favor of highly parallel small arithmetic units and deep, low-power on-chip buffers.

A specialized design for such systems would emphasize very wide, low-precision memory buses into on-chip scratchpads, simple and highly pipelined add-and-accumulate lanes that operate on packed ternary-scalar encodings, and routing fabrics optimized for moving sparse slices of representation to the active units. Removing or greatly simplifying general floating multiply units reduces transistor count, energy per operation, and heat generation, enabling denser packing of compute on silicon and higher sustained throughput for language workloads expressed in this ternary paradigm.

The authors call for hardware teams to explore these tradeoffs: co-designing the numeric representation, the software kernels, and the silicon datapath yields much larger system-level gains than optimizing any single layer in isolation. For students and researchers, this is a concrete example of how algorithmic choices at the representation level should shape microarchitectural decisions.

# Research roadmap: scaling learning recipes, narrowing internal signals, and lossless key–value compression

Although the paper demonstrates that ternary-coefficient systems can match richer representations in many regimes, it also highlights several open technical directions for the community. First, the recipe for effective optimization at very large scales — including data preparation, scheduling of learning steps, and regularization tuned to a three-valued coefficient set — deserves systematic exploration. The authors report experiments that use large corpora and show favorable generalization, but extending these findings to even larger training runs and to diverse data mixes is an important next step.

Second, pushing the internal signal representation toward ever-smaller formats without degrading behavior remains an active avenue. The work shows promising results when layer outputs are kept at a modest small-width format, but there is room for research into quantization-aware training methods, learned per-channel scalings, and calibration techniques that preserve expressivity while reducing memory and arithmetic needs further.

Third, compressing the key–value cache in a lossless or near-lossless way is a high-leverage area. The paper demonstrates that explicit zeros and per-step scalings make such compression more effective; building coding schemes that exploit the structure induced by ternary coefficients could yield further reductions in on-line memory for long-context operation. Techniques from entropy coding, block-wise transform coding adapted to discrete coefficient alphabets, and learned reversible transforms are natural candidates.

Finally, integrating these strands — large-scale optimization recipes, aggressive internal signal narrowing, and efficient cache compression — into coherent toolchains will be necessary to realize the full systems-level benefits. The referenced experiments already point the way, but broader replication, ablation studies, and public benchmarks will accelerate adoption and clarify tradeoffs.

# Environmental and economic implications

One of the most salient claims of the referenced work is an order-of-magnitude reduction in arithmetic energy for the dominant compute kernel when moving from richer floating formats to the ternary-plus-narrow-signal regime on modern process nodes. While exact multipliers depend on fabrication and microarchitecture, the reported analysis shows that replacing costly mixed multiply–accumulate pipelines with wide summation-style pipelines operating on narrow whole-number encodings can reduce arithmetic energy use by multiple tens of times for the heaviest operations. Because the heavy linear maps consume a growing share of the system cost as scale increases, the per-query energy advantage compounds with scale: larger systems realize proportionally larger savings.

These energy reductions translate directly into economic advantages. Lower per-request energy and smaller memory and interconnect needs reduce operational cost for services and lower the capital required to field comparable capability. In deployment scenarios that were previously feasible only in large datacenters, the ternary approach enables distribution of capability to smaller edge sites and to on-premise hardware, reducing latency, cloud bills, and exposure of sensitive data.

There are also environmental benefits: reduced energy per inference, fewer machines required for the same throughput, and the ability to run useful services on lower-power hardware all shrink the carbon footprint associated with serving large language workloads. The authors caution that precise savings will vary by workload and deployment, but the systemic direction is clear: rethinking numerical representations and co-designing hardware can be a high-impact lever in reducing both cost and environmental load.

Concluding thoughts

The move to three-valued stored coefficients and narrower internal representations represents not merely an optimization of existing pipelines, but an invitation to rethink stack-wide design: learning recipes, runtime systems, routing strategies for gated ensembles, and silicon datapaths. The reference study demonstrates that with careful design this approach can match richer numerical baselines in capability while offering large system-level advantages in memory use, response time, and energy. For researchers and practitioners, the next phase is to mature the optimization recipes, expand reproducible evaluations across diverse tasks and scales, and collaborate across algorithm, system, and hardware teams to translate the promising lab results into broadly deployable solutions.

Summary and Practitioner’s Checklist

# Core ideas recap — three-value coefficients, eight-level intermediate signals, per-item symmetric scaling

At the heart of the approach is a compact representation for the learned numeric tables that drive a language engine. Each entry in those tables is constrained to one of three possible numerical choices: negative one, zero, or positive one. Because each stored number can only take three values, the effective storage per number is roughly one point five eight binary units; this is the operating point that gives the technique its short name in the paper. The reduced alphabet for stored numbers dramatically shrinks the storage footprint and, more importantly, changes the arithmetic pattern during runtime: large matrix multiplications between inputs and these three-valued tables reduce to a small set of integer additions and sign flips, with very little need for expensive floating-point multiplication.

Those three-valued tables are paired with a higher-resolution representation for the intermediate signals that flow between modules. The paper uses an eight-level quantization for those intermediate signals (that is, each intermediate is represented with eight distinct levels rather than with a tiny alphabet). Those higher-resolution signals preserve expressive power in the forward path while keeping the run-time storage for transient values small.

A small but important engineering choice is to perform symmetric scaling for each processed item (each sequence position) so the quantization of intermediate signals does not require an offset. Concretely, the ternary mapping applied to a coefficient matrix W is:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)
\]

with the element-wise clipping-then-rounding operator defined as

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))
\]

and the per-matrix normalization scalar given by

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} \big|W_{ij}\big|.
\]

The scalar $\gamma$ rescales the matrix so that the ternary alphabet is assigned sensibly relative to the matrix magnitude; the small constant $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero. For the intermediate signals, the method scales each sequence position symmetrically to a range $[-Q,Q]$ before the quantized nonlinearity, which removes the need for an asymmetric offset and simplifies runtime kernels.

Intuitively, the three-valued stored numbers act both as selectors (zero drops a contribution) and as signed gates (plus or minus one passes direction). That explicit support for a zero value provides a feature-filtering capability that pure two-valued sign-only schemes lack, and that capability was a key factor in matching predictive performance at scale.

# When to choose the one-point-five-eight binary-resolution variant over standard half-precision floating or four-level alternatives

Choosing this compact representation is an engineering tradeoff between run-time resource limits and the need for predictive fidelity. The paper’s experiments show that for small engines the compact representation can lag behind conventional half-precision floating representations on held-out predictive loss and downstream task scores, but as the system size grows to mid and large regimes the compact representation reaches parity and then becomes the more efficient choice. Put differently: if your deployment target values rack-space, memory bandwidth, or power draw very highly, and you are working at mid-to-large scale, the one-point-five-eight binary-resolution design is especially attractive.

There are three concrete operational scenarios where this choice is likely appropriate. First, when the cost of moving stored tables from main memory into on-chip memory dominates run-time, shrinking the representation reduces the number of transfers and thus the end-to-end response delay and power consumed for data motion. Second, when the inference-time arithmetic energy budget is constrained (for example on lower-power accelerators or on edge CPUs), replacing many floating multiply–adds with integer add-based kernels gives large savings. Third, when you must increase the usable context length or the batch concurrency under a fixed memory envelope, the smaller stored-table footprint and smaller transient representation let you allocate more space to caches or longer contexts.

If you prioritize absolute best predictive quality at the very smallest scales, or you depend on exact behavior of a legacy floating-based runtime without access to optimized integer kernels, then staying with conventional half-precision floating or a four-level compact scheme may be preferable. Otherwise, the compact ternary-plus-eight-level regime offers a strong Pareto improvement across resource and predictive axes starting at medium size.

# Minimal design changes to migrate LLaMA-style systems

Adopting the compact representation in an existing LLaMA-style setup requires only a handful of focused changes rather than a full redesign. The most direct substitution is to replace each dense "fully connected" mapping that would normally be stored in floating form by a variant that stores the table as three-valued numbers and that implements the scaled integer-based product described above; the paper refers to such a primitive as BitLinear in its implementation notes. The remainder of the computational pipeline can largely be kept the same: the normalization module used in the LLaMA-family designs remains effective, a gated fused feed-forward nonlinearity of the SwiGLU family is retained, and rotary-style position encodings are kept for positional information. The paper also omits additive offset terms in these submodules; in practice that means avoiding small learned shifts and relying on the symmetric scaling and normalization to shape signal magnitudes.

A practical migration recipe is: keep embeddings and any input preprocessing in higher-resolution floating form, swap dense matrix storages to three-valued tables with the per-matrix normalization scalar saved as a small extra floating scalar, adopt eight-level transient signal representation, and use the same optimizer schedules and regularizers used previously (adjusting learning rates as required by the new numerical regime). Because the compact storage reduces memory traffic, you can often expand system configuration (longer context, larger batch) without changing hardware.

# Measurement do’s and don’ts for fair resource and predictive comparisons

Comparing systems fairly requires controlled measurement across several axes. First, ensure learning-phase exposure is held equal: the total number of data passes, the mix of data sources, and the optimizer schedule should match across alternatives. Measure predictive quality using the same held-out evaluation set and the same post-processing pipeline so that reported scores are comparable.

When reporting run-time behavior, avoid reporting only peak arithmetic counts or only peak memory use. Instead, measure end-to-end response delay per output element under a realistic batching and sequence-length distribution, and report sustained processing rate as you increase concurrency until the working storage limit is reached. Include the working storage that matters in practice: the storage for the embedding table, the cached context buffers that grow with sequence length, and any temporary buffers the runtime allocates.

Power-related comparisons should be based on end-to-end device draw under the same workload, not on coarse arithmetic-energy models alone. Still, it is useful to break down energy use by dominant operations: in these systems the bulk of arithmetic cost is the dense multiply-like operations, and converting those to integer-add dominated kernels reduces arithmetic energy dramatically. When measuring the compact representation, be explicit about which kernels were used (vendor optimized kernels, a specific 2-bit or ladder kernel, or a general fallback) because kernel efficiency strongly affects latency and throughput.

Finally, make sure both systems are using similarly mature runtimes: if you compare a highly optimized floating implementation against an unoptimized compact implementation, the runtime difference will be misleading. The paper’s experiments used well-optimized production kernels for both sides to ensure apples-to-apples comparisons.

# Common pitfalls and debugging tips in low-resolution fitting and serving

Working in this mixed regime (three-valued stored tables and eight-level transients) introduces a small set of recurring operational issues.

A common training-phase problem is improper scaling before the ternary mapping: if the per-matrix normalization scalar is too small the scaled table collapses to zeros after rounding, starving gradients; if it is too large the table saturates to only plus or minus ones and loses nuance. The remedy is to monitor the distribution of the pre-quantized entries and the fraction of zeros after quantization, and to adjust the initial scaling strategy or add a small floor to $\gamma$ to avoid collapse.

Gradient propagation through discrete choices needs attention. In practice one uses a straight-through estimator or another surrogate during the fitting phase so that the learning algorithm does not stop at the quantization step. Watch for vanishing or exploding updates by tracking norms of the surrogate gradients and, if necessary, reduce learning rates or clip updates early during convergence.

On the serving side, numerical stability can be affected by the hybrid precision of the stored tables and the transient signals. Keep the embedding and input pre-processing in higher precision, and test the rounding behavior across the full dynamic range of possible inputs. If you observe quality regressions at particular sequence lengths, evaluate the per-position scaling step and the cache formatting for the context buffers; asymmetric scaling or misapplied offsets are frequent causes of silent errors.

Another practical pitfall is relying on unoptimized kernels for the integer-add dominated operations; without vendor or community-optimized kernels you may not realize the latency and power benefits. The paper integrated a ladder-style 2-bit kernel and used optimized inference runtimes to demonstrate the resource advantages — follow that example when benchmarking.
