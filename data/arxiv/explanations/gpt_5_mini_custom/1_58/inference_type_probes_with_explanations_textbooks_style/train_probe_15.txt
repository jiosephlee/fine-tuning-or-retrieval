Chapter Title: Quantization Dynamics in BitNet b1.58: Why Small Weights Become Zero

# Overview of BitNet b1.58 and Its Quantization Strategy

BitNet b1.58 is a 1.58-bit variant of Transformer-based large language models in which every parameter (or weight) takes values in the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The model replaces the standard dense linear layers with BitLinear, and it is trained from scratch using 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. A key component of BitNet b1.58 is its quantization function for weights, which constrains a full-precision weight matrix $W$ to a ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}$. This quantization pipeline consists of scaling by an average magnitude, followed by rounding to the nearest integer and clipping into a bounded range. The resulting mechanics lead to a natural tendency for small-magnitude weights near $0$ to be mapped to the zero state, which is central both to the efficiency and the modeling capability of BitNet b1.58.

To place this behavior in context, it helps to recall the goals of the design. BitNet b1.58 is intended to match full-precision baselines (e.g., FP16 LLaMA-like models) in perplexity and downstream task performance at comparable model sizes and training budgets, while drastically reducing inference cost across latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption. The inclusion of $0$ in the weight set differentiates BitNet b1.58 from pure $\{-1, 1\}$ schemes by explicitly enabling feature filtering, which empirically strengthens modeling capability. The quantization function is the mechanism through which this structure emerges and is maintained throughout training.

# The Absmean Quantization Function

The weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 uses an absmean scaling followed by integer rounding and clipping. Given a full-precision weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the ternary quantized matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is defined as:
\[
\widetilde{W} \;=\; \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\gamma \;=\; \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i j} |W_{i j}|,
\]
and $\epsilon$ is a small constant added for numerical stability. The function $\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b)$ first rounds $x$ to the nearest integer, and then clips the result into the interval $[a, b]$:
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) \;=\; \max\!\left(a, \min\!\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right).
\]

This pipeline has three phases: scale, round, and clip. The scaling step normalizes the matrix by the average absolute value $\gamma$, which sets the “unit” against which individual entries are compared. The rounding step maps the scaled values to the closest integer. The clipping step ensures the output lies in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ even when rounding would otherwise produce integers outside this range. Together, these steps define a simple, uniform rule that divides the real line into three regions associated with $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ after scaling.

# Intuition for Scaling by the Average Absolute Value

The scaling by $\gamma$ calibrates the weight matrix so that its average magnitude corresponds to a normalized scale of $1$. Concretely, dividing $W$ by $\gamma + \epsilon$ transforms each entry $W_{i j}$ into
\[
\frac{W_{i j}}{\gamma + \epsilon}.
\]
When $|W_{i j}|$ is approximately equal to the average magnitude $\gamma$, the scaled value is near $\pm 1$. When $|W_{i j}|$ is much smaller than $\gamma$, the scaled value is near $0$. When $|W_{i j}|$ is larger than $\gamma$, the scaled value has magnitude greater than $1$, but clipping will eventually cap it at $\pm 1$.

This normalization has two important effects. First, it induces a consistent thresholding behavior across layers and matrices: what counts as “small,” “medium,” or “large” is measured relative to the matrix’s own average scale, not an external constant. Second, it stabilizes the ternary mapping during training because the quantization decision is tied to the current distribution of weights. As the model learns, the average magnitude $\gamma$ adapts, and small perturbations around $0$ are kept near $0$ after scaling, while dominant signal-bearing weights align with $\pm 1$.

# Rounding and Clipping: Three Regions on the Real Line

After scaling, rounding to the nearest integer and clipping to $[-1, 1]$ collapses the continuous scaled values into three discrete outcomes. The rounding step maps any scaled value in the interval $\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)$ to $0$, values in the interval $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2}\right)$ to $1$, and values in the interval $\left(-\tfrac{3}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right)$ to $-1$. Values outside these ranges would round to integers with magnitude greater than $1$, but clipping enforces the final outputs to be $-1$ or $+1$ at the extremes.

The consequence is that, in the scaled domain, there is a central basin around $0$ whose width is determined by the rounding thresholds. Any scaled weight that falls in this central basin is sent to $0$ after rounding. Because the scaling step centers the matrix’s average magnitude at $1$, a substantial portion of entries that are below roughly half the average magnitude are treated as small and are rounded to zero. Therefore, entries with small absolute value relative to $\gamma$ are most likely mapped to zero under the scale–round–clip pipeline.

To make this concrete, consider an entry $W_{i j}$ such that $|W_{i j}| \ll \gamma$. Then
\[
\left|\frac{W_{i j}}{\gamma + \epsilon}\right| \approx 0,
\]
which lies firmly inside the rounding basin for $0$. After rounding and clipping, this entry becomes $\widetilde{W}_{i j} = 0$. By contrast, if $|W_{i j}|$ is near or above $\gamma$, then the scaled value is near or above $1$, and rounding will tend to produce $\pm 1$, which clipping will preserve.

# Why Small-Magnitude Weights Map to Zero

Given the above mechanics, the mapping of small-magnitude weights near $0$ to the zero state is a direct result of the combined normalization and rounding thresholds. The scaling by $\gamma$ ensures that the average absolute magnitude sits near the boundary between the nonzero states. The rounding defines a central interval around $0$ that absorbs values whose magnitudes are less than approximately half the average magnitude. Clipping then removes any possibility of intermediate integers, enforcing the ternary set.

Intuitively, this behavior aligns with a principle of feature filtering: entries whose contribution is weak relative to the matrix’s prevailing scale do not warrant a nonzero state. The quantization function is implicitly computing a relative importance measure. If a weight is sufficiently small compared to the average absolute weight, it is considered unimportant and is set to $0$. If it is sufficiently large, it is promoted to $\pm 1$. This scheme is simple yet powerful, because it is based entirely on the current distribution of weights and does not require hand-tuned thresholds per layer or per parameter group.

# Feature Filtering Enabled by the Zero State

BitNet b1.58 explicitly supports feature filtering through the inclusion of $0$ in the weight set. This is not merely a storage convenience; it creates a modeling capability that improves performance over pure $\{-1, 1\}$ quantization. When the quantization pipeline maps small-magnitude weights to $0$, it suppresses weak or noisy signals and can reduce interference among features. The model preserves strong directional signals via $\pm 1$ while pruning marginal ones via $0$, which can lead to cleaner representations and improved learning dynamics.

In effect, the quantization induces structured sparsity at the level of individual weights. Because the mapping is relative to $\gamma$, this sparsity adapts automatically: as learning progresses and the distribution of weights changes, the model maintains a balance between retaining salient connections and filtering out negligible ones. The paper reports that this explicit feature filtering “can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs,” and that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in both perplexity and end-task performance beginning at the 3B model size under the same training tokens and configuration.

# System-Level Benefits Linked to the Zero Mapping

The mapping of small weights to zero has practical implications beyond modeling. First, it contributes to the overall efficiency of the computation paradigm. BitNet b1.58’s matrix multiplications rely predominantly on integer addition rather than floating-point multiplication and addition. Ternary weights reduce the complexity of the operations and lower energy costs associated with arithmetic. According to the energy model cited, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on $7$nm chips compared to FP16 baselines.

Second, the prevalence of zeros reduces effective memory footprint and bandwidth needs during inference. Parameters must be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory, and lower-bit representations are cheaper to move. With weights confined to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and activations reduced to $8$ bits, BitNet b1.58 significantly cuts latency and memory consumption. The results indicate that BitNet b1.58 is faster and more memory-efficient across sizes, with speedups that grow as model size scales. For example, at $70$B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be $4.1$ times faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline.

These system-level improvements and their scaling behavior are tied to the quantization structure. The more decisive the mapping of small values to zero, the more streamlined the data movement and compute, and the stronger the advantages in latency, throughput, and energy. The paper further shows that BitNet b1.58 enables higher batch sizes and throughput at large scale, supporting up to $11$ times the batch size and yielding $8.9$ times the throughput at $70$B parameters compared to an FP16 baseline under the stated setup.

# Interaction with Activation Quantization

While the fact in focus concerns weight quantization, activations in BitNet b1.58 are also quantized to $8$ bits. The implementation follows BitNet with a modification: activations are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than being scaled to $[0, Q_b]$ before nonlinearities. This change removes the need for zero-point quantization, simplifying implementation and system optimization. Although this activation scaling is independent of the weight rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, it complements the overall scheme by ensuring the computational graph operates within low-bit constraints throughout, further contributing to the efficiency gains reported.

# Empirical Outcomes Consistent with Zero Mapping

The empirical results in the paper are consistent with the intuition that small-magnitude weights are mapped to zero and that the resulting feature filtering benefits performance. BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines at the $3$B scale in both perplexity and end-task accuracy. For instance, BitNet b1.58 at $3.9$B outperforms LLaMA LLM at $3$B while being $2.4$ times faster and using $3.32$ times less memory. As models scale to $7$B, $13$B, and $70$B, the latency and memory advantages grow, and energy savings are significant. In token-scaling experiments, a BitNet b1.58 model trained with $2$T tokens achieves superior zero-shot performance over a state-of-the-art open-source $3$B model trained under a similar data recipe, indicating strong generalization.

These outcomes reflect that the ternary quantization scheme, and in particular the central role of zero as the destination for small-magnitude weights, does not hinder learning when properly designed. Instead, it can reinforce useful inductive biases, suppress noise, and enable the model to operate efficiently at scale, all while retaining competitive performance on standard benchmarks.

# Summary

In BitNet b1.58, the weight quantization pipeline—scaling by the average absolute value, rounding to the nearest integer, and clipping to $[-1, 1]$—creates a robust mechanism that maps small-magnitude weights near $0$ to the zero state. The scaling step defines what counts as “small” relative to the matrix’s own average magnitude, the rounding step provides a central basin that absorbs those small values, and the clipping step enforces the ternary bounds. This behavior underpins the model’s explicit support for feature filtering, strengthens its modeling capability compared to purely binary schemes, and contributes directly to the substantial gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy efficiency reported across model sizes.
