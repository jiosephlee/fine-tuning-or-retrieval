Chapter Title: Absmean Quantization and the Scaling Factor in 1.58-bit Large Language Models

# The 1.58-bit Paradigm in Large Language Models

Large Language Models have traditionally relied on full-precision parameters, typically stored in FP16 or BF16 formats. While this yields strong performance across perplexity and end-task metrics, it also imposes substantial costs in energy, latency, memory footprint, and throughput, particularly because the core computational workload in Transformers is dominated by matrix multiplication with floating-point addition and multiplication. A new line of work examines fundamentally different numerical representations and computation paradigms to alleviate these costs without sacrificing performance. In this context, BitNet b1.58 is a 1-bit model variant in which every parameter is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, yet achieves parity with full-precision baselines of the same size and training tokens on perplexity and end-task performance, beginning at the 3B scale.

BitNet b1.58 extends prior 1-bit designs by explicitly including $0$ as a representable weight value. This addition enables feature filtering—turning off features through zero weights—and strengthens modeling capability while retaining the computation, memory, and energy advantages of integer operations. Its design preserves the benefits of the original BitNet matrix multiplication paradigm, which substantially reduces or removes multiplications and allows highly optimized integer arithmetic. This reconfiguration—from FP16 to ternary weights and low-bit activations—translates into improved latency, memory, energy efficiency, and throughput, and it suggests a new scaling law for balancing performance against inference cost as models grow.

# Absmean Quantization and the Role of $\gamma$ as a Scaling Factor

At the heart of BitNet b1.58 is the quantization function that constrains the weight matrix to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This function employs an “absmean” scaling that normalizes the weight matrix by its average absolute value before rounding and clipping to the ternary set. Formally, for a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the procedure is:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
\quad \text{and} \quad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
Conceptually, $\gamma$ acts as a scaling factor. Because $\gamma$ is the average magnitude of entries in $W$, dividing $W$ by $\gamma$ normalizes the typical scale of its elements to be around $1$. This places many entries near the integers $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in normalized space, making the subsequent rounding-and-clipping step yield stable ternary assignments without requiring layer-specific heuristics. The small constant $\epsilon$ is included to avoid division by zero and to ensure numerical stability, but it does not change the conceptual role of $\gamma$ as the quantity that determines the scale of the weight matrix prior to discretization.

To build intuition, consider that without normalization, weights in different layers or blocks may have very different magnitudes. Directly rounding such values to the nearest integer could produce highly imbalanced ternary matrices—some dominated by $\pm 1$, others by $0$—simply due to scale. By rescaling to an average absolute value of approximately $1$, $\gamma$ centers the rounding decision around a consistent reference across matrices. Entries whose magnitudes are near the average are likely to be mapped to $\pm 1$; entries much smaller than the average are likely to be mapped to $0$; and entries much larger than the average are clipped at $\pm 1$. In this way, $\gamma$ sets the effective thresholding regime for mapping real-valued weights into a ternary alphabet and standardizes quantization behavior across parameters.

The effect of this scaling factor is twofold. First, it improves the expressiveness of the ternary encoding by aligning the “unit” of quantization with the matrix’s own scale, allowing the three symbols $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ to capture relative significance among weights. Second, it enhances system-level convenience and optimizability, because the downstream integer operations will see weights predominantly clustered at these discrete values, enabling efficient addition-centric computation while retaining functional fidelity to the full-precision model.

# RoundClip and Ternary Weights

The $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot, -1, 1)$ operator encapsulates two operations: conventional rounding to the nearest integer and clipping to the range $[-1, 1]$. After $W$ is scaled by $\gamma + \epsilon$, the rounding step maps normalized entries to integers, and clipping ensures the final set of values is exactly $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This guarantees that the weight matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is ternary.

The interplay between rounding and clipping ensures robustness to outliers. Entries that would round beyond $1$ in magnitude, perhaps because their original values were significantly larger than $\gamma$, are clipped to $\pm 1$. Meanwhile, entries whose magnitudes shrink under scaling to below one-half will round to $0$. Because $\gamma$ reflects the average absolute magnitude across all entries, this procedure implicitly sets a dynamic threshold: typical entries are encouraged to become $\pm 1$, and atypically small ones become $0$, enabling explicit feature filtering. These filtered entries can be interpreted as deactivations of certain learned features within the model’s linear transformations, and their presence is part of the increased modeling capability attributed to the inclusion of the $0$ value compared to strictly binary schemes.

By design, the ternary weights promote efficient integer matrix multiplication during inference. The requirement for almost no multiplication arises because the weights take on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, allowing products with activations to be implemented via addition and subtraction when nonzero, and skipped entirely when zero. This structure contributes directly to the energy savings observed, as integer addition is substantially cheaper than floating-point multiplication and addition, and the prevalence of zero weights reduces both arithmetic and memory bandwidth demands.

# Activation Quantization and Symmetric Scaling

BitNet b1.58 adopts 8-bit activations and applies a quantization strategy similar to the original BitNet, with one key change: activations are scaled to the symmetric interval $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, rather than to $[0, Q_b]$ with an explicit zero-point. This eliminates zero-point quantization and is described as more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization in practice, with negligible effects on performance in the experiments reported.

This choice aligns naturally with the ternary weight design. Symmetric activation ranges pair cleanly with signed weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ because the representation is centered at zero for both operands. In the context of the overall computation paradigm, this symmetry simplifies data handling and arithmetic, complementing the benefits of integer-only matrix multiplication. While the activation quantization does not directly involve $\gamma$, it participates in the broader theme of scale management: appropriately scaling activations ensures they are well matched to the dynamic range needed by the ternary-weight multiplications and additions, sustaining fidelity while maximizing system-level efficiency.

# Feature Filtering via Zero Weights

A central modeling advantage of BitNet b1.58 over strictly binary designs is the explicit inclusion of $0$ among representable weight values. When an entry in $\widetilde{W}$ is quantized to $0$, it effectively filters out the corresponding feature contribution in the linear transformation. In the absmean quantization mechanism, the presence and prevalence of zeros are regulated by the scaling factor $\gamma$ through its influence on rounding. Entries much smaller than the average absolute magnitude are naturally mapped to $0$, while those near or above that scale map to $\pm 1$.

This feature filtering can improve performance because it introduces sparsity patterns that align with the learned distribution of useful and less useful features. Instead of forcing all connections to be active at $\pm 1$, allowing some to be zero affords the model the flexibility to suppress noisy or redundant signals. In practice, the paper’s experiments show that including $0$ strengthens modeling capability and yields strong results in perplexity and end-task accuracy, matching full precision baselines starting at the 3B size. Importantly, this improvement occurs without sacrificing the computation, memory, and energy advantages inherent to the 1-bit computation paradigm.

# System-Level Implications of Scale and Ternarization

The normalization by $\gamma$ is a small algorithmic step with large system implications. By producing well-behaved ternary weight matrices, BitNet b1.58 enables inference implementations that rely primarily on integer addition, greatly reducing multiplication operations. Since matrix multiplication dominates the runtime and energy profile of Transformer models, reconfiguring this core kernel yields substantial benefits. The paper reports that on 7nm chips, the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication is reduced by a factor of $71.4$ when comparing BitNet b1.58 against FP16 LLaMA baselines, attributing the savings to the replacement of FP16 addition and multiplication with predominantly INT8 addition.

Memory movement is similarly impacted. Ternary weights reduce the model’s memory footprint, lowering both capacity and bandwidth requirements. This reduces the cost of transferring parameters from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM, an important factor in inference latency. Because the activation quantization uses 8 bits and adopts symmetric scaling, KV caches for long-sequence inference become smaller as well, effectively doubling context length under the same resource constraints. Combined, these properties contribute to notable improvements in latency, memory usage, and throughput, and they scale favorably with model size because the proportion of time spent in linear layers grows as models become larger.

Empirically, the reported results show that BitNet b1.58 is $2.71\times$ faster and uses $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than FP16 LLaMA at the 3B scale while matching performance. At 70B, it is $4.1\times$ faster, and it achieves $8.9\times$ higher throughput by supporting up to $11\times$ the batch size on two 80GB A100 cards. These gains illustrate how the quantization design—with $\gamma$ as a scaling factor central to producing ternary weights—cascades into practical system advantages.

# Empirical Performance and Scaling Law

The experiments compare BitNet b1.58 to reproduced FP16 LLaMA models trained on $100$ billion tokens and evaluated across standard benchmarks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ, as well as validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. The performance gap narrows with increasing model size, and at 3B, BitNet b1.58 matches full precision in both perplexity and end-task accuracy. Moreover, a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model outperforms a 3B FP16 LLaMA while being faster and more memory efficient.

Beyond these individual comparisons, the paper articulates a new scaling law regarding model performance and inference cost. Based on trends in latency, memory, and energy, a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM; a 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM; and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. This framing emphasizes that, with the ternary quantization anchored by the absmean scaling factor, larger models can become practically deployable by significantly lowering inference costs while retaining or improving performance.

The findings extend to longer training horizons as well. A BitNet b1.58 model trained with $2$ trillion tokens, following the StableLM-3B data recipe, achieves superior zero-shot accuracy across several end tasks compared to the StableLM 3B baseline, indicating strong generalization capability in the 1.58-bit regime.

# Architectural Compatibility and Implementation Simplicity

BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components, including RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and removal of biases. This design choice facilitates integration into popular open-source ecosystems such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. The activation quantization’s symmetric scaling eliminates zero-point handling, which the paper highlights as more convenient and simple for implementation and system-level optimization, with negligible performance impact in experiments.

From the perspective of the absmean quantization, these architectural choices do not alter the role of $\gamma$ but provide a familiar Transformer backbone in which the scaling factor can consistently produce ternary weights. The result is a straightforward path to practical deployments where the benefits of integer computation and reduced memory usage are realized without the need to overhaul the full software stack.

# Broader Implications and Future Directions

The paper discusses several forward-looking directions that naturally build on the 1.58-bit paradigm. Mixture-of-Experts architectures stand to benefit from the reduced memory footprint and communication overhead, potentially enabling deployments on fewer devices or even a single chip if capacity allows. Long-sequence inference becomes more tractable as activations are already reduced from $16$ bits to $8$ bits, with the prospect of further lossless compression for KV caches. Edge and mobile deployments are aided by lower memory and energy demands and by the friendliness of 1.58-bit computation to CPU devices. Finally, the work calls for hardware specifically optimized for 1-bit LLMs, taking advantage of the new computation paradigm facilitated by ternary weights and low-bit activations.

Each of these directions relies on the stability and predictability of the quantization scheme. By defining $\gamma$ as the average absolute value and using it as a scaling factor, BitNet b1.58 ensures that the ternary mapping is consistent and functional across layers, enabling the integer-centric computation that underpins these broader system and application benefits.

# Summary: $\gamma$ as the Enabler of Ternary Quantization

The absmean quantization function in BitNet b1.58 defines
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
and uses $\gamma$ to scale the weight matrix before rounding and clipping:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right).
\]
Conceptually, $\gamma$ acts as a scaling factor that normalizes $W$ to a standard magnitude prior to discretization. This normalization aligns the rounding decision with the matrix’s own scale, yielding stable and expressive ternary weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The result is a well-structured quantization that enables feature filtering via zeros, leverages efficient integer addition in matrix multiplication, reduces energy and memory costs, and supports a new scaling law in which larger models become more efficient and practical to deploy. Through this single component—the scaling factor $\gamma$—BitNet b1.58 demonstrates how carefully designed quantization can unlock a fundamentally different and cost-effective computation paradigm for Large Language Models.
