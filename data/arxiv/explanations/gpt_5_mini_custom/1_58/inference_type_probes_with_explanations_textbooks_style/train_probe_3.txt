Chapter Title: Zero-Valued Weights in BitNet b1.58 and Their Role as Feature Filtering

# Introduction

BitNet b1.58 introduces a ternary parameterization in which every weight takes one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $1$. This design retains the computation paradigm of the original 1-bit BitNet, while adding the explicit capability to set weights to zero. The presence of $0$ is central: it enables feature filtering and strengthens the modeling capability of 1-bit Large Language Models, which in turn helps the model match full-precision baselines at practical scales. Understanding why the zero weight is so powerful—and how it relates to the intuition behind sparsity—requires attention to both the quantization procedure that produces ternary weights and the empirical performance and efficiency results reported for BitNet b1.58.

This chapter develops the intuition that zero-valued weights act as explicit filters in the learned transformations of a Transformer. In effect, they allow the model to drop unhelpful contributions from inputs or intermediate features. That behavior is closely analogous to the common intuition of sparsity, where many weights are effectively inactive. While the paper does not rely on an explicit sparse computation mechanism, the inclusion of $0$ in the quantized weight set provides the representational leverage to filter features and thereby improve performance at the same energy and bit-width cost as the original 1-bit BitNet.

# Ternary Quantization and the Emergence of Zero-Valued Weights

BitNet b1.58 implements a Transformer in which the standard linear layers are replaced by BitLinear layers. These layers are trained from scratch with 1.58-bit (ternary) weights and 8-bit activations. The quantization function constrains the weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ by scaling and rounding. Given a full-precision weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the procedure first normalizes by the average absolute magnitude
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
then rounds to the nearest integer within the range $[-1, 1]$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
Here, $\epsilon$ is a small constant to stabilize the division. The operation $\mathrm{round}(\cdot)$ maps scaled values to the nearest integer, and $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ ensures they stay within the ternary range. Thus, a weight becomes $0$ whenever its scaled value lies near $0$. Concretely, weights whose magnitudes are smaller than the implicit rounding threshold normalizing by $\gamma$ will be pulled to $0$.

This quantization encourages the emergence of zero-valued weights in a principled way: small-magnitude weights are suppressed, while sufficiently large positive or negative weights are kept at $+1$ or $-1$. The normalization by $\gamma$ ensures the rounding threshold is adaptive to the average scale in $W$, so that ternary assignments depend on relative magnitudes rather than absolute scale. The outcome is a compact, discrete representation in which many weak contributions are removed, and strong contributions are retained with their sign. This is precisely the “explicit support for feature filtering” emphasized in the paper: the model can block pathways and features that do not contribute meaningfully, by setting the corresponding weights to $0$.

# Feature Filtering and Its Analogy to Sparsity

Within BitNet b1.58, zero-valued weights act as feature filters. Consider a BitLinear layer computing a matrix product $Y = \widetilde{W} X$, where $\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^{n \times m}$ and $X$ is an activation vector or matrix in 8-bit integer form. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$, then the contribution of $X_j$ to the output coordinate $Y_i$ is blocked. The layer learns where such blocking is beneficial through training, because the quantization procedure is applied throughout, and the model adapts its full-precision pre-quantized weights so that, after scaling and rounding, useful connections are retained and harmful or noisy ones are set to zero.

This behavior is analogous to the intuitive concept of sparsity: many connections being inactive, thereby removing unhelpful signal and focusing computation and representation on the features that matter most. In BitNet b1.58, the inactive state is explicitly represented by $0$. The analogy extends to the idea that sparse models tend to generalize well when they suppress noisy or redundant components. The paper’s claim that including $0$ strengthens modeling capability through feature filtering aligns with this intuition: the model is better at selectively passing information forward when it can nullify specific weights rather than being forced to choose only the signs $-1$ or $+1$.

It is important to note what this analogy does and does not imply in the reported system. The paper does not claim any specialized sparse kernels or computational skipping driven by the positions of zeros. Instead, the focus is on representational benefits: the ternary choice, particularly the presence of $0$, enables the model to learn when to suppress features. That suppression acts like sparsity in the sense of zeroing connections, but the measured energy and latency improvements reported in the paper primarily derive from the low-bit computation paradigm (integer addition-based matrix multiplication) and reduced memory footprint compared to full precision, rather than from explicit sparse computation.

# Computation Paradigm and the Role of Zero Weights

BitNet b1.58 retains the original BitNet computation paradigm, in which the dominant operation—matrix multiplication—uses integer addition rather than floating-point addition and multiplication. Vanilla LLMs rely on FP16 or BF16, with matrix multiplications incurring the cost of floating-point multiplications and additions. BitNet b1.58 shifts this burden to INT8 addition for the majority of the arithmetic in the BitLinear layers. As the paper notes, this paradigm saves orders of energy compared to floating-point operations, and because power is a fundamental limit to performance in chips, these energy savings can translate into faster computation.

Where do zero weights fit in this paradigm? When a weight is $0$, its contribution in a dot product is null. In algebraic terms, summing $0 \cdot x$ adds nothing. Consequently, the model’s behavior at the representational level differs from a strictly sign-only scheme: it can eliminate contributions rather than merely flipping or reinforcing them. This adjustment provides finer control over information flow without introducing multiplication costs—since the arithmetic is still grounded in integer addition and sign-based accumulation. The paper emphasizes that BitNet b1.58 has the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet in this core arithmetic sense, but gains modeling strength through feature filtering. Thus, the zero weight improves representational expressiveness at the same energy class, bridging performance and efficiency.

Another system-level consideration is the movement of parameters between DRAM and on-chip memory (e.g., SRAM). The ternary representation reduces memory footprint compared to full precision. While the paper discusses lower bandwidth and faster loading due to smaller bit-widths, the inclusion of zero does not by itself change bit-width; rather, it changes which connections contribute. The overarching efficiency improvements are attributed to the low-bit regime and the integer-addition computation, with zero weights playing a modeling role that improves performance without increasing energy or memory relative to the original 1-bit design.

# Empirical Evidence: Performance Matches Full Precision at Practical Scales

The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision baselines (FP16) in perplexity and end-task performance starting from approximately a 3B parameter scale, when trained under the same conditions (e.g., model size and 100 billion training tokens on RedPajama). The inclusion of zero weights as feature filters is highlighted as a reason for stronger modeling capability compared to a strictly 1-bit design. In experiments, BitNet b1.58 3.9B outperforms LLaMA LLM 3B while being faster and using less memory. As model size scales, the performance gap narrows, and BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly cost-effective: at 70B, it is reported to be $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline, with energy savings in arithmetic operations for matrix multiplication reaching $71.4\times$ on 7nm chips according to the cited energy model.

These results support the idea that feature filtering via zero weights enhances the representational capacity necessary to reach full-precision performance without sacrificing the efficiency benefits of low-bit computation. The training with 2 trillion tokens further indicates strong generalization: following the StableLM-3B data recipe, BitNet b1.58 achieves superior zero-shot accuracy on Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy, reinforcing that the ternary design scales in both data and parameters.

# Activations, Long Sequences, and System-Level Benefits

While zero weights target feature filtering in the weight space, activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized to 8 bits and scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, avoiding zero-point quantization. This simplifies implementation and system optimization and yielded negligible performance impact in experiments. The combination of 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations reduces runtime memory compared to FP16 baselines, which the paper connects to multiple practical benefits.

One such benefit is the native support for longer sequences. In modern LLM inference, memory consumed by key-value caches is a major bottleneck. Reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits doubles the effective context length under the same resource budget. The paper suggests that, for 1.58-bit LLMs, this can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower in future work, potentially extending sequence length even more. Although zero weights are not directly modifying activation bit-width, the overarching theme is that the low-bit design makes deployment and inference more viable for longer contexts, and the inclusion of zero weights strengthens modeling quality in this constrained regime.

# Mixture-of-Experts and Deployment on Edge Devices

The paper discusses how 1.58-bit LLMs can alleviate the practical challenges of Mixture-of-Experts architectures. MoE reduces computation FLOPs, but memory consumption and inter-chip communication can be limiting. A lower memory footprint reduces the number of devices needed and lowers the cost of transferring activations across networks. If the entire model fits on a single chip, communication overhead disappears. While this section is forward-looking, the relevance to zero weights is conceptual: BitNet b1.58’s ternary representation with explicit feature filtering allows stronger modeling while preserving the benefits of low-bit memory and computation, which is attractive for MoE designs.

On edge and mobile devices, where CPU processing, limited memory, and strict energy budgets dominate, the reduced memory and energy consumption of 1.58-bit LLMs enable deployments that would be impractical with full precision. By keeping the computation in integer addition and representing weights compactly—including zero-valued filters—the model is more amenable to efficient execution. This echoes the general message of the paper: BitNet b1.58 maintains or improves performance while significantly reducing the cost profile, making new applications feasible.

# A New Scaling Law and the Place of Zero Weights

The paper proposes that BitNet b1.58 enables a new scaling law connecting model performance to inference cost. It provides equivalences such as a 13B BitNet b1.58 being more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM, a 30B BitNet b1.58 being more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM, and a 70B BitNet b1.58 being more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. These statements crystallize the idea that low-bit computation can change the trade-offs between scale and cost.

Within this broader picture, the inclusion of zero in the ternary weight set is a small but pivotal design choice: it upgrades the representational power of the 1-bit paradigm by enabling explicit feature filtering. This change does not alter the core efficiency determinants—integer addition-based matrix multiplications and lower bit-width memory transfers—but it does improve how effectively larger models can utilize their parameters to match full-precision performance. That is the essence of the analogy to sparsity: by setting some weights to $0$, the model learns to ignore certain features or connections, focusing its capacity on what matters. In BitNet b1.58, this analogy is realized through quantization-induced zeros, in a way that is consistent with the low-bit, efficient computation regime.

# Summary

BitNet b1.58 extends the original 1-bit BitNet by adding $0$ to the weight set, yielding ternary weights $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. The quantization process—scaling by the average absolute value and rounding with clipping—naturally produces zero-valued weights when magnitudes are small relative to the learned scale. These zeros act as explicit feature filters: they block unhelpful contributions and allow the model to pass forward only the signals it deems useful. This filtering behavior is closely analogous to the intuition of sparsity, where many connections are inactive. Crucially, this enhancement in modeling capability does not compromise the central efficiency advantages of the 1-bit computation paradigm: matrix multiplications remain dominated by integer additions, memory footprints are reduced compared to FP16 baselines, and energy consumption is significantly lower.

Empirically, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision performance at practical scales (from roughly 3B parameters) and exhibits superior cost profile in latency, memory, throughput, and energy. As model and token counts scale, the benefits become more pronounced, supporting longer sequences, MoE designs, and deployment on edge and mobile devices. In sum, the ability to set weights to zero in BitNet b1.58 provides the representational leverage needed to reach full-precision performance within an efficient low-bit framework, embodying the intuitive advantages of sparsity through a simple, principled ternary quantization scheme.
