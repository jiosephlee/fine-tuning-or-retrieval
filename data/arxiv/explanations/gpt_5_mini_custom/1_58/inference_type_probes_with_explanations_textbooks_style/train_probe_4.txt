Chapter: Training 1.58-bit Large Language Models: Why Quantization-Aware Training Enables Performance Parity

# The context: from post-training quantization to 1-bit-era models

The recent progression of large language models has exposed a central tension in model design: while scaling up models has driven impressive advances in capability, the computational and memory costs of full-precision Transformers hinder efficient deployment. A prominent response to this challenge is post-training quantization, a technique that reduces the bit-width of weights and activations after training a full-precision model. This approach is widely used in practice and has been pushed to low-bit regimes by recent work. However, the core claim examined in this chapter is that, for the 1.58-bit regime of BitNet b1.58, achieving the reported performance parity with full-precision models requires training the model under the quantization constraints themselves, rather than quantizing after the fact. In other words, quantization-aware training, implemented here as training from scratch with ternary weights and low-bit activations, is the implicitly preferred path to parity.

The paper sets the stage by emphasizing that post-training quantization is sub-optimal, particularly as one pushes precision toward extreme regimes. It argues that a new computation paradigm is possible and desirable: a Transformer architecture in which the dominant matrix multiplications operate without multiplications at all, relying instead on integer additions due to the structure of the quantized weights. This paradigm shift relies on the model learning in the restricted parameter space from the beginning, making the learned representations and dynamics naturally aligned with the low-bit constraints rather than forcing a high-precision solution to fit into a low-bit mold after training.

# Why post-training quantization is insufficient in the 1.58-bit regime

Post-training quantization adjusts a trained, full-precision model by mapping learned weights and activations to low-bit representations. While this can work tolerably well at moderate bit-widths, the paper explicitly positions it as sub-optimal, particularly for the regime targeted by BitNet b1.58. The reason is intuitive: a full-precision model discovers solutions in a vast continuous parameter space. Its internal representations, sensitivity structure, and reliance on fine-grained weight magnitudes are all adapted to that space. Post hoc projection of those weights to a highly discrete set such as \{-1, 0, 1\} can severely distort the learned operators, leading to degradation in perplexity and downstream accuracy, unless the model’s structure and training already anticipate and accommodate discrete computation.

When weights are constrained to \{-1, 0, 1\}, the representational geometry is not just coarsened but fundamentally restructured. In this regime, both the magnitude and sparsity patterns become architectural features rather than incidental properties. The inclusion of the 0 value is crucial for BitNet b1.58, as it enables what the paper refers to as explicit feature filtering. This allows the model to learn when to suppress features outright, an ability that plays a central role in recovering modeling capacity at extremely low precision. Post-training quantization cannot reliably induce such behavior because it attempts to compress a solution that was not optimized to use the power of zero-valued weights as a modeling tool. Instead, training must internalize the ternary structure: the network must learn to represent computations in ways that are robust to the discretization itself.

Additionally, low-bit activations reshape the flow of information across layers. The paper adopts 8-bit activations that are scaled to the range \([ -Q_b, Q_b ]\) per token rather than using zero-point quantization, simplifying both implementation and optimization. This activation design is not an afterthought added to a trained model; it is part of the training-time computation and likely influences how the network distributes information and builds invariances. Post-training quantization lacks this synergy between training dynamics and inference mechanics and thus tends to underperform in such extreme regimes.

# Quantization-aware training in BitNet b1.58: training from scratch with ternary weights

In contrast to post-training quantization, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. Practically, this means the model is optimized directly under the constraints it will face at inference time. The core mechanism that enforces the ternary structure is a carefully designed quantization function that maps the full-precision weight matrix \(W\) to a ternary matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) using the average absolute weight magnitude as a scale:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\Bigr),
\quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]

Here, \(\gamma\) normalizes weight magnitudes by the average absolute value, and \(\mathrm{RoundClip}\) projects the scaled entries onto the set \{-1, 0, 1\} by rounding and clamping:

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]

The use of \(\gamma\) balances the tendency of weights to collapse toward a single sign when naively scaled, and it calibrates the mapping to ensure that non-trivial fractions of weights are assigned to \(-1\), \(0\), and \(1\) as appropriate for the current state of learning. This not only preserves learning signal but also encourages meaningful utilization of the zero weight, thereby enabling the aforementioned feature filtering. Because this ternarization is applied during training, the model progressively adapts to the ternary representation, learning weight patterns that are stable under this discretization.

Activations are also quantized during training in a way consistent with deployment. The paper follows the activation quantization approach from BitNet but, crucially, scales activations to \([ -Q_b, Q_b ]\) per token rather than using a zero-point range \([0, Q_b]\). This choice removes the need for zero-point quantization and simplifies the computation path. Training under these activation constraints shapes the internal representations to be resilient to low-bit saturation and to make efficient use of the available dynamic range. Again, this is a property that emerges only when the quantization is part of the training loop.

# Architectural choices that support quantization-aware training

BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components that are known to provide strong baselines for Transformer LLMs. It uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary positional embeddings, while removing all biases. These architectural choices are aligned with modern LLM training and facilitate integration with standard open-source tooling. More importantly for quantization-aware training, they form a stable design space within which ternary weights and low-bit activations can be trained effectively from scratch.

The replacement of \(\mathrm{nn.Linear}\) with \(\mathrm{BitLinear}\) is central. With ternary weights, the matrix multiplications at the heart of Transformer layers reduce to computations that are dominated by integer additions, enabling significant energy savings and latency reductions. Training with these operators leads the model to utilize patterns that are efficient under these constraints, rather than imposing them post hoc. The architectural consistency between training and inference ensures that the learned behavior is faithful to deployed computation.

# Intuition: why training under quantization preserves performance

The empirical results in the paper demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision baselines in terms of perplexity and downstream zero-shot accuracy starting at around the 3B parameter scale, given the same model size and the same number of training tokens. This observation is not incidental; it reflects how quantization-aware training reconciles the low-precision constraints with the breadth of tasks and contexts that LLMs must handle.

From an intuitive perspective, training with ternary weights lets the optimizer discover sparse, sign-patterned operators that are intrinsically robust to discretization. The presence of the 0 weight value encourages the network to learn structured sparsity in weight matrices, which acts as a form of explicit feature gating or filtering. This is quite different from merely approximating a full-precision operator after training; here, the operator’s structure emerges under the ternary restriction itself. The result is that the model learns to allocate representational capacity across layers and heads in ways that use sign and sparsity as fundamental primitives. Such solutions do not need to survive a quantization step; they are born in quantization.

Similarly, the use of low-bit activations during training allows the model to calibrate its internal pre-activation scales and post-activation distributions so that most computation occurs within the reliable dynamic range. By scaling activations to \([ -Q_b, Q_b ]\) per token, the model avoids complications introduced by zero-point handling and learns to use symmetric ranges that align well with integer arithmetic. Thus, both feedforward layers and attention computations evolve to be quantization-compatible, preserving the fidelity required for language modeling tasks.

The contrast with post-training quantization is stark: a model trained without these constraints is unlikely to discover such quantization-compatible structures, and forcing them afterward introduces systematic distortions. The paper’s position that post-training quantization is sub-optimal reflects this misalignment. In the 1.58-bit regime, the degree of discretization is so extreme that only training-time alignment can deliver parity.

# Evidence: parity in perplexity and end-task accuracy with reduced cost

The paper reports extensive comparisons between BitNet b1.58 and reproduced FP16 LLaMA baselines across multiple model sizes, all trained on 100 billion tokens. It evaluates both perplexity on standard validation corpora and zero-shot accuracy on a range of benchmarks, observing that the performance gap narrows with increasing model size and reaches parity starting at 3B parameters. Notably, a BitNet b1.58 model of 3.9B parameters outperforms a 3B LLaMA baseline while being faster and more memory-efficient.

The reported system-level benefits underscore the practicality of the approach. Latency and memory usage improve significantly, and the advantages grow with model scale because the cost of \(\mathrm{nn.Linear}\) dominates more at larger sizes. At 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 achieves a 4.1x speed-up over the FP16 baseline. Energy modeling further indicates dramatic savings in arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplications, with BitNet b1.58 relying primarily on INT8 additions rather than FP16 additions and multiplications. These improvements culminate in higher throughput at scale, where BitNet b1.58 supports much larger batch sizes within the same memory budget, yielding substantial end-to-end throughput gains.

All of these gains are enabled by the fact that the model is trained end-to-end under the low-bit constraints, thereby aligning algorithmic performance with system efficiency. The new scaling equivalences proposed in the paper, where much larger 1.58-bit models are more efficient than significantly smaller 16-bit models, rely on this training approach to maintain accuracy while reducing computational burden.

# The role of the ternary zero: feature filtering as a capacity amplifier

A central enhancement from the original 1-bit BitNet to BitNet b1.58 is the inclusion of \(0\) among the allowed weight values. The paper emphasizes that this explicit support for feature filtering strengthens modeling capability. When a weight can be exactly zero, layers can suppress specific features rather than merely reversing their sign. This capacity for selective shutoff complements the sign structure and allows more nuanced control over information flow.

Training under quantization is crucial to exploit this mechanism. The absmean scaling and rounding place the decision boundary between \(-1\), \(0\), and \(1\) in a data- and layer-adaptive position determined by \(\gamma\). The model learns to adjust weight magnitudes relative to \(\gamma\) so that important connections cross the threshold into \(\pm 1\), while unhelpful ones settle near zero and are rounded to \(0\). Post-training quantization cannot reliably induce such fine control because the trained full-precision magnitudes were not organized under a ternary-aware loss landscape. By contrast, quantization-aware training continuously shapes the distribution of weights so that the ternary projection preserves the learned function.

# Training at scale and generalization under quantization constraints

The paper further investigates scalability by training a BitNet b1.58 model with 2 trillion tokens following a known data recipe for a 3B model. It observes that BitNet b1.58 achieves superior zero-shot performance across multiple end tasks compared to a strong open-source baseline at the same token budget. This result suggests that the quantization-aware approach does not hinder generalization; rather, it can harness vast training data effectively even under strict low-bit constraints.

This observation supports the broader implication that quantization-aware training is not merely a workaround for efficient inference, but a viable paradigm for training high-capacity models. By ensuring that training dynamics and inference mechanics are aligned from the outset, the approach enables models to scale in both size and data while maintaining accuracy and unlocking efficiency.

# System and deployment ramifications of training-time quantization

The systems-level consequences of training under 1.58-bit constraints are significant. Because weights are ternary and activations are low-bit, the memory footprint for both parameters and intermediate states is drastically reduced. This reduction lightens DRAM-to-accelerator bandwidth demands and shrinks on-chip storage requirements, yielding latency benefits in addition to compute savings. As model size increases, these advantages amplify because the proportion of time and energy spent in \(\mathrm{nn.Linear}\) grows, and BitNet b1.58’s arithmetic becomes increasingly favorable.

These properties reshape scaling laws relating model size to performance and cost. The paper explicitly proposes equivalences showing that much larger BitNet b1.58 models can be more efficient than much smaller FP16 baselines in latency, memory, and energy, enabling a new frontier for model scaling. Such a frontier is only accessible when the learned solution is intrinsically quantized, reinforcing the role of quantization-aware training as the enabler of both accuracy and efficiency.

# Conclusion: quantization-aware training as the implicit prerequisite for 1.58-bit parity

The central fact supported by the paper is that matching full-precision performance in the 1.58-bit regime requires training the model under the quantization constraints. Post-training quantization is identified as sub-optimal, and the results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 achieves parity in perplexity and end-task performance by being trained from scratch with ternary weights and low-bit activations. The quantization function based on absmean scaling, the activation scaling to symmetric ranges per token, and the architecture aligned with efficient integer arithmetic collectively ensure that the learned computations are faithful to the deployed computations.

This alignment is what allows BitNet b1.58 to deliver a Pareto improvement: maintaining or exceeding accuracy while dramatically reducing latency, memory consumption, throughput bottlenecks, and energy usage. As the paper emphasizes, this combination outlines a new scaling law for LLMs and opens the door to hardware and systems designed specifically for 1-bit-era models.
