Understanding 1.58 Bits: Encoding Ternary Weights in BitNet b1.58

# From Full Precision to 1.58 Bits: Motivation and Context

Large Language Models have traditionally used full-precision weights, typically 16-bit floating-point values such as FP16 or BF16. While these models achieve strong performance, their computational, memory, and energy costs are substantial because the dominant operation—matrix multiplication—relies on floating-point addition and multiplication. Recent work on 1-bit model architectures proposes a radically different approach: constrain weights to very low precision so that matrix multiplication can be implemented using inexpensive integer addition, reducing energy consumption and enabling faster computation.

BitNet b1.58 follows this direction and introduces an architecture in which every parameter is ternary, taking values in the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The model retains the computational advantages of the original 1-bit BitNet while improving modeling capability by explicitly allowing the value $0$ in its weights. In practice, this ternary parameterization enables BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision baselines (e.g., FP16) in terms of perplexity and end-task accuracy starting from a 3B model size, while achieving significantly lower latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. These improvements stem directly from the low-bit representation of weights and activations and from the corresponding shift in the computation paradigm away from expensive floating-point operations.

Within this context, understanding why ternary weights correspond to “1.58 bits” in the binary system is essential. This quantity captures how many binary digits, on average, are necessary to encode a weight that can take one of three possible values. The answer connects the discrete nature of ternary weights to the binary notion of bits.

# Ternary Weights in BitNet b1.58

BitNet b1.58 constrains each weight to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This is achieved using an absmean quantization function that scales a weight matrix by the average absolute value of its entries and rounds to the nearest integer within the ternary set. Given a full-precision weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the quantized weights are computed as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min\!\big(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
This procedure maps each continuous weight value to one of the three discrete levels $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ based on its scaled magnitude. The inclusion of $0$ is significant: it permits explicit feature filtering in the learned representations and empirically improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs.

Activations in BitNet b1.58 are 8-bit and are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, removing zero-point quantization. This choice simplifies implementation and system-level optimization and maintains performance in practice. Together, ternary weights and 8-bit activations define a compact and efficient representation for both model parameters and intermediate computation, enabling the model to capitalize on the energy and memory advantages of integer arithmetic.

# Why “1.58 Bits” for Ternary Weights?

A ternary weight can take three distinct values. In a binary system, the number of bits required to distinguish among a set of $k$ discrete states is given by the base-2 logarithm of $k$. For ternary weights, this quantity is
\[
\log_2(3).
\]
Numerically, $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$, which explains the claim that the ternary weight system corresponds to “1.58 bits” in the binary system. This expression captures the representational capacity of a ternary variable when measured in binary units: it is the amount of binary information needed, on average, to encode one of three possible outcomes.

To build intuition, consider that a binary digit can represent two states and thus has capacity $\log_2(2) = 1$. A ternary variable has three states and thus has capacity $\log_2(3)$ in binary terms. The jump from two to three states is not as large as doubling the number of states, and consequently the required bits rise from $1$ to approximately $1.58$ rather than to $2$. By introducing the $0$ state into otherwise $\{-1, +1\}$ weights, BitNet moves from two levels to three, increasing the expressiveness of each parameter while preserving the computational advantages: integer addition remains sufficient for matrix multiplication, and the memory footprint is far below that of full precision.

This perspective clarifies the meaning of “1.58-bit LLM” in BitNet b1.58. It does not imply storing fractional bits per parameter in hardware; rather, it quantifies the effective discrete capacity of a ternary parameter in binary units via $\log_2(3)$. This view aligns with how model designers reason about precision, representation, and the trade-off between expressiveness and cost.

# Practical Implications of Ternary Encoding

The ternary encoding underlying BitNet b1.58 has several important consequences for computation, memory, and system performance. First, using weights with three discrete levels means that matrix multiplication—the dominant operation in Transformers—can be implemented with integer addition, avoiding floating-point multiplication altogether. Since the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, replacing costly floating-point arithmetic with inexpensive integer operations translates into substantial energy savings and faster computation.

Second, the memory footprint is dramatically reduced. Models spend considerable time and energy transferring parameters from off-chip memory (e.g., DRAM) to on-chip accelerators (e.g., SRAM). Ternary weights, together with 8-bit activations, reduce both the amount of data to be transferred and the required bandwidth. This lowers the cost and time of loading weights and computing during inference, which directly improves throughput and latency.

These advantages compound as model sizes grow. Measurements reported for BitNet b1.58 show that speed-ups increase with model size because the time cost of linear layers grows with scale. For example, at 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1 times faster than the FP16 baseline, while also showing large reductions in memory usage. Energy analysis further demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on $7\text{ nm}$ chips, owing to the shift from FP16 addition and multiplication to predominantly INT8 addition.

# Architecture and Quantization Choices in BitNet b1.58

BitNet b1.58 adopts widely used LLaMA-like components to maximize compatibility and ease of integration. It uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary position embeddings, and removes all biases, allowing it to plug into popular open-source systems with minimal effort. The architectural backbone remains that of a Transformer, but the linear layers are replaced with BitLinear modules that operate with ternary weights and 8-bit activations.

The activation quantization follows the same implementation used in BitNet, with a key simplification: activations are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ without zero-point quantization. This symmetry removes complications from offset handling and facilitates system-level optimization while having negligible impact on performance in experiments. The weight quantization via absmean scaling and ternary rounding ensures that each parameter belongs to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, consistent with the “1.58-bit” characterization derived from $\log_2(3)$.

These design choices strike a balance between compact representation and modeling strength. By allowing the $0$ weight value, BitNet b1.58 gains explicit feature filtering capability, improving the expressiveness of the low-bit model. The result is a system that retains the computational benefits of 1-bit designs while achieving accuracy competitive with full precision at practical scales.

# Performance, Efficiency, and Scaling Laws

Empirical results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision baselines starting at a 3B model size in terms of both perplexity and end-task accuracy, while being faster and using less memory. For instance, BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B parameters is 2.4 times faster and consumes 3.32 times less memory than an FP16 3B LLaMA-like model, and it performs better on evaluated tasks. As models scale to 7B, 13B, and 70B, both latency and memory savings become more pronounced.

Throughput measurements on two $80\text{ GB}$ A100 GPUs show that BitNet b1.58 at 70B parameters supports up to 11 times the batch size of the FP16 baseline, yielding an 8.9 times higher throughput under pipeline parallelism with a sequence length of $512$. Energy analysis confirms that the ternary-plus-8-bit design becomes increasingly efficient at larger scales because the proportion of time spent in linear layers grows with model size, amplifying the benefits of integer arithmetic.

These observations support a new scaling law that relates performance to inference cost under low-bit parameterization. Concretely, as measured in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM; a 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM; and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. The underlying principle is that the discrete encoding of weights and activations—quantified by the “1.58-bit” figure $\log_2(3)$ for ternary weights—allows scaling model size and performance while reducing inference cost.

# Outlook: Capabilities Enabled by 1.58-Bit Design

BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights and 8-bit activations open several promising directions. Mixture-of-Experts models can benefit from reduced memory footprint and lower inter-chip communication overhead, potentially enabling deployment on fewer devices or even a single chip. Long-sequence processing becomes more tractable because activations are reduced from 16 bits to 8 bits, effectively doubling context length under the same resources, with further compression feasible for 1.58-bit LLMs. The compactness and energy efficiency also improve feasibility on edge and mobile devices, where CPUs dominate and memory and power are constrained. Finally, the new computation paradigm encourages the design of hardware and systems optimized for 1-bit and 1.58-bit LLMs.

At the core of these advances is the shift from full precision to ternary representation. Understanding that a ternary weight corresponds to $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits in the binary system clarifies how BitNet b1.58 achieves its balance of expressiveness and efficiency. This simple mathematical relationship connects the discrete design of the model to its practical advantages across computation, memory, and energy, providing a foundation for the emerging era of low-bit LLMs.
