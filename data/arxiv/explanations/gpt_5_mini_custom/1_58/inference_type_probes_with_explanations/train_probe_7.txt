According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if BitNet b1.58 were reverted to have weights only in $\{-1,+1\}$ (removing $0$), the most likely outcome for modeling capability compared to BitNet b1.58 would be worse performance. The paper explicitly argues that introducing the zero weight confers “explicit support for feature filtering,” strengthening modeling capability by allowing the network to suppress weak or noisy features, which a purely binary $\{-1,+1\}$ parameterization cannot do; concretely, BitNet b1.58 quantizes via an absmean scaler $\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$ and rounds $\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right)$ so that many small-magnitude weights with $|W_{ij}|/(\gamma+\epsilon)<\tfrac{1}{2}$ map to $0$ rather than being forced to $\pm 1$, thereby reducing quantization error and preventing spurious contributions from weak features. In a binary-only setting, those same small weights would be inflated to $\pm 1$, increasing interference in linear layers, degrading the quality of learned filters (no ability to gate features off), and accumulating more quantization noise across layers. Empirically, the paper shows that this ternary design enables BitNet b1.58 to match FP16 perplexity and end-task accuracy from $\sim 3$B parameters while retaining the original BitNet’s computational and energy benefits, so removing the $0$ state would likely erode precisely the advantage the authors identify as responsible for the improved modeling capacity.
