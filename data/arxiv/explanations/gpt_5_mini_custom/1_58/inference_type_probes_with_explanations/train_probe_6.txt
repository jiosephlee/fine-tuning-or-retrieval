According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', conceptually the quantity $\gamma$, defined as $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$, in the absmean quantization function — which divides $W$ by $\gamma + \epsilon$ before applying $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot, -1, 1)$ to produce values in $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ — acts as a scaling factor. Because $\gamma$ is the average absolute magnitude of the weight matrix, dividing $W$ by $\gamma$ normalizes the matrix so that its typical entries have unit scale, positioning most values near the quantization targets $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ and making the subsequent rounding and clipping well-calibrated; this per-matrix normalization preserves the relative structure of weights while preventing saturation (large values being clipped to $\pm 1$) or collapse (small values all rounding to $0$), and the small $\epsilon$ term guarantees numerical stability when $\gamma \approx 0$, so operationally $\gamma$ sets the dynamic range for mapping continuous weights into the ternary grid, i.e., it is a scale parameter.
