According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', after applying the scale, rounding, and clipping pipeline, small-magnitude weights near 0 are most likely mapped to zero. This follows directly from the absmean quantization rule they use: weights are first normalized by the average absolute value, \( \gamma = \tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}| \), and then mapped by \( \widetilde{W}_{ij} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(\tfrac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\big) \), where \( \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,-1,1) = \max(-1,\min(1,\mathrm{round}(x))) \). Because rounding is to the nearest integer in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), any normalized value with magnitude \(< \tfrac{1}{2}\) is rounded to 0, i.e., \( |W_{ij}| < \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon) \Rightarrow \widetilde{W}_{ij}=0 \), and clipping does not affect this case since the input is already in \([-1,1]\). The absmean scaling sets a global scale so that “typical” weights have normalized magnitude around 1, making sub-typical small-magnitude weights most likely fall below the \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) threshold and thus map to 0, which the authors also note as enabling explicit “feature filtering” due to the inclusion of the 0 level.
