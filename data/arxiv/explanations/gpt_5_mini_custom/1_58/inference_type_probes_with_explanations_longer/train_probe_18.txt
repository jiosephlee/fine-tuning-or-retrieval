According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if the average absolute weight $\gamma$ becomes larger, the proportion of weights quantized to zero after $\mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$ increases. This follows directly from the absmean quantization rule they adopt: each weight is first normalized by $\gamma+\epsilon$, then rounded to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,1\}$. In a nearest-integer scheme, rounding to zero happens whenever the normalized magnitude falls below the half-integer threshold, i.e., when $\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon}\right|<\tfrac{1}{2}$, which is equivalent to the absolute-value condition $\lvert W_{ij}\rvert < \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)$. As $\gamma$ increases, the right-hand side threshold $\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)$ increases, so a larger set of entries satisfy the inequality and are rounded to zero. Geometrically, the “zero bin” in the original weight space widens with larger $\gamma$, enlarging the portion of weights mapped to $0$ by the ternary quantizer.

Moreover, because $\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert$ is a global scale computed per matrix, it is sensitive to heavy tails. If a subset of weights grows large, $\gamma$ rises, which expands the zero-quantization region for the many moderate and small weights, pushing more of them into the interval $\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon),\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)\right)$ and thus to $0$. This behavior is consistent with the paper’s emphasis on explicit feature filtering enabled by the inclusion of $0$: the absmean scaling creates a data-adaptive sparsification mechanism where a higher global scale concentrates representational capacity on the few large-magnitude directions (quantized to $\pm 1$) while more aggressively suppressing weaker features (quantized to $0$). Hence, a larger $\gamma$ naturally increases the proportion of zeros under the stated quantization, improving the filtering effect the authors highlight.
