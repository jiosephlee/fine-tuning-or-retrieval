According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if BitNet b1.58 reverted to only $\{-1, 1\}$ weights (no $0$), the most directly lost modeling capability would be feature filtering. The paper explicitly motivates adding the $0$ value to the original 1-bit BitNet to enable “explicit support for feature filtering,” which means selectively suppressing input features or intermediate channels by setting corresponding weights to $0$. In a linear layer $y = \widetilde{W} x$, having $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$ implements a learned mask that drops input feature $x_j$ from contributing to output $y_i$, while $\widetilde{W}_{ij} \in \{-1, 1\}$ can only flip or pass features with fixed magnitude. Thus, removing $0$ eliminates the capacity to mask features at the weight level, forcing every connection to be either excitatory or inhibitory with equal strength and preventing the model from learning sparse, selective transformations that are central to filtering.

This filtering capability is tightly coupled to the absmean quantization the paper uses, $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1)$ with $\gamma = \tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, which maps small-magnitude weights to $0$ and larger ones to $\pm 1$. When $0$ is available, small or noisy connections (where $|W_{ij}| \ll \gamma$) are quantized to $0$, reducing interference and preserving signal by sparsifying $y = \widetilde{W}x$ along informative pathways; formally, the induced mask $M = \mathbf{1}[\widetilde{W} \neq 0]$ selects features so that $y_i = \sum_{j : M_{ij}=1} \mathrm{sign}(\widetilde{W}_{ij}) x_j$. If $0$ were disallowed, these small weights would be forced to $\pm 1$, injecting spurious contributions and destroying the model’s ability to gate features. Under the constrained operations of BitLinear (integer additions with 8-bit activations), this learned sparsity is the most direct architectural mechanism for filtering, so removing $0$ directly removes feature filtering capacity and degrades representational control, which the paper cites as a key reason the ternary 1.58-bit variant matches or surpasses the 1-bit baseline.
