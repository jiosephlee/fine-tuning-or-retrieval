In the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', the conceptual role of $\gamma$ in the weight quantization formula is to act as a scale factor. The quantizer maps floating weights to ternary values via
$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1\big)$ with $\gamma = \tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. Dividing by $\gamma$ normalizes the weight matrix so that one unit in the normalized domain corresponds to the average absolute magnitude of the weights. This makes the rounding thresholds ($\pm 0.5$, $\pm 1.5$ in the normalized domain) adapt to the layer’s statistics, rather than being fixed in the raw scale of $W$. Without such normalization, layers with larger or smaller overall amplitudes would either saturate to $\{-1, +1\}$ or collapse to $0$, harming modeling capacity. Thus, $\gamma$ serves as a per-matrix scale factor that renders the ternary mapping invariant to the overall magnitude of $W$ and ensures the quantizer uses the ternary alphabet in a balanced, informative way.

More concretely, because the output set is $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and there is no post-scale multiplied back, the effective decision boundaries in the original scale are set by $\gamma$: values with $|W_{ij}| < 0.5(\gamma+\epsilon)$ become $0$, and those with $|W_{ij}| \ge 0.5(\gamma+\epsilon)$ are rounded to $\pm 1$. Hence, $\gamma$ directly controls the fraction of weights mapped to $0$ (feature filtering) versus $\pm 1$ (signal passing), aligning the ternary sparsity pattern with the statistics of each layer. This adaptive scaling is essential for BitNet b1.58 to retain modeling strength while enabling the integer-addition computation paradigm: it prevents pathological quantization outcomes across layers with different norms and maintains stable training and inference by keeping the quantizer’s thresholds meaningful relative to the distribution of $W$.
