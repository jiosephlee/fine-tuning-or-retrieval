\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does adding the zero weight improve 'feature filtering' and modeling capacity relative to binary weights?
Question:
The paper claims the inclusion of 0 in weights leads to explicit support for feature filtering and significantly improves performance over 1-bit (binary) models. Can you provide an intuitive explanation or formal argument for why allowing zero improves representational capacity or optimization? Do you have measurements of learned weight sparsity over training (e.g., ternary distribution over {-1, 0, 1}) and ablations that correlate sparsity with downstream performance?
Answer:
Short answer: allowing a weight value of $0$ gives the model an explicit, learned way to turn off (filter) individual features or input channels. That both increases representational capacity relative to strict $\{-1,+1\}$ binary weights and makes optimization easier in practice. The paper argues this qualitatively and shows big empirical gains (BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 starting at 3B), but it does not publish detailed per-weight ternary histograms or a dedicated ablation that correlates learned sparsity with downstream accuracy—so some of the empirical verification you ask for is not in the paper.

Why zero helps (intuitive explanation). A binary $\{-1,+1\}$ weight can only flip sign and scale (by a global scaling factor), so every input dimension always contributes (either positively or negatively) to the output. Introducing $0$ gives a third action: completely ignore a feature. That turns each weight into a tiny learned gate (allow / block) in addition to controlling polarity. Intuitively, this supports two behaviours that are hard for pure binary weights:

1. Feature filtering / gating. When a weight is $0$ the corresponding input/component is removed from the linear combination. This is qualitatively different from just flipping sign — it can remove noisy or irrelevant features and thus reduce interference between features, which is especially useful in deep nets where many linear maps stack.

2. Sparse, selective representations. $0$ enables sparse effective connectivity: the network can route information through a small subset of pathways per token or neuron. Sparse routing increases effective capacity because the same parameter matrix can implement many different (sparser) linear maps, reducing crosstalk and improving feature reuse.

A short formal-ish view. Let $W$ be a quantized weight matrix and $x$ an input. With ternary weights we can write
$W = S \circ M$
where $S \in \{-1,+1\}^{n\times m}$ gives signs and $M \in \{0,1\}^{n\times m}$ is a mask ($\circ$ is elementwise product). Then $W x = S \circ (M x)$. The mask $M$ lets the model choose a subspace of input coordinates to use for each output dimension. In contrast, binary weights correspond to $M$ all-ones, so the model can only change signs but not select coordinates. Counting representational possibilities: for a matrix with $k$ entries, binary has $2^k$ configurations, ternary has $3^k$ — a strictly larger discrete hypothesis space. More usefully, the mask dimension produces combinatorial choices of which input columns participate, enabling simpler sparse linear maps that can approximate functions with fewer interfering terms.

Optimization benefits. From an optimization perspective, $0$ allows the training dynamics to reduce harmful contributions smoothly (via straight-through gradients or other surrogate gradients) by moving entries toward zero before deciding sign flips. That can reduce gradient noise and instability that arises when the only option is flipping sign. Practically, sparsity can also stabilise deeper networks by reducing effective layerwise magnitudes and interactions.

Link to the paper. The paper implements ternary quantization via an abs-mean scale and RoundClip to $\{-1,0,1\}$, and reports that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance from 3B up while keeping large system-level gains. The authors explicitly claim the extra $0$ value gives “explicit support for feature filtering” and “significantly improves performance over 1-bit (binary) models.” Those empirical results are the primary evidence provided.

What the paper does and does not give you about sparsity measurements. The manuscript does not include per-layer or global histograms of learned weights over $\{-1,0,1\}$, nor an ablation that varies the induced sparsity (e.g., by regularisation or by forcing different $0/\pm1$ ratios) and plots downstream accuracy versus sparsity. So while the paper shows end-to-end improvement of the ternary design versus full-precision and versus prior 1-bit BitNet in aggregate metrics (perplexity, zero-shot tasks, latency/memory/energy), it does not publish the detailed ternary-distribution statistics you asked for.

Recommended experiments / diagnostics to run (so you or future work can quantify the claim). To make the connection concrete, measure per-layer and global fractions $p_{-1}, p_0, p_{+1}$ over training checkpoints and plot them against validation perplexity. Run ablations that (a) force different target sparsity levels (e.g., add $L_1$ on pre-quantized weights or thresholding) and (b) compare performance when you collapse $0$ into $+1$ or $-1$ (i.e., enforce pure binary) while keeping the same training recipe. Also inspect per-token / input-dependent activation sparsity to see whether $0$-weights correlate with reduced feature interference. These diagnostics will show whether performance improvements track increasing or decreasing sparsity and reveal which layers benefit most from filtering.

Summary. The extra $0$ increases the discrete hypothesis space and gives an explicit masking / gating mechanism that enables feature filtering and sparser routing, which both improve representational efficiency and make optimization less brittle than pure sign flips. The paper demonstrates the practical benefit (better accuracy/efficiency trade-offs) but does not include the detailed ternary-distribution or sparsity vs. performance ablation plots you requested; running the measurements and ablations above would directly quantify the role of learned zeros.
