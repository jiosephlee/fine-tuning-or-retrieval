\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is attention softmax computed under 8-bit activations: quantized or in higher precision?
Question:
Attention involves computing \(QK^T / \sqrt{d}\), softmax, and then a weighted sum with \(V\). In BitNet b1.58, are Q, K, V projections ternary while Q, K, V activations are int8? Is the dot-product and softmax computed in FP16/FP32 after dequantizing activations, or performed partly in integer arithmetic? What precision and scaling is used to ensure numerical stability for softmax and to avoid underflow/overflow?
Answer:
Short answer: in BitNet b1.58 the projections (weights) are ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ implemented via BitLinear, activations are quantized to signed $8$-bit per the paper, and the bulk of the matmuls are implemented as integer additions/accumulations. However, the numerically sensitive softmax is not left purely in tiny integers — in practice the integer accumulation is paired with scaling and the softmax (and the division by $\sqrt{d}$) is handled in higher precision ($\text{FP16}/\text{FP32}$) after applying the appropriate scale. The paper does not give a full low-level kernel spec, but the text and the integration choices (FasterTransformer + Ladder 2-bit kernel) make this mixed integer+$\text{FP}$ pipeline the intended design.

Why this is the natural implementation and how it maintains numerical stability. The paper states weights are ternary via the absmean quantizer and activations are scaled to a signed range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to remove zero point. That gives you, for each token, an integer activation value $a_i$ in $\text{int8}$ and a known per-token scale $s_a$ so the true activation is $x_i = s_a \cdot a_i$. The ternary weight can be represented as $w_j \in \{-1,0,1\}$ with its own scale $\gamma$ (the absmean). When you compute the attention pre-softmax scores you need the quantity

$$S = \frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}.$$

If $Q$ and $K$ come from BitLinear with ternary weights and $\text{int8}$ activations, an efficient implementation computes the integer dot products (sums of products of small integers) using integer additions/accumulators. Converting that integer accumulator to the real-valued dot product is then a single scale multiplication:

$\text{real\_dot} = s_q \cdot s_k \cdot \text{int\_dot},$

where $s_q$ and $s_k$ are the scales of $Q$ and $K$ produced by their per-token/per-layer quantization (and $\text{int\_dot}$ is the integer accumulator). Then you divide by $\sqrt{d}$. That division and the softmax exponentiation/subtraction (the max trick) are numerically sensitive and are normally done in floating point ($\text{FP16}$ or $\text{FP32}$). In other words, the workflow that the paper implies and that is standard in similar low-bit LLM kernels is: do the heavy integer accumulation for throughput/energy, then dequantize the accumulator to FP and run the normalization and softmax in FP to avoid underflow/overflow.

Concrete numerical-stability points you should expect and why they matter. Softmax is unstable if you exponentiate large positive or negative numbers or if your dynamic range is very small so everything underflows. Practically this is avoided by (a) computing scores in FP after applying the integer→real scale, (b) using the subtraction-by-max trick before exponentiation:
$\mathrm{softmax}(x)_i = \frac{\exp(x_i - \max_j x_j)}{\sum_j \exp(x_j - \max_j x_j)},$
and (c) using $\text{FP16}$ or $\text{FP32}$ for the exponentials and sums. Using $\text{FP16}$ for the softmax is common and usually sufficient; $\text{FP32}$ can be used if extreme precision is required. The paper’s emphasis that “the majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation” together with their integration into FasterTransformer and a 2-bit kernel implies a mixed integer accumulation + FP post-processing approach rather than a fully integer softmax pipeline.

What the paper explicitly gives you to implement this correctly. The two relevant facts in the paper are: (1) weights are ternary via an absmean scale $\gamma$, and (2) activations are scaled per token to a signed int8 range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (so you have a per-token activation scale). Those two scales let you recover the real-valued dot product from an integer dot product with a single multiplication, after which you divide by $\sqrt{d}$ and run max-subtract + exp + normalization in FP. That pattern preserves numerical stability and avoids integer underflow/overflow in the exponentials.

Practical check / where to look in code. The paper reports using FasterTransformer and the Ladder 2-bit kernel; these codepaths typically implement integer matmuls with dequantize-to-FP accumulators before softmax. If you need to be certain for a given build, inspect the attention kernel in the FasterTransformer/Ladder integration: look for integer-accumulate -> multiply-by-scale -> cast-to-FP -> softmax steps. If you’re reimplementing, follow the integer-accumulate + single-scale dequantization + FP softmax recipe described above.

Summary: ternary weights + $\text{int8}$ activations feed fast integer accumulators; the integer dot-products are rescaled (using weight and activation scales) and then the division by $\sqrt{d}$, max-subtraction and softmax exponentials/sums are done in FP ($\text{FP16}$ or $\text{FP32}$) to ensure numerical stability. The paper doesn’t give low-level kernel bit-for-bit code, but its design and cited tooling (FasterTransformer, Ladder) point to precisely this mixed integer→FP approach.
