\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Does adding zeros reduce energy further than binary weights, and why does the paper say energy is 'the same' as 1-bit?
Question:
Intuitively, allowing 0 should skip some additions compared to binary {-1, +1}, potentially reducing energy further. However, the paper states 'it has the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet.' Are zeros actually skipped in kernels, and if so, why would the energy be the same? Is the statement meant in an asymptotic or worst-case sense rather than measured practice?
Answer:
Short answer: No contradiction — adding a $0$ value can reduce work in principle, but in the way BitNet b1.58 is implemented and measured in the paper it doesn’t translate into lower arithmetic energy than the original $1$-bit BitNet. When the authors say “the same energy consumption as the original $1$-bit BitNet” they mean under their computation model and current kernels/hardware choices (the $2$-bit kernel they use and the arithmetic-energy model they cite), not that zeros can never reduce energy in every possible implementation.

Why that is true in practice (intuitive + technical):

1) Energy model / what they measure
The paper’s energy claims focus on arithmetic energy for matrix multiply (they explicitly use an energy model from prior work). That model compares the cost of $\mathrm{FP16}$ multiply+add vs integer additions used by BitNet. It treats the bit-width and operation types ($\mathrm{FP16}$ MUL/ADD vs integer ADDs) as the main determinants. Moving from $\{-1,+1\}$ to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ does not change the fundamental operation type used in their kernels (still integer add-style computation), so the arithmetic-energy numbers are essentially the same under that model.

2) Kernel implementation: sparse-aware skipping is not automatic
A ternary weight matrix can in principle skip contributions where the weight is $0$. But that requires either:
  - a sparse representation (store indices of nonzeros and use sparse matmul), or
  - a kernel that detects zeros and avoids the multiply/add per zero (or a specialized compressed/bitpacked sparse routine/hardware).
The paper uses an efficient $2$-bit dense kernel (Ladder / FasterTransformer) and measures latency/energy with that. Those kernels treat the weight matrix as dense $2$-bit values and compute using integer-add style routines; they do not dynamically skip per-element operations for zeros. So the presence of zeros does not remove per-weight arithmetic in that implementation.

3) Memory and control overheads reduce savings
Even if you could skip adds for zeros, practical savings can be small unless sparsity is high and the system is designed for it. Fetching weights from DRAM/SRAM and controlling branches or indirect indexing for sparse ops adds energy and latency. Many hardware/accelerator designs find that the cost of index handling, memory access patterns, and control outweighs the saved add for moderate sparsity. The paper’s discussion acknowledges that most cost is in nn.Linear and memory transfer; their kernels and energy accounting thus reflect density-preserving implementations.

4) How +/-1 is handled
In many $1$-bit implementations the multiplication by $\{-1,+1\}$ is implemented as a sign-flip (a conditional negation) or by using bitplanes and integer accumulation. Those implementations are already very cheap compared to FP multiplies. Replacing some sign flips with “skip” for zeros only changes the control/dataflow; it does not necessarily reduce the dominant integer-addition or memory-transfer energy in the current kernel.

What the statement is really saying
The claim is effectively: under our chosen implementation ($2$-bit dense kernel, FasterTransformer integration) and under the cited arithmetic-energy model, BitNet b1.58’s arithmetic-energy for matrix multiplications is the same order/quantity as the original $1$-bit BitNet. This is not a universal physical law — it’s an empirical/theoretical statement relative to their energy accounting and implementation choices, not a claim that zeros can never save energy in an alternative hardware kernel.

When zeros would actually reduce measured energy
If you want measured lower energy from zeros you need one (or more) of:
  - sparse/CSR-style storage and sparse matmul kernels that avoid loading and accumulating zeros, and hardware that makes indexed loads cheap,
  - hardware that implements ternary operations natively (e.g., skip wires or gating logic for zero weights) so that both fetch and arithmetic are cheaper,
  - very high sparsity so the benefit outweighs indexing/control overhead.
Without those, adding $0$ mostly helps modeling (filtering) but not the arithmetic-energy numbers in the measured/dense-kernel setup used in the paper.

Bottom line: the paper’s “same energy consumption as original $1$-bit BitNet” is grounded in their chosen kernels and energy model (dense $2$-bit kernel + integer-add energy accounting). Zeros can save energy in principle, but only if you change the compute/memory/kernel/hardware assumptions to exploit sparsity; the paper did not assume or measure that case.
