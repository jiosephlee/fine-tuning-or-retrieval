According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', for the claimed energy and speed advantages to actually occur, the implicit hardware property that must hold is that integer operations are cheaper. This follows because BitNet b1.58 replaces FP16/BF16 matrix multiplications with operations induced by ternary weights \(W_{ij}\in\{-1,0,1\}\), so computing \(y = \widetilde{W}x\) collapses multiplications into conditional additions/subtractions (or skips) on low-precision integers, i.e., each term is \(s\cdot x\) with \(s\in\{-1,0,1\}\), which on optimized kernels becomes primarily INT8 additions; the paper explicitly models energy with prior hardware data showing \(E_{\mathrm{FP16\ multiply}} \gg E_{\mathrm{FP16\ add}} \gg E_{\mathrm{INT8\ add}}\) on 7nm, quantifies a \(71.4\times\) reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplications, and argues that when power is the performance limiter, lower per-operation energy translates into higher attainable throughput; furthermore, the memory subsystem benefits from 1.58-bit weights, cutting DRAM-to-SRAM bandwidth and hence latency, but these gains materialize only if the underlying accelerators actually execute INT additions at lower energy and higher throughput than FP operations (as assumed by their use of a 2-bit kernel and INT8 activation path), so the speed and efficiency improvements hinge on the hardware property that integer ops, especially INT8 adds, are substantially cheaper than FP16 mul/add.
