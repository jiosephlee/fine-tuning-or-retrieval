According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', to achieve the performance parity claimed for BitNet b1.58, the implicitly preferred training approach over post-training quantization is quantization-aware training. This follows because the authors explicitly deem post-training quantization “sub-optimal,” then train BitNet b1.58 from scratch with weights constrained to ternary values via an in-the-loop quantizer, concretely applying an absmean-based mapping \( \widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\tfrac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right) \) with \( \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}| \), and using 8-bit activations scaled per token to \([ -Q_b, Q_b]\) without zero-point—i.e., the optimization directly accounts for quantization effects on both weights and activations rather than retrofitting them after training; they then show this procedure matches FP16 perplexity and end-task accuracy starting at 3B parameters while delivering major efficiency gains. Moreover, BitNet b1.58’s key advantages—e.g., leveraging the explicit zero in \(\{-1,0,1\}\) for feature filtering and adopting LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases)—require the model to learn under the ternary and 8-bit constraints so that representational capacity is reallocated appropriately; such adaptation arises during quantization-aware training, not from post hoc weight rounding. The reported scaling of latency, memory, and energy benefits with model size is tied to the architecture’s BitLinear operations and the ternary/INT8 arithmetic pathway, which the network must internalize during training to preserve accuracy, further reinforcing that the path to performance parity is training-time quantization rather than post-training quantization.
