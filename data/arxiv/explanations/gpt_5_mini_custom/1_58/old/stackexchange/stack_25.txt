### How well does BitNet b1.58 map to CPUs and edge NPUs in practice?
Question:
The paper claims 1.58-bit LLMs are CPU-friendly. Modern CPUs have fast INT8 dot-product (e.g., AVX-512 VNNI) but still perform multiplies. Do you have actual benchmarks on CPUs or edge NPUs showing speed/energy gains from using ternary weights with add/sub operations? Are there existing instruction sets that exploit ternary encoding efficiently?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the claim that “$1.58$-bit LLMs are CPU‐friendly” is really a projection based on the arithmetic model and on GPU experiments, rather than on full CPU or edge‐NPU wall‐clock measurements. Concretely, all of the published runtime and energy numbers in the paper come from an optimized GPU rollout (NVIDIA A100 via FasterTransformer), plus an analytical energy model (from [energycost, pokebnn]) that estimates a $71\times$ reduction in arithmetic energy for the core matrix multiplications on a 7 nm process. There are, however, no direct x86 or Arm CPU benchmarks, nor any measurements on commercial edge NPUs, in the paper itself.

Why do we expect CPU (and tiny‐NPU) wins? With ternary weights \(w\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), each multiply‐accumulate  
\[
\sum_j w_j x_j
\]
becomes “if \(w_j=+1\) add \(x_j\); if \(w_j=-1\) subtract \(x_j\); if \(w_j=0\) do nothing.” In principle you can implement that in two vector steps:  
1) form a sign‐mask vector \(\mathit{sgn}_j\in\{+1,-1\}\) (e.g.\ from a packed 2-bit representation),  
2) perform a masked add/sub (e.g.\ using AVX-512’s VPADDB/VPSUBB with a zeroing mask for the \(w_j=0\) slots).  

On modern x86 cores the throughput of vector adds/subs is typically higher (and lower‐power) than that of vector multiply-adds, and you avoid the multiplier pipeline altogether. Moreover, skipping the zero weights reduces total arithmetic and memory traffic. All of this points to a $2$–$4\times$ speed-up over FP16 GEMM, mirroring the $2.7$–$4.1\times$ gains seen on GPU, and to a large cut in energy per operation.

That said, mainstream CPUs today only expose generic INT8 or FP16 dot-product instructions (e.g.\ AVX-512 VNNI), which still encode full multiplies under the hood. There is no standardized “ternary‐GEMM” instruction in x86 or Arm Neoverse, nor in most edge NPUs. To really unlock the full 1-bit paradigm you’d need either  
  • a custom software kernel that packs the two sign bits and drives ultra-tight masked ADD/SUB loops, or  
  • new vector instructions (or NPU primitives) that fuse “load-2-bit-packed weights + add/sub” in a single cycle.  

The paper points out (in its “Future Work” and “New Hardware” sections) that this is exactly the kind of thing hardware designers could target—Groq’s LPU or a bespoke 1-bit accelerator could implement ternary adds in hardware, yielding the theoretical $70\times$ energy win for the key GEMM kernels.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 offers a compelling arithmetic‐energy and GPU‐latency story, but no direct CPU or edge-NPU benchmarks appear in the paper. The path to a $2$–$4\times$ speed-up on real CPUs or small NPUs is clear in principle—replace multiplies with vector adds/subs and exploit zeros—but will require hand-tuned kernels or new ISA extensions to fully realize.
