### How precise are the energy savings estimates on 7 nm and which operations dominate?
Question:
The paper cites a 71.4× arithmetic energy saving for matmul based on references for 7 nm. Are these per-operation energy figures for FP16 add/mul vs INT8 add still representative on modern GPUs/NPUs? How sensitive is the saving if, in practice, memory movement dominates energy over arithmetic? Do you include SRAM/DRAM access costs in the end-to-end energy numbers?
Answer:
In the paper the “$71.4\times$” figure comes from a classic per-operation energy model at the $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ node (e.g.\ Horowitz 2014, Pokelab’s follow-up), which tells you roughly how much energy a single $\mathrm{INT8}$ add costs vs one $\mathrm{FP16}$ add $+$ $\mathrm{FP16}$ mul. It is important to stress three points:

1) That $71.4\times$ is a _pure-arithmetic_ number, not a GPU or NPU measurement. It assumes you have separate $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ circuits running those elementary ALUs in isolation. In practice, modern GPUs/TPUs fuse arithmetic into wide vector or systolic arrays, amortize control overhead, clock-gate idles, and sometimes do $\mathrm{FP16}$ or $\mathrm{INT8}$ on the same physical units. Empirical studies of, say, NVIDIA Ampere or Google TPUv4 typically report only a $\sim2\text{--}8\times$ energy gap between $\mathrm{INT8}$ and $\mathrm{FP16}$ for _equivalent_ matrix-multiply throughput, not $71\times$.

2) _In real accelerators_ memory movement usually _dominates_ arithmetic energy once your operand sizes and reuse patterns are fixed. If you let
   \[
     \alpha = \text{fraction of total inference energy that is pure mat-mul arithmetic},
   \]
   then replacing that arithmetic with a $71\times$ “cheaper” version yields an overall speedup of only
   $$
     \frac{1}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha/71}\,.
   $$
   So if $\alpha$ is $50\%$ of your energy budget, you end up with only $\approx2\times$ total savings. If $\alpha$ is $20\%$, the end-to-end gain is $\approx1.23\times$. In many LLM inference workloads the on-chip SRAM reads, register-file accesses, interconnect routing, and DRAM fetches together can swamp the math, driving $\alpha$ well below $50\%$.

3) In the paper’s _energy section_ we explicitly state that the $71.4\times$ is an _arithmetic_ estimate for matrix-multiply FLOPs. When we then quote “end-to-end” energy (for a 512-token inference), we _add up_ the arithmetic cost of all mat-muls plus the arithmetic cost of other operators (layer-norms, softmax, etc.), all under the _same_ $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ energy model. We do _not_ explicitly model DRAM access energy for streaming in weights or activations, nor the full SRAM network energy in a real GPU. In other words, our “system” cost still abstracts away off-chip traffic.

Taken together, this means:

• The $71.4\times$ number is a _best-case_ arithmetic savings at $7\,\mathrm{nm}$, not a measured GPU/TPU end-to-end reduction.  
• On-device, dedicated mixed-precision units will narrow that gap to something more like $2\text{--}8\times$.  
• If memory transfers dominate your power (often the case once you tile big GEMMs), then the net system-level win will be well below even that.  
• A truly end-to-end energy study needs to fold in SRAM/DRAM, interconnect, PE setup, and possibly cooling/PSU inefficiencies—which the current paper does not.

So you should view the $71.4\times$ as a “physics-of-circuits” bound on arithmetic savings, a useful lower bound, but not the final word on real-world GPU/NPU energy reductions when everything else (memory, control, I/O) is taken into account.
