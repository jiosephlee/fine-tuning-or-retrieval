### What is the end-to-end energy model including memory traffic?
Question:
Arithmetic energy is only part of total energy. For end-to-end inference, how do memory bandwidth and DRAM/SRAM transfers compare between 1.58-bit and FP16, especially for large models with pipeline/tensor parallelism? Do you account for packing/unpacking ternary weights and any overheads in real deployments?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper you correctly point out that arithmetic energy (the $71\times$ reduction in FP-mult/add cost for matrix-multiply on a $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ process) is only part of the story. For any realistic inference workload, you must also budget the energy to move bits in and out of both on-chip SRAM (scratchpads and register files) and off-chip DRAM. In fact, as model size grows and you do pipeline or tensor-parallel inference, the memory-energy term can dominate. Here is how we think about the end-to-end model, what numbers you can pull from the paper, and how packing/unpacking fits in.

1. The high-level “roofline” energy model  
   We decompose total energy per output token as  
   $$ 
     E_{\rm total} = E_{\rm compute}
                   + E_{\rm SRAM}
                   + E_{\rm DRAM}\,. 
   $$  
   • $E_{\rm compute}$ is dominated by the giant GEMMs (nn.Linear). In the paper’s Figure 7 (left panel) that is where you get the $71\times$ savings at $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ by going from FP16 mul+add to INT8 add-only.  
   • $E_{\rm SRAM}$ covers all on-chip reads/writes—loading weight tiles from SRAM into the PE’s registers, moving KV-cache activations around, etc.  
   • $E_{\rm DRAM}$ is every fetch from DRAM (or HBM) into on-chip SRAM: model-weight loads at startup, shuffling pipeline checkpoints, etc.  

2. How much less memory traffic?  
   Because BitNet b1.58 uses $1.58$ bits per weight (ternary, packed into $2$ bits) versus $16$ bits for FP16, you immediately cut the weight-traffic volume by up to $8\times$. Activations are stored as $8$ bits instead of $16$ bits, which is a $2\times$ cut there. In a real system you’ll usually pack ternary weights into $8$-bit lanes (so a $4\times$ cut instead of $8\times$), but even then you halve the activation footprint. Concretely:  
   – If you can keep all weights resident in on-chip SRAM (as is common once you shard a $70\,\mathrm{B}$ model across multiple A100s in pipeline parallel), you essentially eliminate DRAM reads for each attention/mat-mul, amortizing one DRAM load per model partition at startup over thousands of tokens.  
   – The KV cache (which grows linearly with sequence length) is now $8$ bits per element instead of $16$ bits. In our 512-token measurements this doubles the maximum context you could squeeze in the same SRAM footprint.  

3. Energy numbers in the paper  
   In Figure 7 (right panel) the authors superimpose an “end-to-end” energy curve for a 512-token inference. That plot folds in (a) the arithmetic energy from the left panel, (b) a simple DRAM/SRAM-access model taken from Horowitz (2014) as re-used in [energycost, pokebnn], and (c) the much smaller overhead of layernorms, softmax, etc. You can read off that BitNet b1.58 still saves on the order of $10\times$–$25\times$ total energy from $3\,\mathrm{B}$ all the way to $70\,\mathrm{B}$, even once memory is included.  

4. Packing/unpacking and real-world overheads  
   – In our NVIDIA-optimized kernels (FasterTransformer + Ladder’s 2-bit matmul), the packing of ternary weights into 2-bit-packed format and the tiny unpack step on the PE is fused into the GEMM inner loop. The extra cost is on the order of a few pJ per weight fetch—an $O(1)$ overhead that is buried beneath the massive savings of the $8\times$ smaller traffic and $71\times$ cheaper add-only compute.  
   – If you were to implement this on a non-specialized CPU or GPU, you might see an extra pass to bit-pack and then unpack into byte lanes; that can add maybe $10\text{–}20\%$ to the raw mat-mul overhead, but even that is small compared to the $4\times$–$8\times$ reduction in total memory volume plus the $70\times$ arithmetic reduction.  

5. Pipeline/tensor-parallel considerations  
   When you shard a $70\,\mathrm{B}$ model across, say, four GPUs, each device holds only $\sim17.5\,\mathrm{B}$ of weights. BitNet b1.58 brings that down to $\sim3.5\,\mathrm{B}$ of raw ternary data (or $\sim7\,\mathrm{B}$ if you pack in bytes). Thus each device’s DRAM-to-SRAM energy at startup is slashed by $4\times$–$8\times$. During steady-state autoregression, almost all weight reuse happens inside SRAM/registers, so the only ongoing memory-energy is the 8-bit KV cache peeks and/or cross-GPU activation swaps in pipeline parallel. And there, too, you’ve cut that by $2\times$ versus FP16.  

In summary, the paper’s “$71\times$ arithmetic” claim is only the first pillar. When you fold in Horowitz-style DRAM/SRAM energy, the authors show (in Figure 7 right) that BitNet b1.58 still wins by an order of magnitude end to end. Packing/unpacking costs are either fused into the accelerator kernel (for custom hardware or optimized GPU code) or negligible on commodity devices. And as you scale to hundreds-of-billions of parameters in pipeline or tensor-parallel setups, the smaller weight-footprint both shrinks the one-time DRAM loading cost and eliminates a DRAM bottleneck, further widening the real-world energy advantage over FP16.
