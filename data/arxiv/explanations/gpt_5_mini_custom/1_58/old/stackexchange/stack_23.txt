### How does BitNet b1.58 handle long-context inference beyond 8-bit KV caches?
Question:
You state activations are 8-bit and that this enables doubling context length. You also suggest lossless compression to 4 bits or lower for 1.58-bit LLMs as future work. Is there a scheme to store K/V at 4 bits without accuracy loss (e.g., per-head scaling with blockwise quantization)? How does per-token scaling complicate this for cached keys/values?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58, the key/value (KV) cache is treated exactly like any other activation: after the matrix‐multiply produces the raw FP16/KV vectors, they are quantized to 8 bits by first computing a per‐token scale and then rounding into the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. Because attention must store these K and V vectors over all past tokens, moving from 16 bits down to 8 bits halves the memory footprint of the cache and thus, for a fixed memory budget, roughly doubles the maximum context length you can support.

You correctly observe that in the paper we mention “lossless compression to 4 bits or even lower” as future work. At present, BitNet b1.58 does **not** implement a 4-bit KV cache—everything remains at 8 bits—and so there is no off-the-shelf, zero-accuracy-loss scheme described in the paper. The crux of the difficulty is that our 8-bit activation quantization is **per token**, not global: for each new token $t$, we compute

$$
s_t \;=\; \max_{i}\bigl|\hat{a}_{t,i}\bigr|\,,\quad 
q_{t,i} \;=\;\round\!\bigl(\hat{a}_{t,i}/s_t\bigr)
\quad\text{clipped to }[-Q_b, Q_b],
$$

and then store $\{q_{t,i},\,s_t\}$. If you were to halve the bit-width to 4 bits **without** changing the scheme, you would end up with only 16 quantization levels—which, given the per-token scaling, would introduce non-negligible rounding error unless you somehow also increase the overhead in side-information (e.g.\ storing residuals or finer-grained scales).

One plausible path toward “lossless” 4-bit KV caching would be to move from **per-token** scaling to a more coarse-grained “per-head” or “per-block” scaling factor. In that scenario you might decide:
1. Partition each attention head’s sequence of $d$-dimensional K (or V) vectors into blocks of $B$ tokens.
2. For each block, compute a single scale $s_{h,b}$ (where $h$ is head index and $b$ is block index).
3. Quantize the entire block’s activations into 16 levels (4 bits) using that shared scale.

By raising the quantization granularity (fewer distinct scales), you regain enough headroom in each block that the 4-bit dynamic range can still cover the variation across all tokens in that block. In principle, if the variation within a block is small, you can make the 4-bit fit the 8-bit → 4-bit mapping exactly (i.e.\ “losslessly”) by packing extra metadata or choosing your block sizes so that the original 8-bit values lie exactly on the 4-bit grid. In practice, however, per-head or per-block scaling will incur some mismatch unless you either (a) store residual bits (which offsets your savings) or (b) choose your blocks very narrowly (which raises overhead again).

In short, BitNet b1.58’s current inference engine simply halves the activations to 8 bits via per-token scales and leaves KV caches at 8 bits. Any **future** scheme to go to 4 bits **without** degrading accuracy must grapple with the fact that every token’s KV vector comes with its own scale. Coarsening scales to heads or blocks is the natural remedy, but it trades off flexibility (and thus introduces quantization error) against the savings in scale-storage overhead. Designing the perfect block- or head-wise quantizer that remains truly lossless—and experimentally validating that it matches full 8-bit fidelity—remains an open question that the paper earmarks for follow-up work.
