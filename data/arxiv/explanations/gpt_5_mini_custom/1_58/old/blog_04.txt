# The new scaling law: Planning model size and cost with 1.58-bit LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have grown tremendously in size and power over the past few years, but this progress hasn’t come for free. As model sizes move from billions to tens or even hundreds of billions of parameters, cloud bills grow, latency increases, and memory requirements skyrocket. Enter BitNet b1.58, a ternary (−1, 0, +1) variant of Transformer LLMs that uses just $1.58$ bits per weight. Remarkably, BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and end-task performance of full-precision (FP16) models at the same scale while slashing inference costs—in GPU memory, latency, throughput, and energy—by multiple factors.

In this post, we’ll introduce a new “scaling law” for model planning: how to translate a target inference budget into the right model choice when you have the option of deploying $1.58$-bit LLMs. You’ll learn at what scale BitNet b1.58 reaches performance parity with FP16, the cost-performance equivalences across different sizes, and practical guidance for capacity planning and product strategy.

## When does 1.58-bit parity kick in?

Every AI team asking “Should I quantize?” wants to know: at what model size does a low-bit model match the full-precision baseline? For BitNet b1.58, extensive experiments show that starting around a 3 billion-parameter model, the ternary variant matches FP16 LLaMA in both perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy. Below that threshold, the small models lose a bit of language modeling finesse, but above it, you get identical accuracy at a fraction of the cost.

In practice, this means:

  • If you need a tiny prototype around 1 B–2 B parameters, you might stick with FP16 for simplicity.  
  • Once you hit 3 B parameters and beyond, BitNet b1.58 seamlessly steps in without any performance trade-off.  

From that 3 B sweet spot upward, you can scale model capacity more cheaply and still maintain top-tier results.

## The new equivalence chart: cost vs. capacity

With BitNet b1.58 in your toolbox, you can establish cost-performance equivalences. Based on measured latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption, the paper identifies three key pairings between $1.58$-bit models and their FP16 cousins:

  • **13 B BitNet b1.58 ≃ 3 B FP16 LLM**  
    In latency, memory, and energy terms, deploying a 13 billion-parameter ternary model costs about the same as a 3 billion-parameter full-precision model.  

  • **30 B BitNet b1.58 ≃ 7 B FP16 LLM**  
    If you aim for performance similar to a 7 B FP16 model, simply double your bit-efficient model to 30 B, and you pay no extra inference cost.  

  • **70 B BitNet b1.58 ≃ 13 B FP16 LLM**  
    For large deployments, a 70 B ternary model runs at roughly the same latency and memory budget as a 13 B FP16 instance—yet delivers more capacity and often better accuracy on downstream tasks.

These equivalences define a fresh scaling law: every time you want to boost model size by roughly a factor of four (3 B→13 B, 7 B→30 B, 13 B→70 B), you can do so by switching from FP16 to BitNet’s $1.58$-bit format and pay virtually no extra cost.

## Translating cloud budgets into model choices

Suppose your team has settled on an inference budget—say, you can afford the latency and GPU memory footprint of a 7 B FP16 model. With BitNet b1.58, you suddenly gain the option to run a 30 B model under that same budget. That means:

  • **Richer features**: A larger model typically captures more language nuances, context, and factual knowledge.  
  • **Future-proofing**: As new tasks arise, your 30 B model may generalize better than a 7 B.  
  • **Fixed cost**: You don’t have to renegotiate VM sizes or double your cloud spending.  

Conversely, if you’ve been running a 13 B FP16 model and find its throughput or memory usage limiting, migrating to a 70 B $1.58$-bit model unlocks more capacity while keeping inference costs constant.

## Picking the right model size

Here’s a quick rule of thumb for product or platform teams considering BitNet b1.58:

  • **Entry-level (up to 3 B parameters)**  
    – Use FP16 if you want simplicity and you’re not yet hitting memory or latency limits.  
  • **Mid-tier (3 B–13 B parameters)**  
    – Switch to $1.58$-bit to maintain performance parity while enjoying 2–3× faster inference and similar memory savings.  
  • **Large-scale (13 B–70 B parameters and beyond)**  
    – Embrace $1.58$-bit to quadruple your model size without increasing your cloud bill, plus save on energy, which is increasingly critical at hyperscale.  

Of course, choosing model size also depends on your data, prompt complexity, and downstream task demands. But if you’re hitting cost ceilings on FP16 inference, BitNet b1.58 immediately expands your leeway.

## Implications for product strategy and capacity planning

Adopting $1.58$-bit LLMs has ripple effects beyond just raw cost savings. Here are some areas to consider:

  • **Cloud spend optimization**  
    As providers charge based on GPU type and hours, reducing memory usage and latency directly shrinks your unit economics. You may pack more model shards per GPU or reduce autoscaling spikes during peak traffic.  
  • **Faster iteration**  
    When training or fine-tuning at 1.58 bits, experiments run quicker and require less device memory. You can spin up larger baselines at lower cost, accelerating your research and development cycle.  
  • **Energy and sustainability**  
    By cutting arithmetic energy costs by over 70× on matrix multiplications alone, you reduce your carbon footprint and align with green computing goals. This can become a competitive advantage for responsible AI.  
  • **Edge and on-device AI**  
    Memory-constrained environments like mobile or embedded hardware can host larger models than ever before, thanks to the ternary weight format and 8-bit activations.  

In short, 1.58-bit models let you re-imagine capacity planning: instead of “How small a model can I squeeze onto this GPU?”, you can ask “How big a model can I run within my existing budget?”

## Looking forward

BitNet b1.58 is just the beginning of a broader shift toward ultra-efficient LLMs. As hardware evolves to support integer-only arithmetic, and software runtimes optimize ternary kernels, we expect these cost-performance benefits to only widen. Moreover, specialized chips designed around $1$-bit or $1.58$-bit operations can push the envelope further in edge AI and large-scale inference.

For teams and organizations planning their next LLM deployment, the message is clear: with the new scaling law of $1.58$-bit LLMs, you can achieve larger models, higher throughput, and lower energy—all without paying extra. It’s time to rethink your model size roadmap and unlock the full potential of efficient, high-performance LLMs.
