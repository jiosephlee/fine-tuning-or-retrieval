According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', for the claimed energy and speed advantages to actually occur, the implicit hardware property that must hold is that integer operations are cheaper. The paper’s core efficiency argument hinges on replacing FP16/BF16 multiply–accumulate in Transformer linear layers with operations induced by ternary weights \( \widetilde{W} \in \{-1,0,1\} \). With ternary weights, matrix multiplication reduces to additions, subtractions, and zero-skips on INT8 activations, i.e., computing \( y = \widetilde{W}x \) entails no floating-point multiplications and predominantly INT8 additions. The authors explicitly ground their energy claims in hardware energy models (citing prior work) and report that, on 7nm chips, arithmetic energy for these integer additions is dramatically lower than for FP16 addition and multiplication, yielding a reported \( \sim 71.4\times \) reduction for matrix multiplication. They also note that “the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power,” so when per-operation energy is lower for integer ops, the same power budget can sustain more operations per unit time, translating directly into higher throughput and lower latency.

Furthermore, all the system-level advantages they measure—lower latency, higher throughput, and reduced end-to-end energy—are tied to narrower data paths and cheaper primitives. BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations and 1.58-bit weights, reducing memory bandwidth and DRAM-to-SRAM transfer energy, which is proportional to the number of bits moved; this is only beneficial if hardware treats INT8 transfers and operations as cheaper than FP16. Their latency scaling with model size is explained by the growing share of time in \( \mathrm{nn.Linear} \), where BitNet replaces FP16 multiply–accumulate with optimized 2-bit/INT8 kernels; the reported \(4.1\times\) speedup at 70B presumes that \(E(\text{INT8 add}) \ll E(\text{FP16 mul})\) and that hardware pipelines can exploit this difference. In short, the paper’s computational recipe—ternary weights, INT8 additions, zero-point avoidance, and sparse-like filtering via zeros—yields the claimed energy and speed advantages precisely under the widely observed, implicit hardware property that integer operations (and narrower memory transactions) are cheaper than floating-point ones.
