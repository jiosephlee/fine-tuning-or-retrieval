\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 9: Related Methods and Conceptual Connections

# Linear attention approaches: Synthesizer, Linformer, Performer, Hyena, GLA
A central motivation behind linear-attention methods is to remove the quadratic dependency on context length that standard Transformer attention introduces. The paper groups several lines of work under this umbrella and describes them as different strategies for approximating or replacing the full token–token dot-product softmax attention while keeping the Transformer-style computation graph.

Synthesizer replaces learned token-token interactions by synthetic attention weights that do not depend on pairwise token comparisons. Linformer projects the attention matrix into a low-rank factorization so that attention can be computed with linear complexity in the sequence length. Performer obtains a linearized attention by replacing the softmax kernel with a positive-random-feature approximation so that attention becomes a sum of feature maps and thus linear in context length. Hyena departs from attention entirely by using structured global convolutions (long convolutions) to capture long-range dependencies; in the paper Hyena is presented as another route to linear complexity. Gated Linear Attention (GLA) incorporates gating into linear attention mechanisms to modulate contributions across the sequence.

What unites these methods in the paper’s treatment is the objective of achieving scalability to long contexts by changing the way tokens interact: either by approximating the softmax attention kernel, by low-rank compression, by replacing token–token comparisons with learned or structured filters, or by using gating to reweight contributions. These approaches are contrasted with xLSTM’s strategy: xLSTM does not rely on explicit pairwise attention during inference but instead embeds long-term storage and retrieval mechanisms into recurrent memory structures (scalar or matrix) that are linear in context length and—when designed appropriately—parallelizable.

# State Space Models: S4, DSS, S5, H3, Mamba, BiGS
State Space Models (SSMs) are another family of approaches the paper highlights as recent and popular alternatives to full attention. The SSM line (including S4, DSS, S5, H3 and related variants) achieves linearity in the context length by modeling sequence dynamics via linear state-space recurrences that can be implemented efficiently with structured kernels or specialized discretizations. These models typically embed the input sequence into a latent linear dynamical system whose impulse response can be convolved with the inputs to produce outputs; because convolutions can be implemented with linear cost (often with FFTs, kernel structure, or recurrence tricks), SSMs scale to long contexts.

The paper treats Mamba as a high-performing SSM variant; BiGS and related gated SSM variants introduce gating to combine linear state evolution with multiplicative control, thereby increasing flexibility. The SSM family is presented as a complementary route to long-context modeling: like linear attention and unlike standard Transformers, SSMs are asymptotically linear in the context length and have been shown to be competitive on language modeling and long-range tasks.

Within the paper’s scope, SSMs are contrasted with RNNs and xLSTM in two respects. First, SSMs generally do not include memory mixing of the recurrent hidden–hidden type used in classical LSTM; second, the paper argues that this lack of memory mixing constrains state-tracking expressivity in some tasks. Empirically, the paper compares xLSTM to leading SSMs (e.g., Mamba, H3) and finds that xLSTM performs favorably, while noting conceptual differences in how histories are represented and mixed.

# RNN alternatives: LRUs, HGRN/HGRN2, RWKV
The recent revival of recurrent architectures is represented in the paper by several models that seek to combine the efficiency of recurrence (linear in context length) with modern scaling and training practices. Deep Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs) are one such direction; they preserve linear recurrence in specially designed linear submodules and showed promising language-modeling results in prior work. HGRN and HGRN2 introduce hierarchical gating structures inside RNNs to control information flow at multiple time scales. RWKV is a particularly noteworthy family: it is an RNN-style model whose update rule, when implemented carefully, yields efficient long-context behavior competitive with Transformers. The paper cites RWKV variants (RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6) as empirical baselines in comparisons.

These RNN-based approaches are linked in the paper to xLSTM by common desiderata: linear complexity in context length and strong gating mechanisms. Yet the paper emphasizes structural differences. Some modern RNNs trade away certain LSTM traits (for example, recurrent hidden–hidden mixing) to achieve parallelization or other computational benefits; RWKV in particular exemplifies how an RNN-like recurrence can be reformulated for improved runtime behavior. xLSTM situates itself among these approaches by offering two complementary variants: sLSTM, which retains recurrent memory mixing and introduces exponential gating; and mLSTM, which adopts a matrix memory and a covariance-style update rule permitting a parallel reformulation. Thus, xLSTM bridges classical LSTM expressivity (via memory mixing) and the parallel/linear-time aims of modern RNNs.

# Gating as a unifying principle across architectures
A recurring theme emphasized in the paper is that gating—originally central to LSTM’s success—is a unifying and powerful mechanism that has been rediscovered across many modern architectures. The paper explicitly lists HGRN, HGRN2, GLA, GSS (gated SSMs), BiGS, MEGA, RWKV, and Mamba as recent models that incorporate gating in various forms. In xLSTM, gating is taken further: the authors introduce exponential gating (replacing sigmoid gating for some gates) and pair it with stabilization and normalization to enable stronger, more flexible modulation of memory updates.

Intuitively, gating supplies a multiplicative control on how past representations are preserved, forgotten, or combined with new inputs. In LSTM the gates (input $i_t$, forget $f_t$, output $o_t$) enter the canonical update c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t and govern additive memory dynamics. The paper argues that modern models sustain this insight: even when attention, convolution, or linear state-space kernels are used, gating is often reintroduced to selectively control contributions across time steps or feature channels. What differs across models is the form of the gate (additive vs. multiplicative, sigmoid vs. exponential), where in the computation graph it is applied, and whether gates depend on hidden state, input, or both. xLSTM’s exponential gates—together with a normalizer that tracks cumulative gate strength—are presented as a principled extension that remedies specific LSTM limitations (notably the inability to revise previously stored content) while preserving the benefits of multiplicative control.

# Covariance-based updates in RetNet, Linear Transformer, RWKV, HGRN2
The paper highlights a shared lineage of ideas centered on covariance or outer-product-based memory updates. The mLSTM’s matrix memory uses the covariance (outer product) update rule

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

which the authors relate directly to earlier notions of Bidirectional Associative Memories and to Fast Weight Programmers. This update stores associations between keys $\mathbf{k}_t$ and values $\mathbf{v}_t$ in a single matrix $\mathbf{C}$, enabling retrieval via a matrix–vector product $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$. The paper notes that with appropriate scaling and gating, this covariance-style memory offers high separability of stored items (high signal/noise) under the assumption of zero-mean keys.

Several recent methods are cited as building on related ideas. Fast Weight Programmers historically used outer-product updates to create short-term associative memories. Contemporary models that exploit similar covariance or linearized attention principles include Linear Transformer variants, Retention, RWKV-5/RWKV-6, and HGRN2. The paper positions mLSTM among these approaches, but with a distinct integration into the LSTM framework: the forget gate acts as a decay on the matrix memory (analogous to weight decay of fast weights), the input gate acts like a learning rate for the new outer product term, and the output gate scales the retrieved vector when producing the hidden state. By framing covariance updates inside a gated recurrent framework, xLSTM connects the associative memory literature with modern gating-enabled sequence models.

# Distinctive feature of xLSTM: memory mixing enables state tracking and expressivity
One of the paper’s central conceptual claims is that “memory mixing”—the hidden–hidden recurrent connections that allow different memory cells to interact across time—is a distinguishing feature that endows recurrent models like LSTMs (and sLSTM) with a form of expressivity not available to many SSMs or vanilla Transformer-like architectures. Memory mixing means that the hidden state at the previous timestep participates directly in computing the current gates and cell inputs via learned recurrent weight matrices; this creates a feedback loop among memory cells that can implement complex state machines and carry persistent, interacting state over arbitrarily long horizons.

The paper supports this claim empirically and conceptually. Empirically, it shows that models lacking memory mixing (e.g., many Transformers and SSMs) fail to solve certain formal-language tasks and regular grammars that require state tracking, such as parity-like problems. Conceptually, it cites recent theoretical and empirical work indicating that RNNs with recurrent inter-cell mixing can represent state machines and carry out stateful computations that are provably or practically more difficult for architectures lacking such mixing. sLSTM retains and extends this capability by combining memory mixing with exponential gating, which improves the ability to revise previously stored information (an explicit failure mode of vanilla LSTM discussed in the paper). mLSTM, in contrast, drops memory mixing in order to attain a fully parallelizable update rule based on matrix memories; the paper emphasizes that while mLSTM sacrifices inter-cell mixing, it gains much larger per-step storage capacity via the matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ and can be implemented with parallel operations analogous to linear attention.

Thus, the paper positions xLSTM not as a mere rehash of earlier linear or gated architectures but as a deliberate family that spans a design space: sLSTM sits closer to classical LSTM with enhanced gating and preserved memory mixing (for state-tracking power), while mLSTM sits closer to associative fast-weight models with high-capacity, parallelizable matrix memories. By combining these two variants within a residual, block-stacked architecture (xLSTM blocks and xLSTM architectures), the design aims to capture the best of both worlds—stateful mixing where needed and large parallel associative memory where beneficial—while leveraging gating as the organizing principle.

# Synthesis: how the literature connects to xLSTM’s design choices
Taken together, the survey in the paper reveals recurring motifs across modern sequence models: the importance of gating, the desire to achieve linear complexity in context length, and the use of associative or covariance-style memories to increase storage capacity. Linear attention methods and SSMs focus on computation and scaling, often approximating or replacing token–token interactions with cheaper operations; modern RNN variants concentrate on efficient recurrences and gating; and covariance-based updates reconnect to an older line of associative memory research that is proving useful again in large-scale models.

xLSTM is presented in the paper as an integrative response to this landscape. It adopts exponential and stabilized gates (extending the long-standing LSTM gating idea), introduces a matrix covariance memory to boost storage capacity (linking to fast weights and linearized attention ideas), and retains the option of memory mixing (preserving the state-tracking expressivity of classical recurrent networks). Where other methods emphasize one axis (speed/parallelism, low-rank approximations, or gating for control), xLSTM explicitly designs two complementary variants—sLSTM for mixing-driven expressivity and mLSTM for high-capacity, parallel associative memory—then composes them inside residual blocks to achieve competitive performance while drawing on the same conceptual building blocks seen throughout the recent literature.
