\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 12: Implementing and Training xLSTM in Practice

# Gate parameterization, biases, and activation choice for forget gates
xLSTM extends the classic LSTM gating design in two ways that are central to both expressivity and stability. First, the input gate in both sLSTM and mLSTM uses an exponential activation,
i.e. for the raw pre-activation $\tilde{i}_t$ the gate is
$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ (see Equations (4) and (10) in the paper). Second, the forget gate may be implemented either as a conventional sigmoid,
$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$, or as an exponential,
$f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$, depending on whether you prefer a bounded retention factor or want the revisable, multiplicative dynamics that exponentials permit. The output gate remains a sigmoid $o_t=\sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$.

Structurally, xLSTM keeps the usual affine parameterization of gate pre-activations: each gate pre-activation $\tilde{g}_t$ (for $g\in\{i,f,o\}$) is computed from a linear projection of inputs (and in sLSTM also from the previous hidden state if memory mixing is enabled), plus a bias term. The paper’s ablation studies report incremental gains when gates are learnable and input-dependent; in other words, keep the gates parameterized by trainable weights on inputs (and on hidden state where applicable) and include learnable biases. That design gives the model the flexibility to modulate storage and decay dynamically.

Practically, if you want the revisability behavior highlighted in the paper (e.g., to allow an earlier stored item to be diminished once a stronger later candidate arrives), adopt exponential input gating and consider an exponential forget gate. If you prefer bounded forget dynamics (more conservative retention), use sigmoid for $f_t$. The sLSTM formulation allows richer memory-mixing when recurrent connections are used, while mLSTM removes hidden–hidden recurrence and therefore typically uses input-dependent gates (no recurrent term in Equations (10)–(12) of the paper).

# Stabilization hyperparameters: denominator threshold and log-max trick
Two stabilization mechanisms in xLSTM are essential when using exponential gates and matrix memory.

1) Log-max stabilizer for exponential gates. Exponentials can overflow or produce numerically huge values. The paper stabilizes the pair of exponential gates (input and forget) with an auxiliary stabilizer state $m_t$ computed as
\[
m_t \;=\; \max\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t)\bigr),
\]
and then uses the shifted versions
\[
i'_t = \exp\bigl(\log(i_t) - m_t\bigr),\qquad
f'_t = \exp\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\]
Replacing $i_t,f_t$ by $i'_t,f'_t$ leaves the network outputs and parameter gradients unchanged but prevents numerical overflow by keeping the effective multiplicative factors in a numerically stable range. This is the log-max trick (Equation (5) in the paper). Implementing $m_t$ and using $i'_t,f'_t$ wherever $i_t,f_t$ appear is therefore required when exponential activations are used.

2) Normalizer denominator threshold for mLSTM. In the mLSTM the retrieved vector is normalized by the dot product between the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$. To avoid division by values near zero (which would amplify noise and cause instability) the paper uses the absolute value of the dot product and lower bounds it by a threshold:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\}}.
\]
The paper states that the threshold is typically 1.0. Using this lower bound (and the absolute value) prevents extreme amplifications and yields numerically stable retrievals from the matrix memory (see Equations (8) and (9) in the paper).

Both stabilization mechanisms are not optional when exponentials and the covariance-style matrix updates are in play: omit them and you are likely to encounter overflow and training instability.

# Initialization strategies for gates and memory states
The paper emphasizes that the initialization of forget gates requires careful choice (Limitation (iv)). While the xLSTM equations retain standard affine pre-activations and biases for gates, the presence of exponentials makes their initial scale particularly sensitive: small differences in initial gate pre-activations can be amplified due to exponentiation and then compounded over time through the recurrent dynamics.

From the paper’s experiments and ablations the practical takeaways are:
- Treat gate biases as tunable hyperparameters to be validated. The paper’s ablations show incremental benefits when gates are learnable and input-dependent; consequently, initial bias choices for gates matter and should be tuned on held-out validation data.
- Use the stabilizer $m_t$ implementation when exponential gates are enabled; this reduces the sensitivity of initial gate magnitudes to numeric overflow.
- For mLSTM, the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ and cell matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ are part of the recurrent state (Equations (8)–(12)). The paper does not specify exotic non-standard initializations for these state matrices; follow conventional recurrent initialization semantics (i.e., initialize recurrent state to the usual start-of-sequence states the framework uses) and focus hyperparameter search on gate biases and stabilizer behavior, since the limitations section explicitly flags forget gate initialization as a point of care.

In short: pay special attention to forget-gate bias (tune it), always include the $m_t$ stabilizer with exponentials, and monitor early training dynamics to detect mis-initialized retention behavior.

# Choosing block ratios: xLSTM[1:0] vs xLSTM[7:1] and task fit
The paper evaluates architectures using the notation xLSTM[a:b], where $a/b$ is the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks (Section 4). Practical guidance from the experimental findings:

- Use more mLSTM blocks (e.g., xLSTM[1:0], i.e., only mLSTM) for general large-scale language modeling. In the experiments on 300B tokens and PALOMA domains, xLSTM[1:0] performs very well and often outperforms mixed variants and other baselines. mLSTM’s matrix memory provides strong retrieval capacity and, being parallelizable, is efficient for large models.

- Include sLSTM blocks when the problem requires state tracking or when memory mixing (hidden–hidden recurrent mixing across cells) is important. The sLSTM’s memory-mixing capability and exponential gating let it solve formal/state-tracking tasks that transformer- and SSM-style architectures without memory mixing cannot (the formal languages experiments in Section 4.1). In associative-recall experiments, mixed configurations such as xLSTM[1:1] performed best among non-Transformer methods, indicating that a judicious blend can yield strong retrieval plus state-tracking abilities.

- Use ratios such as xLSTM[7:1] when you want a primarily mLSTM backbone augmented with a small number of sLSTM blocks to provide memory-mixing capacity. The paper’s 48-block example (xLSTM[7:1] → 42 mLSTM + 6 sLSTM) is a practical instantiation used in experiments.

Thus, choose xLSTM[1:0] for throughput- and retrieval-dominant language modeling, and include sLSTM blocks (e.g., xLSTM[7:1] or xLSTM[1:1]) when the target tasks include stateful computations, formal languages, or long-term entity tracking.

# Dimensionality, number of heads, and number of cells
xLSTM generalizes the LSTM notion of scalar cell to richer memory formats; the paper provides the following structural guidance.

- Dimensionality and matrix memory cost. mLSTM uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with covariance-style updates. This yields much higher storage capacity but also increases computational cost because updates and retrievals are $d\times d$ operations. The paper explicitly notes the trade-off: matrix memory increases capacity at the cost of $d\times d$ compute, which can still be parallelized on GPUs.

- Heads and cells: for sLSTM, multiple scalar memory cells can be organized into heads. The sLSTM permits memory mixing across cells and allows multiple heads where mixing occurs within a head but not across heads. This introduces a way to structure memory-mixing selectively. For mLSTM, the paper states that multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent, because mLSTM has no recurrent memory-mixing.

- Practical model sizing in experiments. When matching a GPT-3-like configuration (350M), the paper used embedding dimension 1024 and 24 residual blocks as a baseline comparison. This gives an experimental point of reference for dimension choices in comparable setups. Keep in mind that higher $d$ increases both representational power and the $d\times d$ costs for mLSTM.

Use these trade-offs to decide: if your hardware and latency budget permit $d\times d$ operations, exploit mLSTM’s matrix memory for improved rare-token performance and retrieval; if you need lower compute, rely more on sLSTM and scalar cells with memory-mixing but accept lower representational capacity per cell.

# Mixed-precision training and compilation considerations
The paper’s large-scale experiments used mixed precision for many models. Mixed-precision training reduces memory and speeds up training, but certain recurrent or non-standard architectures may require careful handling. Specifically, the paper reports that while most models were trained with mixed precision, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, and GLA did not utilize the PyTorch automated mixed precision flow in their experiments. This highlights that:

- Mixed precision is viable and used in the xLSTM experiments, but you should validate that your chosen automatic mixed-precision toolchain (AMP) interacts correctly with custom recurrent kernels, exponential gates, and the stabilizer $m_t$ implementation.

- Keep a validation run or short pilot to confirm that exponentials and the log-max stabilizer remain numerically stable under mixed precision. If automatic AMP causes issues, consider customized casting or selective higher-precision compute (for example, keep parts of the stabilizer arithmetic in float32) — the paper’s mixed-precision caveats suggest such practical adjustments were necessary for some competing architectures.

Regarding compilation (e.g., torch.compile), the paper mentions that at the time of experiments some models (the Transformer with full KV cache compilation and the Mamba model compilation) did not compile successfully. This suggests that when using compilation facilities, test compilation early and be prepared for engineering workarounds for custom recurrent kernels or matrix-memory updates.

# Batching strategies, prefill handling, and decoding throughput
xLSTM’s runtime characteristics differ markedly from Transformer-style models because its memory usage is constant in the sequence length (linear compute but constant memory with respect to context). The experiments show:

- Prefill experiments: in the generation-time measurements the paper used batch size 1 and prefill 16 tokens as a test case that favors the Transformer. This is a useful benchmark configuration to compare generation latency.

- Decoding throughput: since xLSTM’s state is constant-size (it does not need to store and reuse per-token key/value caches as Transformer decoders do), xLSTM can use much larger batch sizes during decoding without running out of memory. The paper reports that xLSTM achieves the highest throughput in their decoding experiments precisely because of this constant memory property.

- Practical recommendation: for inference, exploit larger batch sizes with xLSTM to maximize throughput. For latency-sensitive single-sequence generation, note xLSTM’s per-step computations are linear in state dimension but avoid the quadratic overhead of Transformers; for high-throughput decoding, the constant memory allows batching that Transformer decoders cannot match at large context lengths.

# Debugging overflow, underflow, and training instability
The paper identifies three concrete numerical and stability failure modes and prescribes the corresponding remedies it used. Use these as a checklist when debugging xLSTM training:

1) Overflow from exponential gates. Symptom: NaNs or infinities in forward pass when exponential gates are used. Remedy: implement the log-max stabilizer state $m_t$ and use the stabilized gates $i'_t,f'_t$ as in Equations (5). This change preserves model mathematics while preventing unbounded exponentials.

2) Instability from near-zero normalizer in mLSTM. Symptom: extremely large retrieved vectors or wild gradients when $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t\approx 0$. Remedy: use the absolute value and lower-bound threshold in the denominator, i.e., divide by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$ (typical threshold 1.0). This ensures retrieval magnitudes remain controlled.

3) General recurrence and forget-gate sensitivity. Symptom: exploding or vanishing contributions from past steps, poor early training. Remedy: carefully select forget-gate initialization and monitor early training dynamics. The paper emphasizes that the forget-gate initialization must be chosen carefully; treat forget-bias (and gate pre-activation scales) as tunable hyperparameters and watch how early steps are being retained or forgotten.

General debugging workflow based on the paper:
- Turn on tight numeric checks: detect NaNs/Infs early, trace their origin to exponential places (gates) or divisive normalizer operations.
- Verify the stabilizer $m_t$ and the denominator threshold are implemented exactly as in the equations; these are mathematically neutral transforms that merely preserve outputs while improving numeric robustness.
- Run small-scale tests on synthetic tasks (the paper’s synthetic experiments are instructive) to confirm that the model can represent the expected behaviors (e.g., revisability with exponentials, associative recall for mLSTM).
- If mixed precision is used, monitor whether reduced numerical precision interacts with exponentials or stabilizers; use the mixed-precision caveats above.

By following the paper’s stabilization prescriptions and treating forget-gate initialization as an important hyperparameter, you will address most of the numeric failure modes reported.

# Final practical checklist (based on the paper)
- When you enable exponential gating: implement the $m_t$ log-max stabilizer and use the stabilized gates in the forward pass.
- For mLSTM retrieval: normalize by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\}$ to avoid division-by-small-number issues.
- Decide your block ratio according to task: use predominantly mLSTM (xLSTM[1:0]) for large-scale language modeling and throughput; add sLSTM blocks when state tracking and memory mixing are required (xLSTM[7:1] or balanced mixes).
- Treat gate parameterization as fully learnable and input-dependent (ablation results favor this).
- Expect higher compute for mLSTM due to $d\times d$ memory; ensure your hardware and parallelization strategy support it.
- Use mixed precision but validate numerics carefully; be prepared to adjust precision handling for custom kernels.
- For inference, exploit xLSTM’s constant memory to increase batch sizes and maximize throughput; test prefill/batch-size trade-offs as done in the paper’s generation experiments.
- If you encounter NaNs or exploding activations, check exponentials and denominator usage first; then investigate forget-gate initializations.

Following the equations, stabilization mechanisms, and architectural choices documented in the paper will give you a reliable foundation for implementing, training, and deploying xLSTM models across the range of tasks the authors evaluated.
