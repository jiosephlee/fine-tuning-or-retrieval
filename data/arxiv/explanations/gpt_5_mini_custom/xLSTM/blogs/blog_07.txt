# Do xLSTMs Scale? What the 15B and 300B Token Results Really Say

## TL;DR
The xLSTM paper scales enhanced LSTMs to modern LLM sizes and tests them on two large-scale regimes: models trained on 15B tokens and models trained on 300B tokens of SlimPajama. Across validation perplexity, downstream reasoning tasks, per-domain perplexities (PALOMA, 571 domains), and long-context extrapolation (up to 16k), xLSTM variants—especially the mLSTM-only variant xLSTM[1:0] and the mixed xLSTM[7:1]—consistently outperform leading Transformer, State Space, and RNN baselines in these experiments. The measured scaling laws follow power-law trends similar to other families but with a clear offset advantage for xLSTM, implying the gap should persist (or widen) at larger model sizes.

Below I walk through the experimental evidence, explain what the scaling plots mean in plain terms, and highlight the practical takeaways and caveats.

## What was tested
The paper compares xLSTM against representative models from three classes:
- Transformers (e.g., GPT-3, Llama),
- State Space Models (SSMs) (e.g., Mamba, H3),
- Recurrent/linear attention methods (e.g., RWKV variants, RetNet, GLA, HGRN/HGRN2).

Key experimental axes:
- Validation perplexity after training on 15B tokens (SlimPajama) — a direct language-modeling quality measure.
- Larger-scale training on 300B tokens: (i) context-length extrapolation up to 16k (models trained with context 2048), (ii) validation perplexity across multiple model sizes (125M–1.3B), (iii) downstream common-sense reasoning tasks, (iv) domain-wise next-token perplexity on PALOMA (571 domains), and (v) scaling-law behavior.
- Generation timing and decoding throughput at 1.3B scale to contrast constant vs. quadratic memory regimes.

Two xLSTM configurations are emphasized:
- xLSTM[1:0]: all blocks use mLSTM (matrix memory, parallelizable).
- xLSTM[7:1]: a majority of blocks are mLSTM with some sLSTM blocks (adds memory mixing).

## 15B-token results — validation perplexity
When trained on 15B tokens, xLSTM models achieve lower validation perplexities than the tested Transformers, SSMs, and RNN-based baselines (the paper compares matched-size models roughly corresponding to a GPT-3 350M configuration). The ablation studies show that two ingredients are primarily responsible for the gain:

1. Exponential gating (stabilized via a normalizer and max-stabilizer): helps revise storage decisions and improves temporal credit assignment.
2. Matrix memory (mLSTM): increases storage capacity and improves rare-token prediction / associative recall.

Bottom line: in the 15B-token regime, xLSTM yields a measurable improvement in the language-modeling objective versus strong baselines.

## 300B-token regime — what changed and why it matters
Scaling to 300B tokens is closer to realistic LLM data budgets. The paper presents several focused evaluations for models trained on 300B tokens at multiple parameter scales (125M–1.3B).

1. Length extrapolation (context up to 16k). Models were trained with context length 2048 and evaluated up to 16k. xLSTM models maintain low perplexities as context length increases, whereas comparators degrade faster. This suggests xLSTM’s memory mechanism (matrix memory + gating) generalizes better to longer contexts than the baselines tested.

2. Validation perplexity and downstream tasks. For all model sizes, both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] were the best on SlimPajama validation perplexity. On a suite of downstream common-sense reasoning tasks, xLSTM models are the best in most tasks and sizes (with occasional wins by Mamba on ARC). This indicates that improved perplexity translates to stronger task performance, not just marginal log-loss gains.

3. PALOMA (571 domains). xLSTM[1:0] beats Mamba in 568/571 domains (99.5%), Llama in 486/571 (85.1%), and RWKV-4 in 570/571 (99.8%) at next-token perplexity. This per-domain consistency suggests xLSTM improvements are broad and not concentrated in a small subset of domains.

4. Scaling laws. The measured learning curves across model sizes follow power-law trends (loss roughly falling as a power of model size). xLSTM shares the same slope/shape as other families but sits at a better offset: for the same size and data regime, xLSTM has lower loss. Because all methods follow similar power-law exponents, an offset advantage typically persists as models scale: increasing size by a given factor reduces loss similarly across families, so the better-off family tends to remain better at larger sizes absent method-specific saturation.

5. Generation time and throughput. xLSTM (and other recurrent families) show linear generation-time scaling with context length and have constant memory in the context length dimension, unlike Transformers (quadratic memory without special caching). This enables higher batch sizes at decoding time and higher maximal throughput for xLSTM in the experiments reported. Practically, that means lower latency and better utilization for long-context decoding when memory is the bottleneck.

## Interpreting the scaling-law plots in plain language
The scaling-law plots depict model loss (or perplexity) versus model size on log–log axes and typically fit a power law:
loss(N) ≈ A * N^{-α} + C, where N is parameter count, α is the slope, and C is irreducible loss.

Two things to look for:
- Slope (α): how quickly performance improves as model size grows. A similar slope across families means they all benefit from scaling at comparable rates.
- Vertical offset (A or C differences): a model family with a lower curve (lower loss at same size) has an effective advantage; if slopes are similar, that advantage persists as you scale.

In the xLSTM experiments the slopes are similar between xLSTM, Mamba, and Llama, but xLSTM sits consistently lower—an offset advantage. Plainly: make every model 10× bigger, and xLSTM will still likely have the edge it has now, assuming the same data and training procedures apply.

## Practical takeaways
- xLSTM variants provide consistent, robust gains in perplexity across small to mid-range LLM sizes and continue to show advantages when scaled to 300B-token training budgets.
- The combination of exponential gating and matrix memory is the key driver: gating improves state revision and temporal credit assignment, matrix memory improves capacity (rare tokens, associative recall).
- xLSTM maintains performance across 571 diverse domains and extrapolates to much longer contexts (up to 16k) better than tested baselines.
- For deployment, xLSTM’s linear compute and constant memory in sequence length make it attractive for long-context generation, enabling higher decoding throughput versus quadratic Transformers without special engineering.
- Measured scaling laws indicate the advantage is not a fluke at one size: similar power-law slopes with a vertical offset imply xLSTM should remain competitive at larger scales.

## Caveats and limitations (important to keep in mind)
- sLSTM memory mixing prevents full parallelization; the authors' fast CUDA kernel is reported to be less than 2× slower than the parallel mLSTM but is still sequential. mLSTM is parallelizable, but its matrix memory has $d\times d$ computational costs per step.
- mLSTM CUDA kernels are not yet highly optimized and are reported ~4× slower than FlashAttention or some fast scans; engineering improvements could change cost trade-offs.
- The matrix memory size is fixed per-layer: extremely long contexts could overload the compressive memory, though experiments up to 16k did not reveal this as a problem.
- The experiments reported did not fully hyperparameter-tune large xLSTM models; further tuning could improve or change relative gaps.
- Results compare to a selection of baselines trained under similar conditions, but LLM performance is sensitive to many implementation details; independent replication and broader benchmarks are important.

## Final thoughts
The experimental evidence in the paper shows xLSTM is more than a nostalgic revisit of LSTMs: with targeted innovations (exponential gating + matrix memory) and modern residual/backbone design, LSTM-derived architectures can match and in these tests outperform contemporary Transformers and State Space Models on language modeling metrics, long-context extrapolation, and domain-wise perplexities. The scaling-law analysis suggests that xLSTM’s advantage is likely to persist as models grow, making xLSTM a credible architecture family for larger LLMs, especially where long-context efficiency and per-step memory constraints matter.

That said, the computational profile (matrix updates, parallelization trade-offs) and implementation maturity need attention before xLSTM can be considered a drop-in replacement in large production stacks. The results are promising and motivate more engineering and reproducibility work to confirm how these gains translate at truly massive scales.
