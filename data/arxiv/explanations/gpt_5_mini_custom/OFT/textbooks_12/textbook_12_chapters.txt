\title{A Textbook about the Paper: OFT (12 chapters)}

Introduction to Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

# Why text guidance is insufficient for fine-grained control

Large text-to-image diffusion models are remarkably good at turning a written prompt into a visually coherent image, but text alone is often ambiguous. A single natural-language prompt typically admits many plausible visual realizations: the words may specify high-level scene elements ("a person on a beach"), but they rarely fix every detail that matters for a downstream application (pose, exact identity features, precise geometry, fine-grained textures). The paper frames this problem succinctly: text guidance can be "ambiguous and insufficient to provide fine-grained and accurate control to the generated images." In practice this means users or downstream systems that need tight, repeatable control over attributes of the generated images—such as preserving the identity of a photographed subject or matching an explicit edge map or segmentation—will often get inconsistent or semantically incorrect outputs when they rely on text alone.

This insufficiency appears in two concrete ways. First, text prompts do not uniquely determine all perceptual details, so models must implicitly sample many degrees of freedom; that sampling can produce outputs that violate application constraints (e.g., changing a subject's identity or producing unrealistic geometry). Second, even when a text prompt intends to convey a specific visual attribute, language is coarse: it can fail to pin down exact pose, local structure, or subtle identity cues that are important in subject-driven tasks or in tightly constrained controllable generation. These limitations motivate approaches that augment or adapt pretrained diffusion models so they can reliably follow stronger forms of guidance beyond plain text.

# Two target tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

The paper focuses on two practically important downstream tasks where text alone is not enough.

Subject-driven generation asks: given a small collection of images of a particular subject (for example, photos of a person, an animal, or a unique object), can the model generate new images of that same subject in new contexts described by a text prompt? The goal is to preserve identity-related details from the few provided images while changing background, pose, or other contextual elements. This is a few-shot personalization problem: only a few images of the subject are available, and the pretrained text-to-image model must be adapted so that future text prompts reliably produce images that contain the same subject.

Controllable generation asks: given an additional structured control signal (for example, Canny edge maps, semantic segmentation maps, or facial landmarks) plus a text prompt, can the model generate images that follow the control signal faithfully while remaining consistent with the prompt? Here the control input supplies precise spatial or structural constraints that text cannot specify easily. The objective is to make the pretrained model accept that additional modality of guidance and produce images that both match the control signal and retain the model's high-fidelity generation.

Both tasks reduce to the same core technical question: how do we adapt a large pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so it obeys stronger task-specific constraints without destroying the model’s prior general-purpose generative capabilities?

# Desiderata for finetuning: training efficiency and generalizability preservation

The paper lays out two central desiderata for any finetuning method applied to these tasks.

First, training efficiency. Practical finetuning should be parameter- and data-efficient: it should require updating only a small fraction of model parameters, run for relatively few training epochs, and work well with the small datasets typical of subject-driven personalization or specialized control-signal pairs. Efficiency matters because large diffusion models are expensive to finetune and because downstream users often have only a handful of examples.

Second, generalizability preservation. Adapting a pretrained diffusion model must not discard the rich, diverse generation behavior learned during pretraining. The finetuned model should retain high-fidelity and diverse generative performance across prompts not seen during finetuning. This requirement is especially delicate: aggressive finetuning can overfit to the finetuning data and produce collapsed or degenerate outputs, while overly conservative finetuning can fail to implement the desired control.

These two desiderata are often at odds: more parameter flexibility can make it easier to implement a new control but also increases the risk of overfitting and losing the pretrained model’s generalization. A successful finetuning strategy must therefore strike a balance between flexibility (to encode task-specific changes) and regularity (to preserve pretrained structure).

# Pitfalls of naive finetuning: model collapse, overfitting, loss of diversity

The straightforward approach to finetuning is to continue gradient-based training on the pretrained weights with a small learning rate, or to add a small parametric adapter and optimize it while keeping the rest of the model fixed. Both approaches are intuitive but have well-documented pitfalls.

One failure mode is model collapse: the finetuned model begins to generate low-quality or semantically wrong outputs (for example, losing a subject's identity or producing textures that contradict the prompt). The paper reports that naive finetuning and some adapter approaches can produce such collapse after extended training, and that they can also be highly sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations. This sensitivity makes it difficult to choose training schedules: too few iterations and the model fails to learn the new control; too many and the model may catastrophically diverge from the pretrained behavior.

Overfitting is a related issue, particularly acute in subject-driven generation where only a few images are available. A finetuned model may memorize the small training set and lose variability in the outputs, leading to decreased sample diversity. The paper highlights that simple notions of closeness to the pretrained model—measured by Euclidean distance in parameter space—are often used implicitly (e.g., via tiny learning rates) to avoid such problems, but they are incomplete as structural measures of semantic preservation. In particular, two parameter vectors can be close in Euclidean norm yet correspond to models with very different semantic behavior, while other transformations that change parameters more in Euclidean norm may nonetheless preserve important semantic structure.

Another pitfall is losing the ability to follow textual prompts. Some finetuning approaches may preserve low-level image features at the cost of decoupling the model’s language-conditioned behavior, so generated images no longer track the text prompt as well as before. Conversely, approaches that emphasize prompt fidelity can wash out subject-specific details. These tradeoffs underline the need for a finetuning principle that explicitly targets the preservation of the pretrained model’s internal structural properties related to semantic generation.

# High-level overview of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT)

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is the paper’s proposed principle for adapting pretrained text-to-image diffusion models while protecting the pretrained semantic abilities. OFT is built around two key observations from the paper.

First, angular (directional) relationships among neurons capture semantic information robustly. Prior empirical evidence cited in the paper suggests that neuron directions on the unit hypersphere carry much of the semantic signal. To quantify a layer’s internal relational structure, the paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE), which aggregates pairwise angular relationships among normalized neurons. For a weight matrix \(\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]\) with normalized columns \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\), the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
HE measures how neuron directions are distributed on the unit hypersphere; its value encodes the pairwise angular relationships that the authors hypothesize are crucial for preserving semantic generation.

Second, HE is invariant to orthogonal transformations: if every neuron in a layer is rotated by the same orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) (so \(\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\)), the normalized pairwise angles among neurons are preserved and thus the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. This invariance suggests a constrained family of parameter updates that preserve the layer’s angular structure exactly.

OFT operationalizes these insights by parameterizing finetuning updates as layer-shared orthogonal transformations applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weights. Concretely, for a pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) and input \(\bm{x}\), OFT changes the forward pass from \(\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\) to
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \qquad \text{with } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]
Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, the hyperspherical energy \(\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)\) equals \(\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\), so the layer's internal angular geometry is preserved by construction. OFT therefore enforces the structural constraint
\[
\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\| = 0
\]
by restricting updates to orthogonal transformations, while still allowing the model to change how neuron directions relate to inputs and thus to express new semantic behavior.

The constrained variant, COFT, augments OFT with an explicit deviation bound to improve stability. COFT continues to require \(\bm{R}\) be orthogonal but also enforces that \(\bm{R}\) remain close to the identity matrix:
\[
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]
for some small \(\epsilon\). Intuitively, COFT keeps the finetuned model within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained weights, explicitly limiting how far the orthogonal transformation can move the neurons. This additional radius constraint captures the implicit small-step regularization commonly used in naive finetuning but integrates it with the angular-preservation principle. Empirically, the paper finds COFT improves finetuning stability and makes hyperparameter choices (like the number of iterations) less sensitive.

At a high level, OFT and COFT aim to balance flexibility and regularity: they allow the model to alter neuron directions in a controlled, structured way that is well matched to semantic changes (changing directions tends to change semantics), while preventing destructive alterations to the pretrained model’s internal relational geometry. This strategy yields several desirable outcomes reported in the paper: better preservation of semantic generation ability, faster and more stable convergence during finetuning, fewer training iterations and less data required, and no added inference-time overhead (the learned orthogonal transformation can be merged into the pretrained weights once finetuning is complete).

Together, these ideas provide a principled alternative to naive small-step finetuning and low-rank adapter methods: rather than minimizing raw Euclidean distance to the pretrained parameters, OFT preserves a deeper structural invariant—the pairwise neuron angles—while still permitting expressive, task-relevant changes.

Foundations of Diffusion Models and Latent Text-to-Image Systems

# Denoising diffusion probabilistic models: forward and reverse processes

Modern text-to-image systems in the paper are built on diffusion-based generative models, a family of methods that have been shown to produce high-fidelity images in recent work (see, e.g., Ho et al. 2020 and follow-ups cited in the paper). At a conceptual level these models are trained to undo a corruption process: a forward process gradually corrupts (adds noise to) real images, and a learned reverse process is trained to step-by-step remove that noise and recover plausible images. The literature cited in the paper frames diffusion models as denoising systems: the training objective is to learn a denoising (reverse) model that, when applied repeatedly, transforms a noise sample into a realistic image. The reverse process is therefore an iterative generative procedure whose learned denoiser is the central neural component of contemporary diffusion pipelines.

The referenced works treat diffusion as a principled probabilistic framework and provide the detailed mathematical formulations; in this chapter we emphasize the conceptual roles that the forward (corruption) and reverse (denoising) processes play in text-to-image systems. The forward process defines the corruption trajectory used for training; the reverse process is implemented by a neural denoiser (typically a U-Net or related architecture) that is conditioned on guidance (text, controls, etc.) and is executed at inference to synthesize images by progressively denoising a noise sample.

# Latent vs pixel-space diffusion (e.g., Stable Diffusion, Imagen, GLIDE)

The literature surveyed in the paper contrasts two broad instantiations of diffusion models for text-to-image generation: pixel-space diffusion and latent-space diffusion.

Pixel-space diffusion models operate directly on image pixels. Examples cited in the paper include GLIDE and Imagen. The paper notes an implementation detail difference between these two: GLIDE trains its text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text–image data, while Imagen employs a frozen pretrained text encoder. Working in the pixel space means the denoiser directly models distributions over images, and the text-conditioning machinery is coupled (jointly trained) or decoupled (frozen) according to the design choice of the system.

Latent-space diffusion models instead operate on a learned compressed representation of images. The paper gives Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 as canonical examples. Stable Diffusion is described in the paper as using VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that defines the latent space on which the diffusion model runs. DALL·E 2, as described, uses CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space for representing images and text. Running the diffusion process in latent space changes where the denoiser acts (it denoises latent codes rather than full-resolution pixels) and is a central design choice for systems like Stable Diffusion. The paper positions Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the experimental backbone for comparisons and finetuning in the work.

These two paradigms—pixel vs latent—are recurring architectural choices in the field and are highlighted by the examples in the paper. The paper refers back to these systems when discussing which pretrained models are adapted (for instance, Stable Diffusion v1.5 is the base model used in the experiments).

# Text conditioning: CLIP and frozen text encoders in T2I systems

Text conditioning is the mechanism by which a diffusion denoiser is guided to produce images consistent with a textual prompt. The paper surveys several architectures and training choices for integrating text: some systems jointly train the text encoder with the diffusion prior (the GLIDE setup cited), while others use a frozen pretrained text encoder (as in Imagen). The choice of frozen versus jointly trained encoders appears explicitly in the paper’s discussion and is an important distinction when adapting and finetuning text-to-image models.

The paper also highlights CLIP as a central representation used in some architectures and evaluation metrics. In the context of generation systems, DALL·E 2 is noted as adopting CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space that represents images and text. Separately, CLIP embeddings are used in the paper’s experiments as evaluation signals (for example, CLIP-I and CLIP-T metrics measure image–image and text–image semantic fidelity, respectively). The broader family of vision–language representation work is cited to situate these encoder choices; in the experiments the authors rely on pretrained components (Stable Diffusion and its text-conditioning machinery) and then apply finetuning approaches that modify parts of the denoiser while leaving other components as-is when appropriate.

When adapting a pretrained text-to-image model, the training choice for the text encoder (frozen or jointly trained) affects how finetuning should be performed. For example, DreamBooth finetuning (used as a baseline in the paper) finetunes the diffusion model with a reconstruction objective and a prior-preservation loss while leveraging text-conditioning via a learned subject token; OFT adopts the DreamBooth framework but replaces the standard weight updates with orthogonal transformations on neural weights to better preserve pretrained behavior.

# U-Net backbones, attention layers, and conditioning mechanisms

The denoiser used in modern diffusion pipelines is commonly implemented with architectures derived from U-Nets and attention mechanisms; Stable Diffusion in particular uses a U-Net-like denoiser with attention layers. Although the paper does not reproduce the full U-Net block diagrams, it explicitly discusses weight matrices and layers in the general neural sense and uses a canonical linear-layer forward pass to explain Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). A convenient abstraction used in the paper is to consider a weight matrix of a layer as a collection of neurons (columns) and to write the layer forward pass as

\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x},
\]

where \(\bm{W}\) is the weight matrix (with columns \(\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\)) and \(\bm{x}\) is the input vector. This same abstraction applies to fully connected layers, to linear projections inside attention mechanisms, and to convolution layers after appropriate unfolding; the paper leverages that view to describe how finetuning can be parameterized.

Attention layers and Transformer-style projections are frequent targets for parameter-efficient finetuning methods. The paper notes that LoRA—the low-rank adaptation strategy widely used in language and vision models—commonly applies its low-rank additive updates to attention weight matrices. For a fair comparison with LoRA, the experiments in the paper apply OFT to the same attention-weight locations where LoRA is normally used. Thus, whether the denoiser’s conditioning signals pass through attention blocks or other layers, the OFT mechanism is designed to act at the neural weight level in the same structural locations used by competing adapters.

Two key ideas about conditioning and layers are made explicit in the paper and motivate OFT’s design. First, the semantics carried by a trained network are tied to the geometric relationships among neurons: the paper formalizes a measure called hyperspherical energy to capture pairwise angular relations among normalized neuron vectors. For a weight matrix \(\bm{W}\) whose normalized neuron vectors are \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\), the hyperspherical energy used in the paper is defined as

\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

This quantity encodes pairwise angular structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere. The paper’s OFT principle is to preserve this angular structure during finetuning so as to maintain pretrained generative behavior while enabling task-specific adaptation.

Second, the paper develops a multiplicative finetuning parameterization that acts directly on pretrained weights. OFT replaces the layer forward pass \(\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\) for pretrained weights \(\bm{W}^0\) with

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]

where \(\bm{R}\) is an orthogonal matrix shared across the neurons of that layer. This parameterization is multiplicative (it rotates or reflects the neuron directions) rather than additive, and it provably preserves pairwise cosine relationships among normalized neurons because orthogonal transforms preserve angles on the hypersphere. The paper further develops practical parameterizations of \(\bm{R}\), including Cayley transforms and block-diagonal structures for parameter efficiency, and discusses how these designs map naturally onto the weight matrices inside attention and U-Net layers.

# Where control enters the pipeline: additional conditions and adapters

Control in text-to-image diffusion pipelines can take two broad forms in the settings studied in the paper: subject-driven control and additional signal–based control.

Subject-driven generation refers to adapting a pretrained text-to-image model so that it can generate images of a particular subject given only a few reference images plus a text prompt. DreamBooth is cited as a representative method: it injects a learned subject token and finetunes the diffusion model with reconstruction and prior-preservation losses so that the model can place the subject into new contexts specified by prompts. OFT builds on this framework but replaces the usual small-gradient finetuning with orthogonal transformations of layer weights, preserving hyperspherical energy and thus the pretrained model’s general semantic capabilities while adapting it to the new subject.

Controllable generation refers to conditioning the generation process on additional non-text controls such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The paper treats ControlNet and T2I-Adapter as representative adapter-based approaches: both adapt a pretrained diffusion model to accept extra control signals by introducing additional learned components (adapters) and finetuning. In the experiments, ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are described as finetuning the pretrained denoiser so it can ingest the control signals and produce outputs consistent with both control and text. The paper finds that these adapter approaches improve controllability but often come with practical trade-offs: they can be parameter- and data-hungry and the added adapter pathways can introduce inference-time overhead.

A central point of the paper is that OFT provides an alternative path to stronger and more stable control without adding inference-time components. Because OFT learns an orthogonal transform \(\bm{R}\) and the finetuned weights can be collapsed into the original weight matrix via \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\), there is no extra network branch or runtime cost introduced at inference. The paper contrasts this property explicitly with ControlNet: "Unlike ControlNet, our OFT framework introduces no additional inference overhead to the finetuned model." Empirically, the paper reports that OFT and its constrained variant COFT converge faster and more stably in both subject-driven and control-signal tasks than the adapter baselines, while using a comparable or smaller number of trainable parameters. The paper also proposes a control consistency evaluation in which the control signal implied by a generated image is recovered and compared to the original control signal as a metric for how faithfully the model followed the control; this evaluation is used to quantify controllability across competing finetuning approaches.

Taken together, the architectural picture emerging from the paper is this: a pretrained diffusion denoiser (often U-Net–based and deploying attention blocks) is conditioned on text by some encoder (frozen or jointly trained). To introduce new control behaviors—whether subject-specific generation or control-signal adherence—practitioners have used adapters (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) or additive low-rank updates (LoRA). OFT offers a principled multiplicative alternative that operates directly on layer weights via orthogonal transforms, preserves angular neuron structure as measured by hyperspherical energy, and integrates with the same conditioning pathways already present in modern text-to-image backbones (Stable Diffusion in the paper’s experiments) without increasing inference complexity.

Finetuning Strategies for Large Generative Models

# Standard finetuning with small learning rates: implicit Euclidean proximity

The most straightforward approach to adapt a large pretrained generative model to a downstream task is standard finetuning: take the pretrained weights and continue training on task-specific data using gradient descent. In practice this is often done with a very small learning rate and sometimes only a subset of layers is updated. The small learning rate is not a magic rule but functions as an implicit regularizer: it biases the optimization to remain close in Euclidean parameter space to the pretrained model. Informally, this strategy tries to solve the downstream optimization problem while roughly minimizing the Euclidean deviation between the finetuned model parameters $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained parameters $\bm{M}^0$, i.e. encouraging a small value of $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$.

This Euclidean-proximity intuition has two practical consequences. First, it stabilizes training by preventing large, abrupt changes to a large model that was trained on massive data; second, it preserves some of the pretrained generative ability simply because the parameters stay numerically close. However, the paper emphasizes that Euclidean closeness alone is not a principled or sufficient measure for preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generation capabilities. Two models can be close in Euclidean norm but differ in structurally important ways (for example in the relative geometry of neuron directions). As a result, standard finetuning can be brittle: continued training with even a small learning rate can still lead to loss of the pretrained model’s generative semantics, model collapse, or sensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations. These shortcomings motivate the search for finetuning methods that impose stronger, more semantically meaningful constraints than raw Euclidean proximity.

# Adapter-based methods and parameter-efficient finetuning

An alternative to updating the full model parameters is to add a small, trainable component — an adapter — while keeping most pretrained parameters fixed. Adapter-based approaches re-parameterize part of a layer with an extra module; during adaptation only the adapter parameters are trained. This family of methods is attractive because it reduces compute and memory cost during finetuning and allows many downstream tasks to be served by a single frozen backbone plus a small task-specific adapter.

Adapter methods for text-to-image diffusion models follow this same philosophy: instead of changing the large diffusion model directly, attach a compact module that can steer generation toward the downstream objective (for example, by conditioning on an additional signal). The paper describes adapter-based approaches as widely studied in NLP and now adopted in image generation work; in controllable generation they take the form of modules that incorporate an additional control signal (edges, segmentation, landmarks, etc.) into the pretrained diffusion backbone.

While adapters reduce the number of trainable parameters and often improve sample-efficiency, the paper points out an important limitation: re-parameterization alone does not provide structural guarantees about the internal relationships of neurons in the pretrained model. In other words, adapter updates do not ensure preservation of geometric relations such as the pairwise angles of neurons — a property the authors argue is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. Adapter methods may therefore suffer from instability or fail to preserve pretrained generalization in some finetuning regimes.

# LoRA: low-rank additive updates and their assumptions

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a concrete parameter-efficient finetuning technique discussed as most relevant to the proposed OFT method. LoRA constrains the change to a weight matrix to be low-rank: the finetuned weight is written as the pretrained weight plus a low-rank additive update. If $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is a pretrained weight matrix, LoRA models the update $\bm{W}-\bm{W}^0$ with a product of two small matrices of rank $r'$, so the number of trainable parameters scales as $r'(d+n)$.

This low-rank assumption is powerful in practice: many useful task-specific changes can be expressed with a small number of degrees of freedom, so LoRA achieves meaningful adaptation with far fewer parameters than full finetuning. Nonetheless, the paper highlights that LoRA’s additive, low-rank formulation does not come with formal guarantees about preserving the pretrained model’s semantic structure. The update may change pairwise neuron relationships in an uncontrolled way, and there is no explicit mechanism that enforces invariants believed to underlie semantic generation. Empirically, this can manifest as unstable or suboptimal preservation of subject identity or control fidelity when LoRA is applied in certain text-to-image finetuning tasks.

The paper also provides a contrasting perspective on parameter efficiency: LoRA reduces parameters by leveraging low-rank structure, whereas the proposed OFT reduces the number of trainable parameters by constraining the form of the transformation (orthogonality and optionally block structure). Both are parameter-efficient but exploit different inductive biases.

# DreamBooth for subject-driven generation

Subject-driven generation asks a model to produce images of a particular subject (person, object) from a handful of example images, placing that subject into new contexts described by a text prompt. DreamBooth is the representative approach in this space referenced by the paper. DreamBooth augments a text-to-image diffusion model by introducing a customized token (a special identifier for the subject) and then finetunes the diffusion model to reproduce the subject in multiple contexts.

Training in DreamBooth combines a reconstruction-style loss on the given subject images with a class-specific prior preservation loss. The prior-preservation term is designed to prevent the model from overfitting the few example images and to preserve the class-level information so that the model does not simply collapse into reproducing only the provided images. DreamBooth finetunes the backbone weights (typically with small learning rates) so that the model learns to associate the new token with the subject visuals and then use that token when generating novel images guided by prompts.

Although effective, DreamBooth is sensitive to finetuning stability and hyperparameters; without careful tuning it can either underfit (fail to capture the subject) or overfit/collapse (lose generality or produce artifacts). The paper treats DreamBooth as a baseline and motivation: we need finetuning strategies that are both data- and iteration-efficient while preserving pretrained semantics robustly.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter for controllable generation

Controllable generation extends text guidance with an additional structured control signal such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The goal is to produce images that not only match a text prompt but also adhere closely to the given control.

ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are contemporary approaches that finetune or append modules to a pretrained diffusion model to ingest these extra control signals. ControlNet trains an additional network that adapts the backbone diffusion model to control inputs, while T2I-Adapter employs adapter modules that condition the diffusion process on auxiliary inputs. Both methods can achieve strong controllability because they explicitly model how the control signal should influence image synthesis.

However, they have trade-offs. A salient issue the paper notes is inference overhead: adapter-style methods or explicit additional networks increase compute and memory requirements at test-time. Moreover, like other adapter or finetuning strategies, they lack structural guarantees ensuring preservation of the pretrained model’s internal semantic geometry. Empirically, the paper reports that adapter-based controllable generation methods sometimes converge slowly and can be less sample-efficient than desired.

# Limitations: lack of structural guarantees and stability issues

Across the finetuning strategies surveyed — standard finetuning, adapter modules, LoRA, DreamBooth, ControlNet/T2I-Adapter — the paper identifies a recurring set of limitations that motivate a new approach.

First, there is a conceptual gap: existing methods typically assume that remaining close in Euclidean parameter space is synonymous with preserving pretrained generative ability. But this Euclidean measure does not account for the structure among neurons that encodes semantic knowledge. The paper proposes that pairwise angular relations among neurons — summarized by the hyperspherical energy of a layer — are a more meaningful structural property. Existing methods do not provide guarantees on preserving such geometric invariants.

Second, practical stability is often problematic. Standard finetuning with small learning rates still requires careful tuning of iteration count and learning rate to avoid collapse or loss of identity. Adapter-based methods reduce parameter count but can still destabilize relationships within the backbone, and ControlNet-style additions increase inference cost. LoRA’s low-rank additive updates are parameter-efficient but may lack the regularity needed to maintain semantic structure, leading to failures on some subject-preservation or control tasks.

Third, many methods are sample- or iteration-inefficient. The paper emphasizes that an effective finetuning method should be both training-efficient (fewer trainable parameters and fewer epochs) and able to preserve the pretrained model’s high-fidelity, diverse generative performance (what the paper calls generalizability preservation). Existing techniques trade off these objectives in different ways, but none provides a principled, provable constraint that targets the pairwise neuron structure that seems critical for semantic preservation.

Finally, there is no principled measure broadly used in prior work for quantifying preservation of pretrained generative abilities during finetuning. Without such a metric, practitioners rely on heuristics (small learning rates, early stopping) that are task- and dataset-dependent. The paper argues for replacing or augmenting Euclidean proximity with structural invariants (hyperspherical energy) and for designing finetuning parameterizations that provably preserve those invariants. This diagnostic gap and the instability it enables establish the need for an approach like Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which the rest of the paper develops as a principled alternative that both preserves pairwise neuron angles and yields practical benefits in stability, convergence speed, and sample-efficiency.

Geometry of Neural Representations: Angles, Hyperspheres, and Energy

# Angular features and cosine similarity as semantic carriers

A recurring empirical observation, emphasized in the paper, is that angular relationships between vectors in neural networks—measured by cosine similarity or equivalent angular metrics—encode semantic information more robustly than raw magnitudes. Intuitively, when a neuron (or filter) is viewed as a vector in parameter space, its direction captures how it responds to different input patterns, while its magnitude largely scales that response. Decoupling direction from magnitude therefore isolates the structure that determines which inputs elicit similar internal representations. Works cited in the paper (e.g., SphereNet and angular-margin studies) show that methods which emphasize angular geometry achieve strong generalization, which supports the idea that direction (angle) is where semantic content is concentrated.

Cosine similarity is the natural measure on directions. For two vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^d$, their cosine similarity is
\[
\cos\theta_{uv} = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|\|v\|},
\]
where $\theta_{uv}$ is the angle between them. Because cosine similarity is invariant to positive rescaling of either vector, it captures a purely directional relationship. In practice, many representation-learning techniques normalize activations or weights onto a unit sphere and use cosine-based objectives; the paper draws on this body of evidence to argue that controlling or modifying neuron directions is an effective route to change semantics in generated images while preserving other desirable properties.

# Hyperspherical representations: normalizing neurons to the unit sphere

The hypersphere view formalizes the focus on direction: given a neuron weight vector $\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, normalize it to
\[
\hat{\bm{w}} = \frac{\bm{w}}{\|\bm{w}\|},
\]
so that $\hat{\bm{w}}$ lies on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$. Considering all neurons in a layer as points on this hypersphere converts problems about pairwise relationships into geometric questions on the sphere. This normalization eliminates magnitude effects and forces analysis to concentrate on angles between neurons.

There are two practical reasons to adopt hyperspherical representations. First, training or analyzing neural components on the hypersphere simplifies the geometry: distances between normalized vectors map directly to angles. For unit vectors $u$ and $v$ we have
\[
\|u-v\|^2 = 2(1 - u^\top v) = 2(1 - \cos\theta_{uv}),
\]
so Euclidean distance on the sphere is equivalent to angular dissimilarity. Second, hyperspherical normalization often improves interpretability and generalization, because it removes scale ambiguities and allows pairwise relationships to be compared directly across neurons. The paper leverages this viewpoint to define measures of pairwise structure (hyperspherical energy) and to motivate finetuning strategies that preserve those measures.

# Hyperspherical energy: definition, intuition, and uniformity

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is the summary statistic used in the paper to quantify pairwise relationships among neurons after normalization. For a layer with neuron vectors $\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, define normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy used in the paper is
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Because $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is a function of the cosine similarity (as shown above), HE aggregates pairwise angular relationships across all neuron pairs. Large contributions to HE occur when pairs of neurons are close on the sphere (small angular separation), because the inverse norm $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is large for small distances.

Intuitively, HE measures how “clumped” or “uniform” the neuron directions are on the hypersphere. A small HE corresponds to neurons that are more uniformly spread (pairwise distances relatively large), while a large HE indicates clusters or redundancy (many neuron pairs with small separation). In certain learning settings (e.g., classification), minimizing HE can encourage diversity among neurons and improve generalization. However, the paper stresses a different use of HE in the finetuning context: rather than minimizing HE, a finetuned model should preserve the HE of the pretrained model to retain its learned semantic structure. Thus HE is used as a structural measure of how much the pairwise angular relationships among neurons have been altered by finetuning.

# Invariance of pairwise angles under orthogonal transforms

A central mathematical property exploited in the paper is that orthogonal linear maps preserve angles and distances. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. For any two vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\bm{R}u)^\top(\bm{R}v) \;=\; u^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} v \;=\; u^\top v,
\]
so inner products are exactly preserved. When $u$ and $v$ are unit vectors, this equality implies cosine similarities (and therefore angles) are unchanged:
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}u)^\top(\bm{R}v)}{\|\bm{R}u\|\|\bm{R}v\|} = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|\|v\|}.
\]
Because HE depends only on pairwise distances between normalized neurons, and those distances are functions of inner products, applying the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to every neuron in a layer leaves HE invariant. More concretely, if $\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]$ and $\bm{W}' = \bm{R}\bm{W} = [\bm{R}\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{R}\bm{w}_n]$, then
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}') = \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]

This invariance is the theoretical backbone of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): by restricting the finetuning transformation within a layer to be a (layer-shared) orthogonal matrix, the method guarantees that the global pairwise angular structure—captured by HE—remains exactly preserved. In other words, OFT changes neuron directions only by a rigid rotation or reflection of the coordinate frame, so relative angles among neurons are constant.

# Toy evidence: magnitude vs angle in autoencoders

The paper presents a small but illustrative experiment to demonstrate that angular information in neurons preserves semantic content more than magnitudes do. A standard convolutional autoencoder is trained on flower images using the usual inner-product based feature maps: for a sliding window input $\bm{x}$ and convolutional kernel $\bm{w}$, the element output is the inner product $\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}$. After training, the authors probe what information in the learned filters is responsible for reconstructing the input: they compare three ways to produce the feature map at test time (while the decoder remains the same):

(a) the original inner product $\bm{w}^\top \bm{x}$ as used during training;  
(b) a feature map constructed from magnitude information alone;  
(c) a feature map constructed from angular information alone (i.e., using cosine-like computations).

The reported finding is striking: using angular information almost perfectly reconstructs the input images, whereas using magnitude information yields no useful reconstruction. Importantly, the autoencoder was not trained with cosine activations; it was trained with standard inner products, yet at test time the angular-only reconstruction still retained semantic fidelity. This experiment supports the claim that directions (angles) of neuron vectors encode the primary semantic structure learned by the network, whereas magnitudes mainly scale responses but do not by themselves carry the same semantic content.

The toy evidence motivates finetuning strategies that act on neuron directions: if direction encodes semantics, then carefully controlled rotations of neuron directions can alter semantics in a controlled way while preserving global structure that underpins the pretrained model’s generalization.

# Fourier-phase analogy: phase preserves semantics more than magnitude

To build intuition from another domain, the paper draws an analogy to the 2D Fourier transform of images, which decomposes an image into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. Empirically, the phase spectrum encodes the bulk of the spatial structure and semantics of an image: keeping the phase spectrum and replacing the magnitude spectrum with random values reconstructs an image that still preserves recognizable semantics (edges, shapes, and layout). Conversely, preserving the magnitude spectrum while randomizing phase typically destroys recognizable content.

This Fourier-phase phenomenon is used as an intuitive parallel to the neuron-angle argument. In that analogy, neuron directions correspond to phase (angular) information, while neuron magnitudes correspond to the magnitude spectrum. The fact that phase carries semantic structure in images suggests that angular information among neurons may play an analogous role for neural representations: manipulating angles can change semantics in a controlled way, while preserving relative angles (as with an orthogonal transformation) maintains the semantic relationships that the pretrained model encodes.

By combining the toy autoencoder evidence and the Fourier-phase analogy, the paper motivates an approach where finetuning acts primarily by controlled angular transformations—rigid rotations across neuron sets—so that meaningful semantic structure is modified only insofar as intended for a downstream task, without wholesale disruption of the pretrained model’s learned geometry.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Theory and Core Mechanism

# OFT as learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix acting on pretrained weights

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled finetuning strategy that adapts a pretrained neural layer by learning a single orthogonal transformation shared across the neurons in that layer. Concretely, consider a pretrained weight matrix for a layer, written as $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\bm{w}^0_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are the neurons (filters) in that layer. Instead of directly updating the entries of $\bm{W}^0$ with an additive low-rank change or with small dense updates, OFT parameterizes the finetuned weight matrix as a multiplicative transform of the pretrained weights:
\[
\bm{W} \;=\; \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
where the matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to be orthogonal, i.e. $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. The forward pass of the layer changes from the pretrained $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to the OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} \;=\; (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
and OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity so the finetuning begins exactly at the pretrained model. Implementationally, $\bm{R}$ is learned while maintaining orthogonality via a differentiable parameterization (for example the Cayley transform) and, for parameter efficiency, is often restricted to a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix.

This multiplicative formulation can be algebraically rewritten to display its relation to additive updates:
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \;=\; \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0,
\]
so the effect is equivalent to an additive update $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, but that additive term is constrained by $\bm{R}$ being orthogonal. Because of this multiplicative structure and the orthogonality constraint, OFT modifies neuron directions in a structured, global way across a layer, rather than changing each neuron independently.

# Preserving pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy exactly

A central analytic property of orthogonal transformations is that they preserve inner products and Euclidean norms. For any orthogonal $\bm{R}$ and vectors $\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{v}) \;=\; \bm{u}^\top\bm{v}, \qquad \|\bm{R}\bm{u}\| \;=\; \|\bm{u}\|.
\]
From these equalities follows the invariance of pairwise angles between neurons. The cosine of the angle between two neurons $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ is
\[
\cos\theta_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\|\,\|\bm{w}_j\|}.
\]
Applying the orthogonal transform yields
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j)}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\|\,\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_j\|} \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i^\top\bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\|\,\|\bm{w}_j\|},
\]
so all pairwise cosines (and therefore all pairwise angles) are exactly preserved.

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar measure that aggregates pairwise geometric relations among the normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=[\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n]$, write normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Because orthogonal transforms preserve all normalized vectors up to rotation (i.e. $\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i$) and preserve Euclidean distances, we have for every pair $i\neq j$
\[
\|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}}_i - \widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}}_j\|
\;=\;\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
\;=\;\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|.
\]
Consequently, each summand in the hyperspherical energy remains identical after applying $\bm{R}$, and therefore orthogonal finetuning leaves the hyperspherical energy exactly unchanged:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) \;=\; \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
This is a provable invariance: by optimizing only over layer-shared orthogonal matrices, OFT guarantees zero difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained weights, meeting the objective
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \big\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \big\|
\]
with the minimum attained when $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal $\bm{R}$. The exact preservation of HE is the mathematical backbone for OFT's claim of maintaining the neuron relational structure that encodes semantic information.

# Interpretation as changing the coordinate system of neurons

Viewing OFT through the lens of coordinates gives an intuitive explanation of what finetuning by an orthogonal matrix does. A weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ represents neurons expressed in some canonical basis of the $d$-dimensional input space. Multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to expressing the same neuronal directions in a rotated (or reflected) coordinate system. From this perspective, OFT does not arbitrarily distort the internal geometry among neurons; instead, it re-expresses those neurons in a new orthonormal basis.

Because rotations and reflections are rigid isometries, the relative geometry — the angles and normalized distances among neurons — is unchanged. What changes is how neuron directions align with the input coordinates. Practically, this aligns with the idea that semantic modifications can be achieved by altering how neurons project onto input directions, while preserving the intrinsic relational structure that the pretrained model uses to represent visual concepts. This coordinate-change view also clarifies why OFT can accomplish meaningful adaptation: by changing the basis, the same set of neuron relationships can interact differently with incoming features, enabling new behavior (such as subject-specific generation or conditioning on control signals) without destroying the pretrained representational geometry.

# Comparison to minimizing Euclidean deviation alone

A standard and widely used finetuning intuition is to keep the finetuned weights close in Euclidean norm to the pretrained ones. Formally, typical finetuning enforces small implicit or explicit constraints on $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ is the finetuned weight matrix. Small Euclidean deviation is helpful as a conservative regularizer: it limits drastic changes that can erase pretrained capabilities. However, Euclidean proximity alone does not control the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, which are hypothesized (and empirically observed) to encode semantic structure strongly. Two weight matrices can be close in Frobenius norm yet have different directional structure among their columns, thereby changing the hyperspherical energy and the relational encoding.

OFT addresses this shortcoming by directly preserving the hyperspherical geometry, an objective orthogonal to mere Euclidean closeness. OFT therefore guarantees that the pairwise neuron relationships remain identical, even if the finetuned weight matrix might be non-negligibly different from the pretrained weights in Euclidean terms. To reconcile these two desiderata, the constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), adds an explicit proximity constraint
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]
so that the learned orthogonal transform is not only angle-preserving but also close to the identity in operator norm. Because orthogonal matrices are bounded operators, a small $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ means a small perturbation of the coordinate system; by combining angular preservation and limited deviation, COFT aims to retain pretrained behavior even more strongly while allowing controlled adaptation. Practically, COFT initializes at $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and enforces the constraint (via projection on a suitable parameterization) during training, trading off flexibility and fidelity in an explicit way.

# Connections and contrasts to orthogonal over-parameterized training (classification vs finetuning)

OFT is conceptually related to prior uses of orthogonal parameterizations in neural network training, such as orthogonal over-parameterized training. Both approaches leverage orthogonal transforms to control neuron geometry via rotations on the hypersphere. However, their aims and optimization objectives differ in important ways.

Over-parameterized orthogonal training for classification sought to minimize hyperspherical energy or to keep it small during training; small HE was argued to encourage uniform neuron dispersion on the hypersphere, which is helpful for classification generalization. In contrast, OFT does not attempt to minimize hyperspherical energy; rather, its aim is to preserve the pretrained hyperspherical energy. In finetuning a generative diffusion model, pushing neuron geometry toward a minimal-energy configuration (uniform dispersion) could destroy the pretrained model’s learned semantic structure. Thus, whereas orthogonal over-parameterized training uses orthogonality as a route to a new target geometry (low HE), OFT uses orthogonality to leave the pretrained geometry unchanged while enabling controlled adaptation.

Another contrast lies in objective context: orthogonal over-parameterized training is applied when training a network from random initialization and targets global generalization properties. OFT applies to finetuning a large pretrained model with strong pre-existing capabilities. Because finetuning must preserve those capabilities while introducing task-specific changes, OFT couples orthogonality with initialization at the identity and, in COFT, an explicit radius constraint to limit deviation. These elements are tailored to adaptation rather than to training from scratch.

# Why OFT balances flexibility and regularity for stable adaptation

Finetuning must negotiate a trade-off between flexibility — the capacity to alter behavior enough to solve a new downstream task — and regularity — the need to avoid destroying pretrained competencies. OFT offers a principled balance between these demands. Orthogonal transforms permit rich, structured changes to how neurons project onto inputs (flexibility) while strictly preserving pairwise angular geometry (regularity). This constraint eliminates a large class of destructive modifications that would scramble neuron relationships and semantic encoding.

The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ provides an additional knob controlling flexibility: splitting $\bm{R}$ into $r$ orthogonal blocks reduces parameter count and reduces the degrees of freedom of the allowable rotation, introducing further regularization that often improves stability and parameter efficiency. For example, when $r$ equals the number of input channels in a convolutional layer and each block acts per-channel, OFT resembles depth-wise transformations that are naturally constrained but still expressive.

Moreover, the manifold structure induced by orthogonality leads to smoother optimization landscapes. Optimizing over rotations (an orthogonal group) keeps weight updates on a smooth hyperspherical manifold, which empirically yields faster convergence and greater stability compared to unconstrained additive updates. The constrained variant COFT reinforces stability by explicitly limiting the operator-norm deviation from the identity, providing an additional safeguard against collapse during finetuning. Finally, the re-scaled OFT extension which learns a diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ in combination with $\bm{R}$,
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
adds a small amount of magnitude flexibility (which does not affect hyperspherical energy) while preserving angular structure, allowing OFT to fine-tune both directions and magnitudes with controlled degrees of freedom.

Taken together, these design choices explain OFT’s empirical advantages: the method provides enough representational change to enable subject-driven and controllable generation tasks, while the exact preservation of hyperspherical energy and optional proximity constraints prevent the accidental erosion of pretrained generative semantics. This principled constraint set yields faster, more stable convergence, improved sample efficiency, and predictable finetuning dynamics when adapting large text-to-image diffusion models.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization and Complexity

# Cayley transform for differentiable orthogonal matrices

A core practical challenge in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is how to represent and learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ in a way that is differentiable and efficient. The paper adopts the Cayley parameterization, a classical construction that expresses an orthogonal matrix through a skew‑symmetric parameter matrix. Concretely, let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfy the skew‑symmetry constraint $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley transform maps $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ via

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
$$

This parameterization has two important practical benefits. First, it enforces orthogonality ($\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$) for any skew‑symmetric $\bm{Q}$, so orthogonality is maintained automatically during optimization, avoiding expensive orthogonalization procedures at every step. Second, by parameterizing in terms of $\bm{Q}$ we can initialize from the identity mapping by setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which makes the finetuning start exactly at the pretrained weights (since $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ when $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$). This identity initialization reproduces the desirable “zero update” behavior analogous to zero initialization tricks used in other adaptation methods.

One practical consideration of the Cayley form is that it requires computing the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. This inverse is differentiable, so gradients propagate through the Cayley map, but the inverse operation does carry computational cost and can limit scalability when $\bm{Q}$ is large. The paper addresses this by combining Cayley parameterization with structure (block diagonalization) to make the inverse manageable in practice.

# Special orthogonal group and determinant considerations

A technical property of the Cayley parameterization is that it only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$. Those matrices form the special orthogonal group $\text{SO}(d)$ (rotations, excluding reflections). Thus the Cayley map cannot represent orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ (pure reflections or rotation+reflection combinations with odd parity).

The paper notes this limitation explicitly and reports that, in practice, restricting $\bm{R}$ to the special orthogonal group does not harm finetuning performance. In other words, learning rotations (and compositions of rotations) provides enough flexibility for OFT’s goals in text‑to‑image finetuning. The restriction to $\det(\bm{R})=1$ is therefore an acceptable practical tradeoff for the benefits of a concise, differentiable parameterization.

# Block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices for parameter efficiency

Learning a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has a parameter count that grows like $d(d-1)/2$, which can be large when $d$ (the neuron dimension) is large. To reduce both parameter count and computational cost, OFT uses a block‑diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$. The block‑diagonal form partitions the $d$ dimensions into $r$ equal blocks and places an independent orthogonal matrix on each block:

$$
\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i \in \text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right).
$$

Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is orthogonal on a subspace of dimension $d/r$, and $\bm{R}$ as a whole remains orthogonal. This design achieves two practical aims simultaneously. First, it drastically lowers the number of free parameters compared to a fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix. Second, it reduces the size of the matrix inverses required by the Cayley parameterization: instead of inverting a single $d\times d$ matrix, the implementation inverts $r$ matrices of size $(d/r)\times(d/r)$. Because the Cayley inverse cost grows with block size, partitioning into blocks makes the inverse operations far cheaper in practice.

Beyond efficiency, the block design acts as a form of structural regularization: by constraining interactions to block subspaces, OFT balances flexibility with stability. The number of blocks $r$ controls this tradeoff: $r=1$ yields the full orthogonal matrix (maximal flexibility), while larger $r$ yields more constrained, parameter‑efficient transforms.

# Optional block sharing across channels or heads

Parameter counts can be further reduced by sharing the same block matrix across blocks. That is, instead of learning distinct block matrices $\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r$, OFT can enforce $\bm{R}_1=\bm{R}_2=\cdots=\bm{R}_r=\bm{R}_{\text{shared}}$. This shared‑block design is particularly natural in convolutional or multi‑head architectures, where groups of channels or attention heads have similar roles. Sharing blocks reduces the number of learned parameters to the cost of a single block, while keeping the orthogonality and the structural benefits.

The block sharing option trades off even more flexibility for reduced parameter count and a stronger inductive bias. In convolutional settings, when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, the block‑diagonal transform effectively applies a per‑channel orthogonal transform (analogous to depth‑wise operations). When blocks are shared across channels, the same orthogonal transform is applied to many channels, allowing coordinated rotations that are cheaper to represent.

# Complexity analysis: $O(d^2)$, $O(d^2/r)$, and shared‑block variants

We can quantify the parameter counts of these choices. A full unconstrained orthogonal matrix in $d$ dimensions has

$$
\text{params}_{\text{full}} = \frac{d(d-1)}{2},
$$

because an orthogonal matrix is determined by $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom (the dimension of the orthogonal Lie algebra).

With an $r$‑block diagonal orthogonal matrix, each block has size $d/r$ and thus contributes $\frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2}$ parameters; summing over $r$ blocks gives

$$
\text{params}_{\text{block}} \;=\; r\cdot\frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2}
\;=\; \frac{d\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2}.
$$

This scales asymptotically as $O(d^2/r)$. As $r$ increases (more blocks, smaller block size), parameter count decreases. In the extreme $r=d$ (blocks of size $1$) the transform becomes trivial; in the other extreme $r=1$ the full orthogonal matrix is recovered.

If blocks are shared across the $r$ positions (all $\bm{R}_i$ identical), the total number of learned parameters reduces to that of a single block:

$$
\text{params}_{\text{shared}} \;=\; \frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2},
$$

which scales like $O(d^2/r^2)$. Thus sharing yields a quadratic improvement in parameter efficiency with respect to $r$ compared to the unshared block case.

These parameter reductions also reduce the cost of Cayley inverses. Instead of inverting a single matrix of size $d$, OFT inverts $r$ matrices of size $d/r$, or when blocks are shared only one inverse of size $d/r$ is required repeatedly. This cost reduction is a principal reason the paper adopts block structure as a practical compromise between expressiveness and scalability.

# Parameter‑efficiency comparison with LoRA via concrete examples

To make the differences concrete, we compare OFT’s parameter counts with LoRA, a popular low‑rank additive finetuning method. LoRA represents the additive update to a weight matrix by low‑rank factors with rank $r'$, and for a weight matrix of size $d\times n$ the LoRA trainable parameter count is

$$
\text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = r' (d + n).
$$

OFT’s block‑diagonal orthogonal parameter count for a layer of neuron dimension $d$ (assuming square transformations on the $d$ input dimension) is, as shown above,

$$
\text{params}_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}.
$$

Two instructive regimes highlight the differences:

1. Fixed, small rank/blocks. Suppose the layer dimension is $d=128$ and we choose $r'=8$ for LoRA and $r=8$ blocks for OFT. If the weight matrix is $128\times 128$ (so $n=d$), LoRA uses

   $$
   \text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = 8\cdot(128+128) = 8\cdot 256 = 2048,
   $$

   while OFT uses

   $$
   \text{params}_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{128\bigl(128/8 - 1\bigr)}{2} = \frac{128(16-1)}{2} = 960.
   $$

   Thus in this concrete example OFT attains substantially fewer trainable parameters than LoRA for these hyperparameter choices.

2. Rank or block count scaling with dimension. Suppose both the LoRA rank and the OFT block count scale linearly with $d$, i.e. $r'=\alpha d$ and $r=\alpha d$ for some constant $0<\alpha\le 1$. Then

   $$
   \text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d+n) \approx \alpha d (d+n) = O(d^2 + d n),
   $$

   while

   $$
   \text{params}_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{d(d/r -1)}{2} = \frac{d(1/\alpha - 1)}{2} = O(d).
   $$

   In this scaling regime, OFT becomes asymptotically much more parameter‑efficient than LoRA: LoRA grows quadratically with $d$ (modulo the additional $n$ dimension), whereas OFT grows only linearly with $d$ when $r$ is proportional to $d$.

These comparisons illustrate the essential design tradeoffs: LoRA reduces cost by restricting the update to a low‑rank additive subspace, whereas OFT reduces cost by constraining multiplicative updates to structured orthogonal transforms (block‑diagonal and optionally block‑shared). Which choice is preferable depends on the architecture, the layer shapes, and the desired balance between flexibility and parameter efficiency. The empirical results in the paper show that, for the text‑to‑image finetuning tasks considered, OFT’s block‑structured orthogonal parameterization yields good performance while often requiring fewer trainable parameters and converging faster than competing methods.

# Practical tradeoffs and concluding remarks

Putting the pieces together, the Cayley parameterization provides a differentiable and identity‑initializable map from skew‑symmetric parameters $\bm{Q}$ to orthogonal transforms $\bm{R}$, while the block‑diagonal and block‑sharing choices turn an otherwise costly orthogonal parameterization into a practical and scalable module. The block count $r$ and the decision to share blocks are the key knobs: increasing $r$ or sharing blocks reduces parameter and computational cost but also reduces transform expressiveness. The Cayley inverse operation is the primary computational burden; block structure reduces that burden by limiting inverse sizes. Finally, the Cayley map yields only special orthogonal matrices ($\det=+1$), a restriction that the paper finds acceptable in practice.

These combined design choices yield the parameter complexities and concrete efficiencies summarized above, and they explain why OFT can be both expressive enough to customize a pretrained diffusion model for downstream control tasks and compact enough to be trained efficiently with substantially fewer trainable parameters than many alternatives. The paper also notes as an open problem how to further accelerate the Cayley inverse in a differentiable way and how to improve parameter efficiency without introducing strong structural biases — choices that will determine future refinements of OFT’s practical parameterizations.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): Stability via Radius Control

# Motivation: explicit deviation constraint in addition to orthogonality

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) enforces that the finetuned weight matrix is an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights, which provably preserves pairwise neuron angles and therefore the hyperspherical energy that captures neuron relational structure. While this constraint alone controls angular changes and yields strong stability relative to unconstrained finetuning, it does not by itself limit how far the orthogonal transformation can move from the identity. In practice, some downstream tasks or aggressive optimization can still produce large orthogonal rotations that, although angle-preserving, lead to unwanted changes in the model's behavior or make the finetuning dynamics sensitive to hyperparameters such as learning rate and number of iterations.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) was introduced to address this gap by adding an explicit deviation (radius) constraint around the identity matrix in the orthogonal parameter space. The intuition is straightforward: we want to keep the benefits of orthogonality (angle preservation, smooth manifold optimization) while also guaranteeing the finetuned model remains within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained model. Constraining the orthogonal transform to be near identity reduces the effective degrees of freedom available during adaptation, improving finetuning stability and making training dynamics less sensitive to hyperparameter choices. In other words, COFT adds an explicit regularizer on top of OFT that directly controls how far the model is allowed to move, rather than relying implicitly on small learning rates or indirect penalties.

This explicit deviation constraint matches a common practical desideratum for finetuning large generative models: preserve the pretrained generative prior as much as possible while making only the minimal, semantically meaningful adaptations needed by the downstream task. COFT formalizes this desideratum by introducing a tunable radius parameter that trades flexibility (ability to adapt) against regularity (preservation and stability).

# COFT forward pass and identity initialization

COFT modifies the forward computation of a layer in a simple, principled way. For a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (columns are neurons), COFT applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and computes the layer output as

\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]

subject to two constraints on $\bm{R}$:

1. orthogonality: $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\,\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, and  
2. deviation to identity bounded by $\epsilon$: $\lVert \bm{R} - \bm{I} \rVert \le \epsilon$.

The orthogonality requirement preserves pairwise neuron angles (hence hyperspherical energy), while the second constraint explicitly bounds the angular deviation from the pretrained coordinate frame. Identity initialization is critical for starting finetuning exactly at the pretrained model. In COFT this is realized by initializing the orthogonal transform to the identity, i.e., $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the beginning of training. Practically, COFT achieves this by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ through a skew-symmetric surrogate (see below) and initializing that surrogate to the all-zero matrix. With the zero surrogate, the parameterized $\bm{R}$ evaluates to $\bm{I}$, so the model begins training exactly as the pretrained network.

Starting at identity makes the deviation constraint meaningful from step one: as training proceeds, the orthogonal parameters may move away from identity but are always projected (or otherwise constrained) to remain within the prescribed radius. This explicit control is what gives COFT its improved finetuning stability compared to unconstrained OFT or unrestricted finetuning.

# Neumann series approximation to constrain Cayley parameters

COFT uses the Cayley parameterization to represent orthogonal matrices in a differentiable way. The Cayley map builds an orthogonal matrix from a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (so $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$) via

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]

This parameterization guarantees orthogonality (in fact, it lands in the special orthogonal group because it produces $\det(\bm{R}) = 1$ under standard conditions). COFT needs to enforce the radius constraint $\lVert \bm{R} - \bm{I} \rVert \le \epsilon$, but imposing this constraint directly on $\bm{R}$ when $\bm{R}$ is Cayley-parameterized is awkward because of the matrix inverse in the Cayley formula.

To make the radius constraint practical, COFT invokes a local approximation based on the Neumann series for $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. When $\bm{Q}$ has small operator norm so that the Neumann series converges, we have

\[
(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots,
\]

and substituting into the Cayley formula gives the first-order approximation

\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]

This expansion shows that for small $\bm{Q}$, the deviation $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is approximately linear in $\bm{Q}$. Therefore, bounding $\lVert \bm{R} - \bm{I} \rVert \le \epsilon$ can be approximated by bounding $\lVert \bm{Q} \rVert \le \epsilon'$ for some suitably chosen $\epsilon'$ (roughly $\epsilon' \approx \epsilon/2$ under the linear approximation, with higher-order corrections absorbed into the mapping between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$). Because $\bm{Q}$ is the direct parameter we optimize, enforcing $\lVert \bm{Q} \rVert \le \epsilon'$ is far simpler and enables efficient projected updates (see next section).

Two caveats are worth noting. First, the Neumann expansion (and therefore the approximation $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}$) is only valid when $\bm{Q}$ is sufficiently small in operator norm so that the series converges. COFT deliberately keeps $\bm{Q}$ small through projection, so the approximation is internally consistent. Second, the mapping from the desired radius $\epsilon$ on $\bm{R}$ to the surrogate radius $\epsilon'$ on $\bm{Q}$ is not exact; COFT treats $\epsilon'$ as a hyperparameter chosen to achieve the intended behavior in practice.

# Projected gradient descent on skew-symmetric parameters

With the Cayley parameterization and the Neumann-based approximation, COFT trains by optimizing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ subject to a simple norm ball constraint. The overall scheme is conceptually straightforward and efficient to implement:

1. Parameterize $\bm{R}$ by a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ via the Cayley map, initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. If desired, use a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{Q}$ (and hence for $\bm{R}$) to reduce parameter cost.  
2. In each training iteration, compute forward and backward passes using $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ to get gradients with respect to $\bm{Q}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric by construction, ensure that the parameter representation enforces skew-symmetry throughout (for example, store and update only the upper-triangular entries and reflect them to the lower triangle with a sign change).  
3. Take an unconstrained gradient step on $\bm{Q}$ (e.g., SGD or Adam).  
4. Project the updated $\bm{Q}$ back onto the allowed ball $\{ \bm{Q} : \lVert \bm{Q} \rVert \le \epsilon' \}$ to enforce the COFT radius constraint. The projection is straightforward: if the norm of the updated $\bm{Q}$ is within the ball, do nothing; otherwise, rescale the matrix so its norm equals $\epsilon'$. The projection preserves skew-symmetry because rescaling a skew-symmetric matrix yields a skew-symmetric matrix.

The choice of matrix norm for the projection (operator norm vs. Frobenius norm) can be implementation dependent. The paper writes the constraint as $\lVert \bm{Q} - \bm{0} \rVert \le \epsilon'$, leaving the norm unspecified; in practice, a Frobenius-norm projection is simple and effective: if $\lVert \bm{Q} \rVert_F > \epsilon'$, replace $\bm{Q}$ by $(\epsilon'/\lVert \bm{Q} \rVert_F)\,\bm{Q}$. This projected gradient descent loop enforces both the orthogonality (via Cayley map) and the radius constraint (via projection on $\bm{Q}$), while identity initialization guarantees the model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

Algorithmically this is inexpensive: the main extra cost is forming the Cayley map (involving $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$) during forward/backward passes and occasionally projecting $\bm{Q}$. To reduce cost and parameters further, COFT may adopt block-diagonal structure for $\bm{Q}$ so that the inverse and projections act blockwise.

# Effect of epsilon: flexibility-stability trade-off and behavior limits

The radius parameter $\epsilon$ (and its surrogate $\epsilon'$ on $\bm{Q}$) is the single knob that directly controls the flexibility–stability trade-off in COFT. Understanding its limiting behaviors helps place COFT on the spectrum between the pretrained model and unconstrained OFT:

- Epsilon approaching zero ($\epsilon\to 0$). When the radius is essentially zero, the orthogonal matrix is forced to remain at identity and no adaptation occurs. In the limit this reproduces the pretrained model exactly. This extreme is useful as a conceptual boundary and makes precise the idea that COFT interpolates between 'no change' and 'full OFT'.

- Small epsilon. With a small but nonzero radius, COFT allows only tiny orthogonal perturbations to the pretrained coordinates. This setting preserves pretraining behavior tightly and makes finetuning extremely stable and insensitive to learning rate and number of iterations. It may, however, limit the model's ability to adapt to difficult downstream tasks that require larger angular shifts.

- Large epsilon. As $\epsilon$ increases and the radius constraint becomes loose, COFT approaches the behavior of unconstrained OFT. In the limit where the radius constraint is effectively inactive, COFT and OFT behave similarly and enjoy the same angle-preserving properties. The model has higher flexibility but may be less robust to aggressive optimization and may require more careful tuning of hyperparameters.

In summary, the radius parameter allows explicit control over adaptation capacity. Practically, practitioners pick $\epsilon$ by trading off how much deviation from the pretrained model they are willing to tolerate versus how much adaptation the downstream task demands. The paper provides empirical examples showing that moderate values of $\epsilon$ yield strong practical benefits: COFT is more stable than OFT while still allowing effective adaptation.

# Empirical stability benefits and convergence smoothing

COFT was evaluated on two representative text-to-image finetuning tasks: subject-driven generation (adaptation to a few images of a subject) and controllable generation (adding control signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks). The empirical findings in the paper demonstrate COFT's practical advantages:

First, COFT improves finetuning stability. In subject-driven generation experiments, both OFT and COFT finetuned models achieved stable control faster than baseline methods like DreamBooth and LoRA, but COFT exhibited even better stability than OFT in practice. COFT's explicit radius constraint reduced sensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations and learning rate, making it possible to set these hyperparameters without delicate tuning. In particular, COFT did not collapse or produce degraded identity preservation even when training for many iterations where unconstrained finetuning or other methods became unstable.

Second, COFT smooths convergence. In controllable generation experiments (for example, landmark-to-face), the training curves for COFT showed steady, gradual improvement in control-consistency metrics as epochs progressed. This contrasts with some prior methods where successful controllability could emerge suddenly after several epochs (a “phase transition” behavior), making training unpredictable. COFT's constrained parameter space and orthogonal geometry produced a smoother optimization landscape and more predictable dynamics.

Third, COFT maintains sample efficiency. The paper shows COFT converges faster than alternatives such as LoRA and adapter-based approaches, frequently reaching similar or better performance in fewer epochs and with fewer trainable parameters. This efficiency is attributed to two factors working together: (1) orthogonal updates target neuron directions that are most relevant for semantic changes, and (2) the radius constraint prevents overstepping and oscillation in training.

Finally, COFT's improvements are not limited to numerical metrics. Qualitative results in both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks show COFT produces high-fidelity images that better preserve subject identity and more accurately follow control signals. Because COFT imposes no additional test-time overhead—learned orthogonal transforms can be fused into the pretrained weights at inference—the stability and convergence benefits come with no runtime cost.

# Practical recommendations and open considerations

COFT provides a principled and practical way to add explicit deviation control to orthogonal finetuning. A few operational recommendations follow directly from the method and the empirical observations:

- Initialize the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ to zero so the model starts at the pretrained weights.  
- Choose a moderate $\epsilon$ (and corresponding $\epsilon'$ for $\bm{Q}$) to allow meaningful adaptation while keeping optimization stable. Empirically, moderate values that permit small but nontrivial rotations tend to work well; very small values recover the pretrained model, and very large values reduce COFT to OFT.  
- Use block-diagonal structure for $\bm{Q}$ when parameter or computational budgets demand it; this reduces cost while preserving the core orthogonality property.  
- Enforce the radius constraint by projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ after each optimizer step. A Frobenius-norm projection is simple and effective in practice.

COFT also surfaces open implementation and research questions. The Cayley parameterization requires solving $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, which can be computationally burdensome for very large block sizes; block-diagonalization mitigates this but introduces bias. The Neumann-based approximation used to map the $\bm{R}$ radius to a $\bm{Q}$ radius is valid only when $\bm{Q}$ remains small; COFT's projection ensures this but the precise calibration between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ can require tuning. Finally, the paper points toward intriguing compositional properties of orthogonal transforms (multiplicative composition remains orthogonal) and how COFT might enable combining multiple task-specific transforms, but whether composition preserves all downstream behaviors is an open question.

In sum, COFT augments OFT by making the deviation from pretrained coordinates an explicit, controllable constraint. This simple addition produces marked empirical gains in finetuning stability, convergence smoothness, and robustness to hyperparameter settings—without changing inference-time behavior—making COFT a practical approach for stable adaptation of large text-to-image diffusion models.

Extensions and Applicability: Re-scaled OFT and Layer Choices

# Re-scaled OFT: learning a per-neuron positive scaling without affecting energy

The re-scaled variant of Orthogonal Finetuning (re-scaled OFT) augments the orthogonal transformation by also learning per-neuron magnitudes. In the original OFT forward pass a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is transformed by a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ so that the finetuned weights are $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and the layer output is
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Re-scaled OFT introduces a diagonal, positive scaling matrix $\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and uses the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy depends only on normalized neuron directions, scaling each neuron by a positive scalar does not change the pairwise angles after normalization; in other words, multiplying columns of $\bm{W}$ by positive scalars leaves the hyperspherical energy invariant. This is the key reason the diagonal scaling can be learned without violating OFT’s central invariance property.

From a practical point of view, the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ provides an inexpensive degree of freedom to adapt neuron magnitudes per output channel. It increases the expressive power of the finetuning step with relatively few additional parameters: only one scalar per neuron. The paper notes that re-scaled OFT generally improves flexibility and performance with a negligible parameter cost, while preserving the hyperspherical energy objective that motivates OFT. An implementation detail called out in the paper's errata is that the correct re-scaled forward pass is $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$; the camera-ready text had a mistaken ordering, but the implementation and reported results used the correct ordering. Initialization is chosen so that $\bm{R}$ starts as identity and $\bm{D}$ typically starts close to the all-ones diagonal, ensuring the finetuning begins from the pretrained model.

# Applying OFT to attention weights (fair comparison to LoRA)

When adapting OFT to diffusion models built with Transformer-style blocks, a natural and practical place to apply the method is the attention weight matrices. The paper follows this choice in experiments to enable a fair comparison with LoRA, because LoRA commonly targets attention layers (in particular the query, key, value, and output projection matrices). The rationale is straightforward: attention weight matrices are prominent, highly parameterized dense layers whose neurons (columns) are meaningful units to rotate and scale, so they fit OFT’s formulation of applying a layer-shared orthogonal transform.

Concretely, suppose an attention projection matrix in a Transformer block is $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. OFT replaces the forward computation $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ by $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with a learned orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. To make a fair head-to-head with LoRA, the same attention projections that LoRA augments should be the ones where OFT is applied. This parity ensures the comparison evaluates the structural differences between additive low-rank updates (LoRA) and multiplicative orthogonal transforms (OFT), rather than differences in which parts of the model were adapted.

The experimental choice also has practical benefits: attention matrices are dense and typically have manageable row dimension $d$ for orthogonal parameterization; they thus permit efficient Cayley parameterizations and block-diagonal formulations (see below). Moreover, applying OFT only to attention weights keeps the number of extra trainable parameters comparable to LoRA, so observed improvements can be attributed to the method rather than to a larger parameter budget.

# Applying OFT to convolutional layers: depth-wise and shared-block views

Convolutional layers require special consideration because their weight tensors are not organized as simple dense matrices of independent neuron columns in the same way as fully connected or attention projections. Nevertheless, OFT adapts naturally to convolution by exploiting the block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal transform.

The paper suggests using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)}$ is orthogonal. When this block structure aligns with convolution channels, it admits two interpretable extremes. If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, then each block $\bm{R}_i$ acts independently on a single channel’s neurons; this resembles depth-wise transformations and is analogous to learning a depth-wise orthogonal kernel across that channel’s basis. At the other extreme, when all blocks are shared (i.e., $\bm{R}_1=\bm{R}_2=\dots=\bm{R}_r$), the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels, which corresponds to a channel-shared rotation of neuron directions.

This block view accomplishes two things that are practically valuable. First, it dramatically reduces the number of parameters needed to represent $\bm{R}$. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $d(d-1)/2$ parameters; an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix requires only $d(d/r-1)/2$ parameters, costing roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and sharing blocks can reduce that further toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Second, the block structure creates inductive biases suited to convolutional architectures: depth-wise-like blocks localize the adaptivity to channel-specific bases while shared blocks encourage consistent rotations across channels. The paper reports a preliminary study on convolutional OFT in an appendix and indicates these interpretations are meaningful for finetuning convolutional kernels.

Finally, because the orthogonal transform acts multiplicatively on pretrained convolution weights, it preserves hyperspherical energy per transformed neuron and can be fused into the convolution kernel at inference time without extra compute: multiply $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}$ to obtain $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and use $\bm{W}$ directly.

# Architectural agnosticism: transformers, CNNs, and hybrids

One of OFT’s conceptual strengths is its architectural agnosticism. The central operation — multiplying a pretrained weight tensor by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix that rotates neuron directions — applies to any layer that can be interpreted as a linear map from inputs to neuron activations. This includes fully connected layers, attention projections in Transformers, and appropriately interpreted convolutional kernels. In principle, OFT can be applied wherever neurons (columns of a weight matrix) are meaningful axes to rotate.

From an implementation viewpoint, the main accommodations are (1) reshaping or grouping convolutional weight tensors to align neurons with the orthogonal block structure, and (2) selecting which layers to adapt given computational budget and comparison goals. In the experiments the authors limited OFT to the same attention layers LoRA modifies so that comparisons were fair and parameter counts matched. For other uses, one can extend OFT to multiple layers across the network, or combine OFT on attention layers with block-diagonal OFT on convolutional layers, yielding a hybrid finetuning strategy that respects the architectural primitives of each component.

Two practical points follow from the agnosticism claim. First, there is no algorithmic change required in generation-time inference: learned orthogonal transforms can be absorbed into pretrained weights to produce an equivalent finetuned model with identical inference cost. Second, the choice of which layers to finetune remains a design decision guided by trade-offs between flexibility and parameter efficiency: applying OFT broadly increases expressivity but raises parameter and compute demands during finetuning, while targeted application (e.g., to attention heads) offers a compact, effective adaptation.

# Compositionality potential: chaining orthogonal transforms across tasks

An intriguing property of OFT stems from group structure: the set of orthogonal matrices is closed under multiplication. If a pretrained model $\bm{W}^0$ is finetuned with OFT for task A producing $\bm{R}_A$, and later finetuned with OFT for task B producing $\bm{R}_B$, then applying both transforms sequentially produces the combined orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_{\mathrm{combined}}=\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$, which is itself orthogonal. Equivalently, the finetuned weight after two tasks can be written as
\[
\bm{W}_{\text{after B}}=\bm{R}_B(\bm{R}_A\bm{W}^0)=(\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A)\bm{W}^0.
\]
This algebraic fact opens up the possibility of compositionality: orthogonal transforms learned for multiple downstream tasks could in principle be combined (via multiplication) to yield a single orthogonal transform that encodes multiple task-specific adaptations while preserving hyperspherical energy.

The paper identifies this compositional property as an attractive potential and an open problem. The important caveat is that while the product of orthogonal matrices preserves the hyperspherical energy invariance, it is not guaranteed that the composed transform will preserve the functional knowledge of each individual finetuning task simultaneously. In other words, algebraic closure does not imply semantic additivity: chaining multiple task-specific rotations may interfere in ways that harm performance on earlier tasks or produce unintended interactions. Investigating whether and under what conditions a sequence or set of orthogonal matrices can be multiplied to preserve, combine, or recover the knowledge of multiple downstream tasks is an active research question raised by the authors.

From a practical standpoint, the compositionality potential suggests a pathway towards modular adapters: store each task as a compact orthogonal transform (or block-shared variants) and apply, compose, or selectively enable them at inference to produce multi-task behavior without growing the base model. However, the paper highlights this only as an interesting direction rather than an established protocol, leaving empirical validation and constraints (e.g., ordering effects, non-commutativity in practice due to numerical precision and block sharing) to future work.

# Practical recommendations and closing remarks

Taken together, these extensions and layer choices sketch actionable guidelines for applying OFT in real diffusion-model finetuning settings. If you want a strict apples-to-apples comparison with LoRA, apply OFT to the same attention projection layers LoRA modifies. If you need to adapt convolutional parts of the generator or the U-Net backbone, use block-diagonal orthogonal matrices aligned to channel groupings; choosing block counts that match channel partitions yields depth-wise-like behavior, while shared blocks encourage consistent transforms across channels. When more flexibility is desirable but you wish to keep the hyperspherical-energy invariance, consider re-scaled OFT to add per-neuron magnitudes with almost no extra cost. Finally, remember that all OFT variants can be fused into the pretrained weights for inference, so there is no additional runtime overhead.

The architectural agnosticism and the compositional algebra of orthogonal matrices render OFT a conceptually clean and practically versatile finetuning framework. The method preserves the neuron-angle structure motivated by hyperspherical energy while giving controlled, multiplicative updates that have proven empirically to improve convergence, stability, and controllability in the text-to-image diffusion tasks studied in the paper.

Implementation Guide and Training Recipes

# Integrating OFT into Stable Diffusion v1.5

This chapter gives a practical, implementation-oriented walkthrough for applying Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) to a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion v1.5 (the model used for the experiments in the paper). The high-level integration pattern is simple: replace or wrap a selected pretrained weight matrix \($\bm{W}^0$\) with an OFT-parameterized version during training, optimize only the OFT parameters, and finally bake the learned transform into the original weights for inference so that there is no runtime overhead.

Concretely, pick the subset of weights you want to adapt. In the experiments in the paper OFT was applied to the same attention weight matrices that LoRA targets so comparisons are fair; OFT is agnostic to architecture, so you may also apply it to other fully connected or convolutional layers. For a given matrix layer with pretrained weights \($\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$\), replace the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
with the OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where \($\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$\) is an orthogonal matrix to be learned. Train only the OFT parameters (the parameterization of \($\bm{R}$\) and any optional diagonal scaling \($\bm{D}$\)), leaving \($\bm{W}^0$\) frozen. After finetuning, compute the equivalent weight \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$\) (or \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$\) for re-scaled OFT) and replace the layer weights with \($\bm{W}$\) for inference; this yields identical runtime cost to the original model.

# Initialization of orthogonal and scaling parameters

Initialization is important to ensure training starts exactly from the pretrained model and that OFT behaves as a conservative, stable adaptation. Initialize the orthogonal transform as the identity so the finetuned model begins with \($\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0$\). In the Cayley parameterization used in the paper, the orthogonal matrix is constructed from a skew-symmetric matrix \($\bm{Q}$\) via
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
with \($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$\). To initialize \($\bm{R}$\) to identity, initialize \($\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$\). Training then updates \($\bm{Q}$\) (subject to any COFT projection described below); because \($\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$\) maps to \($\bm{R}=\bm{I}$\), the model begins exactly at the pretrained weights.

If you choose the re-scaled OFT extension, the forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}
\]
where \($\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$\) is a learnable diagonal matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries. To preserve the pretrained behavior at initialization, initialize each scale \($s_i=1$\) so \($\bm{D}=\bm{I}$\) and the initial forward pass is unchanged. The paper notes an erratum in the camera-ready version but confirms the implementation uses \($\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$\) (scaling on the column side). Learning \($\bm{D}$\) provides additional flexibility while leaving hyperspherical energy unchanged.

# Choosing block count, block sharing, and learning rates

A full \($d\times d$\) orthogonal matrix has \($d(d-1)/2$\) degrees of freedom and can be expensive to parameterize when \($d$\) is large. The paper adopts a block-diagonal orthogonal structure to reduce parameter cost while preserving the orthogonality guarantee. Represent \($\bm{R}$\) as a block-diagonal matrix with \($r$\) blocks,
\[
\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\mathrm{O}\!\bigl(\tfrac{d}{r}\bigr),
\]
so each block \($\bm{R}_i$\) is an orthogonal matrix of size \($\tfrac{d}{r}\times\tfrac{d}{r}$\). This reduces the number of parameters to approximately \($\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$\). You can further reduce parameters by sharing the same block across multiple positions, in which case parameter complexity becomes approximately \($\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$\).

Choosing the block count \($r$\) trades off flexibility and parameter cost. Small \($r$\) (fewer, larger blocks, with \($r=1$\) a full orthogonal matrix) gives maximal flexibility but highest parameter cost. Larger \($r$\) (many small blocks) constrains the per-layer transformation and reduces trainable parameters. The paper demonstrates that block-diagonal constraint can be interpreted for convolutional layers: if you set the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels, each block acts on a single channel and the transform resembles depthwise channel-wise transforms. Use this interpretation when deciding \($r$\) for convolutional layers.

Block sharing is an additional lever. Sharing blocks reduces the number of unique parameters and adds a structural bias (the same orthogonal transform is applied repeatedly). This can be helpful when you have a tight parameter budget or want stronger regularization; the paper shows block sharing is an explicit parameter-efficiency option.

The paper provides an illustrative parameter-count example: for a \($128\times 128$\) weight matrix, LoRA with \($r'=8$\) has 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with \($r=8$\) has 960 trainable parameters (block sharing not applied). Use similar bookkeeping to estimate your budget and choose \($r$\) accordingly.

Regarding learning rates: OFT is reported to converge faster and to be less sensitive to iteration counts than standard finetuning, and COFT further improves stability. The practical implication is that OFT often requires less aggressive hyperparameter tuning than naive finetuning, but you should still tune the optimizer and step size on a validation set for best results. The paper compares OFT against LoRA applied to the same layers to ensure fairness; following this practice (apply OFT to the same layers LoRA would target) keeps comparison and tuning straightforward.

# Selecting epsilon in COFT; sensitivity and heuristics

COFT augments the orthogonality constraint with an explicit radius (deviation) constraint to keep the learned orthogonal transform near identity. Formally COFT enforces
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},\qquad \text{s.t.}\quad \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon.
\]
Directly constraining \($\bm{R}$\) is inconvenient with the Cayley parameterization, so the paper uses the Neumann-series approximation
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
\]
to move the radius constraint inside the Cayley parameter by constraining \($\bm{Q}$\) instead: approximately \($\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon \Rightarrow \|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$\) for some \($\epsilon'$\). Enforce the \($\bm{Q}$\)-constraint using projected gradient descent: after each gradient update on \($\bm{Q}$\), project \($\bm{Q}$\) back into the feasible ball \($\{\bm{Q}:\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\}$\).

Epsilon controls the flexibility/stability trade-off. The paper emphasizes the empirical pattern: smaller \($\epsilon$\) (and thus smaller \($\epsilon'$\)) forces the finetuned model to stay close to the pretrained model and yields greater finetuning stability; larger \($\epsilon$\) approaches the unconstrained OFT behavior and enables greater flexibility. The paper presents experiments indicating COFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations and that an appropriate \($\epsilon$\) makes both learning rate and iteration count easy to choose. A practical heuristic supported by the paper is to start with a relatively small \($\epsilon$\) when finetuning on limited data or when you want to prioritize preservation of pretrained behavior, and increase \($\epsilon$\) only if the model cannot achieve the downstream task within that restriction.

Implementation notes from the paper: initialize \($\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$\) so \($\bm{R}=\bm{I}$\) at start; enforce skew-symmetry \($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$\) as part of your parameter representation; and use projection to maintain \($\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$\) during optimization. COFT empirically yields improved stability over unconstrained OFT.

# Data efficiency: few images for subject-driven and partial datasets for control

One of OFT's practical strengths reported in the paper is data efficiency. In subject-driven generation (the few-shot personalization task), OFT and COFT require only a few subject images to converge to strong subject fidelity and text-prompt fidelity. In the paper, OFT achieves good control after 400 iterations and remains stable at larger iteration counts; DreamBooth and other baselines were more sensitive to the exact iteration count and sometimes collapsed when trained longer. This robustness makes OFT convenient for subject-driven workflows where the number of images is small and you prefer not to precisely tune the training schedule.

For controllable generation tasks (learning to condition generation on edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks), OFT also shows strong sample efficiency. The experiments finetuned Stable Diffusion on datasets such as ADE20K (segmentation), COCO (Canny→image) and CelebA-HQ (landmarks→face). The paper highlights two concrete efficiency phenomena: first, OFT converges faster in epochs than comparator methods (an example reported is that OFT reaches LoRA’s final performance at about the 8th epoch while LoRA needed 20 epochs); second, OFT still performs well when trained on only a small fraction of the control dataset (the paper reports convergence with only 5% of ADE20K in an experiment). Use these empirical observations as guidance: OFT is attractive where you have limited task-specific data or wish to finetune quickly.

Practically, for subject-driven tasks adopt the DreamBooth training loss and pipeline but replace the finetuning step with OFT/COFT. Because OFT is robust to the number of iterations, you can safely set a reasonably large iteration budget and rely on COFT (if used) to prevent drift. For control tasks, follow the training setup from ControlNet/T2I-Adapter but substitute OFT/COFT in place of their adapter parameterizations; the paper shows that OFT applied in this way produces stronger control with similar or fewer parameters and fewer training epochs.

# Baking in R at inference for zero overhead

The final deployment step is straightforward and critical to realize OFT’s practical advantage: there is no inference-time overhead. After training, compute the equivalent finetuned weights and replace the original layer weights:

• For OFT (no re-scaling): compute \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$\) and store \($\bm{W}$\) in place of \($\bm{W}^0$\).

• For re-scaled OFT: compute \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$\) and store \($\bm{W}$\).

This is an exact algebraic substitution: the forward pass with the learned \($\bm{R}$\) (and optionally \($\bm{D}$\)) is mathematically identical to the forward pass with \($\bm{W}$\). Because the learned orthogonal transform has been multiplied into the pretrained weights, no extra matrix operations, adapters, or control networks are needed at inference time. The paper emphasizes this as a deployment advantage relative to methods that add controller modules (e.g., ControlNet), which increase inference computation and memory.

# Practical step-by-step recipe (putting the pieces together)

The following summarizes a canonical implementation flow consistent with the approach and experiments in the paper. Each step is stated at a level of detail sufficient to implement OFT/COFT without departing from the paper’s descriptions.

1. Select layers to adapt. To compare fairly with LoRA, choose the same attention weight matrices LoRA would target; OFT can also be applied to convolutional and fully connected layers.

2. Choose block-diagonalization: pick the number of blocks \($r$\) and whether to share blocks. Use the parameter-budget guidance in the paper (smaller \($r$\) = more flexible, larger parameter count; larger \($r$\) = more constrained, fewer parameters). If adapting convolutional layers, consider aligning blocks with channels as described.

3. Parameterize the orthogonal transform. Use the Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\qquad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
and initialize \($\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$\) so \($\bm{R}=\bm{I}$\) at start. If you will enforce a COFT radius constraint, plan to project \($\bm{Q}$\) after each update to satisfy \($\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$\).

4. (Optional) Add re-scaling. If you need extra flexibility, add a diagonal \($\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$\) and initialize \($s_i=1$\) for all \($i$\).

5. Training loop. Freeze \($\bm{W}^0$\) and optimize only the OFT parameters (the parameters that represent \($\bm{Q}$\) and, if used, \($\bm{D}$\)). Use the same downstream loss used in the relevant task: DreamBooth reconstruction and prior-preservation losses for subject-driven generation, and the task-specific control losses used in ControlNet/T2I-Adapter setups for controllable generation. If using COFT, after each gradient update project \($\bm{Q}$\) to the constrained set \($\{\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\}$\) (the paper implements this with projected gradient descent).

6. Monitor convergence and metrics. The paper reports OFT typically converges faster and more stably than LoRA and DreamBooth plain finetuning. In subject-driven tasks the paper observes good control after 400 iterations and robustness over longer runs; in controllable generation OFT reached strong performance in fewer epochs (the paper compares 8 vs 20 epochs). Use task-appropriate quantitative metrics (CLIP, DINO, LPIPS for subject fidelity; control consistency metrics such as IoU/F1 or landmark \($\ell_2$\) distance for controls) to pick the final checkpoint.

7. Bake and deploy. After training, compute the equivalent weight \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$\) (or \($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$\) for re-scaled OFT), replace the layer weights in the model with \($\bm{W}$\), and export the model. There is no additional runtime adapter or computational cost compared to the original pretrained model.

# Notes, trade-offs, and practical pointers from the paper

OFT finds a middle ground between flexibility and regularity: orthogonal transforms allow expressive directional changes (angles) among neurons while preserving hyperspherical energy, which the paper argues is important for preserving pretrained generative capabilities. The block-diagonal parameterization provides an explicit handle to trade expressivity for parameter efficiency; block sharing further reduces parameters at the cost of a structural bias.

COFT is particularly useful when you need stronger guarantees of staying near the pretrained model. The radius constraint stabilizes finetuning and makes the learning-rate and iteration-number hyperparameters easier to set. Empirically COFT provided better finetuning stability than OFT alone.

Re-scaled OFT adds per-neuron magnitude adjustment \($\bm{D}$\) which does not change hyperspherical energy but increases flexibility; initialize \($\bm{D}=\bm{I}$\) and expect modest extra parameters.

Finally, because OFT parameters are multiply-applied to the pretrained weights and can be baked in, there is no inference overhead: the trained model runs with identical speed and memory usage to the original model after the simple weight replacement \($\bm{W}\leftarrow\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$\).

This chapter consolidates the design choices and empirical behaviors reported in the paper into a practical recipe. Follow the initialization choices (identity \($\bm{R}$\), zero \($\bm{Q}$\), ones for \($\bm{D}$\)), choose \($r$\) and sharing based on parameter budget and desired flexibility, and use COFT’s radius constraint when stability and preservation are paramount. The result is a finetuning pipeline that is data-efficient, converges rapidly, and deploys with zero runtime overhead.

Subject-Driven Generation: Methods, Metrics, and Results

# Training setup following DreamBooth, identical losses and layers

The subject-driven generation experiments in the paper follow the DreamBooth framework precisely: the same loss function and training protocol are used, and all competing methods are evaluated under identical finetuning targets and conditions so that comparisons are fair. The pretrained backbone is Stable Diffusion v1.5; during finetuning the objective remains the reconstruction / conditioning loss used by DreamBooth to teach the model to associate a small set of subject images with a custom token in prompts.

To make comparisons directly comparable to adapter-style baselines (notably LoRA), OFT and COFT are applied to the same model layers where LoRA is applied in the baselines. OFT is initialized such that the finetuned network exactly equals the pretrained network at the start: the learned layer-shared orthogonal matrix is initialized as the identity. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit neighborhood constraint around that identity so the finetuned weights remain close to the pretrained weights throughout training; in implementation this is achieved by Cayley parameterization of the orthogonal matrix and a small projected-gradient constraint on the Cayley parameter (see the main paper for the parameterization details). These implementation choices ensure the only substantive difference between methods is the parameterization and constraints used for finetuning, not loss function or training data.

# Metrics: DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for prompt fidelity, LPIPS for diversity

Evaluating subject-driven generation requires separate signals for how well the model preserves the subject identity, how faithfully it follows the text prompt, and how diverse the generated samples are. The paper uses the following automated metrics:

- DINO: This is a perceptual similarity computed from ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. Practically, the metric used is the average pairwise cosine similarity between generated images and the real subject images in the DINO embedding space. Higher DINO similarity indicates the generated images better reflect the visual identity of the subject.

- CLIP-I (image-based CLIP similarity): This measures the average cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings of generated images and the real subject images. Like DINO, CLIP-I assesses whether the generated images match the subject’s appearance, but in the CLIP embedding space. Higher CLIP-I scores indicate better subject fidelity.

- CLIP-T (text-based CLIP similarity): This measures the cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of the generated images and the conditioning text prompt. CLIP-T is a measure of prompt fidelity: higher values mean the sampler’s outputs match the text guidance more closely.

- LPIPS (perceptual diversity): LPIPS is used to measure diversity among multiple generated samples of the same subject under the same prompt. The paper reports an average LPIPS-based similarity between generated images; this captures whether a method can produce varied samples rather than collapse to near-identical outputs.

These metrics together separate “does the model preserve the person/thing I gave it?” (DINO, CLIP-I), “does it respect the new description I asked for?” (CLIP-T), and “does it generate a spread of plausible outputs?” (LPIPS).

In the experiments the authors repeatedly finetuned the same pretrained model with 30 different random seeds to reduce randomness and compute stable averages for these metrics. All methods used the same prompt sets and sampling procedures for fair comparison.

# Convergence behavior and insensitivity to iteration count

A core experimental finding is that OFT and its constrained variant COFT converge faster and more stably than both DreamBooth’s vanilla finetuning and LoRA-style low-rank additive updates. Concretely, the paper reports that both OFT and COFT obtain good subject control after only a few hundred iterations (the figures referenced show good control by about 400 iterations). In contrast, LoRA often fails to preserve subject identity even with many more iterations, and DreamBooth’s unconstrained finetuning can initially succeed but later collapse when trained for too long (the paper notes collapse behavior observed after roughly 2000 iterations in some runs).

Two important practical consequences follow from these behaviors:

1. Reduced sensitivity to the finetuning iteration count. Because OFT and especially COFT preserve structure (pairwise neuron angles) and, in COFT, also explicitly limit deviation from the pretrained parameters, they are much less sensitive to overtraining. Practitioners can set a larger iteration budget without careful early-stopping, which greatly simplifies hyperparameter selection.

2. Faster attainment of usable models and greater sample efficiency. OFT reaches acceptable subject preservation in fewer iterations and with fewer subject images than the baselines, so a user can obtain good personalized generations with less compute and fewer example photos.

The paper attributes the faster and more stable convergence to two complementary factors. First, changing neuron directions (which orthogonal transforms do) is an effective and semantically relevant degree of freedom for altering generative behavior while retaining pretrained knowledge. Second, restricting updates to an orthogonal manifold (and further constraining the operator with COFT) yields a smoother optimization landscape and prevents destabilizing shifts that cause collapse.

# Qualitative examples: identity preservation and prompt adherence

Qualitative comparisons in the paper illustrate the different behaviors noted in the metrics and convergence plots. Randomly sampled outputs from the same finetuned checkpoint (so that prompt engineering does not bias some methods) show consistent patterns:

- OFT and COFT produce images that both preserve subject-specific details (facial structure, distinctive clothing, textures) and respond accurately to the text prompt (pose, scene, accessories or context described in the prompt). This indicates good balance of personalization and controllability.

- DreamBooth’s plain finetuning often preserves the subject identity early in training but may generate outputs that stop matching the prompt as training continues, and in some runs it later produces collapsed artifacts when overtrained.

- LoRA frequently fails to retain fine subject identity (examples in the paper include cases where a “yellow shirt” becomes hallucinated as “yellow fur” or where an instance identity is lost entirely). Even when LoRA sometimes attains acceptable CLIP-T, it struggles on CLIP-I and DINO metrics compared to OFT/COFT.

The paper highlights specific illustrative cases: a “bowl” example where LoRA loses the subject identity, and a clothing-color failure where LoRA confuses texture with color. In contrast, OFT/COFT maintain identity while following the prompt—demonstrating that preserving the hyperspherical relationships among neurons helps maintain the pretrained model’s semantic representations while still permitting the desired controlled modification.

# Failure modes and how COFT mitigates collapse

Understanding the failure modes of standard finetuning and LoRA clarifies why OFT and COFT are effective.

- Unconstrained finetuning (DreamBooth-style) is flexible but can be unstable. Small learning-rate updates still permit accumulation of changes that degrade pretrained generative capabilities; in practice this can lead to collapsed outputs or outputs that no longer follow prompts after extended training.

- Low-rank additive updates (LoRA) constrain the update matrix, but because the parameterization changes neurons’ magnitudes and relative geometry in an unconstrained way, LoRA can still shift the model into regions that no longer preserve subject-specific features. LoRA’s additive low-rank structure can be insufficient to disentangle identity preservation from other features, leading to identity loss or semantic confusions.

COFT addresses these failure modes by combining two explicit constraints. First, orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise angles of normalized neurons (hyperspherical energy), which empirically corresponds to preserving semantic structure learned during pretraining. Second, COFT adds an explicit neighborhood constraint around the identity orthogonal matrix, enforcing a bound on how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from no-op. Practically this is implemented by working with the Cayley parameterization and projecting Cayley parameters toward zero if they exceed a set radius; this keeps the learned orthogonal transform within a radius \epsilon of identity (approximated as a small norm constraint on the Cayley parameter). The result is much greater stability: COFT resists the progressive degradation that can arise from iterative updates and can be run with larger iteration counts or slightly more aggressive learning rates without collapse.

The paper shows empirically that COFT often matches or slightly outperforms OFT in stability, and that varying \epsilon trades off flexibility and stability—larger \epsilon moves COFT closer to OFT’s behavior, smaller \epsilon enforces stricter adherence to pretrained behavior.

# Human evaluation insights (where applicable)

Beyond automated metrics and qualitative inspections, the authors report a human evaluation (presented in the paper’s appendix) that further corroborates the automated results: human raters preferred OFT/COFT outputs over the baselines in terms of perceived subject fidelity and prompt adherence. The paper does not list numeric human-evaluation scores in the main text, but it states that the human study supports the conclusion reached by the automated metrics and qualitative examples: OFT and COFT yield more faithful subject-driven generation.

# Practical recommendations and summary

For practitioners interested in subject-driven personalization of large text-to-image diffusion models, the experimental evidence in the paper supports using OFT or COFT rather than naive finetuning or low-rank additive adapters, for these reasons:

- Better subject fidelity: OFT/COFT consistently obtain higher DINO and CLIP-I scores, indicating stronger retention of subject-specific visual features.

- Good prompt fidelity and diversity: CLIP-T and LPIPS results show OFT/COFT preserve or slightly improve prompt adherence and diversity compared to baselines, so personalization does not come at the cost of controllability or mode collapse.

- Faster, more stable convergence: OFT reaches usable performance in far fewer iterations and is robust to training length; COFT adds an explicit radius constraint for even greater safety against collapse.

- Simpler tuning: because OFT/COFT are less sensitive to the number of iterations and learning rate, practitioners can spend less effort tuning early stopping and learning-rate schedules.

- No inference overhead: learned orthogonal matrices can be merged into pretrained weights at inference time, so there is no runtime speed penalty.

COFT is especially recommended when the user expects to finetune for many iterations or with variable amounts of subject data: the explicit deviation bound provides a practical safety valve against destructive updates. OFT without the extra radius constraint is already much more stable than other baselines, but COFT offers an extra layer of robustness in the subject-driven setting.

In short, preserving the angular relationships among neurons (hyperspherical energy) while limiting overall deviation from the pretrained model produces a practical, efficient, and reliable approach to subject-driven generation that outperforms prior finetuning strategies in identity preservation, prompt fidelity, and stability.

Controllable Generation: Tasks, Consistency Metrics, and Comparisons

# Control tasks: edges to image (COCO), segmentation to image (ADE20K), landmarks to face (CelebA-HQ)

Controllable generation in this work refers to finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that, in addition to a text prompt, the model follows an external structural control signal. The paper evaluates Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant COFT on three representative control tasks that stress different types of structural guidance and semantic complexity.

The first task, Canny edges to image (C2I), uses the COCO dataset. The control signal is a Canny edge map derived from an image; the finetuned model should generate an image that is consistent with that edge layout while also matching the text prompt. Edges provide sparse, high-frequency structural cues (object contours, strong boundaries) but leave out texture and color — making this task a good test of how well the model can hallucinate plausible content that adheres to geometry.

The second task, segmentation map to image (S2I), uses ADE20K. Here the control is a semantic segmentation map that encodes class labels for regions (e.g., sky, building, road). This control requires the model to assign appropriate appearance and details consistent with region semantics while preserving global scene layout. Correct object shapes, adjacency relations, and class-conditioned appearance are important for success.

The third task, landmarks to face (L2F), uses CelebA-HQ and focuses on face pose and structure control. The control signal consists of facial landmarks that prescribe facial geometry (eye, nose, mouth positions, etc.). This task demands precise geometric adherence — small deviations in landmark positions correspond to visible pose changes — and therefore provides a sensitive evaluation for alignment between control and output.

Across these three datasets the paper applied the same finetuning setup to Stable Diffusion v1.5 and compared OFT/COFT with ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The diversity of controls (edges, semantic regions, precise landmarks) allows testing different aspects of controllability: sparse structural adherence, semantic coherence, and geometric precision.

# Control consistency metrics: IoU and F1 for edges, mean IoU and accuracies for segmentation, landmark L2 errors for faces

To quantify how well a generated image follows its input control signal, the paper introduces a control consistency metric based on the intuitive two-step procedure: (1) estimate the control signal from the generated image using an appropriate off-the-shelf estimator, and (2) compare that estimated control with the original control applied during generation. This yields an objective measure of adherence that is applicable across control modalities.

For the C2I task (edges), the estimated control is an edge map computed from the generated image (typically via a Canny detector or equivalent). Two standard overlap metrics are used to compare edge maps: Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and F1 score. Formally, if A and B are binary edge maps, IoU is
\[ \text{IoU} = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \]
and F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall computed over matching edge pixels. These metrics reward both accurate localization of edges and coverage of the reference edges.

For S2I (segmentation), the generated image is passed through a semantic segmentation model to estimate class labels per pixel. Comparison to the original segmentation map uses the usual segmentation metrics: per-class IoU averaged (mean IoU), per-class accuracy averaged (mean accuracy), and overall pixel accuracy (overall accuracy). Mean IoU is computed as
\[ \text{mean IoU} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^C \frac{|P_c \cap G_c|}{|P_c \cup G_c|}, \]
where \(P_c\) and \(G_c\) are predicted and ground-truth pixels for class \(c\), and \(C\) is the number of classes. These metrics capture semantic adherence (correct class assignment in corresponding regions) and are sensitive to both shape and label correctness.

For L2F (landmarks), facial keypoints are detected on the generated image to obtain predicted landmark coordinates \(l^{\text{pred}}\). The control consistency metric is the mean Euclidean (L2) distance between predicted landmarks and the original control landmarks \(l^{\text{ctrl}}\):
\[
\text{mean L2 error} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \| l^{\text{pred}}_i - l^{\text{ctrl}}_i \|_2,
\]
where \(N\) indexes landmarks (or samples, depending on aggregation). Smaller mean L2 indicates closer geometric adherence.

A practical caveat noted in the paper is that these consistency scores are upper-bounded by the accuracy of the off-the-shelf estimators (edge detector, segmentation model, landmark detector). The metric therefore measures how well generated images convey the intended control signal under the same perception model used to measure it, which is appropriate for comparative evaluation across methods.

# Convergence curves: faster and smoother behavior than baselines

Convergence speed and training dynamics are a core empirical focus. The paper reports that OFT and COFT converge substantially faster and with smoother dynamics than baseline adapter-style approaches (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) and LoRA, yielding two practical benefits: reduced finetuning time and more predictable training that is less sensitive to the number of epochs.

A concrete instance is given on the L2F task where convergence was measured by the face landmark mean L2 error as a function of epochs. OFT/COFT reach high geometric fidelity after only a few epochs; specifically, the paper notes that LoRA takes 20 epochs to reach the performance that OFT/COFT already achieve by epoch 8. In other words, OFT shortens the required training by well over half in that setup. Beyond faster convergence, OFT/COFT also exhibit smooth, gradual improvement across epochs. This contrasts with ControlNet, which the authors observed to show a "sudden convergence" phenomenon: controllability appears abruptly after a certain number of epochs (around epoch 8 in the reported experiments) rather than emerging smoothly. Sudden convergence can make hyperparameter tuning and stopping criteria brittle; by contrast, OFT's smooth curves make it easier in practice to select training length and learning rates.

The reported speedup and stability are attributed to two interacting factors grounded in the paper's theoretical perspective. First, OFT directly modifies neuron directions (angles) via orthogonal transforms, which—based on the authors' experiments and intuitions—are the most semantically effective degrees of freedom for modifying generated content. Second, restricting updates to orthogonal transformations preserves hyperspherical energy, constraining the optimization to a smoother manifold and reducing the chance of damaging pretrained generative capacities. COFT further stabilizes training by limiting deviation from identity, producing even more predictable behavior when desired.

# Qualitative comparisons: fidelity, geometry, and adherence to control signals

Qualitatively, OFT and COFT produce images that combine high photorealism with faithful adherence to controls across tasks, and they generally outperform baselines on three visible axes: image fidelity, geometric correctness, and adherence to the control signal.

In the S2I task, the paper reports that LoRA often fails to follow the input segmentation maps and can produce images whose object placements or shapes contradict the segmentation. ControlNet, OFT, and COFT are able to respect segmentation layouts, but OFT/COFT produce more vivid details and more coherent geometric structure with fewer parameters. This suggests that OFT both enforces the coarse semantic layout and retains or improves texture/detail synthesis relative to adapters that increase parameter or module complexity.

In the C2I task, OFT/COFT hallucinate semantically plausible image regions consistent with rough edge inputs. Adherence is stronger than for T2I-Adapter and LoRA, which the paper reports perform worse. Because edges supply sparse structure, success depends on the ability to stably translate contours into coherent object surfaces; OFT demonstrates robustness here.

In the L2F task, OFT/COFT achieve the most accurate pose control. Qualitative examples show faces whose poses and part positions align closely with the input landmarks, even in challenging poses. These qualitative observations match the quantitative landmark L2 trends and reinforce that OFT's learned orthogonal transformations can effect precise geometric adjustments while preserving identity and visual quality.

Taken together, these qualitative results support the empirical claim that OFT/COFT provide stronger, cleaner control without sacrificing image realism.

# Data and parameter efficiency relative to adapter-style methods

The paper emphasizes both data efficiency (how much training data is required to reach good performance) and parameter efficiency (how many trainable parameters are introduced during finetuning).

Data efficiency: OFT/COFT are more sample-efficient than ControlNet and LoRA. An example noted in the paper is that OFT can achieve good controllability on the ADE20K S2I setup using only 5% of the ADE20K dataset, whereas ControlNet and LoRA demand substantially more data to reach similar performance. Faster convergence (fewer epochs) further compounds the data-effectiveness because fewer passes over training data are needed.

Parameter efficiency: OFT and COFT are implemented as light-weight modifications applied to the same layers where LoRA is commonly inserted, and the number of additional parameters is comparable to LoRA and far smaller than ControlNet. ControlNet augments the network with substantial new modules and therefore introduces a larger parameter budget. OFT attains strong controllability with a small number of learned orthogonal blocks (and optionally block-sharing), which keeps the finetuning footprint small. Empirically, OFT/COFT match or exceed control performance of much larger adapter modules while using fewer new parameters, yielding a favorable trade-off in parameter efficiency.

The combination of data and parameter efficiency makes OFT particularly attractive when compute, storage, or available curated data are limited.

# No added inference cost vs adapter-based approaches

A practical deployment advantage of OFT singled out in the paper is that it adds no inference-time overhead. Adapter-style methods such as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter append additional modules or condition networks that must be executed during generation, increasing runtime cost and memory usage at inference. By contrast, OFT parameterizes the finetuned model as a transformed version of the pretrained weights: during inference the learned orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) can be folded into the pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) to obtain an equivalent weight \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\). Once folded, the model runs identically to the original backbone: no extra modules, no extra forward passes, and no runtime-control network are needed. This property makes OFT outputs directly deployable and keeps latency and memory identical to the base diffusion model, an important practical benefit compared to adapter-based solutions that trade inference complexity for control.

# Summary and practical implications

The controllable generation experiments in the paper demonstrate that OFT and COFT yield consistently stronger and more stable control across three diverse modalities: sparse structural edges, dense semantic segmentation, and precise facial landmarks. The control consistency framework—estimate control from generated images and compare to the original—provides an interpretable, task-appropriate quantitative benchmark (IoU/F1 for edges, mean IoU/accuracies for segmentation, mean L2 for landmarks). Empirically, OFT/COFT converge faster and more smoothly than LoRA and adapter methods, require less data to reach effective control, and do so with a small parameter footprint and zero extra inference cost. These combined properties make OFT a compelling finetuning strategy for practitioners who need controllable generation with efficient finetuning and simple deployment.

Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Why OFT converges faster: angular updates and smooth manifold optimization

Two complementary intuitions from the paper explain why Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) converges faster than more flexible finetuning schemes. The first intuition is empirical and data-oriented: semantic information in neural representations is primarily carried by directions (angles) of neuron weight vectors rather than their magnitudes. The paper illustrates this with a toy convolutional autoencoder experiment that showed angular information can nearly reconstruct input images while magnitude information contains little semantic content. This motivates focusing updates on neuron directions: by design OFT updates the pretrained weights only through a layer-shared orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ so that the finetuned weights are $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ acts by rotating/reflection in the ambient space, OFT directly modifies neuron directions — the component the experiments suggest matters most for semantic change — instead of wasting optimization effort on magnitudes.

The second, complementary intuition is geometric and optimization-theoretic. Constraining the finetuning transform to be orthogonal restricts optimization to the orthogonal group (or a block-diagonal product of smaller orthogonal groups), a smooth manifold with fewer degrees of freedom and favorable geometry compared to unconstrained Euclidean weight space. In the paper this viewpoint is used to explain the observed smoother and more stable training dynamics: OFT effectively moves on a hypersphere-like manifold of normalized neurons and preserves hyperspherical relationships (pairwise angles). This constrained search space reduces pathological directions that lead to collapse or divergence under standard finetuning and yields a more benign landscape for gradient-based optimization. Empirically, OFT and its constrained variant COFT attain good control in far fewer iterations than baselines and show stable progression rather than the sudden or unstable convergence observed for some competing methods.

Together, focusing updates on angular degrees of freedom (where semantics live) and restricting optimization to a smooth orthogonal manifold explain the faster convergence and robustness of OFT reported in the paper.

# Flexibility vs regularity: role of orthogonality and block structure

Finetuning large pretrained generative models requires a balance between two opposing needs: flexibility to adapt to new downstream tasks, and regularity to preserve the pretrained model’s general-purpose generative ability. The paper frames OFT as an explicit navigation of this trade-off.

Orthogonality enforces a strong regularizer: an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so it preserves pairwise angles among neurons and therefore exactly preserves hyperspherical energy. By preserving these angular relationships the finetuned model maintains a structural property the authors argue is central to pretrained semantic generation. This regularity prevents finetuning from arbitrarily breaking the pretrained representation and explains the model’s stability. At the same time, orthogonal transforms are full-rank and can represent global re-orientations of directions; they are therefore more expressive than very low-dimensional additive updates in certain respects. The paper positions OFT as trading some raw degrees of freedom for structured, semantically meaningful degrees of freedom.

Block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ adds another layer of regularization and a lever to control flexibility versus parameter cost. Writing $\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ breaks a single $d\times d$ orthogonal transform into $r$ independent orthogonal blocks of size $d/r$. Increasing $r$ (more blocks, smaller blocks) reduces the number of parameters and forces transformations to act more locally (for convolutional kernels this can correspond to depthwise-like behavior). Decreasing $r$ (fewer, larger blocks) increases flexibility and parameter count. In the extreme $r=1$ we recover a full orthogonal transform; in other extremes we approach many small independent rotations. The paper shows this block design as a practical mechanism: it preserves the orthogonality guarantee while controlling parameter efficiency and inductive bias. The authors observe that this block structure biases the transform (and thus the finetuned model) in useful ways, but that bias is a double-edged sword: it improves stability and efficiency but can limit expressivity compared to unconstrained finetuning.

COFT (Constrained OFT) further tightens regularity by adding an explicit radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$, which keeps the learned orthogonal matrix close to identity and thereby combines angular preservation with explicit proximity to the pretrained weights. That constraint makes the flexibility–regularity trade-off explicit and tunable via $\epsilon$.

# Limitations: Cayley inverse cost, block-diagonal bias

While OFT achieves desirable empirical properties, the paper candidly identifies several limitations that arise from the orthogonality constraint and the chosen parameterizations.

The computational cost of enforcing orthogonality is one practical limitation. The paper adopts the Cayley transform parameterization
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. Enforcing and differentiating through this transform requires computing a matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, which is more expensive than simple elementwise or low-rank additive updates. Although block-diagonal structure and block sharing reduce the dimensionality of the individual inverses, the matrix inverse remains a bottleneck and limits scalability. The Cayley parameterization also produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (the special orthogonal group), thus excluding reflections with determinant $-1$, although the authors report this restriction did not harm empirical performance.

The block-diagonal parameterization itself is both an advantage and a limitation. It reduces parameter count and stabilizes training, but it imposes an inductive bias: transformations are constrained to act independently within blocks. That bias may be inappropriate for some downstream tasks that require coordinated rotations across many coordinates, and thus block structure can limit expressivity. Moreover, parameter efficiency of OFT is linked to the chosen block size and sharing strategy, so improving efficiency by more aggressive blocking may come at the expense of representational capacity.

Finally, COFT’s radius constraint requires choosing $\epsilon$ (or its proxy $\epsilon'$ on the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ via the Neumann approximation). The paper shows that $\epsilon$ controls flexibility, but selecting it involves a trade-off: too small and finetuning is overly conservative; too large and the method approaches OFT’s full flexibility and may lose some of its stability advantages. Tuning this hyperparameter remains an area of attention.

# Scalability and alternative orthogonal parameterizations

The paper evaluates two ends of the engineering design: exact orthogonalization methods such as Gram–Schmidt (noted as differentiable but expensive) and the Cayley transform (used in experiments for efficiency). It highlights that Cayley is a practical compromise: it provides a differentiable mapping from a skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix while being cheaper than naive orthogonalization, especially when combined with block diagonalization. However, the need to compute a matrix inverse remains the key scalability bottleneck.

To enable larger-scale deployment, the paper points out open engineering directions. Block-diagonalization reduces the cost from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ when block sharing is used, but this is a structural compromise. The Neumann-series approximation $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ provides a way to reason about the $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ constraint and to project $\bm{Q}$ during COFT, but relying on series convergence is itself an approximation and suitable only when $\bm{Q}$ is small. The paper explicitly lists as an open problem how to speed up the required differentiable matrix inverse in a principled manner; addressing that would make orthogonal parameterizations more attractive at large scale without introducing restrictive block biases.

In short, the paper recognizes that OFT’s orthogonal maps are conceptually appealing but introduces computational trade-offs. Engineering work on alternative parameterizations (cheaper exact orthogonal maps, structured approximations that retain expressivity, or fast approximate inverses with well-behaved gradients) is a clear direction for future research highlighted by the authors.

# Compositional finetuning: multiplying orthogonal transforms across tasks

One intriguing property of orthogonal matrices emphasized in the paper is closure under multiplication: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. This algebraic fact suggests a natural compositional finetuning scheme: if a model is finetuned for task A by applying $\bm{R}_A$ and for task B by applying $\bm{R}_B$, then applying $\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ is still an orthogonal transform and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy. The paper raises a question motivated by this property as an open research direction: can orthogonal matrices learned for different downstream tasks be composed (multiplied together) to yield a single orthogonal transform that simultaneously preserves the capabilities required by each task?

This compositionality is attractive because it offers a conceptually simple way to accumulate multiple task adaptations without changing the base pretrained weights or incurring additional inference overhead. Yet the authors rightfully treat the question as unresolved. Preserving hyperspherical energy by itself does not guarantee that the composed orthogonal transform will faithfully implement all task-specific adjustments simultaneously: interactions across tasks may interfere, and the finite parameterization (block structure, proximity constraints) may prevent successful composition. Investigating when and how such compositions retain per-task performance (and how to order or regularize composed transforms) is presented as an open problem. The paper suggests that OFT’s algebraic structure gives it unique potential for compositionality, but empirical and theoretical work is needed to understand capacity limits and methods for preserving multiple downstream behaviors under composition.

# Safety and responsible use: controllability, bias, and misuse considerations

The paper focuses on controllability — enabling stronger, more accurate control of text-to-image diffusion models — and emphasizes that OFT achieves this with no extra inference overhead, faster convergence, and improved stability. While the paper does not study safety, fairness, or misuse empirically, these are natural considerations that follow from improving model controllability and ease of deployment.

Because OFT improves the ability to steer a pretrained generator (for instance by subject-driven generation or by conditioning on control signals such as edges or segmentation), it lowers technical barriers to fine-grained manipulation of generated content. The paper’s observation that OFT introduces no additional inference cost (the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ at inference time) means that such adapted models are inexpensive to deploy. Consequently, developers and researchers should be aware that improved controllability and cheaper deployment can amplify both beneficial applications and potential misuse.

From a research perspective, the paper suggests the following responsible-use directions (as natural extensions of the work, not as items already addressed in the experiments): (1) evaluate how finetuning affects model biases and whether preserving hyperspherical energy suffices to preserve fair behavior across demographic subgroups, (2) assess whether stronger controllability can be abused to produce deceptive or privacy-invasive outputs (for example, easier subject-driven generation could facilitate synthesis of authentic-looking images of private individuals), and (3) develop best-practice guidelines around dataset selection, consent for subject images, and governance of deployed adapted models. The paper does not provide solutions to those issues, but it frames OFT as a tool whose improved efficiency and effectiveness make consideration of such safety, bias, and misuse questions an important direction for future work.

# Concluding perspective

The OFT framework presents a principled, empirically effective approach to finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models by focusing on angular updates and preserving hyperspherical structure. Its orthogonality constraint and block design strike a deliberate balance between flexibility and stability, yielding faster convergence and stronger controllability in the paper’s experiments. At the same time, the method raises concrete engineering and scientific challenges: scaling orthogonal parameterizations without expensive matrix inverses, understanding and mitigating inductive biases introduced by block structure, exploring orthogonal composition across multiple tasks, and bringing safety and fairness considerations into adaptation workflows. These directions form a coherent research agenda that builds directly on the theoretical and empirical observations in the paper.
