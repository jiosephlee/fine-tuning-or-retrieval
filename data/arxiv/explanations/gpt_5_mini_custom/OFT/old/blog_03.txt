# COFT in Practice: Safer, More Predictable Fine-Tuning of Stable Diffusion

Fine-tuning large text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion can feel a bit like navigating a minefield. Turn the dial too much, and you risk collapsing your model’s hard-won knowledge. Train for too long or at too high a rate, and your subject-driven or control-signal prompts go haywire. Constrained Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (COFT) offers a principled remedy. By limiting how far each layer’s neuron directions can rotate, COFT delivers faster, more stable convergence—without sacrificing the photorealism and diversity you expect from your pretrained model. In this post, we’ll walk through the nuts and bolts of COFT in a hands-on, practical way.

## 1. What Is Constrained Orthogonal Fine-Tuning?

At its heart, COFT is an orthogonal re-parameterization of each weight matrix combined with an explicit radius constraint around the pretrained model. Recall that an orthogonal matrix $R$ preserves all pairwise angles between its columns, which in practice means we can “steer” the model’s semantics without scrambling the relationships it learned during pretraining. Mathematically, one layer’s forward pass under COFT becomes:

$$
z \;=\; \bigl(R \, W^0\bigr)^\top x,
\quad
\text{subject to}\;
R^\top R = I,
\;\|R - I\|\le \epsilon,
$$

where:
- $W^0$ is the original (pretrained) weight matrix,  
- $R$ is an orthogonal matrix we learn (initialized as the identity),  
- $\epsilon$ is a small radius guaranteeing $R$ stays close to $I$.  

By forcing $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$, COFT ensures each layer can only “rotate” its neurons by a bounded amount. This extra constraint is what turns Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT) into COFT, giving you tighter control over model drift.

## 2. Choosing Your Key Hyperparameters

### 2.1 Epsilon (Maximum Rotation Radius)
The radius $\epsilon$ directly controls how aggressively the model can adapt. In our experiments:

- **Small $\epsilon$ (e.g.\ 0.05–0.1)**  
  Keeps the model very close to its pretrained state. Useful when you have very few subject images or only mild control modifications.

- **Medium $\epsilon$ (e.g.\ 0.1–0.3)**  
  Strikes a balance between flexibility and stability. A solid default for most subject-driven and image-to-image control tasks.

- **Large $\epsilon$ (above 0.3)**  
  Approaches pure OFT. You may see faster adaptation but risk semantic drift if you overshoot.

### 2.2 Block Count ($r$)
An unconstrained $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters. To keep COFT parameter-efficient, we split $R$ into an $r$-block diagonal matrix:
$$
R = \mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r),\quad R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r}}.
$$
Each block is itself orthogonal. Tuning $r$ gives you a trade-off:

- **r = 1**  
  A single full-size block. Maximum flexibility, more parameters ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$).

- **r = 4 or 8**  
  Typical values. Each block has size $d/4$ or $d/8$, reducing trainable parameters by a factor of $r$.

- **Block sharing**  
  You can even tie all blocks together ($R_1=R_2=\dots=R_r$) for further parameter savings.

In practice, we’ve found $r=4$ or $8$ offers a sweet spot: enough expressivity to handle diverse subjects and control signals, with a fraction of the parameters of a full OFT.

## 3. Which Layers to Tune

COFT works wherever you can insert an orthogonal transform—but to minimize overhead and maximize impact, we recommend:

1. **Self- and cross-attention projections** in the U-Net’s transformer blocks. These layers shape how the model fuses text and image features, making them ideal for subject identity or control-signal adaptation.

2. Optionally, **feed-forward (MLP) layers** if you need extra flexibility. In most cases, attention weights alone suffice.

By focusing on attention, you keep COFT lean: only a small fraction of weights are re-parameterized, which translates to faster training and lower GPU memory usage.

## 4. Training Schedule and Best Practices

### 4.1 Learning Rate
While ordinary fine-tuning often demands very low learning rates, COFT’s explicit constraints let you be a bit bolder:

- Start around $1\!\times\!10^{-4}$ for your optimizer.
- If you see unstable loss spikes, halve it to $5\!\times\!10^{-5}$.
- COFT is relatively insensitive; you can safely sweep in the range $[5\times10^{-5}, 2\times10^{-4}]$.

### 4.2 Number of Epochs / Iterations
- **Subject-driven (DreamBooth style):**  
  We typically run 400–1,000 iterations. COFT converges as early as 400, but you can go up to 2,000 without collapse.

- **Controllable generation (ControlNet style):**  
  About 10–20 epochs on your task-specific dataset (e.g.\ Canny→Image, Segmentation→Image, Landmark→Face). COFT often matches full-data performance by epoch 8–10.

### 4.3 Projection Step for $\epsilon$-Constraint
Implement a simple projected gradient descent loop on $Q$ (the Cayley-parameter skew-symmetric matrix) so that after each update:
1. Compute $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$.
2. If $\|R - I\|>\epsilon$, scale $Q$ back so $R$ lies on the $\epsilon$-ball.

Most deep-learning frameworks let you write that projection in just a few lines.

## 5. Avoiding Collapse Across Subjects and Control Tasks

1. **Monitor Hyperspherical Energy Indirectly**  
   While you don’t need to compute it explicitly, you should watch validation metrics that reflect semantic integrity:  
   - **Subject fidelity:** CLIP-I or DINO scores.  
   - **Prompt fidelity:** CLIP-T similarity.  
   - **Control consistency:** IoU/F1 for edges or segmentation, $\ell_2$ error for landmarks.

2. **Adjust $\epsilon$ If You See Drift**  
   If your subject’s identity degrades after many iterations, dial $\epsilon$ down by 20% and restart.

3. **Block Count Tuning**  
   If your control signals are very fine-grained (e.g.\ precise face landmarks), a smaller block count (e.g.\ $r=1$ or $2$) can give you extra flexibility—just watch for overfitting.

4. **Use Constrained OFT Over Pure OFT**  
   We’ve found that the simple radius constraint alone drastically reduces the risk of mode collapse, even under prolonged training.

## 6. Conclusion

COFT brings structure to the art of fine-tuning. By combining an orthogonal re-parameterization with an explicit $\epsilon$ constraint, you get a model that adapts quickly, preserves its pretrained semantics, and resists collapse—even when you crank up the learning rate or training steps. Best of all, once you’ve learned your orthogonal matrices, you can fold them back into the original weights for zero-overhead inference. Whether you’re teaching Stable Diffusion a new face or dialing in a segmentation-to-image pipeline, COFT delivers a safer, more predictable path to high-quality results. Happy fine-tuning!
