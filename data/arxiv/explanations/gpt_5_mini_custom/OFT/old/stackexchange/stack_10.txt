### Re-scaled OFT: why constrain the diagonal scales to be positive, and does scaling preserve HE?
Question:
The re-scaled variant learns a diagonal \(\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) with \(s_i>0\), applying \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}\). Since HE uses normalized columns \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\), scaling any column leaves HE unchanged. But are there any subtle issues if we allow negative scaling (sign flips), which can be absorbed by an orthogonal reflection? Also, if we scale columns that later feed into LayerNorm, could this interact with normalization statistics in ways that alter training stability?
Answer:
In Re-scaled OFT we introduce a diagonal matrix  
$$
D = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n),\quad s_i>0
$$
so that our new weight is  
$$
W = R\,W^0\,D,\quad R^\top R = I.
$$  
Two questions naturally arise:

1. Why do we insist on $s_i>0$?  After all, scaling by any nonzero real—positive or negative—does not change the *norm* of the column, so one might think it would leave hyperspherical energy (HE) untouched.

2. Even if we keep $s_i>0$, does inserting these per-column scales ever interfere with downstream LayerNorm blocks and thus with training stability?

Below I’ll argue that (a) allowing negative $s_i$ *does* generally *break* the HE invariance we rely on in OFT, and (b) even positive but *unequal* $s_i$ will alter the statistics that a LayerNorm sees (though in practice this tends to be benign if the scales stay in a reasonable range).

---

## 1.  Why *not* allow $s_i<0$ if any nonzero scale preserves column norms?  

Recall that the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $W=[w_1,\dots,w_n]$ is defined in terms of the *unit-normalized* columns
$$
\hat w_i = \frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|},
$$
and
$$
\mathrm{HE}(W) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\;\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}
\;\propto\;\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2\,\hat w_i^\top\hat w_j}}.
$$
When you multiply a column $w_i$ by a positive scalar $s_i>0$, the normalized direction
$$
\widehat{\,s_i\,w_i\,}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{\|s_i\,w_i\|}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{|s_i|\|w_i\|}
=\hat w_i
\quad\text{as long as }s_i>0.
$$
But if you let $s_i<0$, then
$$
\widehat{\,s_i\,w_i\,}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{|s_i|\,\|w_i\|}
= -\,\hat w_i,
$$
so the *direction* of that one column is flipped by $180^\circ$.  Flipping a *single* column changes its angle to *every* other column and thus changes the pairwise terms in HE.  

You might object: “Couldn’t we just absorb an individual sign-flip into the global orthogonal matrix $R$ by choosing $R$ to include a reflection?”  The answer is no, not if you want to reflect *only some* columns and leave others untouched.  An orthogonal $R\in\mathrm{O}(d)$ acts on the *row* space of $W^0$ and therefore must transform *all* columns simultaneously by the *same* rotation/reflection.  It cannot pick out one column $w^0_i$ and flip just that one.  The only column-wise “reflections” you could absorb in $R$ would be a *global* flip of *all* columns, i.e.\ $s_i<0$ for every $i$, which is itself an orthonormal transformation (the map $w\mapsto -w$ on $\mathbb R^d$).  But *mixed* signs—some $s_i>0$, some $s_i<0$—are not a single orthogonal action on the neuron directions and thus break the core invariance  
$$
\mathrm{HE}(R\,W^0) \;=\;\mathrm{HE}(W^0).
$$
Hence in order to guarantee that the finite-scale step
$$
W \;=\; R\,W^0\,D
$$
*exactly* preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained layer, we must enforce $s_i>0$.

---

## 2.  Does *any* positive re-scaling $s_i$ then ever screw up LayerNorm?  

In practice, most modern diffusion backbones sprinkle LayerNorm (or other normalizers) between linear or attention blocks.  A LayerNorm that acts across the *feature* dimension of a single position—say $z = W^\top x\in\mathbb R^n$ is normalized by
$$
\mu = \tfrac1n\sum_i z_i,\quad
\sigma^2=\tfrac1n\sum_i(z_i-\mu)^2,\quad
\mathrm{LN}(z)_i = \gamma_i\,\frac{z_i-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}} + \beta_i.
$$
LayerNorm has two exact invariances:
 (1) it is *shift-invariant*: adding the same constant to every $z_i$ does nothing after centering, and  
 (2) it is *uniform-scale invariant*: multiplying *all* $z_i$ by the same positive $\alpha$ is undone by dividing by $\sigma$.  

However, it is *not* invariant to *individual* per-feature scales $s_i$.  If you replace
$$
z_i \;=\;(W^0)_{:i}^\top x
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
z_i' \;=\;s_i\,(W^0)_{:i}^\top x,
$$
then
$$
\mu' \;=\;\tfrac1n\sum_i s_i\, (W^0)_{:i}^\top x,
\quad
\sigma'^2 \;=\;\tfrac1n\sum_i\bigl(s_i\,(W^0)_{:i}^\top x-\mu'\bigr)^2,
$$
and so the normalized activations
$$
\frac{z_i'-\mu'}{\sqrt{\sigma'^2+\epsilon}}
$$
will in general differ from those with no scaling.  In effect, you have changed the *shape* of the feature-vector distribution.  That said, in practical networks LayerNorm’s learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$ can *absorb* mild per-feature distortions—and indeed we find that modest positive re-scaling of each neuron simply gets baked into the subsequent norm’s affine parameters without catastrophic instability.

By contrast, if you allowed sign-flips ($s_i<0$), every flipped coordinate would invert its contribution, shifting the LayerNorm mean in unpredictable ways, momentarily sending $\sigma^2$ through spurious patterns of cancellation or amplification.  That extra sign–mean–variance entanglement is best avoided, both to keep the HE guarantee *exact* and to avoid coupling the learned $D$ dangerously with the LayerNorm’s own $\gamma,\beta$.
