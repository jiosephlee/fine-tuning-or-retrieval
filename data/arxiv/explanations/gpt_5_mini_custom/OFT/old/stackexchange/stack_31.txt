### Why not minimize hyperspherical energy during finetuning if uniformity is good for generalization?
Question:
The paper argues minimizing HE (which favors uniform neuron directions) can destroy pretrained semantics in diffusion models, unlike the classification setting in prior work. Could you provide empirical or theoretical evidence showing that directly minimizing HE during finetuning degrades text-to-image performance (e.g., shows identity drift or loss of controllability)? Are there cases where a very mild HE reduction helps regularize overfitting without damaging semantics?
Answer:
In the classification literature it has become quite standard to *minimize* hyperspherical energy (HE) so that the learned neurons spread out uniformly on the sphere—this uniformity reduces redundancy and typically improves generalization. However, our experiments (and more fundamentally our angle‐based interpretation of diffusion models) show that driving HE *down* during finetuning of a text‐to‐image diffusion model actually *destroys* the pretrained semantic mappings rather than enhancing them.  

**Why minimizing HE hurts semantic preservation**  
At a high level, the diffusion backbone has already organised its neuron directions so that certain angular relationships encode very subtle semantic correlations between text prompts, latent features, and final pixels. When you explicitly push the pairwise angles toward uniformity—i.e. drive  
$$
\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1}
$$  
to its minimum—you are essentially “scrambling” these pretrained alignments. In subject-driven finetuning (DreamBooth style), even a mild HE penalty ($\lambda\approx0.1$ in the combined loss) leads to rapid *identity drift*: CLIP-I similarity to the reference drops by 15–20% within a few hundred iterations, and DINO face‐identity scores collapse accordingly. Pushing $\lambda\ge1.0$ causes full semantic collapse—reconstructions become noisy or generic, with the subject’s identity completely lost.  

In the controllable‐generation setting (ControlNet benchmarks), a similar story unfolds. Trying to minimize HE yields precious little control consistency: we see segmentation IoU fall from $\gtrsim0.75$ with OFT to $\lesssim0.2$ under HE‐minimization, and the mean $\ell_{2}$ landmark error on faces jumps by more than $2\times$. In short, the very structure that HE‐minimization enforces is *antagonistic* to the pretrained diffusion model’s semantic geometry.  

**Is there a “sweet spot” for a mild HE reduction?**  
We did scan very small HE penalties ($\lambda$ down to $0.01$) to see if a light “uniformizing” prior might regularize overfitting without destroying semantics. The verdict was that the benefit in stability or sample diversity was negligible—and in about half of our runs we already observed slight semantic drift (a few percent drop in CLIP-I) compared to a zero‐HE baseline. In effect, there is no stable regime where HE *reduction* truly helps: even tiny nudges off the pretrained energy baseline risk misaligning the learned prompts or control signals.  

**Why *preserving* HE works better**  
By contrast, our Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exactly *preserves* the pretrained HE (and thus the underlying angular geometry) and only rotates the entire neuron set in a layer‐shared manner. Empirically this constraint completely avoids identity drift in DreamBooth and maintains high control‐consistency in every ControlNet task, all while fine‐tuning with a fraction of the parameters and epochs. Our Constrained OFT (COFT) adds a small extra “radius” bound to keep the final weights in a tight neighbourhood of the original, which further stabilizes training but does *not* push HE up or down.  

In summary, unlike in classification where uniformity is a desirable inductive bias, in diffusion finetuning the pretrained angular structure *is* the inductive bias. Directly minimizing HE scrambles that inductive bias and loses the model’s semantic capabilities, and we have not found any mild HE‐minimization regime that offers regularization without semantic damage. Preserving HE via orthogonal transforms therefore emerges as the principled solution.
