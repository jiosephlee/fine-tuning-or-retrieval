# Measuring Control That Matters: Practical Consistency Metrics for Text-to-Image

Advances in text-to-image diffusion models have unlocked stunning visual creativity, but for many applications we need more than photorealism—we need precise control. Whether you’re guiding an image with a sketch, a segmentation map, or a set of facial landmarks, you want to know: how well did the model obey your instructions?

In this post, we introduce straightforward, reproducible metrics that quantify controllability in three common tasks:
- Edge‐guided generation (Canny edges to image)
- Segmentation‐guided generation (label map to image)
- Landmark‐guided face synthesis (landmarks to face)

These task‐specific “consistency” metrics measure exactly what matters, and they go beyond generic scores like FID that can miss or even contradict your intuitions about control quality.

## Why Generic Metrics Fall Short

The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) has become the de facto standard for evaluating image generators. It captures distributional differences between real and generated images in an embedding space. But FID has two major limitations when you care about control:

1. **Global vs Local Structure**  
   FID focuses on overall appearance statistics. A model might produce realistic textures and colors, yielding a good FID, yet completely ignore your control signal—say, drawing the wrong shapes or misplacing key landmarks.

2. **No Task Semantics**  
   FID does not know about edges, segmentation labels, or facial landmarks. It treats an image as a cloud of features, without any direct correspondence to your input guidance. As a result, good FID scores can mask poor controllability.

To address these gaps, we need metrics that compare the **intended control signal** with the **actual content** of the generated image. In other words, we need **consistency metrics** tailored to each control task.

## Our Task‐Specific Consistency Metrics

Let’s walk through how to measure controllability, task by task.

### 1. Edge Consistency: IoU and F1 Score

In edge‐guided generation, your input is a binary edge map (e.g., from a Canny detector). After generating the image, we run the same edge detector on the synthesized image to recover its “predicted” edges. Then we compute:

- **Intersection over Union (IoU)**  
  $$
    \text{IoU} = \frac{|\text{edges}_{\text{input}} \cap \text{edges}_{\text{pred}}|}
                   {|\text{edges}_{\text{input}} \cup \text{edges}_{\text{pred}}|}
  $$

- **F1 Score**  
  $$
    \text{F1} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}
                         {\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
  $$

These metrics directly measure how well the generated image’s contours match the original sketch.

### 2. Segmentation Consistency: Mean IoU and Accuracy

For segmentation‐guided generation, your input is a label map where each pixel belongs to a class (e.g., “sky,” “road,” “tree”). To check consistency:

1. Use a pretrained segmentation model on the generated image.
2. Compare the predicted labels to the input map and compute:
   - **Mean IoU** (averaged across classes)
   - **Overall Accuracy** (fraction of correctly labeled pixels)

These measures capture whether each region in the image follows the intended semantic layout.

### 3. Landmark Consistency: $\ell_2$ Error

In landmark‐guided face synthesis, you supply keypoints (e.g., eyes, nose tip, mouth corners). After generation, we apply a facial landmark detector to the output, yielding a set of predicted points. We then compute the average $\ell_2$ distance:

$$
  \text{Error} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\,L^{(\text{input})}_i - L^{(\text{pred})}_i\|_2
$$

where $N$ is the number of landmarks. Lower error means the face pose and expression align more faithfully with the guidance.

## Extracting Control Signals from Generated Images

A key ingredient in these metrics is a reliable way to **invert** the generation process:

- For edges, we simply re‐run the same Canny detector with fixed thresholds.
- For segmentation, we apply an off‐the‐shelf segmentation network (e.g., a U‐Net trained on ADE20K).
- For landmarks, we use a standard facial landmark detector (e.g., a pretrained hourglass or MediaPipe model).

Because these invertible modules are deterministic and pretrained, they let us convert any generated image back into the same representation domain as the inputs. This makes the comparison fair and reproducible.

## Why These Metrics Beat FID for Control

1. **Directness**  
   They directly measure the correspondence between input and output, rather than relying on proxy features.

2. **Task‐Relevance**  
   Each metric is conscious of the domain semantics—edges, object classes, facial keypoints—so it can detect subtle failures that FID might miss.

3. **Quantifiable Trade‐offs**  
   You can tune your models (or finetuning strategies) and immediately see how controllability improves or degrades, rather than hoping improvements in FID correlate with better control.

## Putting It All Together: Examples from the Paper

In “Controlling Text‐to‐Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning,” the authors demonstrate these metrics on three tasks using a Stable Diffusion backbone:

- **Canny Edge to Image (COCO)**  
  The IoU and F1 curves show that Orthogonal Finetuning achieves higher edge alignment than baselines like ControlNet or LoRA, even when trained with fewer epochs.

- **Segmentation Map to Image (ADE20K)**  
  Mean IoU rises steadily over training for the proposed method, while accuracy plateaus for adapter‐based approaches, indicating more faithful layout preservation.

- **Landmark to Face (CelebA‐HQ)**  
  The average $\ell_2$ landmark error drops faster and to a lower minimum, demonstrating more precise pose control with Orthogonal Finetuning.

These quantitative curves underscore the value of task‐specific consistency metrics: they clearly separate methods by controllability, something FID curves alone would not reveal.

## Conclusion

When your priority is control, you need metrics that measure exactly how well the model follows your instructions. IoU/F1 for edges, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, and landmark $\ell_2$ error for faces are simple to implement with off‐the‐shelf detectors, and they give you clear, reproducible signals about task performance.

Next time you evaluate a text‐to‐image model, don’t stop at FID. Measure the control that truly matters!
