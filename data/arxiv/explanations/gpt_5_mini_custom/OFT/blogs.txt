# The Simple Twist That Tames Diffusion Models: Orthogonal Finetuning Explained

## What problem are we solving?

Large text-to-image diffusion models can already produce impressive images, but steering them to new, more specific behaviors is tricky. Two common downstream needs are (1) subject-driven generation — make the model draw a specific person or object from a few example images, and (2) controllable generation — make the model respect extra signals like edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks.

Standard finetuning either updates many weights (risking catastrophic change) or adds small adapters (which can be slow to converge or still change behavior unpredictably). The core tension is: we want enough flexibility to learn the new task, but enough constraint to preserve the pretrained model’s broad semantic abilities.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) gives a simple, principled middle ground: rather than changing neuron magnitudes or arbitrary weights, it “rotates” neuron directions with layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons remain intact.

## The key idea in plain terms

Think of each neuron in a layer as a vector on a unit hypersphere (normalize its weights). The angles between these vectors encode relational/semantic structure learned during pretraining. OFT asks: what if we adapt the model by rotating the entire set of neuron vectors in a layer by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$? Rotating preserves all pairwise angles, so the layer’s internal geometry — its hyperspherical energy — stays the same. Practically, OFT replaces the original forward pass
z = (W^0)^\top x
with
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
where $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$) and initialized as identity. That means we start exactly from the pretrained model and only learn a rotation (and optionally a simple per-neuron scaling).

Why is this useful? Angular information often carries most of the semantics (the paper shows toy experiments where phase/angle recovers images while magnitude does not). So changing directions is an effective lever to modify semantics, while preserving pairwise angles prevents destroying the pretrained knowledge.

## What hyperspherical energy is (and why preserve it)

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar that summarizes pairwise similarity among normalized neurons:
HE(W) = ∑_{i≠j} ||ŵ_i − ŵ_j||^{-1},
where ŵ_i = w_i / ||w_i||. Small changes in HE mean the relative geometry of neurons on the hypersphere is preserved. OFT ensures HE(W) = HE(W^0) (provably) by construction, because orthogonal transforms do not change angles or normalized pairwise distances.

Preserving HE empirically correlates with preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability: you get controllability without destroying general image quality or diversity.

## Practical parameterization and variants

A few pragmatic choices make OFT efficient and stable:

- Cayley parameterization. Represent an orthogonal matrix using a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ so $\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$. This produces orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ (with determinant $+1$) and is differentiable. It requires matrix inverses, so block structure is used to keep costs down.

- Block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}$. Instead of a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix, use $\mathbf{R} = \text{diag}(R_1, ..., R_r)$ with smaller blocks. This cuts parameter and compute cost while maintaining orthogonality in each block.

- Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). To improve stability further, COFT adds an explicit constraint that $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon$, keeping the rotation close to identity. Using the Cayley parameter $\mathbf{Q}$, this becomes a small-norm constraint on $\mathbf{Q}$ enforced by projection.

- Re-scaled OFT. Optionally learn a diagonal scaling $\mathbf{D}$ per neuron and use $(R W^0 D)^\top x$. Scaling does not change normalized angles, so HE preservation holds; empirically it gives extra flexibility with negligible parameters.

All variants are initialized so the finetuned model begins exactly as the pretrained model (identity $\mathbf{R}$, zero $\mathbf{Q}$).

## How OFT compares to other finetuning approaches

- Full finetuning: Flexible but risky — can quickly drift and destroy pretrained capabilities. Requires careful small learning rates and hyperparameter tuning.

- LoRA (low-rank additive updates): Adds low-rank additive changes, parameter-efficient and effective in many cases. LoRA constrains the update rank but does not explicitly preserve pairwise neuron angles.

- OFT (multiplicative orthogonal update): Preserves angles by construction. It can be seen as an additive update: (R − I) W^0, but with the strong orthogonality/structure constraint on (R − I). With block structure, OFT achieves competitive parameter efficiency and often faster, more stable convergence. Importantly, OFT adds zero inference overhead: you can precompute R W^0 and use it as a normal weight matrix at test time.

## Empirical highlights (as reported)

- Tasks: OFT is evaluated on subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style: learning a token for a subject from a few images) and controllable generation (adding control signals like edges, segmentation, or landmarks).

- Stability and convergence: OFT and COFT converge faster and with greater stability than LoRA and baseline DreamBooth-style finetuning. COFT’s explicit epsilon constraint makes iteration count and learning rate less sensitive.

- Quality and control: OFT variants achieve better subject fidelity (measured by DINO and CLIP image metrics), comparable prompt fidelity, and similar or better diversity (LPIPS). For controllable generation, OFT yields higher control consistency (measured by reconstructing control signals from generated images and comparing to originals) across tasks like Canny→Image, Segmentation→Image, and Landmark→Face.

- Sample and compute efficiency: OFT needs fewer finetuning images and epochs to reach strong control performance, and requires no extra compute during inference.

(These results are reported in the paper using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained model and baselines such as DreamBooth, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA.)

## Why OFT converges faster and stays stable

Two intuitive reasons from the paper: (1) changing neuron directions is the most semantically meaningful way to adapt representations, so OFT focuses updates where they matter; (2) orthogonal updates restrict optimization to a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), which improves the optimization landscape and reduces destructive drift.

## Limitations and open problems

The paper calls out several limitations and directions for future work:

- Cayley parameterization requires matrix inverses, which can be costly at very large dimension. Block-diagonal structure mitigates but does not eliminate this.

- The block-diagonal parameterization trades off flexibility for efficiency; choosing block size introduces bias and may limit expressivity.

- Compositionality: orthogonal matrices from separate finetuning tasks can be multiplied and remain orthogonal. Whether a sequence/product of such transforms preserves knowledge from multiple tasks is an open question.

- Finding more efficient differentiable orthogonal parameterizations or better ways to compress/parameterize $\mathbf{R}$ without hurting HE preservation is an open engineering problem.

## When to consider OFT

If you need to steer a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to (a) reliably represent a new subject from a few images, or (b) respect additional control signals, and you want fast, stable finetuning without sacrificing the model’s original semantic abilities or inference cost, OFT is a compelling option. COFT is especially attractive when you want extra robustness and easier hyperparameter choices.

## Takeaway

Orthogonal Finetuning is a conceptually simple but effective twist: constrain finetuning to layer-shared orthogonal rotations (plus optional scaling and a small deviation bound). By preserving the geometry of neuron directions — the hyperspherical energy — OFT lets models learn new control behaviors quickly and stably while keeping their pretrained image-generation strengths intact, and it does so without adding inference overhead.



# Angles Over Magnitudes: A Friendly Guide to Hyperspherical Energy

## Why this matters

When we finetune a large text-to-image diffusion model, the goal is usually two-fold: adapt the model to a new control or subject (few-shot subject-driven generation, or additional control signals like edges/segmentation), while not breaking the model’s pretrained ability to generate realistic, diverse images. Common practice tries to keep weight updates small in Euclidean norm, or to add small low-rank adapters. Those heuristics help, but they don't directly measure whether the model's internal "semantic structure" is preserved.

Hyperspherical energy gives a different lens: instead of worrying about magnitudes of neuron weights, it looks at the angles between neurons. Empirically and intuitively, those angles encode semantic relationships. The paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning" leverages this idea: keep pairwise angles stable during finetuning, and you more reliably preserve pretrained semantics while still being able to adapt.

This post explains that intuition, the key math idea, and why orthogonal (angle-preserving) finetuning works well in practice.

## The core intuition: phase (angles) vs magnitude

Think about a 2D Fourier decomposition of an image. The magnitude spectrum encodes how much of each basis frequency is present; the phase spectrum encodes relative alignment and structure. A classic observation is that much of the recognizable structure of an image is carried in the phase: if you pair the original phase with a random magnitude spectrum, you often still see the original image layout.

Translate that to neural networks: each neuron (a column of a weight matrix) has a direction and a length. The direction (normalized weight vector) is an angular object on a unit hypersphere; the length is a magnitude. The paper argues — and experimentally supports with a small autoencoder experiment — that neuron directions carry the bulk of semantic information, while magnitudes are less important for semantics. So, when you want to change semantics (e.g., make a model generate a specific person, or follow a new control signal), it makes sense to operate primarily on directions/angles.

But changing angles arbitrarily is dangerous: it risks destroying the pretrained relationships that capture general semantics. The sweet spot is to permit structured angle changes that keep pairwise relationships stable.

## Hyperspherical energy: a compact way to measure angle structure

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar measure of the pairwise angular relationships among a set of normalized neurons. For a layer with neurons {w_i} normalized to unit length as \hat{w}_i := w_i / \|w_i\|, one common form of HE used in the paper is

HE(W) := \sum_{i \ne j} \| \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \|^{-1}.

This sums a function of pairwise angular distances across all neuron pairs. Lower or comparable HE means the neurons are distributed on the sphere in a way that preserves their relative angles. If two models have the same HE for corresponding layers, their pairwise angular relationships (and thus a large part of semantic structure) are preserved.

Two useful facts:
- HE depends only on normalized neurons — it's insensitive to per-neuron magnitudes.
- Applying the same orthogonal transformation (rotation/reflection) to all neurons in a layer preserves all pairwise angles, and therefore preserves HE exactly.

That second fact is the gateway to the finetuning method in the paper.

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): preserve angles by construction

Instead of adding arbitrary additive updates or tuning every weight directly, OFT parameterizes the finetuned weights in each layer as

W = R · W^0,

where W^0 is the pretrained weight matrix and R is a learnable orthogonal matrix (R^T R = I). Because R is orthogonal, the normalized columns of W are simply rotated/reflected versions of the normalized columns of W^0, so all pairwise angles — and thus HE — are preserved exactly.

Why is this helpful?
- It explicitly enforces the HE preservation desideratum, addressing the semantic-preservation concern head-on.
- It concentrates the model’s flexibility on changing directions in a structured way (global orthogonal transform per layer), instead of unconstrained changes that can break generalization.
- In practice (per the paper), OFT yields faster and more stable convergence on subject-driven and controllable generation tasks, compared with standard finetuning and adapter methods like LoRA.

A few practical details from the paper:
- R is initialized to the identity so optimization starts from the pretrained model.
- The Cayley transform is used to parametrize orthogonal matrices efficiently via a skew-symmetric Q: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}. This yields orthogonals with determinant +1 (rotations).
- For parameter efficiency, R can be block-diagonal (several smaller orthogonal blocks) or even share blocks across channels, which reduces learnable parameters while keeping orthogonality.

## Constrained orthogonal finetuning (COFT): keep angles, but not wander far

Preserving angles can still allow large rotations that, while HE-preserving, move the model far from pretrained weights in Euclidean distance. To explicitly control that, COFT adds a radius constraint around the identity orthogonal matrix:

minimize under R satisfying R^T R = I and \|R - I\| \le \epsilon.

Operationally, using the Cayley parametrization R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) (Neumann series intuition), the constraint can be enforced approximately by constraining \|Q\|. COFT therefore combines two constraints: exact HE preservation (orthogonality) and limited deviation from the pretrained orientation (small \epsilon). Empirically this improves finetuning stability and makes hyperparameters like number of iterations easier to set.

## A small excursion: re-scaled OFT

HE ignores magnitudes, so you can safely pair orthogonal transforms with per-neuron positive scalings. Re-scaled OFT extends OFT to learn a diagonal scaling matrix D (positive entries) so the forward pass becomes

z = (R · W^0 · D)^T x.

This adds a small amount of additional flexibility (magnitudes) while keeping angle structure intact. The paper reports that this slightly improves performance with negligible extra parameters.

## How OFT compares to LoRA and ControlNet (short)

LoRA adds a low-rank additive update ΔW to W^0 (W = W^0 + A B). OFT instead composes W^0 with a structured multiplicative orthogonal transform. The two approaches trade off different inductive biases: LoRA enforces low-rank adaptation; OFT enforces angle-preserving, global per-layer rotations (possibly block-wise). Empirically in the paper, OFT/COFT converge faster, are more stable, and preserve subject identity and controllability with fewer finetuning iterations and parameters than LoRA and adapter-based control approaches like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter.

Important practical advantage: OFT introduces no inference-time overhead — you can multiply R into W^0 once after finetuning and use the model normally.

## Why preserving pairwise angles helps in practice

Putting the pieces together:

- Neural directions (angles) encode semantic relationships; magnitudes are less semantically informative.
- HE summarizes pairwise angular relationships across a layer.
- Orthogonal transforms preserve HE exactly. By restricting finetuning to orthogonal transforms (and optionally a small scaling or radius constraint), you let the model adapt directions but keep the pretrained relational geometry intact.
- That constrained flexibility yields faster, more stable adaptation for both subject-driven generation (few-shot personalization) and controllable generation (adding edge/segmentation/landmark guidance), according to the experiments in the paper.

## Takeaways and open questions

- If you care about preserving a pretrained model's semantic priors during finetuning, think angles not just Euclidean distance.
- Hyperspherical energy is a compact, interpretable measure of how the neuron directions are organized; preserving it is a principled target.
- Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained/rescaled variants) is a practical way to enforce angle preservation and was shown in the paper to improve convergence, stability, and controllability in text-to-image diffusion finetuning tasks.

Open problems mentioned in the paper (good things to explore next): making Cayley-based orthogonal parametrizations more scalable (matrix inverses can be costly), better parameter-efficient representations for orthogonal transforms beyond block-diagonalization, and whether sequences or combinations of task-specific orthogonal transforms can be composed while preserving multiple tasks' knowledge.

If you're experimenting with finetuning large generative models, try thinking in terms of angles first — and consider orthogonality as a useful, low-cost constraint to maintain the model's pretraining wisdom.



# COFT: Guardrails for Stable, High-Quality Personalization

## Overview

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) is a targeted refinement of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) designed to make personalization of large text-to-image diffusion models (think DreamBooth-style subject-driven generation) more stable and predictable. OFT adapts pretrained weights by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms, preserving the pairwise angles between neurons — a property the authors show is important for keeping pretrained semantic abilities intact. COFT adds one simple but powerful extra constraint: keep those orthogonal transforms within a small radius of the identity. The result is a finetuning method that avoids collapse, reduces sensitivity to learning rates and iteration counts, and converges faster while preserving generative quality.

This post distills why COFT works, how it is implemented, and practical tips for using it in DreamBooth-style personalization.

## Recap: Why orthogonal finetuning (OFT)?

Two observations motivate OFT:

1. Angular information matters. Empirical work cited in the paper shows that neuron directions (angles) capture most of the semantic signal. A toy autoencoder experiment in the paper finds that the phase/angle of filters preserves visual content much better than magnitude.

2. Hyperspherical energy as a structural measure. The paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE) to describe the pairwise angular relationships among normalized neurons. HE is defined for a layer with normalized neurons $\hat{\mathbf w}_i$ as
   $$
   \text{HE}(\mathbf W) := \sum_{i\neq j} \| \hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j \|^{-1}.
   $$
   OFT enforces HE preservation by restricting finetuning to orthogonal transforms $\mathbf R$ so that the finetuned weights are $\mathbf W = \mathbf R \mathbf W^0$. Orthogonal transforms preserve angles (and thus HE), so OFT provides a principled structural regularization that keeps pretrained semantic relationships intact.

In practice OFT parameterizes a layer-wise orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ (initialized to identity) and optimizes it while keeping $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I$.

## What COFT adds: an explicit radius constraint

While OFT preserves hyperspherical structure, it still allows arbitrarily large orthogonal rotations, which can be too flexible for delicate personalization. COFT places an extra constraint that limits how far the orthogonal matrix can stray from identity:

- Forward pass: z = (\mathbf R \mathbf W^0)^\top x, with orthogonality constraint \mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I and bounded deviation \| \mathbf R - \mathbf I \| \le \epsilon.

This is the "guardrail": keep rotations small and controlled. Intuitively, preserving angles (OFT) prevents destruction of pretrained semantics; enforcing small angular deviation (COFT) prevents over-correction that can lead to model collapse or runaway behavior.

## Efficient parameterization and enforcing the radius

COFT uses the Cayley transform to parameterize orthogonal matrices efficiently. Let Q be skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^\top). The Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix with determinant 1:
$$
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}.
$$

To make the radius constraint tractable, the paper notes that for small Q we have the Neumann-series approximation
$$
\mathbf R \approx \mathbf I + 2\mathbf Q + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf Q^2),
$$
so the constraint \| \mathbf R - \mathbf I \| \le \epsilon can be approximately enforced by constraining \| \mathbf Q \| \le \epsilon' (with projected gradient steps on Q). Q is initialized to zero so the process starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

To control parameter cost and scale to large layers, COFT (like OFT) uses a block-diagonal structure for R: \mathbf R = diag(R_1, ..., R_r). The number of trainable parameters scales with block size choices; blocks can also be shared to further reduce parameters.

## Why the radius constraint helps (empirical and conceptual)

- Preserves pretrained semantics: OFT already preserves hyperspherical energy; COFT keeps the model near the pretrained point in weight space as well, explicitly encoding the usual finetuning bias (small deviation) but in orthogonal coordinates.

- Prevents collapse: In DreamBooth-style personalization, naive finetuning or overly flexible updates can lead to collapsed outputs or identity loss. COFT reduces this risk by limiting the magnitude of rotations.

- Less sensitive hyperparameters: Because COFT constrains deviation, training becomes less sensitive to choices of learning rate and number of iterations. The paper shows that COFT converges stably across a wide range of iterations and learning rates, reducing the need for delicate tuning.

- Faster, stable convergence: Convergence experiments in the paper show OFT/COFT reach good control faster than LoRA and ControlNet baselines. COFT’s explicit radius constraint also improves finetuning stability compared to OFT alone.

## Evidence in subject-driven generation (DreamBooth setup)

The paper evaluates COFT in DreamBooth-style personalization (few-shot subject-driven generation). Key findings:

- Stability and convergence: Both OFT and COFT achieve stable finetuning behavior earlier (e.g., after 400 iterations) where other methods either fail to preserve identity or later collapse when trained longer. COFT is especially robust with a proper epsilon.

- Quantitative metrics: On identity and prompt-fidelity metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T) and diversity (LPIPS), COFT/OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on identity-preservation metrics while matching or slightly improving prompt fidelity and diversity.

- Qualitative results: COFT maintains subject identity and produces images that follow prompts accurately, even when other methods produce identity drift or prompt mismatch.

These results directly support the claim that an explicit radius constraint acts as effective guardrails for personalization tasks.

## Practical recommendations

From the methodology and experiments in the paper, here are practical points if you want to try COFT for personalization:

- Initialization: Initialize Q = 0 so R starts as identity and the finetuned model starts exactly at pretrained weights.

- Block size: Use a block-diagonal R. Smaller blocks reduce parameters and bias the transform; larger blocks give more flexibility. The paper uses block choices to balance parameter efficiency and capacity.

- Choosing epsilon: Epsilon controls flexibility. Small epsilon → behaves more like the pretrained model; larger epsilon → closer to unconstrained OFT. The paper shows moderate epsilons yield the best stability and performance. Enforce the constraint on Q (the skew matrix) via projected gradient steps to make it practical.

- Optimizer & LR: COFT reduces sensitivity to learning rate, but typical small-to-moderate learning rates used in DreamBooth-style finetuning remain reasonable. The identity initialization plus radius constraint allow you to run a larger number of iterations without risk of collapse.

- Inference: No extra overhead. Once training finishes, multiply R into pretrained weights to produce the final weight matrix W = R W^0. The resulting model runs at the same speed as the original.

## Relation to other parameter-efficient finetuning (LoRA, ControlNet)

- LoRA adds a low-rank additive update; OFT/COFT apply a multiplicative orthogonal transform. Both limit how much the pretrained weights change, but in different structural ways: LoRA constrains rank; COFT constrains angular deviation and preserves hyperspherical energy.

- ControlNet and adapter-based methods introduce extra modules and inference-time overhead. COFT does not: the orthogonal transform can be folded into the base weights at inference, so there is no runtime cost.

## Limitations and open points

The paper notes a few open issues that also apply to COFT:

- Cayley parameterization requires matrix inversion and is somewhat costly; block-diagonalization helps but there is room to improve the scalability of orthogonal parameterizations.

- The block-diagonal structure trades off flexibility for parameter efficiency; selecting block sizes introduces bias which may need tuning per architecture/task.

- COFT approximates the radius constraint by constraining Q; the Neumann-series approximation holds for small Q, so large epsilons push the approximation regime.

## Bottom line

COFT is a compact, principled addition to OFT that enforces a simple radius constraint around identity in orthogonal parameter space. That single constraint provides practical guardrails for personalization: it stabilizes training, reduces sensitivity to hyperparameters, prevents collapse, and preserves pretrained semantic capabilities — all with no extra inference cost. If you finetune diffusion models for subject personalization (DreamBooth-style) and find standard finetuning brittle, COFT is a conceptually simple option worth trying.



# Faster, Cheaper, Better: OFT vs. LoRA vs. ControlNet

Fine-tuning large pretrained text-to-image diffusion models is now a standard way to get task-specific behavior: subject-driven generation (learn a person or object from a few images) and controllable generation (honor an extra input like edges, segmentation or landmarks). Popular approaches take different routes: LoRA adds a low-rank additive update, ControlNet attaches an adapter network and finetunes it, and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) rotates the pretrained neurons with learned orthogonal transforms. This post breaks down how these approaches compare in parameter efficiency, convergence speed, data needs, controllability and final quality — and explains why OFT’s block-diagonal rotations often achieve a better trade-off.

## The core ideas (short)

- LoRA: model the weight update as a low-rank additive term. Good parameter saving and simple to implement, but it does not constrain neuron geometry explicitly.
- ControlNet / adapters: add new network modules that provide control signals; these can be large and introduce inference-time overhead.
- OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning): multiply pretrained layer weights by a learned orthogonal matrix R so that W = R W^0. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles between neuron vectors, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy — a structural statistic tied to semantic capacity — while still allowing directional changes that alter semantics. A constrained variant (COFT) also limits how far R can deviate from identity.

## Why neuron angles matter

A recurring observation in representation work is that angular relations (directions on the unit hypersphere) encode semantic information well. Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a layer-level summary of pairwise neuron relations, typically defined from normalized neurons \hat{w}_i and computed as a sum over pairwise inverse distances (or related cosine-based measures). The paper’s key hypothesis: finetuning should preserve HE of the pretrained model to maintain its general semantic generation ability.

OFT exploits a clean invariance: applying the same orthogonal matrix to all neurons in a layer preserves their pairwise angles exactly, so HE remains unchanged. That gives OFT a principled regularizer-like guarantee that the pretrained geometry — and with it, much of the pretrained model’s generative competence — is not destroyed by finetuning.

## Parameter efficiency

- LoRA: trainable parameters scale with the low-rank choice r' as O(r'(d + n)) for a d × n weight matrix. This is often compact but still depends on chosen rank and both input/output sizes.
- ControlNet / adapter-heavy methods: need an auxiliary network with its own weights; often much larger and add runtime cost.
- OFT: parameterizes orthogonal R, but to keep it efficient the paper uses block-diagonal R with r blocks (and optionally shared blocks). That reduces the parameter cost to roughly O(d^2 / r) or better with sharing; in practice OFT with modest block-sizes can have similar or fewer extra parameters than LoRA for the same layer. The paper gives a concrete example: for a 128×128 weight matrix, LoRA with r'=8 uses 2,048 params while OFT with r=8 uses 960 params.

Takeaway: OFT is parameter-efficient, and its block structure gives a tunable knob: more blocks → more capacity; more sharing → fewer params.

## Convergence speed and stability

Empirical findings in the paper:

- OFT and COFT converge significantly faster than LoRA and adapter-based methods on the evaluated tasks. For the landmark-to-face task (L2F), OFT matched LoRA’s 20-epoch performance in about 8 epochs.
- OFT variants exhibit smoother and more stable training dynamics. ControlNet-style adapters show sudden emergence of controllability (a sudden jump in performance), while OFT improves smoothly. DreamBooth-style naive finetuning can collapse if run too long; OFT is much more robust to the number of iterations.
- COFT (OFT with an explicit constraint ||R − I|| ≤ ε approximated via Cayley parametrization/Q projection) further improves stability by explicitly limiting deviation from pretrained weights.

Why faster and more stable? Two intuitions supported by the paper: (1) changing neuron directions is the most effective way to modify semantic behavior, so OFT directly operates in that effective subspace; (2) orthogonal constraints keep optimization on a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), which improves the optimization landscape versus unconstrained, high-degree-of-freedom updates.

## Data needs and sample efficiency

OFT is more sample efficient in the experiments. The paper reports that OFT/COFT can reach good controllability using a fraction of the dataset (e.g., converging well with only 5% of ADE20K in one example), while ControlNet and LoRA needed more data to reach similar control quality. The combination of preserving pretrained geometry and focusing updates on directions lets OFT extract task-specific transformations with fewer examples.

## Controllability and final image quality

- Control consistency metric: the paper introduces a practical evaluation for controllable generation — estimate the control signal from the generated image (e.g., predicted segmentation, edges, or landmarks) and compare it to the input control. Across Canny→Image, Segmentation→Image and Landmark→Face tasks, OFT and COFT achieved higher control consistency than LoRA and adapter methods in the paper.
- Subject-driven generation: on DreamBooth-style personalization, OFT variants achieved better subject fidelity metrics (DINO, CLIP-I) and maintained prompt fidelity and diversity, while LoRA sometimes lost identity and naive DreamBooth collapsed for long finetuning.
- Qualitatively, OFT outputs were more faithful to control inputs and exhibited richer details and geometric correctness than baselines in many sampled examples.

An important practical plus: OFT introduces no runtime overhead. After finetuning you can pre-multiply R into W^0 and deploy W = R W^0; the inference cost matches the original pretrained model. ControlNet/adapters typically require running extra modules at inference time.

## Practical considerations and limitations

- Orthogonal parameterization: the paper uses the Cayley transform to parameterize R via a skew-symmetric Q with R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1}. Cayley restricts you to determinant 1 orthogonals (rotations), which worked fine empirically, but the matrix inverse is a computational cost. The block-diagonal trick mitigates cost and gives a way to trade capacity vs. parameters.
- COFT: adding an explicit deviation constraint (approximate ||R − I|| ≤ ε via bounding Q) stabilizes finetuning and makes hyperparameter choices (learning rate, iteration count) less sensitive.
- Flexibility vs. regularity: OFT enforces a structural regularity (angle preservation) that reduces the risk of destroying pretrained knowledge, but the block diagonalization introduces bias — too small blocks might limit fine-grained adaptation. The paper notes this trade-off and treats block-size as a tuning knob.
- Not minimizing HE: unlike orthogonal training work that aims to minimize hyperspherical energy for classification, OFT aims to preserve the pretrained HE, because lowering HE arbitrarily would destroy learned semantic structure.

## When to prefer each approach

- Use OFT / COFT when you want a stable, fast-to-converge finetuning method that preserves pretrained generative ability, needs fewer examples, and adds little or no inference overhead. It is especially attractive when you can afford a modest extra parameter budget and desire robustness to finetuning iteration counts.
- Use LoRA when you prefer extremely simple, low-rank additive updates and your task benefits from that particular inductive bias; LoRA is widely supported and easy to deploy.
- Use ControlNet / adapters when you need modular, possibly richer conditioning networks or when you want to attach large custom control pipelines and accept additional inference-time costs.

## Bottom line

OFT offers a principled middle ground: it directly targets the neuron's directional degrees of freedom (where semantic changes live) while provably preserving layerwise neuron geometry (hyperspherical energy). With block-diagonal orthogonals and Cayley parameterization, OFT achieves better sample efficiency, faster and more stable convergence, strong controllability, and competitive or superior final image quality — all with no runtime penalty after merging R into the base weights. COFT tightens this further by constraining deviation, making finetuning even more stable.

If you care about fast, reliable finetuning that keeps the pretrained model’s generative strengths intact, OFT (and COFT) is a technique worth trying alongside LoRA and adapter strategies.



# From Edges and Segments to Photos: OFT for Controllable Generation

Modern text-to-image diffusion models are powerful, but text alone can be ambiguous when you need precise image structure: a rough edge map, a segmentation layout, or a set of facial landmarks. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled finetuning strategy that adapts pretrained diffusion models to take these control signals and produce photorealistic images that faithfully follow them. This post explains how OFT converts rough controls (Canny edges, segmentation maps, face landmarks) into accurate images, why it’s more stable and efficient than prior adapters, and how the paper measures control fidelity with a new control consistency metric.

## The basic idea in one line

Instead of adding a new adapter or doing unconstrained weight updates, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal transformation R to apply to pretrained layer weights W^0, so the finetuned weights are W = R W^0. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles on the unit hypersphere, OFT preserves a structural property the paper finds important for keeping pretrained generative ability while adding control.

Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit small-radius constraint around the identity (‖R − I‖ ≤ ε), trading some flexibility for extra stability.

## Why orthogonality helps controllable generation

Two empirical/intuition pillars support OFT:

1. Angular information (neuron directions) encodes semantic structure. Toy experiments in the paper show neuron angles alone reconstruct image semantics far better than magnitudes, suggesting direction changes are the most effective way to alter generation semantics.

2. Orthogonal transforms rotate neuron directions collectively without changing pairwise angles; this preserves a measure called hyperspherical energy (HE), defined for a layer W with normalized neurons \hat{w}_i as HE(W) = sum_{i≠j} ||\hat{w}_i − \hat{w}_j||^{−1}. Preserving HE empirically keeps the pretrained model’s general semantic ability while allowing controlled, stable adaptation.

Because OFT restricts finetuning to orthogonal transforms, it hits a sweet spot: enough flexibility to incorporate control signals, but strong regularity that avoids model collapse and preserves pretrained generative behavior.

## Applying OFT to control tasks

The paper evaluates OFT / COFT on three representative controllable generation tasks using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model:

- C2I: Canny edge → image (COCO). The input is an edge map; the model should produce a plausible photo matching that structure.
- S2I: Segmentation map → image (ADE20K). The model must respect per-pixel semantic regions.
- L2F: Facial landmark → face (CelebA-HQ). The model must match facial pose and landmark layout.

OFT is applied to the same layers where LoRA adapters were evaluated, so comparisons are fair in parameter budget.

## Control consistency: measuring whether the control was followed

A key contribution is the control consistency metric: instead of just eyeballing results, the paper measures how well generated images reproduce the original control signal by extracting the control back from the generated image (using off-the-shelf detectors/segmenters) and comparing it to the input.

Examples of the metric per task:

- C2I: IoU and F1 between input edges and edges detected from the generated image.
- S2I: mean IoU, mean / overall accuracy between input segmentation and predicted segmentation from the generated image.
- L2F: mean L2 distance between input landmarks and landmarks predicted from the generated image.

This metric directly captures fidelity to the control modality rather than only image realism or CLIP alignment.

## How OFT performs vs. baselines

Baselines include ControlNet and T2I-Adapter (both adapter-style finetunes) and LoRA (low-rank additive updates). The empirical findings relevant to controllable generation:

- Strongly improved control fidelity: OFT and COFT achieve higher control consistency across C2I, S2I, and L2F compared to ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The paper reports higher IoU/F1 for C2I, higher segmentation accuracy / mIoU for S2I, and lower landmark L2 error for L2F.
- Faster, more stable convergence: OFT reaches comparable or better control performance in far fewer epochs. For the L2F task, OFT converged in about 8 epochs to the performance other methods required 20 epochs to reach. Finetuning dynamics are smooth; ControlNet-style sudden emergence of control (rapid, unstable phase) is not observed with OFT.
- Sample efficiency: OFT converges well with less data (e.g., performing strongly with only 5% of ADE20K in C2I experiments), showing better data efficiency than ControlNet and LoRA.
- Parameter efficiency and inference cost: OFT uses a parameter budget similar to LoRA (and much less than ControlNet) and, crucially, introduces no extra inference-time overhead — the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into pretrained weights to produce an equivalent network at test time.
- Qualitative quality: Generated images from OFT/COFT are not only more faithful to the control signals but also higher-fidelity and more geometrically consistent than those from baselines in the studied tasks.

## Practical considerations from the paper

- Efficient orthogonal parameterization: OFT uses a Cayley parameterization to produce orthogonal matrices and employs block-diagonal structure to control parameter count and computational cost. Blocks can be shared to further reduce parameters.
- Constrained OFT (COFT): Adding the explicit bound ‖R − I‖ ≤ ε (implemented approximately via constraining the Cayley skew-symmetric parameter Q) delivers extra finetuning stability and makes hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, number of iterations) easier.
- Optional re-scaling: A small diagonal scaling D can be learned (W = R W^0 D) to add flexibility without changing hyperspherical energy; the paper finds re-scaled OFT often improves results but focuses experiments on plain OFT to isolate the orthogonal effect.
- Layer choices: OFT is architecture-agnostic; in practice the paper applies OFT to attention weights (to match LoRA comparisons) and shows it can also be applied to convolutional layers with meaningful block interpretations.

## Why this matters

For developers and researchers who want controllable generation without training huge adapters or paying test-time costs, OFT offers:

- A principled way to preserve pretrained generative knowledge (via hyperspherical energy invariance) while adding control.
- Faster, more predictable training with fewer hyperparameter headaches.
- Stronger alignment to structure-based control signals as measured by the control consistency metrics.

If your workflow involves guiding a pretrained diffusion model with edges, layouts, or landmarks, OFT gives you a stable, efficient path to high-fidelity, faithful outputs.

## Limitations and open points

The paper notes a few remaining issues to watch:

- Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse; scaling this for very large layer dimensions is nontrivial (the paper mitigates this with block diagonalization).
- Block structure trades flexibility for parameter efficiency and introduces bias; finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is an open problem.
- Compositional finetuning—multiplying orthogonal matrices from multiple tasks—looks promising but whether such compositions preserve the abilities of multiple downstream tasks needs more study.

## Takeaway

OFT reframes controllable finetuning as a constrained rotation of the pretrained model’s neuron directions. That simple, principled constraint preserves the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities while enabling accurate, stable control from rough signals like Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks. With the control consistency metric, the paper gives a concrete, task-aligned way to show that OFT doesn’t just make pretty pictures — it makes pictures that follow the control.



# Implementing OFT Today: A Practical Starter Kit

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a lightweight, provably structure-preserving way to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) for subject-driven or controllable generation. This guide turns the theory from the paper into concrete engineering steps you can drop into your finetuning stack. It focuses on practical choices: where to place OFT, how to parameterize orthogonal matrices with the Cayley transform, how to choose block sizes, how to initialize and constrain training, and when to add the small extra flexibility of re-scaled OFT.

## Quick recap: what OFT does (one sentence)
OFT multiplies pretrained weight matrices by a learnable layer-shared orthogonal transform so that pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) are preserved while you adapt model behavior.

## Where to apply OFT in a Stable Diffusion stack
- Attention weights (self-attention and cross-attention) are the most straightforward and effective places to apply OFT when you want to compare fairly to LoRA-based baselines. The paper applies OFT to attention weights for parity with LoRA.
- Convolutional/MLP layers are also compatible. For convolutional kernels, a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix has natural interpretations:
  - If you set the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels, each block acts like an orthogonal transform per channel (akin to depthwise transforms).
  - If you share blocks across channels, the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels.
Recommendation: for an out-of-the-box start, finetune attention projection matrices (query/key/value/output) first — this produces a big effect for prompt/subject control with low parameter cost.

## The Cayley parameterization (how to implement an orthogonal matrix)
OFT enforces orthogonality via the Cayley transform. Implement it like this:

- Parameterize with a skew-symmetric matrix Q (i.e., Q = -Q^T).
- Compute the orthogonal matrix R as
  R = (I + Q) (I - Q)^{-1},
  which yields R^T R = I and det(R) = 1 (special orthogonal group).

Implementation notes:
- Represent Q as a learnable dense tensor but maintain skew-symmetry by storing a raw matrix A and forming Q = A - A^T (or store only the strict upper triangle and mirror).
- Compute the matrix inverse (I - Q)^{-1} directly or via a linear solver. (The paper uses the explicit inverse.)
- Initializing Q = 0 yields R = I, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights (zero-change initialization).
Caveats:
- Cayley transform can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant +1 (rotations, not reflections). The paper finds this sufficient in practice.
- Ensure (I - Q) is invertible; keeping Q small (via constraints or small initial learning steps) helps numerical stability.

## Making the orthogonal parameterization efficient: block-diagonal R
A full d×d R is expensive when d is large. Use a block-diagonal R composed of r blocks:

R = diag(R_1, R_2, ..., R_r),  R_i ∈ O(d/r)

Practical consequences:
- Parameter count reduces roughly by factor r (roughly O(d^2 / r) instead of O(d^2)).
- If you share the same block across groups of neurons, you reduce parameters further (paper discusses block sharing).
- A reasonable practical choice is to set r so each block size (d/r) is modest (e.g., 16–64) depending on memory/time constraints.

Concrete guidance from the paper and examples:
- The paper gives an example: for a 128×128 weight matrix, with r = 8 blocks, OFT had fewer trainable parameters than a LoRA configuration with rank 8.
- For convolution layers, consider aligning blocks with input-channel groups (e.g., one block per channel group), or start with r such that block size d/r ≈ 16–32 as a pragmatic balance.

## Identity initialization and training stability
- Initialize Q = 0 (thus R = I) so your finetuned model begins exactly at pretrained weights. This mirrors “zero-init” behavior used by LoRA and is crucial to preserve pretrained performance early in training.
- Use a projected gradient step if you add deviation constraints (see COFT below).
- The orthogonality constraint (via Cayley) is enforced analytically; you don't need an extra orthogonality penalty in the loss.

## Constrained OFT (COFT) — keep the model near pretrained weights
If you want extra stability (especially when you might run many iterations), use COFT:

- Impose an explicit radius constraint: ||R - I|| ≤ ε during finetuning.
- Practically, approximate via Cayley-Neumann: R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2), so enforce a constraint on Q (e.g., ||Q|| ≤ ε') and project Q back into the feasible ball after each update (projected gradient descent).
Why COFT?
- COFT provides a direct control knob for flexibility vs. preservation. Smaller ε makes the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained one and tends to avoid collapse even under long training runs.
- The paper finds COFT yields better finetuning stability than plain OFT.

Practical tips for the constraint:
- Project Q by scaling if ||Q|| exceeds ε': Q ← Q * (ε' / ||Q||).
- Keep ε' small enough that (I - Q) remains well-conditioned (helps invertibility of (I-Q)).
- If you want an automatic start: let ε' be permissive initially, then tighten it if you detect instability.

## Re-scaled OFT — when to enable extra flexibility
Re-scaled OFT adds a positive diagonal per-output-neuron scaling D (learnable) producing forward pass

z = (R W^0 D)^T x

- D = diag(s_1, ..., s_n) with s_i > 0. This does not change hyperspherical energy (angles unaffected) but adds magnitude flexibility.
- Use re-scaled OFT if you need a little more expressiveness than pure orthogonal rotation gives you. The paper reports re-scaled OFT typically improves flexibility with a negligible parameter cost.
When to enable:
- If you find pure OFT is slightly underfitting the control (e.g., unable to realize a color/brightness change that requires scaled neuron magnitudes), enable re-scaled OFT.
- If COFT with a tight ε is too restrictive, adding D can recover needed capacity without breaking the angle-preservation principle.

Implementation note:
- Learn D as a small vector of positive scalars; parameterize via exponentiation (s_i = exp(u_i)) or softplus to enforce >0.

## Training tips (practical routines)
- Start by applying OFT to attention projection matrices (query/key/value/output) with block-diagonal Cayley parameterization and Q initialized to zero.
- Use the same task losses you would for DreamBooth / ControlNet. OFT is compatible with existing loss functions.
- Because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy and begins at identity, it typically converges faster and more stably than unconstrained finetuning. You can therefore:
  - Be less conservative about the number of training iterations; COFT reduces sensitivity to total iteration count.
  - Use similar learning rates to LoRA for the OFT parameters. (Exact LR schedules depend on your task; the paper keeps other hyperparameters consistent with LoRA/DreamBooth baselines.)
- Monitor both task losses and qualitative outputs (identity/prompt fidelity and control consistency). The paper introduced a "control consistency" metric (e.g., reconstruct control signal from generated image) — useful to detect whether control is being learned.
- If you observe collapse or loss of pretrained capability, reduce allowed ||Q|| (use COFT) or enable the diagonal rescaling D.

## Numerical/stability pitfalls and remedies
- Matrix inverse stability: Cayley requires (I - Q)^{-1}. Keep Q reasonably small or add a small damping (I - Q + δI) where δ is tiny to stabilize inversion if needed.
- Skew-symmetric enforcement: form Q = A - A^T rather than updating Q directly to reduce drift from skew-symmetry.
- Block size extremes: very large blocks approach full R (heavy compute), very small blocks approach identity (insufficient flexibility). Test a few moderate block sizes first.

## Putting it together: a minimal implementation sketch (pseudocode)
1. Select target weights W^0 (e.g., attention projection matrix of shape d×n).
2. Choose block count r (blocks of size d/r).
3. For each block, parameterize a raw matrix A of shape (d/r, d/r).
4. Form Q_block = A - A^T and compute R_block = (I + Q_block) (I - Q_block)^{-1}.
5. If using shared blocks, reuse the same R_block for multiple groups.
6. Construct R = diag(R_1, ..., R_r). Initialize all A = 0 ⇒ all Q = 0 ⇒ R = I.
7. Optionally add D = diag(s) with s_i = exp(u_i) initialized with u=0.
8. Forward pass uses z = (R W^0 D)^T x (or D omitted).
9. If using COFT, after each gradient step project Q blocks to satisfy ||Q|| ≤ ε'.
10. Train only the OFT parameters (A, and optionally u) while keeping W^0 frozen; apply task loss as usual.

## Final notes
- OFT is model-agnostic — it works in transformers, MLPs, and conv layers — but for immediate impact use attention matrices in Stable Diffusion (parity with LoRA).
- Identity initialization (Q = 0) and Cayley parameterization give you provable angle preservation, and COFT lets you explicitly control how far you deviate from pretrained weights.
- Re-scaled OFT (adding small diagonal D) is recommended when you need extra flexibility but want to keep angle structure intact.
- OFT introduces no inference overhead: you can fold R into W^0 once training finishes, so runtime is identical to the original model.

# Composable Control: Stacking Orthogonal Finetunes for Multi-Skill Models

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a neat constrained way to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models: instead of adding arbitrary weight updates, learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix R that rotates the pretrained neuron basis so that pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy) are preserved. One simple algebraic fact follows directly from this design: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. That opens up an intriguing and practical possibility—compose multiple finetunes by multiplying their orthogonal transforms together to build multi-skill models without retraining from scratch.

Below I walk through what this composability means, what it might enable (and what it doesn’t), and the concrete open questions you’d need to answer before shipping a composable OFT system.

## Recap: why OFT composes

In OFT a pretrained layer W^0 is finetuned by replacing the forward pass
z = (W^0)^\top x
with
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
where R is orthogonal (R^\top R = I). After one finetune we can form W^1 = R_1 W^0. After a second finetune with orthogonal R_2 we obtain W^2 = R_2 W^1 = (R_2 R_1) W^0. Because R_2 R_1 is orthogonal, W^2 is exactly the same form OFT would produce if we had trained a single orthogonal transform jointly. Algebraically: composition by multiplication stays within the OFT family and preserves the key invariance (pairwise neuron angles / hyperspherical energy) that motivates OFT.

That simple observation suggests a modular workflow: train an OFT per capability (personalization, a particular control like edge-to-image, or a visual style) and combine the learned R’s at inference time by multiplying them into a single orthogonal matrix to obtain a model with multiple abilities.

## What composability could enable in practice

- Modular personalization + controls. Train a subject-driven OFT (to inject a user’s identity) and separately train a control OFT (e.g., Canny→image). Multiply their Rs at inference to get a personalized model that also respects controls—no joint retraining required.

- Style or domain adapters. Keep a small library of style Rs (photoreal, oil-painting, anime) and multiply the desired style R with the task R and the personalization R to synthesize combinations of skills.

- Lightweight multi-skill distribution. Instead of shipping full finetuned checkpoints per skill, distribute small orthogonal factors. The runtime cost is minimal: multiply the R factors into the pretrained weights once (or multiply W^0 by the composed R on-the-fly), so no extra inference-time network or compute overhead is needed beyond a single matrix multiply to materialize W = R_composed W^0.

- Iterative, incremental upgrades. Add new skills by learning an additional orthogonal R_new and composing it with previously deployed Rs rather than retraining or merging large checkpoints.

These use-cases naturally fit OFT's motivations: preserve pretrained generative behavior (by preserving hyperspherical energy), keep finetuning parameter-efficient, and avoid inference overhead.

## Important caveats and technical realities

Composability sounds powerful, but there are several nontrivial limitations and practical considerations rooted in OFT’s design and the paper’s own discussion.

1. Orthogonality ≠ non-interference. While the product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal (so hyperspherical energy is preserved), that guarantee is structural, not semantic. The paper explicitly lists an open problem: "Whether this set of orthogonal matrices preserve the knowledge of all the downstream tasks remains an interesting direction to study." In short, composing Rs may not preserve each finetune’s effectiveness simultaneously. Two Rs trained independently can interact (constructively or destructively) when applied together to the same W^0.

2. Non-commutativity matters. Orthogonal matrix multiplication is not commutative in general, so the order in which you multiply adapters matters. R_a R_b ≠ R_b R_a, so "style then identity" may differ from "identity then style". The paper does not settle whether a canonical ordering should be used or whether ordering can be learned/optimized.

3. Block structure and parameterization compatibility. OFT uses block-diagonal parameterizations (or block-sharing) and Cayley parameterization for efficiency. For compositions to be straightforward, the per-skill Rs must be compatible in shape and blocking (same layer sizes and same block partitioning). In heterogeneous deployments you must ensure consistent parameterization across adapters.

4. Deviation control and COFT. The constrained variant COFT adds an explicit small-deviation constraint (||R − I|| ≤ ε) to keep the finetuned model near W^0 and improve stability. Composing several COFT transforms with small εs may keep the cumulative deviation controlled, but the composition of multiple small rotations can still amount to a large net rotation. You may need to manage or rescale per-adapter ε to avoid excessive drift.

5. Re-scaling / magnitude changes. Original OFT preserves directions and therefore hyperspherical energy; re-scaled OFT also learns a diagonal magnitude matrix D (so z = (R W^0 D)^\top x). Magnitude factors do not alter hyperspherical energy (because normalization removes them), but magnitude composition is multiplicative in D and can change the effective scale of neurons. If you plan to compose adapters that include diagonal scalings, you must handle how D factors combine (they commute, but can blow up or vanish without constraints).

6. Training dynamics: conditional vs joint finetuning. An R trained in isolation for a skill may exploit interaction effects with the downstream loss that won’t generalize when other Rs are present. Joint finetuning of multiple Rs (train R_a and R_b together) is different from sequentially composing pre-trained Rs. Which procedure yields better multi-skill performance is an empirical question.

7. Numerical & compute issues. The paper notes Cayley parameterization involves a matrix inverse and can be a scalability bottleneck; block-diagonalization helps but introduces biases. Composing many block-structured Rs is cheap algebraically, but training and maintaining many adapters still has memory and compute considerations.

## Concrete experiments to evaluate composability

If you want to test whether composing orthogonal finetunes actually yields practical multi-skill models, here are experiments grounded in the paper’s metrics and claims:

- Sequential composition vs joint training. Train adapter R_a for subject personalization and R_b for a control task independently. Evaluate:
  - Subject fidelity (DINO, CLIP-I) on generated images after applying R_a alone, R_b alone, and R_b R_a (both orders). Compare to a jointly trained adapter R_joint and to a baseline DreamBooth/ControlNet result.
  - Control consistency (the paper’s control metric) after composition.

- Order-sensitivity sweep. For several pairs of adapters (identity/style, control/style), compare outputs for R1 R2 vs R2 R1. Measure differences in CLIP similarity to prompts, control consistency, and perceptual metrics (LPIPS).

- Multi-adapter cascade. Compose 3+ adapters (personalization, control, style). Track cumulative deviation from identity (||R_composed − I||) and downstream metrics; test how COFT-constrained adapters with small ε behave compared to unconstrained OFT adapters.

- Interference / catastrophic degradation. Measure whether composing adapter B degrades performance on the skill provided by adapter A (and vice versa). Quantify the drop in per-skill metrics compared to single-adapter baselines.

- Block-sharing and compatibility tests. Train adapters with different block partitions and measure whether composition requires identical blocking. Evaluate whether a shared-block scheme helps or hurts composability.

- Ablate re-scaled OFT. Compare composition behavior for pure OFT (R only) versus re-scaled OFT (R and D). Track whether diagonal scales amplify or mute other adapters.

These experiments directly use metrics and setups the paper already employs (DINO, CLIP-I/T, LPIPS, control consistency), so they are realistic extensions of the reported work.

## Design patterns and practical recommendations (based on paper insights)

- Prefer COFT (small ε) for user-facing adapters intended to compose. Explicit small-deviation constraints make finetuning stable and make the composed transform less likely to produce large, unexpected behavior changes.

- Standardize adapter parameterization. Use the same block-diagonality and Cayley settings across adapters so composition is a simple multiply.

- Provide a canonical composition order or tooling to search for the best order—since multiplication is non-commutative, allow user-facing APIs to specify ordering and to preview effects.

- Offer a composed-sanity-check step: after multiplying Rs, evaluate important metrics (subject fidelity, control consistency) on a small held-out set to detect destructive interference before deployment.

- If scale factors are used (re-scaled OFT), normalize magnitudes after multiplication to avoid runaway intensity changes.

## Open research questions

The paper itself points to several open problems that are central for composition:

- Do composed orthogonal matrices preserve the knowledge of all downstream tasks? In other words, is there a generalization guarantee (or at least empirical conditions) under which tasks remain separable and non-interfering after composition?

- How should we schedule or order composed adapters? Is there an associative or commutative subset of adapters we can exploit, or can we learn a small "router" that reorders or slightly retrains composed R to reduce interference?

- Can we make parameter-efficient orthogonal adapters that are simultaneously more flexible and less biased than block-diagonalization, so composition is both cheap and expressive?

- How to scale Cayley/inverse-based parameterizations for very large layers or very many adapters without sacrificing orthogonality or training efficiency?

- Can we characterize when two tasks are compatible for composition (e.g., personalization and style might be more orthogonal than two different control tasks that target the same spatial structure)?

Answering these questions will determine whether composable OFT adapters remain a practical mechanism for modular model building or remain an elegant but limited algebraic curiosity.

## Takeaway

The algebraic fact that orthogonal transforms compose is powerful: it enables a modular, parameter-efficient, and inference-light approach to combining finetunes. But orthogonality is a structural invariant, not a semantic guarantee. Whether you can stack personalization, style, and control into a single composed model that reliably preserves each skill is an empirical question with many dependencies: adapter constraints (COFT ε), block parameterization, order, and whether adapters were trained jointly or independently.

If you’re building multi-skill systems, OFT gives you a promising toolset. The immediate next steps are pragmatic: standardize adapter formats, run the composition experiments above, and build safety checks that detect interference. From a research perspective, the composability question opens a clean line of inquiry into task compatibility, adapter ordering, and structured parameterizations that balance expressivity and compositional robustness.



# Zero Overhead at Inference: Deploying Personalized Diffusion on a Budget

Large text-to-image diffusion models are powerful, but shipping personalized or controllable variants often comes with runtime costs: new adapter modules, bigger memory footprint, or extra compute during sampling. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) changes that calculus. The key takeaway: once you finish finetuning, OFT’s learned rotations can be folded into the base weights, producing an equivalent model with no extra inference latency or architectural overhead.

Below I explain how that works, what it means for memory and on-device deployment, and practical trade-offs teams should consider.

## How folding works (the simple math)

OFT finetunes a pretrained layer by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) and applying it multiplicatively to the pretrained weights \(\mathbf{W}^0\). The forward pass during finetuning is:

\(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}.\)

After training, you can precompute and store the product

\(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0.\)

At inference time you then run the exact same forward computation the original pretrained model used:

\(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}.\)

Because \(\mathbf{W}\) is a standard weight matrix, there is no extra matrix multiplication, no additional control modules, and no extra memory accesses beyond what the base model already required. In the paper: "we can simply multiply the learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) into the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\) and obtain an equivalent weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). Thus the inference speed is the same as the pretrained model."

## Practical deployment implications

- Zero runtime latency overhead. Folding yields a single weight tensor per layer. Sampling code, optimized kernels, and hardware accelerators can run unchanged — latency is identical to the pretrained model.
- No extra module graph or memory indirections. Unlike adapter-style methods (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) that put extra blocks in the forward pass, OFT requires no extra operators or branching at inference.
- Same memory footprint for model weights. If you replace \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with \(\mathbf{W}\) in your stored model, the total parameter storage for the model is unchanged. There is no permanent need to store both \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{W}^0\) for inference.
- Optionally reversible: if you prefer to keep the original checkpoint and apply the rotation at load time, you can compute \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) once when loading the model on device. That introduces one-time cost on load, not during sampling.
- No extra runtime memory for control signals. OFT is trained to incorporate controllability into the model’s existing weights; it does not require passing additional aux inputs through extra networks at inference.

## On-device benefits

- Fits constrained devices. Because the final model is a standard weight-only variant, device drivers and quantization pipelines already designed for the base model still apply. You do not need to re-engineer inference kernels.
- Efficient storage strategies. Teams can either store the folded \(\mathbf{W}\) (recommended for lowest runtime complexity) or store a compact representation of \(\mathbf{R}\) (if you want to reconstruct \(\mathbf{W}\) on demand). The paper provides parameter-efficient variants to make storing \(\mathbf{R}\) itself feasible if desired.
- No extra latency for multi-turn or interactive usage. Since no additional modules execute during sampling, tight latency budgets (e.g., interactive editors, on-device photo apps) remain satisfied.

## Parameter efficiency and what you store

OFT is designed to be parameter-efficient during finetuning, and that efficiency helps when you need to keep many personalized variants:

- Block-diagonal orthogonals. OFT commonly parameterizes \(\mathbf{R}\) as block-diagonal with \(r\) blocks; this reduces trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) for a \(d\times d\) input dimension. Blocks can also be shared across layers to further shrink the finetuned footprint.
- Concrete comparison to LoRA (from the paper): for a \(128\times128\) weight matrix with rank \(r'=8\), LoRA uses 2,048 trainable parameters while OFT with \(r=8\) uses 960 parameters (no block sharing). That shows OFT can be more compact for the same finetuning capacity.
- Re-scaled OFT. If you want slightly more flexibility, re-scaled OFT learns a small diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\) (per-neuron scales) in addition to \(\mathbf{R}\). This adds very few parameters and still folds into \(\mathbf{W}\) as \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}\).

Note: during deployment if you fold and store \(\mathbf{W}\), these finetuning parameter counts matter mostly for offline storage of alternative variants (e.g., many personalized models). If you want to store only compact finetuning deltas (\(\mathbf{R}\) and optionally \(\mathbf{D}\)) to reconstruct the folded weights on-device, the parameter-efficiency of OFT makes that practical.

## Training vs inference costs

- Training: OFT uses Cayley parameterization (or other differentiable orthogonalization) and may involve matrix inverses during training. The paper notes this is an implementation cost during finetuning, not inference; block-diagonalization mitigates the cost.
- Inference: after folding, there is no matrix inverse or extra operation required. All the heavy training-specific math is a one-time expense done offline.

## Stability and operational advantages

Two practical properties that help shipping models reliably:

- Better finetuning stability. The constrained variant (COFT) enforces \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon\), which yields predictable behavior across different numbers of iterations and subjects. For engineering teams this reduces hyperparameter tuning and risk of model collapse during personalization.
- Smooth convergence and sample efficiency. The paper reports faster, more stable convergence than competing adapter or low-rank updates, which shortens finetuning cycles and reduces compute billed to cloud/edge training infrastructure.

## Limitations and trade-offs to keep in mind

- Training-side cost. Cayley transforms involve matrix inverses (or other orthogonalization steps). The authors mitigate this with block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\), but training complexity is higher than simple additive low-rank adapters.
- Block-diagonal structure is a bias. Parameter efficiency comes from restricting \(\mathbf{R}\) to blocks; that can reduce flexibility compared to fully unconstrained finetuning. The paper reports this trade-off is favorable for stability and final controllability, but teams should validate for their specific tasks.
- If you choose not to fold on-device and instead apply \(\mathbf{R}\) at load time, you pay a one-time cost when constructing \(\mathbf{W}\). This is usually acceptable, but it is an operational decision.

## Quick checklist for shipping OFT-personalized models

1. Train OFT/COFT offline using the preferred block size \(r\) and (optionally) COFT’s \(\epsilon\) to control deviation from \(\mathbf{I}\).
2. After training, compute the folded weight matrices \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) (and \(\mathbf{D}\) if re-scaled OFT was used).
3. Replace pretrained layers with \(\mathbf{W}\) in your deployment artifact. Optionally, re-run quantization/acceleration pipelines on the folded model.
4. For multiple personalized variants: either store full folded checkpoints (fastest inference) or store compact finetuning parameters \((\mathbf{R},\mathbf{D})\) and fold at load time if storage is constrained.

## Bottom line

OFT gives you controllability and personalization without paying a runtime tax. By design, learned orthogonal transforms can be folded into the base weights producing a standard weight-only model: identical runtime graph, identical latency, and identical per-sample compute. For teams shipping many personalized or controllable variants under strict latency or on-device constraints, OFT is an attractive approach: build richer, controllable diffusion models while keeping your runtime budget unchanged.
