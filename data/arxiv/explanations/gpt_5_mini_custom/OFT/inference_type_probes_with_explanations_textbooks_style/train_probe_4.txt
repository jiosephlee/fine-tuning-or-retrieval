Chapter: Orthogonal Transformations and Hyperspherical Similarity Preservation in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Motivation: Preserving Semantics in Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Text-to-image diffusion models achieve remarkable generation quality but are difficult to guide toward specific downstream behaviors without sacrificing their pretrained semantic abilities. The paper introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as a principled approach to adapt these models while preserving the pretrained structure that underlies semantic generation. The central intuition is that the semantic content encoded by a neural network depends primarily on the angular relationships among neurons rather than their magnitudes. Thus, when finetuning, one should preserve the pairwise angular structure across neurons within each layer.

A key construct to formalize this idea is hyperspherical energy, defined in terms of pairwise similarities between neuron directions on the unit hypersphere. The paper shows that if all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, then their pairwise hyperspherical similarities remain unchanged. This invariance is the mathematical backbone of OFT: by restricting finetuning to orthogonal transformations, OFT preserves hyperspherical energy and thus maintains the pretrained semantic structure. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) further restricts the transformation to remain within a small neighborhood of the identity, improving stability without compromising the core invariant.

This chapter explains, with careful mathematical detail and intuition, why orthogonal transformations preserve hyperspherical similarities. We will connect this property to the invariance of hyperspherical energy and explain how these ideas drive the design and performance of OFT and COFT.

# Hyperspherical Similarity and Hyperspherical Energy

To reason about neuron geometry, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \ldots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denotes the $i$-th neuron (column vector). The direction of a neuron is its normalized form $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, which lies on the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The paper emphasizes that directions encode the essential semantic information: varying directions changes semantics, while magnitudes play a secondary role.

Hyperspherical similarity is any similarity measure that depends on the relative positions of unit vectors on the hypersphere, such as cosine similarity. Equivalently, the Euclidean distance between two unit vectors is a function of the angle between them. This relationship makes the following energy functional natural:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This hyperspherical energy measures how uniformly neuron directions are distributed on the unit hypersphere. In the context of finetuning diffusion models, the paper’s goal is not to minimize this energy but to preserve it, thereby preserving the pairwise angular structure of the pretrained model.

Geometrically, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ depends solely on the angle between $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. If a transformation leaves all pairwise angles unchanged, then every term in the sum remains identical, and thus $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is preserved. This observation motivates OFT’s core constraint: apply the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer.

# Orthogonal Transformations and the Preservation of Pairwise Inner Products

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. The paper adopts such matrices as layer-shared transformations during finetuning, replacing a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ in the forward pass:
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Orthogonal transformations include rotations and reflections. Crucially, they preserve inner products and norms. For any $\bm{u}, \bm{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top (\bm{R}\bm{v}) = \bm{u}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{v} = \bm{u}^\top \bm{v},
\]
and
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{u}\|^2 = (\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top (\bm{R}\bm{u}) = \bm{u}^\top \bm{u} = \|\bm{u}\|^2.
\]
Thus, orthogonal transformations preserve both pairwise inner products and vector norms.

This property immediately implies the preservation of pairwise angles. For nonzero vectors $\bm{u}, \bm{v}$, the cosine of the angle between them is
\[
\cos \theta = \frac{\bm{u}^\top \bm{v}}{\|\bm{u}\|\,\|\bm{v}\|}.
\]
Under an orthogonal $\bm{R}$,
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top (\bm{R}\bm{v})}{\|\bm{R}\bm{u}\|\,\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\|} = \frac{\bm{u}^\top \bm{v}}{\|\bm{u}\|\,\|\bm{v}\|}.
\]
Therefore, orthogonal transformations preserve all pairwise cosine similarities. Since hyperspherical similarities are functions of such angles among unit-normalized neurons, they remain unchanged under $\bm{R}$. This invariance is the precise mathematical statement underlying OFT’s claim that “pairwise hyperspherical similarity is provably preserved if we apply the same orthogonal transformation for all neurons.”

# Invariance of Hyperspherical Energy Under Orthogonal Transformations

Because orthogonal transformations preserve both norms and inner products, they also preserve distances between normalized vectors. For any neurons $\bm{w}_i, \bm{w}_j$, their normalized versions become
\[
\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\|} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i,
\]
and similarly for $j$. Then
\[
\|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i} - \widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_j}\| = \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j)\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|.
\]
Hence, each pairwise distance on the unit hypersphere is invariant under a common orthogonal transformation. Consequently, every term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ in the hyperspherical energy is unchanged, and
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]
This invariance ensures that OFT, which finetunes via $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, achieves a minimal possible difference in hyperspherical energy relative to $\bm{W}^0$, namely zero. In other words,
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\| = 0
\]
is attained by any $\bm{W}$ of the form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal.

Intuitively, the entire configuration of neuron directions on the hypersphere is rigidly rotated or reflected as a whole; relative angles, distances, and thus hyperspherical energy remain unchanged. This is precisely the kind of structural preservation the paper argues is crucial for maintaining the semantic generation ability of pretrained diffusion models during finetuning.

# From Invariance to OFT: Design and Stability

The paper translates the invariance principle into a finetuning method by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ per layer, initialized at identity so that finetuning starts from the pretrained model. The forward mapping changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, with the orthogonality enforced during training. Because pairwise inner products and norms are preserved by $\bm{R}$, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical similarities and thus hyperspherical energy at every step. This design constrains finetuning to operate on a smooth manifold of orthogonal transformations, striking a balance between flexibility and regularity.

The invariance has two major implications for stability:

First, preserving hyperspherical energy retains the pretrained relational structure among neurons, which the paper hypothesizes is a key carrier of semantic knowledge in text-to-image models. The experimental results support that OFT preserves semantic generation while enabling new control capabilities, achieving faster and more stable convergence than conventional or low-rank finetuning.

Second, the restriction to orthogonal transforms avoids the uncontrolled deformation of neuron configurations that could otherwise destroy semantic structures. The paper provides empirical evidence that removing the orthogonality constraint yields unstable training and failed generation, whereas imposing it leads to stable and accurate control.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization and Its Effect on Invariance

To enforce orthogonality efficiently, the paper adopts the Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization guarantees $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, and hence the invariance of pairwise inner products is preserved exactly, even though the Cayley transform produces only special orthogonal matrices (determinant $1$). The paper reports that this limitation does not affect performance in practice.

To improve parameter efficiency, the paper represents $\bm{R}$ as block-diagonal with $r$ blocks,
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]
optionally sharing blocks across channels. Each block remains orthogonal, so the full matrix is orthogonal, and thus pairwise inner products of transformed vectors remain preserved. This implies that the hyperspherical energy invariance still holds under the block-diagonal parameterization, since the transformation is orthogonal as a whole.

The key point is that all these parameterizations maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$, thereby preserving the invariants that matter: pairwise inner products, angles, and hyperspherical energy.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): Adding a Radius Constraint

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by design, the paper further stabilizes finetuning via COFT by constraining the deviation from the identity:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \ \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon.
\]
The orthogonality constraint preserves the pairwise inner products and ensures hyperspherical energy invariance; the additional constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ limits the extent of rotation or reflection, keeping the finetuned model close to the pretrained one. To implement this efficiently under the Cayley parameterization, the paper uses a Neumann-series-based approximation to move the deviation constraint inside the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ and then applies projected gradient descent to enforce $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$.

Crucially, this added constraint does not affect the core invariance. Orthogonality is still maintained, so pairwise inner products and hyperspherical similarities remain unchanged; COFT simply restricts how far the model can rotate from the pretrained configuration, improving stability and making hyperparameter selection easier.

# Re-scaled OFT: Preserving Invariance While Adding Magnitude Flexibility

The paper introduces a re-scaled extension that multiplies the post-orthogonalized weights by a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy is computed from normalized neurons, rescaling each neuron by a positive scalar does not change its direction on the unit sphere. Therefore, re-scaling preserves hyperspherical energy. Orthogonality continues to preserve pairwise inner products and norms prior to normalization; diagonal rescaling does not affect the directions after normalization. As a result, the hyperspherical similarities that drive semantic preservation remain unchanged, even as the method gains additional flexibility through magnitude adjustment.

# Why Preserving Pairwise Inner Products Matters for Finetuning

The preservation of pairwise inner products is the mathematical property that ensures invariance of angles, distances on the unit hypersphere, cosine similarities, and thus hyperspherical energy. This is not merely a geometric curiosity; it is central to the paper’s thesis that semantic generation ability is encoded in the angular arrangement of neurons. By constraining finetuning to orthogonal transformations, OFT preserves this arrangement exactly.

The paper contrasts this approach with alternatives:

Standard finetuning updates weights freely, implicitly attempting to limit Euclidean distance from the pretrained model through small learning rates. However, small Euclidean deviations do not guarantee preservation of pairwise angles or hyperspherical energy. This can lead to semantic drift or instability.

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) imposes a different structural constraint by restricting the update to be low-rank. While this reduces parameter count, it does not guarantee preservation of pairwise inner products or angles. The paper shows empirically that OFT achieves better stability and convergence speed, consistent with the idea that maintaining angular structure is more directly aligned with preserving pretrained semantics.

Furthermore, minimizing hyperspherical energy, as in prior work on training from scratch for classification, would move neurons toward uniformity on the sphere, potentially destroying pretrained semantic structures in diffusion models. OFT explicitly avoids this by preserving, rather than minimizing, the pretrained energy.

# Empirical Evidence: Stability, Convergence, and Control

The paper’s experiments support the theoretical invariance-based design. In both subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks, OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than baselines. Notably:

- In subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT maintain subject identity and adhere to prompt guidance over many iterations, whereas standard finetuning (DreamBooth) collapses with long training and LoRA often loses identity.

- In controllable generation, OFT and COFT achieve stronger and more accurate control signals across tasks such as edges-to-image, segmentation-to-image, and landmarks-to-face. The paper introduces control consistency metrics computed by extracting control signals from generated images and comparing them to inputs, and OFT/COFT outperform baselines by these metrics.

These outcomes are consistent with the hypothesis that preserving pairwise inner products (and thus hyperspherical energy) preserves pretrained semantic structures while allowing targeted changes through rotational degrees of freedom.

# Conclusion: The Role of Inner Product Preservation in OFT

The core mathematical fact underpinning OFT is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise inner products. This preservation implies invariance of norms, cosine similarities, angles, and distances on the unit hypersphere. Because hyperspherical energy is a function of these distances among normalized neurons, it remains unchanged under a common orthogonal transformation applied to all neurons in a layer. The paper leverages this invariant to formulate OFT, a finetuning strategy that preserves the semantic structure of pretrained text-to-image diffusion models while enabling efficient and stable adaptation.

COFT refines this by adding a constraint that limits deviation from the identity, further stabilizing training without compromising the invariance. Efficient orthogonal parameterizations, such as the Cayley transform with block-diagonal structure, make this approach tractable while maintaining exact orthogonality and thus exact preservation of pairwise inner products.

In sum, the preservation of pairwise inner products by orthogonal transformations is the mathematical property that ensures hyperspherical similarities remain unchanged. This property is the theoretical cornerstone of OFT’s ability to adapt powerful diffusion models to downstream tasks without sacrificing their pretrained semantic capabilities.
