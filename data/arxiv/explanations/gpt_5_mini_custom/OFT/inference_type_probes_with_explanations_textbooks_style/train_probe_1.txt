Chapter Title: Geometric Constraints in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) for Text-to-Image Diffusion

# From Preservation to Control: Why Geometry Matters in Finetuning

Text-to-image diffusion models pretrain a rich semantic structure that enables high-fidelity and diverse generation. When adapting such models to downstream tasks, a key challenge is controlling the model’s behavior without destroying its pretrained generative ability. The paper introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) to preserve the pairwise angular structure among neurons through layer-shared orthogonal transformations, and then strengthens stability through a constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which explicitly limits how far the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained one.

The conventional approach to finetuning implicitly relies on small updates and small learning rates to keep the finetuned model close to the pretrained model. This is often understood in terms of the Euclidean difference between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights, written as the norm of the deviation, such as the quantity $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, where $\bm{M}$ denotes the finetuned model parameters and $\bm{M}^0$ the pretrained parameters. While this proximity can help prevent collapse, the paper argues that Euclidean closeness alone does not capture the semantic preservation needed for diffusion models, and instead proposes to preserve hyperspherical energy—an angular, relational measure among neurons—while also explicitly controlling the deviation through COFT. This dual perspective, angular preservation with a radial bound, underlies the geometric intuition for why COFT works so stably and why its constraint can be understood as bounding the finetuned weights within a hypersphere centered at the pretrained weights.

# Hyperspherical Energy and the Invariance of Orthogonal Transformations

The central idea of OFT is that the semantic capacity of neurons is primarily stored in their directions, not their magnitudes. The paper formalizes this by using hyperspherical energy to measure the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in a layer. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{ \bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n \} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \big\| \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \big\|^{-1},
\]
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \| \bm{w}_i \|$ is the normalized neuron. This quantity depends only on angles between neurons because normalization removes magnitudes. The paper hypothesizes that preserving $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ during finetuning helps preserve the semantic generation ability of a pretrained diffusion model.

Orthogonal transformations are key here because they preserve angles. If we replace $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is orthogonal with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, then the pairwise angular relationships among neurons are exactly preserved, and hence $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. This invariance makes the OFT update a natural way to change neuron directions without altering the geometry that encodes semantic structure. In other words, OFT modifies the coordinate system of the neurons via rotation or reflection, which leaves the hyperspherical energy unchanged.

This angular preservation provides a robust backbone for finetuning. However, even if angles are preserved, unconstrained orthogonal updates may still move the model too far from the pretrained weights in Euclidean terms, potentially destabilizing training or introducing unwanted shifts. This motivates COFT.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: A Direction-Preserving Update

In OFT, finetuning a layer changes the forward pass from
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
to
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
with the constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ and initialization $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ so that finetuning starts from the pretrained model. By construction, OFT optimizes only the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained weights. It can also be rewritten to reveal how OFT deviates from the pretrained model:
\[
\bm{z} = \left( \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0 \right)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
This decomposition makes clear that OFT’s effect is entirely controlled by the deviation term $(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$, which depends on how far $\bm{R}$ moves from identity. Thus, although OFT preserves angles, it does not by itself limit how far the weights can travel in Euclidean space.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): A Radius Constraint on Deviation

COFT strengthens OFT by explicitly constraining the orthogonal transform to remain within a small neighborhood of the identity. The forward pass remains
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
but now $\bm{R}$ must satisfy two constraints:
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \qquad \| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \leq \epsilon.
\]
The first constraint preserves hyperspherical energy. The second constraint bounds the deviation from the pretrained model by forcing the learned orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ to stay within a ball of radius $\epsilon$ centered at $\bm{I}$ in parameter space. The paper explicitly interprets this as constraining the finetuned model “within the hypersphere of a fixed radius centered on the pretrained neurons.”

To connect this constraint to the finetuned weights, recall $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. Subtracting the pretrained weights gives
\[
\bm{W} - \bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0.
\]
Thus, COFT controls the deviation between finetuned and pretrained weights through the factor $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$. Bounding $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \leq \epsilon$ geometrically limits how far $\bm{W}$ can move away from $\bm{W}^0$ in Euclidean terms, because every deviation is mediated by $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$. In this way, the COFT constraint primarily acts on the Euclidean distance between finetuned and pretrained weights, stabilizing training by keeping the model within a radius-$\epsilon$ neighborhood of the original.

# Implementing the Constraint Efficiently via Cayley Parameterization

Enforcing orthogonality naively can be expensive. To make the constraint practical, the paper uses a differentiable Cayley parameterization to generate the orthogonal matrix:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This guarantees $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. To connect the radius constraint to $\bm{Q}$, the paper applies the Neumann series to approximate
\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2 \bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
under the assumption that the series converges in the operator norm. With this approximation, the constraint $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \leq \epsilon$ can be moved inside the Cayley transform, yielding an equivalent small-deviation constraint on $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\| \bm{Q} - \bm{0} \| \leq \epsilon',
\]
for some error hyperparameter $\epsilon'$ related to $\epsilon$. This constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is then enforced by projected gradient descent. Identity initialization—needed to start finetuning exactly at $\bm{W}^0$—is achieved by setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$.

This parameterization ensures orthogonality while enabling an explicit and computationally tractable way to impose the radius constraint. The result is a finetuning procedure that both preserves angular structure and limits Euclidean deviation.

# Bounding Euclidean Distance: Geometric Interpretation and Stability

The geometric interpretation of COFT’s deviation constraint is straightforward: finetuned weights are confined to lie within a hypersphere centered at the pretrained weights. Because $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ and the deviation is $(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$, restricting $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \leq \epsilon$ primarily bounds the Euclidean distance $\| \bm{W} - \bm{W}^0 \|$. In effect, COFT couples two complementary forms of preservation:

1. Angular preservation via orthogonality, which keeps the hyperspherical energy unchanged and thereby preserves the relational structure among neurons that encodes semantic generation capacity.

2. Radial boundedness via the deviation constraint, which keeps the finetuned weights within a radius-$\epsilon$ neighborhood of the pretrained weights, stabilizing optimization and preventing destructive shifts.

The paper emphasizes that this explicit deviation constraint is what sets COFT apart from standard finetuning, which only implicitly encourages small Euclidean differences through small learning rates. By controlling $\epsilon$, COFT provides a knob for trading off flexibility and regularity: larger $\epsilon$ resembles OFT’s flexibility, while smaller $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like the original pretrained diffusion model. Empirically, the paper shows that COFT with a proper $\epsilon$ yields superior finetuning stability, smooth convergence, and strong controllability across tasks, while remaining insensitive to the exact number of training iterations.

# Practical Consequences in Subject-Driven and Controllable Generation

The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on two finetuning regimes: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. In subject-driven generation, COFT and OFT deliver fast yet stable convergence and strong subject preservation, outperforming baselines in metrics that measure identity fidelity and prompt adherence. Notably, when training iterations are increased, standard methods collapse or drift, whereas OFT and COFT remain stable—an outcome consistent with the preservation of angular structure and the Euclidean bounding enforced by COFT.

In controllable generation, COFT and OFT also converge faster and achieve stronger control consistency compared to adapter-based and low-rank update baselines. The paper provides a detailed quantitative evaluation through control-consistency metrics tailored to each control signal (e.g., Canny edges, segmentation maps, landmarks), and reports that COFT and OFT yield superior alignment between generated images and input control signals. This behavior aligns with the geometric insights: preserving angles maintains semantic coherence, while bounding the Euclidean deviation maintains proximity to pretrained knowledge, jointly enabling controllable yet stable adaptation.

# Summary: COFT’s Radius Constraint as an Explicit Euclidean Bound

COFT modifies OFT by adding an explicit radius constraint $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \leq \epsilon$ on the orthogonal transform. Because $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ and $\bm{W} - \bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$, this constraint primarily bounds the Euclidean distance $\| \bm{W} - \bm{W}^0 \|$. Geometrically, COFT confines the finetuned weights within a hypersphere of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the pretrained weights, while simultaneously preserving hyperspherical energy through orthogonality. This dual constraint—angular invariance and radial boundedness—explains COFT’s stability, fast convergence, and strong controllability in adapting text-to-image diffusion models.
