Chapter: Preserving Neuronal Geometry in Text-to-Image Diffusion via Hyperspherical Energy

# Introduction

Large text-to-image diffusion models derive much of their generative prowess from structures learned during pretraining. When adapting such models to downstream tasks, naive finetuning can inadvertently distort these learned structures, degrading semantic fidelity and stability. The core idea developed in the paper is to identify and preserve the geometric relationships among neurons—specifically, their pairwise angles—during finetuning. This is achieved by minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained models. In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), this principle is implemented by constraining the finetuning transform to be orthogonal, which provably preserves pairwise angles and, consequently, the hyperspherical energy.

This chapter explains why preserving hyperspherical energy preserves the relationships between neurons in terms of their pairwise angles, how OFT enforces this preservation, and why this structural constraint stabilizes finetuning while maintaining semantic generation abilities. We also discuss the constrained variant COFT, efficient parameterizations of the orthogonal transform, and the practical advantages this framework brings to subject-driven and controllable text-to-image generation.

# Hyperspherical Energy and Pairwise Neuronal Geometry

To reason about semantic preservation, the paper introduces hyperspherical energy as a layer-wise measure that captures the relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each column $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a neuron. Let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denote the normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This energy aggregates over all unordered pairs, penalizing configurations where normalized neurons cluster too closely. Because the neurons are normalized to the unit hypersphere, their Euclidean distance is determined entirely by their pairwise angles. Specifically, for any pair $(i,j)$,
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 \;=\; \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i\|^2 + \|\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 - 2\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^{\top}\hat{\bm{w}}_j \;=\; 2 - 2\,\cos\theta_{ij},
\]
where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. Thus, $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is a function solely of the set of pairwise angles $\{\theta_{ij}\}_{i\neq j}$. When these angles are preserved, $\text{HE}$ is preserved; when these angles change, $\text{HE}$ changes accordingly.

This is central to the paper’s hypothesis: the directions of neurons, and the angular relationships among them, encode semantic information learned during pretraining. Preserving these pairwise angles while adapting to a downstream task maintains the relational geometry that supports high-fidelity, semantically consistent generation. Conversely, unconstrained finetuning that alters these angles may degrade the pretrained structure, leading to semantic drift or instability.

# Why Minimizing Hyperspherical Energy Difference Preserves Pairwise Angles

The finetuning objective proposed in the paper explicitly targets preservation of hyperspherical energy by minimizing the deviation from the pretrained layer’s energy:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|,
\]
where $\bm{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained layer. Because $\text{HE}$ depends only on normalized pairwise distances, and these distances are monotone functions of angles, minimizing the difference in $\text{HE}$ encourages the finetuned layer to retain the same pairwise angular configuration as the pretrained one.

Crucially, there is a family of transformations that preserves pairwise angles exactly: orthogonal transformations. If we set
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \quad \text{with} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
then for any $i\neq j$,
\[
\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|}=\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|}, \quad
\left(\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|}\right)^\top \left(\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}\right) 
= \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|} 
= \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]
Hence the cosine of every pairwise angle is preserved, which implies that every pairwise angle $\theta_{ij}$ is preserved. Since $\text{HE}$ can be written as a sum over functions of $\theta_{ij}$, it follows that $\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. Therefore, the attainable minimum in the energy-difference objective is zero and is achieved by orthogonal transformations of the pretrained weights.

The paper’s main conclusion about the preservation role of $\text{HE}$ can thus be summarized: because $\text{HE}$ is a function of pairwise angles, minimizing the hyperspherical energy difference primarily preserves the relationships between neurons in terms of their pairwise angles. Implementing finetuning as an orthogonal transformation achieves this preservation exactly, while still allowing the model to adapt by rotating or reflecting the entire set of neurons in a shared manner.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Formulation and Intuition

OFT operationalizes the angle-preserving principle by making the layer output depend on an orthogonally transformed weight matrix. For an input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, a pretrained layer produces $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. OFT replaces $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal, so the forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},\quad \text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
The orthogonal matrix is initialized as $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so that finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model. During training, the learnable degrees of freedom are confined to orthogonal transformations, ensuring that pairwise neuron angles—and hence hyperspherical energy—are preserved.

The intuition for why this is an effective design is twofold. First, empirical evidence in the paper shows that neuron directions capture semantic content, while magnitudes are comparatively uninformative for semantics. This suggests that modifying directions is the semantically meaningful axis for finetuning, and doing so in a structured way—by common rotations/reflections—preserves the pretrained relational geometry. Second, by restricting updates to lie on the smooth manifold of orthogonal matrices, OFT yields stable optimization dynamics with predictable convergence behavior, which is observed across subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: Balancing Flexibility and Stability

While OFT preserves angles exactly, one may further want to keep the finetuned model within a small neighborhood of the pretrained parameters to enhance stability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) enforces this via an additional constraint that limits deviation from identity in the orthogonal transform:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},\quad \text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I},\ \ \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon.
\]
To implement both constraints efficiently, the paper uses a Cayley parameterization of orthogonal matrices. Let $\bm{Q}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix, $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley transform constructs
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
which is orthogonal with determinant $1$. To incorporate the deviation constraint, the paper observes via a Neumann-series approximation that
\[
\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
so constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$ can be enforced by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ for a suitably chosen $\epsilon'$. Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ then suffices to maintain both orthogonality (by construction) and bounded deviation (by projection).

COFT thus explicitly combines two preservation principles: exact preservation of pairwise angles (and hence hyperspherical energy) through orthogonality, and a bounded deviation from the pretrained model to further stabilize finetuning. Empirically, the paper shows that COFT improves robustness to hyperparameters and number of iterations, while retaining the strong controllability and fidelity of OFT.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

Directly learning a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix carries $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. The paper proposes a block-diagonal parameterization to improve parameter efficiency:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r), \quad \bm{R}_k\in \text{O}(d/r),
\]
optionally with block sharing, $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$. This reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ with shared blocks, while maintaining orthogonality. Because orthogonality is preserved, the hyperspherical energy invariance is also preserved regardless of the block structure, meaning that the pairwise angles among neurons are still preserved.

The Cayley parameterization, combined with block structure, yields a practical and scalable approach. Although the Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse, which can pose computational challenges, the block-diagonal design mitigates this by reducing the dimensionality of each block-wise inverse, and the paper notes this scalability aspect as an open problem worth further optimization.

# Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning: Decoupling Direction and Magnitude

Since hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, OFT can be extended to allow magnitude changes without affecting $\text{HE}$. The paper introduces a re-scaled variant in which a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ with positive entries modulates the per-neuron magnitudes:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Here, $\bm{R}$ preserves directions and pairwise angles, while $\bm{D}$ adjusts magnitudes. Because normalization removes magnitude, re-scaling does not alter the hyperspherical energy, and thus does not disrupt the preservation of pairwise angular relationships. This variant increases flexibility at a negligible parameter cost and is reported to further improve performance, though the paper’s main experiments emphasize the effectiveness of pure orthogonal transformation.

# Connections to Existing Finetuning Approaches

OFT contrasts with standard finetuning and low-rank adaptation methods by making the preservation of pairwise angles explicit and provable. Standard finetuning implicitly encourages small Euclidean deviations, which does not guarantee preservation of relational geometry. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) constrains updates to low-rank additive perturbations, controlling the extent of change but not the angular structure of neurons. In OFT, the forward pass can be rewritten as
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=\left(\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\right)^\top\bm{x},
\]
revealing an additive update $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ that can be low-rank if $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank. However, the defining constraint in OFT is orthogonality, which preserves pairwise angles and, by extension, hyperspherical energy. This is the key difference: OFT’s constraint is geometric (angle-preserving), whereas LoRA’s is algebraic (low rank). The block-diagonal structure in OFT can be viewed as a sparsity-based route to parameter efficiency, complementary to the low-rank route in LoRA.

# Why Preserve Rather Than Minimize Hyperspherical Energy

The paper emphasizes that minimizing hyperspherical energy itself is not an appropriate objective for finetuning pretrained diffusion models. In classification-from-scratch settings, driving neurons toward uniform angular dispersion (low energy) can be beneficial for generalization. In finetuning diffusion models, however, the pretrained relational geometry is already meaningful and encodes semantics crucial for generation. Minimizing $\text{HE}$ would push neurons toward uniform spacing on the hypersphere, potentially destroying pretrained semantic structure. The correct goal is preservation: keeping the hyperspherical energy the same, thereby preserving the set of pairwise angles learned during pretraining, while allowing controlled adaptation via a shared orthogonal transform.

# Practical Consequences in Text-to-Image Finetuning

The angle-preserving design yields several practical benefits demonstrated in the paper:

First, finetuning stability improves markedly. By constraining updates to orthogonal transforms, OFT avoids the collapse or semantic drift observed in unconstrained finetuning as iterations grow. COFT further enhances stability by bounding deviations from the identity transform, making learning rate and iteration count less sensitive and easier to set.

Second, convergence accelerates. Because OFT operates along the degree of freedom that most directly modulates semantics—neuron directions—it achieves effective control with fewer iterations and often fewer training examples. Empirically, on subject-driven generation and multiple controllable generation tasks, OFT and COFT converge faster and to better solutions than baselines.

Third, inference remains efficient. Orthogonal transforms can be fused into the pretrained weights, $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, so test-time cost is unchanged relative to the base model.

Together, these observations reinforce the central fact: minimizing the hyperspherical energy difference in OFT preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. Orthogonal finetuning enforces this preservation by construction, yielding a principled, stable, and efficient pathway to adapt powerful diffusion models without sacrificing their pretrained semantic capabilities.

# Concluding Remarks

The paper’s finetuning paradigm is built on a simple but powerful geometric principle: preserve what matters—the pairwise angles among neurons—by preserving hyperspherical energy. Orthogonal Finetuning achieves this exactly through layer-shared orthogonal transformations, while Constrained OFT enhances robustness by additionally bounding deviations from the pretrained parameters. Efficient parameterizations via Cayley transforms and block-diagonal structure make the method practical for large models. Empirically, this approach delivers superior controllability, faster convergence, and greater stability in both subject-driven and controllable text-to-image diffusion tasks, all while incurring no extra inference overhead.
