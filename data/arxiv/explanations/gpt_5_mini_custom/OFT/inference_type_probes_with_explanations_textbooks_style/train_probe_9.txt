Chapter Title: Orthogonal Finetuning in Convolutional Layers and Its Depth-Wise Interpretation

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning and Hyperspherical Energy

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach for adapting large text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks while preserving the structure that encodes semantic generation ability. The central idea of OFT is to finetune neural networks by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations that act on neuron weights. Such transformations preserve pairwise geometric relationships among neurons on the unit hypersphere, maintaining what the authors refer to as hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy is defined in terms of pairwise angular similarity and is motivated by the empirical finding that angular information encodes semantics strongly. By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT maintains the relational structure learned during pretraining while enabling controlled adaptation through rotation or reflection of neuron directions.

To formalize this, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and its pretrained counterpart $\bm{W}^0$. Let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denote the normalized neurons. The hyperspherical energy of $\bm{W}$ is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \left\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\right\|^{-1}.
\]
The OFT principle seeks to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained layers:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\| \Leftrightarrow \min_{\bm{W}} \bigg\| \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} - \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1} \bigg\|.
\]
This objective attains its minimum (zero) when the finetuned weights differ from the pretrained weights only by an orthogonal transform, that is, $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$. The forward computation then becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{R}$ is learned during finetuning but initialized at $\bm{I}$ to start exactly from the pretrained solution.

This orthogonal mechanism targets the most semantically meaningful degrees of freedom: neuron directions (angles). By rotating or reflecting all neurons in a common, layer-shared way, OFT preserves pairwise angles and thus the hyperspherical structure, while allowing the model to adapt to new tasks via coordinated angular modifications rather than unconstrained magnitude and direction changes. In addition to OFT, the constrained variant (COFT) further restricts the deviation from the identity transform via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$, improving finetuning stability without sacrificing the preservation of hyperspherical energy.

# Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Parameterization

While a full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ preserves hyperspherical energy by design, its parameterization can be expensive. The authors adopt the Cayley transform to efficiently generate special orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}, \quad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
and further reduce parameter cost with a block-diagonal structure. Specifically,
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r) \in \text{O}(d),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r}\times \frac{d}{r}}$ is orthogonal. This block-diagonal structure reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full $\bm{R}$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and with block sharing (i.e., $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$), further down to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Despite these reductions, the resulting $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, so hyperspherical energy is still preserved exactly.

This sparsity-oriented parameter efficiency is complementary to low-rank approaches like LoRA, which reduce the trainable parameter count by constraining the update to be low-rank. OFT instead limits the structure of the orthogonal transform while keeping it full-rank within each block, thereby retaining strong control over neuron directions without introducing the instabilities observed with unconstrained finetuning.

# Applying OFT to Convolutional Layers

OFT is agnostic to architecture and can be applied to fully connected, attention, and convolutional layers. For convolutional layers, the block-diagonal parameterization leads to particularly intuitive interpretations. Convolutional layers operate on multi-channel inputs. When OFT is applied to such layers, the weight tensors associated with convolution can be viewed in terms of per-channel neurons, and the block structure in $\bm{R}$ aligns naturally with channel-wise transformations.

The authors highlight that when the number of diagonal blocks equals the number of input channels, each block exclusively transforms the neurons associated with a single input channel. This interpretation arises from the way block-diagonal matrices act: each block is confined to a subspace, and with one block per channel, $\bm{R}$ becomes a set of independent orthogonal transforms, one per channel. Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angular relationships within each channel’s neuron set, this maintains hyperspherical energy at the channel level while allowing per-channel directional adaptation.

Moreover, the authors note a second configuration: when blocks are shared across channels (i.e., $\bm{R}_i$ are tied), OFT applies the same orthogonal transform to all channels. This is akin to a global channel-wise rotation that maintains uniform behavior across channels and further improves parameter efficiency.

# The Case $r = C$: OFT as Channel-Wise (Depth-Wise) Transform

A particularly insightful special case is when the number of diagonal blocks equals the number of input channels, that is, $r=C$. In this setting, each block $\bm{R}_i$ corresponds to a unique input channel, and transforms only the neurons associated with that channel. This makes OFT’s transform most akin to a depth-wise convolution.

Intuitively, depth-wise convolution applies independent filters to each input channel without mixing information across channels. Analogously, when $r=C$, the block-diagonal orthogonal transform acts independently within each channel’s subspace, rotating or reflecting neuron directions within that channel without coupling them to other channels. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles, OFT maintains the channel’s internal hyperspherical structure while adapting the directional configuration within that channel to the downstream task.

This perspective clarifies why OFT is well suited to convolutional layers. Depth-wise behavior at $r=C$ aligns with the convolutional practice of channel-wise specialization, enabling fine-grained, per-channel adaptation. At the same time, because OFT’s transform is orthogonal, it does not distort the learned relational geometry among neurons, thereby preserving semantics learned during pretraining. The result is controlled flexibility: the model adapts via per-channel rotations or reflections of neuron directions, which is a targeted way of modifying semantics while keeping the foundational structure intact.

It is important to emphasize that this depth-wise analogy does not alter OFT’s core preservation property. Even with $r=C$, the overall $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, so the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. The resemblance to depth-wise convolution is in the independence of per-channel transforms, not in any loss of orthogonality or in any change to the preservation of pairwise angular structure within channels.

# Sharing Blocks Across Channels: Contrast and Trade-offs

The authors also discuss the scenario where all blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared across channels. In this case, the same orthogonal transform is applied to each channel. This configuration trades per-channel flexibility for stronger regularity and even greater parameter efficiency. Because a single block is reused, the number of parameters drops, yet the transform remains orthogonal and still preserves hyperspherical energy.

Comparing $r=C$ to shared-block configurations reveals a spectrum of control:
- With $r=C$, OFT aligns with depth-wise behavior, offering per-channel independence and fine-grained directional adaptation.
- With shared blocks, OFT enforces uniformity across channels, promoting consistency and reducing parameter count.

Both designs maintain the invariance of hyperspherical energy, but they differ in how much per-channel variation they allow. This articulates a broader theme in OFT: finding a balance between flexibility (to adapt effectively) and regularity (to preserve stability and pretrained semantics). The block-diagonal structure serves as a tunable regularizer, determining the granularity at which orthogonal transformations are applied.

# Implications for Stability, Efficiency, and Semantic Preservation

The convolutional-layer interpretations of OFT highlight several implications:

First, preserving hyperspherical energy through orthogonal transformations yields finetuning stability. By construction, OFT does not alter the pairwise angles among neurons, which the authors argue are crucial for retaining the generative semantics learned during pretraining. This stability is reinforced in COFT by explicitly constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$; via a Neumann-series approximation of the Cayley transform, this constraint is implemented as a bound on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$, ensuring small, controlled deviations.

Second, the block-diagonal parameterization enables parameter efficiency without sacrificing preservation. Whether $r=C$ (depth-wise interpretation) or blocks are shared across channels, OFT reduces trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ with sharing, while the transform remains orthogonal and the hyperspherical energy invariance holds. This sparsity-based efficiency is distinct from the low-rank approach of LoRA. In fact, OFT can be expressed as $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, making $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ analogous to a weight update. When $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank, OFT implicitly performs a low-rank update, while its block structure controls parameter count through sparsity.

Third, adapting neuron directions rather than magnitudes aligns with the semantic role of angular information. The authors present empirical evidence that the angular component of neurons dominates semantic reconstruction, while magnitudes carry little useful information. OFT leverages this by concentrating updates in the angular domain via orthogonal transforms, thereby achieving faster and more stable convergence in downstream tasks.

Finally, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and inference proceeds exactly as in the original model. This property holds regardless of the block-diagonal configuration, including the $r=C$ depth-wise case and the shared-block case.

In summary, when $r=C$ in convolutional layers, OFT performs independent, per-channel orthogonal transformations that are most akin to depth-wise convolution. This channel-wise design provides fine-grained control while preserving hyperspherical energy, yielding stable, efficient, and semantically faithful finetuning for text-to-image diffusion models.
