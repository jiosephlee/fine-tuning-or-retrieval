Chapter Title: Angular Semantics, Magnitude Scaling, and Orthogonal Finetuning in Text-to-Image Diffusion

# Introduction

Large text-to-image diffusion models excel at generating photorealistic images guided by text prompts, yet downstream control tasks—such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation with additional signals—require adapting these models without eroding their pretrained capabilities. A central challenge is how to finetune such models so that their semantic competence is preserved while enabling targeted behavioral changes.

This chapter presents a cohesive account of orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT), with special emphasis on the role of neuron directions (angles) and magnitudes in preserving and modulating semantics. The paper motivating this chapter proposes to preserve the pairwise angular relations among neurons (captured via hyperspherical energy) by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations. Crucially, it also demonstrates that scaling neuron magnitudes leaves these angular relations intact and thus maintains semantic structure—an operation directly analogous to modifying only Fourier amplitudes while preserving Fourier phases. Building from first principles provided in the paper, we explain why angles encode semantics, how orthogonal transformations provide the right balance between flexibility and stability, and why magnitude scaling is the natural counterpart to amplitude-only changes in the Fourier view.

# Hyperspherical Semantics: Why Neuron Directions Matter

The paper argues that angular information among neurons is a key carrier of semantic structure. To formalize this, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each neuron $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ contributes to the output $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ via $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$ for input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$. Let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denote the direction (unit vector) of neuron $i$. The pairwise relationships among the directions $\{\hat{\bm{w}}_i\}$ can be summarized by the hyperspherical energy
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\big\|^{-1}.
\]
This quantity depends only on directions, not magnitudes. When the set of directions is preserved, the pairwise angular geometry is preserved, and so is $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$.

Intuitively, directions encode what features neurons are “looking for,” whereas magnitudes adjust how strongly those features are emphasized. The paper provides two complementary pieces of intuition and evidence:

1) Empirical observation in prior work cited by the paper indicates that angular differences align well with semantic differences. If neurons are normalized to lie on the unit hypersphere, the direction of a neuron is the primary carrier of semantic selectivity.

2) A toy autoencoder experiment described in the paper shows that, at test time, computing responses using only angular information (cosine similarity) can almost perfectly reconstruct inputs, while using only magnitude information contains no useful signal. Importantly, training used standard inner products, yet the angular-only evaluation retained semantics, highlighting that directions were storing the crucial information.

Taken together, this motivates the central design of finetuning through angle-preserving transformations: if we change neuron directions arbitrarily, we risk destroying the pretrained semantic structure; if we preserve the pairwise angles among neurons, we preserve the relational geometry that underpins semantic competence.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Preserving Pairwise Angles by Design

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is built on a central invariance: pairwise hyperspherical similarity is preserved under a common orthogonal transformation. If $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, then replacing $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ rotates or reflects all neurons in a layer by the same transformation. Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products, they preserve angles between any pair $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$, and thus preserve $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ exactly.

Formally, OFT proposes to finetune by learning layer-shared orthogonal matrices and using the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
where $\bm{W}^0$ denotes pretrained weights. The identity initialization $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ ensures the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model. The key optimization principle is equivalently to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|=\min_{\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)} 0,
\]
achieved trivially when $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This hard preservation of hyperspherical energy stabilizes finetuning by constraining the model to remain on a manifold of angle-preserving deformations of the pretrained network.

For computational efficiency, OFT uses Cayley parameterization to represent orthogonal matrices:
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\quad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
optionally with a block-diagonal structure to reduce parameters while retaining orthogonality. The block-diagonal choice exploits a sparsity structure to make parameter counts and compute manageable, all while exactly preserving hyperspherical energy.

# Constraining Deviation: Stability via COFT

While OFT preserves angles, its full orthogonal freedom can still be too flexible in practice. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) further limits deviation from the identity by adding a radius constraint:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\quad \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon.
\]
Under the Cayley parameterization with $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ and $\bm{Q}$ skew-symmetric, a Neumann-series approximation yields $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This connects the radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ to a norm constraint on $\bm{Q}$, which can be enforced by projected gradient descent. COFT therefore combines two explicit principles: preserve hyperspherical energy (by orthogonality) and constrain deviation from the pretrained model (by radius). This further improves finetuning stability, as the paper’s experiments show.

# Fourier Analogy: Phase as Direction, Amplitude as Magnitude

The motivation for OFT includes a powerful analogy to the 2D Fourier transform: an image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. Changing the phase carries dramatic semantic impact, while the phase alone, even combined with a randomized magnitude, can reconstruct much of the image’s perceived semantics. Translating this insight to neural networks, the paper posits the following correspondence:

- Neuron direction (angle) plays a role analogous to Fourier phase.
- Neuron magnitude plays a role analogous to Fourier amplitude.

This analogy is supported by the toy autoencoder experiment described in the paper: using only angular information at test time nearly reconstructs the input, while using only magnitude information does not. Thus, semantics in neural representations are tied primarily to angles.

With this analogy in mind, OFT’s orthogonal transformations correspond to modifying the “phase-like” angular structure of neurons in a controlled, geometry-preserving manner. By preserving pairwise angles exactly, OFT avoids destroying the pretrained semantic scaffold, even as it redirects the model toward a downstream task.

# Magnitude Scaling as Amplitude-Only Modification

If neuron directions are analogous to Fourier phase, then changing only the Fourier amplitude corresponds to modifying neuron magnitudes while leaving directions fixed. The paper introduces a re-scaled version of OFT to capture precisely this operation:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ has positive diagonal entries. In this formulation, $\bm{R}$ performs an orthogonal (angle-preserving) transformation in the input space of the layer, while $\bm{D}$ scales each neuron’s output magnitude. Because hyperspherical energy depends only on directions $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, multiplying by a positive scalar $s_i$ does not change $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$, and therefore does not change $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$. In other words, magnitude scaling leaves the hyperspherical geometry invariant.

This is the precise neural analogue of modifying only the Fourier amplitude while keeping the phase fixed. In Fourier analysis, amplitude-only modifications adjust the strength of frequency components without reassigning their relationships, leaving much of the perceived structure intact. Here, scaling neuron magnitudes adjusts the strength with which each direction-specific detector contributes, without altering the angular relationships among detectors. Since the paper shows that semantics are principally encoded in angles, magnitude scaling preserves semantics to a large extent while modulating emphasis.

The re-scaled OFT thus augments OFT’s phase-like control with amplitude-like flexibility. Importantly, this added flexibility comes with only a negligible increase in parameters: a single positive scalar per neuron in the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$. Because the hyperspherical energy is unaffected by such scaling, the core semantic-preserving property remains intact.

# Why Magnitude Scaling Preserves Semantics in OFT

To make the invariance precise, let $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$, and write its neurons as columns $\bm{w}_i=\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i s_i$. The normalized neuron is
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}=\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i s_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i s_i\|}=\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|},
\]
since $s_i>0$ and $\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\|=\|\bm{v}\|$ for orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Thus $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ is independent of $s_i$, and so the pairwise distances $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ are independent of $\bm{D}$. Therefore,
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\big\|^{-1}=\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}-\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j}\big\|^{-1}=\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0),
\]
establishing that magnitude scaling leaves the hyperspherical energy unchanged, just as amplitude-only changes leave Fourier phase unchanged. Because the paper identifies hyperspherical energy preservation as a proxy for preserving semantic generation ability, magnitude scaling is a principled way to modulate model behavior without eroding semantics.

# Practical Consequences for Finetuning and Control

The paper studies two finetuning tasks—subject-driven generation and controllable generation—and shows that OFT and COFT achieve faster, more stable convergence and stronger controllability than baselines. Within this framework, the Fourier analogy clarifies the distinct roles of angle and magnitude:

1) Angle modifications via $\bm{R}$: These correspond to phase-like changes that reorient neuron directions while preserving pairwise angular relations. Because semantics are encoded in directions, OFT’s orthogonal constraint protects the semantic scaffold and stabilizes training. The block-diagonal parameterization reduces trainable parameters while keeping the transformation orthogonal and angle-preserving.

2) Magnitude modifications via $\bm{D}$: These correspond to amplitude-like rescaling that strengthens or weakens specific neuron responses. Since hyperspherical energy is unaffected, semantic structure remains intact. This added degree of freedom can further improve flexibility with negligible parameter cost, as the paper notes in its re-scaled OFT extension.

The constrained variant COFT further enhances stability by bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$, which the paper shows empirically to smooth optimization and reduce sensitivity to hyperparameters like iteration count. In all cases, the inference-time cost remains unchanged: one can pre-multiply $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, so the forward pass uses the same computational graph as the pretrained model.

# Summary

This chapter developed the paper’s central insight: semantics in text-to-image diffusion models are largely encoded in neuron directions, not magnitudes. Orthogonal finetuning preserves the pairwise angular geometry—and thus hyperspherical energy—by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations. This preserves semantic generation ability while enabling targeted adaptation. Extending OFT with a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ realizes the precise analogue of Fourier amplitude-only modification: it rescaled neuron magnitudes without altering directions, leaving hyperspherical energy unchanged and thereby keeping semantics largely intact. Constrained OFT adds a radius constraint for further stability. Altogether, angle-preserving orthogonal transformations and magnitude scaling form a principled, efficient, and stable finetuning toolkit for controlling large text-to-image diffusion models.
