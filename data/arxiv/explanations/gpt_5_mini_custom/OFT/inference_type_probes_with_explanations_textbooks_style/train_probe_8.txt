Chapter: Preserving Neuron Geometry in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Hyperspherical Similarity and Energy as a Measure of Neural Structure

Text-to-image diffusion models rely on large neural networks whose internal representations encode semantics. A central idea in the paper is to characterize the structural relationships among neurons using hyperspherical similarity, and to summarize those relationships via hyperspherical energy. For a weight matrix of a fully connected layer, denoted by $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a neuron (a column vector). Hyperspherical similarity refers to the angular relationship between neurons when they are normalized onto the unit hypersphere, and hyperspherical energy aggregates these relationships across all pairs in the layer.

Formally, define the normalized neuron $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \big\| \hat{\bm{w}}_{i} - \hat{\bm{w}}_{j} \big\|^{-1},
\]
which captures the uniformity of neuron directions on the unit hypersphere. The inverse distance penalizes pairs that are close, and hence lower energy corresponds to more uniformly spread directions in classification settings. In this work, the crucial insight is not to minimize energy but to preserve it during finetuning, because the pretrained diffusion model’s semantic structure is reflected in these pairwise angles and distances. Preserving hyperspherical energy therefore preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, and in turn, preserves semantic generation ability.

This perspective provides a structural measure that goes beyond the naive use of Euclidean closeness between finetuned and pretrained weights. While a smaller Euclidean difference $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$ can be useful, it does not guarantee the preservation of pairwise angular geometry that encodes semantics. By explicitly preserving hyperspherical energy, the finetuning process maintains the neuron geometry that the pretrained model discovered.

# Orthogonal Transformations and Invariance of Pairwise Angles

The key invariance underpinning Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is that applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer preserves their pairwise angles. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, and represents a rotation or reflection. If we set $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then each neuron transforms as $\bm{w}_i=\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$. Because $\bm{R}$ preserves inner products and norms, the cosine similarity between any two transformed neurons equals that of the original pair:
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j)}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\|}
= \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}
= \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]
Equivalently, the normalized vectors transform as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$, and their pairwise Euclidean distance is preserved:
\[
\big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \big\|
= \big\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j \big\|
= \big\|\bm{R}\big(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\big)\big\|
= \big\|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\big\|,
\]
where the last equality follows because orthogonal matrices preserve norms. Consequently, the hyperspherical energy is invariant under a shared orthogonal transform:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\big\|^{-1}
= \sum_{i\neq j} \big\|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\big\|^{-1}
= \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
This invariance directly supports the OFT principle: to adapt a pretrained layer while preserving its pairwise neuron angles and hyperspherical energy, learn a shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and apply it to all neurons in that layer.

The preservation of pairwise angles is precisely the property needed to maintain the semantic geometry learned during pretraining. It constrains finetuning to operate by changing neuron directions coherently, rather than distorting the angular relationships that encode semantic structure.

# Why Per-Neuron Orthogonal Transformations Break Angle Preservation

The OFT design choice to share a single orthogonal transform across all neurons is not arbitrary; it is essential for preserving pairwise angles. Intuitively, angle preservation hinges on two operations being applied uniformly. If different orthogonal matrices were applied to different neurons, the resulting pairwise angles would in general change, because relative orientation between two directions depends on how each direction is transformed.

To see the mechanism, suppose we were to transform each neuron $\bm{w}^0_i$ by its own orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i$, producing $\bm{w}_i=\bm{R}_i\bm{w}^0_i$. Consider the cosine similarity between two transformed neurons:
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}_i\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}_j\bm{w}^0_j)}{\|\bm{R}_i\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{R}_j\bm{w}^0_j\|}
= \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]
Unlike the shared-transform case, the matrix between the two original vectors is now $\bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j$, which generally is not the identity unless $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$. Therefore, the inner product—and hence the angle—between the transformed vectors is altered by the mismatch between $\bm{R}_i$ and $\bm{R}_j$. In other words, independent orthogonal transforms per neuron do not preserve pairwise angles unless they coincide.

From the perspective of hyperspherical energy, the normalized vectors become $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{R}_i\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$, and the pairwise distances transform to $\|\bm{R}_i\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}_j\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$, which, due to the different $\bm{R}_i$ and $\bm{R}_j$, are no longer equal to $\|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$. Consequently, the sum of inverse distances that defines hyperspherical energy changes. This explains why OFT’s layer-shared orthogonal matrix is the principled design: it is exactly the configuration that provably preserves the neuron geometry that the pretrained model relies on.

# The OFT Principle: Preserve Hyperspherical Energy via Shared Orthogonality

Guided by the invariance above, OFT formulates finetuning as learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix to transform all neurons while preserving hyperspherical energy. For a pretrained layer with weights $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, OFT replaces the forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
with
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is learned and initialized as the identity so that finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model. Conceptually, OFT adjusts the canonical coordinate system for the neurons via a shared rotation/reflection, thereby changing directions coherently while preserving all pairwise angles.

This design can be viewed as explicitly solving the optimization
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \big\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \big\|
\]
by restricting $\bm{W}$ to the orbit $\{\bm{R}\bm{W}^0: \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\}$, for which the minimum value of zero is attainable. In contrast to methods that implicitly hope to preserve semantics via small Euclidean deviations, OFT preserves a structural measure directly tied to neuron geometry.

Importantly, the paper emphasizes that this preservation is crucial for retaining the semantic generation ability of text-to-image diffusion models. Finetuning that disrupts pairwise angles risks degrading the pretrained model’s generative semantics, whereas OFT’s shared orthogonality provides a strong regularization that balances flexibility with stability.

# Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Transforms

Implementing OFT efficiently requires differentiable parameterizations of orthogonal matrices. Direct methods like Gram–Schmidt are expensive at scale. The paper adopts the Cayley transform to parameterize $\bm{R}$:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}, \quad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q},
\]
which generates special orthogonal matrices (determinant $1$). This provides a practical and differentiable route to maintain orthogonality during training. To improve parameter efficiency further, the paper proposes a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$,
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
with each block $\bm{R}_k\in\text{O}(d/r)$. This reduces complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and, if blocks are shared across positions, to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Crucially, even with block-diagonal constraints, $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal and thus preserves hyperspherical energy. The shared nature of $\bm{R}$ across all neurons in the layer is maintained, sustaining the pairwise angle invariance central to OFT.

The paper also discusses parameter-efficiency comparisons with LoRA. While LoRA reduces parameters via low-rank structure in additive updates, OFT reduces parameters via sparsity (block-diagonal orthogonality) in multiplicative updates, while still preserving the critical geometry.

# Constrained OFT: Explicitly Limiting Deviation from Pretraining

Beyond preserving hyperspherical energy, the paper proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which further limits deviation from the pretrained model:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\ \ \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon.
\]
This constraint controls the flexibility of the orthogonal transform, tempering how far neuron directions can move from pretraining. To make this tractable with the Cayley transform, the paper uses a Neumann-series approximation
\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
and turns the radius constraint into $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$, enforceable by projected gradient descent. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning, aligning with the identity initialization principle.

COFT thus combines two explicit constraints: exact preservation of hyperspherical energy via shared orthogonality, and bounded deviation from the pretrained directions via the radius constraint. The paper finds that COFT provides even better finetuning stability, with $\epsilon$ controlling the trade-off: larger $\epsilon$ approaches OFT’s flexibility; smaller $\epsilon$ adheres more closely to the pretrained behavior.

# Why Preserving Pairwise Angles Matters for Semantic Control

The paper motivates OFT with the observation that neuron angles encode semantic information. Empirical evidence from training a convolutional autoencoder shows that angular information alone can almost perfectly recover input images, whereas magnitude information contains little semantic content. This supports the hypothesis that adjusting neuron directions is the most effective way to modify semantics in generated images, which is exactly the goal in subject-driven and controllable generation.

However, unconstrained changes in directions can destroy the pretrained generative performance. Preserving the angles between all pairs of neurons—achieved by a shared orthogonal transform—strikes a balance: it provides enough flexibility to change directions coherently, while regularizing the geometry to maintain semantic structure. The paper’s experiments confirm that imposing orthogonality as a shared constraint yields stable finetuning and accurate control, whereas removing the orthogonality constraint leads to failure modes with unrealistic images and poor control.

This directly connects back to the central fact: a shared orthogonal transform preserves pairwise angles, and that preservation is critical for keeping the pretrained semantics intact during adaptation. If each neuron were transformed independently—even if each transform were orthogonal—the pairwise angles would not be preserved, which would undermine the semantic geometry the model depends on.

# Practical Implications in Text-to-Image Diffusion Finetuning

Applying OFT to subject-driven generation and controllable generation demonstrates several practical benefits grounded in geometric preservation:

1. Stability and convergence: By finetuning on the hypersphere manifold with shared orthogonality, OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than baselines. This smooth training dynamics reduces sensitivity to hyperparameters like iteration count and learning rate.

2. Semantic preservation with controllability: Preserving neuron angles maintains subject identity and pretrained generative quality, while coherent directional adjustments enable accurate control from text prompts and additional signals (e.g., edges, segmentation maps, landmarks).

3. Parameter efficiency without inference overhead: Block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization achieves strong performance with fewer trainable parameters. At inference, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ to produce $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, incurring no overhead relative to the pretrained model.

These outcomes reinforce the geometric rationale: the layer-shared orthogonal transform preserves pairwise neuron angles; deviating to per-neuron transforms would fail to preserve this geometry, and thus would not maintain the pretrained model’s semantics as reliably.

# Extensions: Re-Scaled OFT and Architectural Agnosticism

The paper also discusses a simple extension, re-scaled OFT, which introduces a learnable diagonal scaling $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with $s_i>0$:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, per-neuron scaling does not affect angle preservation. This adds modest flexibility while maintaining the core invariance. The paper reports that this generally improves performance but focuses on the original OFT to isolate the effect of orthogonal transformation itself.

Moreover, OFT is agnostic to architectures: while comparisons to LoRA are made by finetuning attention weights in Transformers, the block-diagonal interpretation also aligns naturally with convolutional layers, enabling per-channel or shared-channel transforms. Across settings, the shared orthogonality principle remains the same, and with it, the preservation of pairwise neuron angles.

# Summary: Shared Orthogonality Is Essential for Geometric Preservation

The central geometric fact in the paper is that applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer preserves pairwise neuron angles and thus preserves hyperspherical energy. This invariance motivates Orthogonal Finetuning: learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to adapt the pretrained diffusion model while retaining its semantic geometry. If, instead, each neuron were transformed by its own orthogonal matrix, the pairwise angles would, in general, change because the inner products become mediated by $\bm{R}_i^\top\bm{R}_j\neq \bm{I}$, breaking the invariance.

By adhering to shared orthogonality, OFT and its constrained variant COFT achieve a principled balance between flexibility—modifying neuron directions to control generation—and regularity—preserving the pretrained model’s semantic structure encoded in pairwise angles. This balance yields stable, efficient finetuning with strong controllability and no inference-time overhead.
