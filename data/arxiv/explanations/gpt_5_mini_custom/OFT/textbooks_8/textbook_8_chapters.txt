\title{A Textbook about the Paper: OFT (8 chapters)}

Introduction: Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion via Finetuning

# Limits of text-only guidance in diffusion models (GLIDE, Imagen, Stable Diffusion)

Large text-to-image diffusion models such as GLIDE, Imagen and Stable Diffusion have dramatically advanced the ability to synthesize high-fidelity images from natural language prompts. These models learn rich multimodal priors during large-scale pretraining that map text to plausible images. However, text alone is often ambiguous: natural language can omit fine-grained appearance details, exact spatial layouts, or precise identities one might want preserved. As a result, purely text-conditioned generation can fail when the user needs tight, reproducible control over a generated image — for example, to place a particular subject in a new scene, to follow a specific sketch or segmentation map, or to guarantee precise pose for a generated face.

Two broad directions have emerged to reduce this ambiguity. One is to provide more or better textual descriptions, which quickly becomes unwieldy or insufficient for fine-grained control. The other is to incorporate additional conditioning signals or to adapt the pretrained generator so it responds in a controlled way to inputs beyond plain text. The latter requires either adding new modules that take the extra signal or finetuning parts of the pretrained diffusion model so it learns to respect the new conditioning. Both approaches must balance flexibility (ability to learn the downstream task) and the need to preserve the pretrained model's generative quality and diversity.

# Two target tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

The paper formalizes two representative downstream tasks that require stronger control than text alone:

- Subject-driven generation: Given a small set (few images) of a particular subject (for instance, a person, pet, or object), the goal is to generate new images of that same subject in different contexts specified by a text prompt. The key difficulty is to adapt the model to reproduce subject-specific identity and appearance details reliably from very few examples. DreamBooth is a recent approach in this setting that finetunes a text-to-image model using a customized token and a reconstruction-style loss; OFT adopts the same task framing but changes how the model is finetuned.

- Controllable generation: Here the model is given an additional non-text control signal — for example, Canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmark coordinates — and must produce images consistent with both the text prompt and the control. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are representative methods that adapt pretrained diffusion models to accept such signals, usually by adding and training extra subnetworks. The challenge is to make the pretrained generator respect the control while keeping image quality and avoiding slow, unstable finetuning.

Both tasks reduce to a finetuning problem: adapt a powerful pretrained diffusion model so it performs a new behavior (subject preservation or responding to control signals) while retaining the model’s broad generative ability.

# Finetuning desiderata: training efficiency and preservation of generalizability

When designing finetuning methods for large pretrained diffusion models, two desiderata stand out.

Training efficiency: The method should require relatively few trainable parameters and converge in a small number of epochs or iterations. This matters in practice because finetuning on many different subjects or many different control modalities must be fast and cheap.

Preservation of generalizability: The finetuned model should keep the pretrained model’s ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images and to follow a wide range of prompts. A finetuning method that achieves the downstream task but destroys the pretrained model’s semantics or collapses to degenerate outputs is not acceptable.

These two desiderata are in tension: more flexible finetuning schemes (large parameter counts, many degrees of freedom) can fit the new task quickly but risk destroying the pretrained prior; heavily constrained schemes preserve the prior but may not learn the downstream behavior. The goal is to find a principled middle ground that gives enough flexibility to learn control while preserving the structural knowledge encoded in the pretrained weights.

# Shortcomings of standard finetuning and adapter-based updates (small LR, Euclidean proximity assumptions)

Standard finetuning typically applies gradient descent to update weights starting from the pretrained model, often with a very small learning rate. Adapter approaches like LoRA instead introduce a reparameterized small module (for example, an additive low-rank update) and train only those added parameters. Both strategies implicitly rely on a notion of Euclidean proximity to the pretrained model: small learning rate or small additive updates are assumed to keep the finetuned weights close in Euclidean norm to the original weights, and that closeness is taken as a proxy for preserving the pretrained model’s generative ability.

However, Euclidean proximity alone is an incomplete measure of semantic preservation. Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm but differ in the pairwise geometric relationships between neurons that encode semantic structure. Conversely, a change that rotates all neurons together (an orthogonal transform) can change weights in Euclidean terms but preserve the angles between neurons and therefore preserve certain relational structure. Because of this, simply constraining Euclidean distance is not always sufficient to preserve the pretraining behavior, and relying on it leads practitioners to use tiny learning rates and painstaking tuning of the number of iterations.

This observation motivates searching for structural measures that better capture the aspects of the pretrained model that should be preserved during finetuning. The paper focuses on one such structural measure: hyperspherical energy, which summarizes the pairwise angular relationships among neuron weight vectors after normalization.

# Hyperspherical energy and its role as a structural preservation measure

Hyperspherical energy is a scalar measure that captures how neuron directions (unit-normalized weight vectors) are arranged on the unit hypersphere. For a layer with neuron weight vectors $\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$, define the normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy (HE) used in the paper is

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

Intuitively, HE aggregates pairwise inverse distances between normalized neuron directions: smaller HE corresponds to neurons more uniformly spread on the sphere, larger HE corresponds to more concentrated or redundant directions. Prior work has shown that angular relations between features and neurons often encode semantic information more robustly than magnitudes, and that training with normalized neurons can work well for many tasks. Thus preserving the pairwise angular structure encoded by HE is a natural target when we want to keep the pretrained semantic representation intact.

Minimizing the difference in HE between a finetuned model and the pretrained model is one way to formalize the desideratum "preserve pretrained structure." The paper observes that there exists a simple operation that provably preserves pairwise angles: applying the same orthogonal transformation to every neuron in a layer. That observation motivates Orthogonal Finetuning.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): preserving hyperspherical energy by learning orthogonal transforms

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) enforces that the finetuned layer weights differ from the pretrained weights by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix. For a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, OFT parameterizes the finetuned weights as

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\qquad
\text{with }\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]

so the forward pass for an input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ becomes $\bm{z}=(\bm{W})^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves inner products and angles: the pairwise angular relationships among the columns (neurons) of $\bm{W}$ are identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$. Therefore, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ equals $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, and the HE difference target

\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|
\]

is satisfied exactly if $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal $\bm{R}$. In other words, OFT is a natural way to impose the structural constraint that motivates hyperspherical energy preservation.

Why might rotating all neurons together be a good finetuning move? The paper gives two intuitions. First, empirical evidence suggests that neuronal directions (angles) carry much of the semantic content. A toy autoencoder experiment described in the paper shows that reconstructing images using only angular information about convolution kernels recovers semantics well, whereas using only magnitudes does not. Second, there is an analogy to the Fourier phase spectrum: the phase (angle) often retains major semantic structure. Thus, to change semantics in a controlled way one should alter neuron directions; but changing directions arbitrarily risks destroying global structure. OFT restricts direction changes to global orthogonal transforms per layer, giving a controlled way to steer semantic outputs without breaking pairwise neuron geometry learned during pretraining.

Practical details: OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity so the finetuning starts from the pretrained model exactly. The orthogonality constraint can be enforced via differentiable parameterizations of orthogonal matrices (discussed next).

# Efficient orthogonal parameterization: Cayley transform and block-diagonal structure

Directly parameterizing an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and can be expensive. The paper adopts two practical strategies to generate orthogonal matrices efficiently.

Cayley parameterization. Any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ can be mapped to an orthogonal matrix (with determinant $+1$) via the Cayley transform:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]

This representation guarantees orthogonality by construction, and $\bm{Q}$ can be freely optimized under the skew-symmetric constraint. Using the Cayley transform allows enforcing orthogonality without repeated Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization. A small caveat is that the Cayley transform maps to special orthogonal matrices (determinant $+1$), excluding reflections (determinant $-1$), but the paper reports this limitation is not practically harmful.

Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. To reduce parameter count further, OFT restricts $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal with $r$ blocks, each an orthogonal matrix of size $d/r$. This leads to

\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r).
\]

A block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ still preserves the pairwise angles among neurons within the same block and is orthogonal overall, but reduces the number of parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ (and further reductions are possible by sharing blocks across channels, giving $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$). For convolutional layers there is an interpretation: choosing one block per input channel yields channel-wise orthogonal transforms resembling depth-wise transforms.

Parameter-efficiency comparison with LoRA. LoRA uses additive low-rank updates of rank $r'$, leading to $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ weight matrix. OFT with block-diagonal parameterization has parameter complexity that can be lower in practice. For a concrete example from the paper: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix, LoRA with $r'=8$ requires $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ requires $960$ parameters (no block sharing). Thus OFT can be competitive or better in parameter efficiency while enforcing a stronger structural constraint.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): adding an explicit proximity bound

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy, it still allows large orthogonal rotations and thus could deviate substantially in Euclidean terms from the pretrained weights. Many finetuning practices favor explicit bounds on how far the finetuned model wanders from the initial model. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments OFT by explicitly constraining the learned orthogonal matrix to be close to the identity:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},\qquad
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I},\qquad
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon.
\]

This enforces that the orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ deviates from identity by at most $\epsilon$ in operator norm (or another suitable matrix norm), keeping the finetuned weights in a small neighborhood of the pretrained weights.

To make the constraint practical with the Cayley parameterization, the paper applies a Neumann series approximation to the Cayley transform. For small $\bm{Q}$, the Cayley form can be approximated as

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]

Thus, constraining $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$ is approximately equivalent to constraining $\|\bm{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$ for some $\epsilon'$. The latter constraint on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ can be enforced straightforwardly with projected gradient descent: after each gradient step, project $\bm{Q}$ back to the ball of radius $\epsilon'$ if necessary. COFT therefore combines two explicit objectives: preserve hyperspherical energy via orthogonality and preserve Euclidean proximity via an explicit $\epsilon$-ball around the identity. Empirically COFT is observed to improve finetuning stability, especially when users want a reliable, easy-to-tune finetuning process.

# Re-scaled OFT and flexibility considerations

Preserving hyperspherical energy only constrains angles; magnitudes of neurons can still change. The paper proposes a simple extension, re-scaled OFT, that learns a diagonal positive scaling matrix $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ in addition to the orthogonal transform, so the forward pass becomes

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]

Because HE depends solely on normalized neuron directions, scaling by $\bm{D}$ does not change HE. Learning diagonal scaling coefficients increases flexibility with negligible parameter cost and can further improve performance while retaining the HE-preserving property. The paper primarily evaluates the simpler OFT and COFT to demonstrate the effect of orthogonal constraints alone, but reports that re-scaled OFT can offer improvements in practice.

# Why orthogonal finetuning works: intuitions, connections, and trade-offs

Several intuitions explain why OFT strikes a useful balance for finetuning large diffusion models.

Directional (angular) information carries semantics. Empirical evidence and prior literature suggest neuron directions capture semantic content strongly; magnitudes are often less informative. Thus, adapting neuron directions is a principled way to alter semantics when finetuning for new control tasks.

Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron geometry. By restricting updates to the manifold of orthogonal matrices, OFT preserves the pairwise angles between neurons (and therefore hyperspherical energy). This keeps relational structure learned during pretraining intact, which helps preserve the generator’s semantic capabilities.

Improved optimization landscape and faster convergence. Because OFT optimizes on a constrained manifold (the hypersphere manifold for normalized neuron directions), optimization can be smoother and faster. Empirically OFT and COFT converge faster than alternative methods like LoRA or adapter-based methods in the tasks studied.

A middle ground between flexibility and regularity. Standard unfettered finetuning is extremely flexible but can be unstable and destroy pretrained knowledge. Minimizing HE (as in some orthogonal over-parameterized training regimes) would force neurons to be maximally uniform, which would also destroy pretrained structure. OFT achieves a compromise: it allows a full-rank multiplicative update per layer but constrains it to be orthogonal and optionally close to identity. Block-diagonal parametrization further controls flexibility via the block size $r$.

Connections to other parameter-efficient methods. LoRA uses additive low-rank updates; OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal transform. Rewriting OFT gives

\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0,
\]

so $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ plays the role of an additive update. If $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank, the effective update is low-rank as in LoRA, but OFT enforces orthogonality on the transform itself and therefore preserves HE exactly. OFT and LoRA therefore represent two different avenues for parameter efficiency: low-rank additive updates versus sparse/block-structured multiplicative orthogonal updates.

No additional inference overhead. A practical advantage of OFT is that at inference time one can multiply the learned $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This yields no additional run-time computation compared to the original model and simplifies deployment.

# Contributions overview and empirical claims

The paper's main conceptual and empirical contributions are:

- Proposing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled finetuning approach that adapts pretrained text-to-image diffusion models by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms so as to preserve hyperspherical energy and thereby maintain pretrained semantic relationships among neurons.

- Introducing Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which augments OFT with an explicit proximity constraint to the identity orthogonal transform, improving finetuning stability and making hyperparameter choices like learning rate and iteration counts less critical.

- Demonstrating that OFT/COFT outperform prior finetuning approaches on two representative downstream tasks: subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style few-shot personalization) and controllable generation (adding control signals like edges or segmentation). Empirically, OFT and COFT show faster convergence, stronger subject and control fidelity, and better stability compared to DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet and T2I-Adapter baselines.

- Designing a control consistency metric for controllable generation that measures how faithfully a generated image reproduces the control signal: estimate the control from the generated image and compare it to the original input control. This provides an interpretable quantitative measure for controllability.

- Providing practical parameterizations (Cayley transform, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, optional rescaling) that make OFT feasible and parameter-efficient for large-pretrained models like Stable Diffusion.

# Practical recommendations and open problems

From the methodology and experiments in the paper the following practical lessons emerge. Initialize orthogonal transforms to identity so finetuning begins exactly from pretrained weights. Use Cayley parameterization to maintain orthogonality while optimizing, and use block-diagonal structure to reduce parameter cost; share blocks across channels if further parameter savings are required. If stronger proximity stability is desired, use COFT with a small $\epsilon$ (implemented as a projection on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$) to keep orthogonal updates near the identity. Optionally add neuron-wise re-scaling via a diagonal $\bm{D}$ if additional flexibility is needed.

The paper also highlights open problems. Cayley parameterization involves a matrix inverse and can be computationally limiting for very large layers; faster scalable differentiable orthogonalization is an engineering and research question. The compositionality of orthogonal transforms (multiplying orthogonal matrices learned for separate tasks produces another orthogonal matrix) raises the question whether a product of task-specific OFT matrices can preserve knowledge for multiple downstream tasks — an intriguing direction for continual or multi-task personalization. Finally, block-diagonal parameterization introduces bias that trades flexibility for parameter efficiency; designing less biased compact orthogonal parameterizations is another open challenge.

# Summary

In short, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a conceptually simple but principled way to adapt pretrained text-to-image diffusion models: it constrains finetuning to layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that hyperspherical energy — a compact measure of the pairwise angular geometry among neurons — remains unchanged. This preserves important relational structure learned during pretraining, produces stable and fast convergence, supports parameter-efficient implementations, and integrates naturally with existing diffusion generators without inference overhead. Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit proximity bound to further stabilize finetuning. Empirically these methods improve subject-driven personalization and controllable generation tasks, providing a promising direction for controllable text-to-image synthesis that balances flexibility and generalizability.

Geometry and Semantics: Hyperspherical Energy and Angular Representations

# Angular similarity as a semantic measure (cosine similarity, angular margin literature)

A recurring empirical theme in modern representation learning is that angular relationships between vectors often carry the most semantically meaningful information. In the context of neural networks, a “neuron” can be viewed as a weight vector, and the angle between two neurons reflects how similarly they respond to inputs when activations are normalized. This observation motivated prior work such as SphereNet and angular-margin methods, which explicitly normalize neurons onto a unit hypersphere and build losses that depend primarily on angles rather than raw magnitudes. The paper emphasizes that these prior findings suggest neuron directions (angles) encode semantic structure: if two neurons point in similar directions, they tend to capture similar concepts; if they are well separated on the sphere, they represent diverse or competing features.

Formally, angular similarity between two vectors is commonly measured by cosine similarity. For weight vectors $\bm{w}_i,\bm{w}_j\in\mathbb{R}^d$, their cosine similarity is
\[
\cos\theta_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\langle \bm{w}_i,\bm{w}_j\rangle}{\|\bm{w}_i\|\|\bm{w}_j\|},
\]
and the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between them satisfies $\cos\theta_{ij}\in[-1,1]$. When neurons are normalized to unit length, cosine similarity reduces to the inner product, and the semantic relationship among a set of neurons becomes a purely angular geometry on the unit hypersphere. The paper draws on these angular-margin results to argue that modifying neuron directions is a natural and effective lever for changing semantic behavior of a pretrained model while leaving magnitude-dependent aspects (which tend to be less semantically informative) intact.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy: pairwise relationships of normalized neurons

Hyperspherical energy is introduced in the paper as a quantitative measure of the layer-wise relational structure among neurons once they are normalized onto the unit hypersphere. Given a weight matrix $\bm{W}=[\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns are neuron vectors, define the normalized neurons
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.
\]
The hyperspherical energy of this layer is defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Intuitively, the quantity $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is large when two normalized neurons are close on the sphere (small geodesic/Euclidean separation) and small when they are far apart; summing over all pairs captures the overall level of clustering or uniformity of neurons on the hypersphere.

Because normalized Euclidean distance and cosine similarity are algebraically related, hyperspherical energy is closely tied to pairwise angular relations. Indeed,
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 \;=\; 2 - 2\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle \;=\; 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij}),
\]
so each term of $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})$ is a monotone function of the cosine similarity between the pair $(i,j)$. In this way, hyperspherical energy succinctly aggregates all pairwise angular similarities in a layer and therefore serves as a compact descriptor of the layer’s angular geometry.

# Why Euclidean weight distance is an incomplete preservation metric

A common implicit metric when finetuning a pretrained model is to keep the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$ small. That is, standard fine-tuning with a tiny learning rate implicitly attempts to minimize something like $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$. While preserving Euclidean closeness does limit how far weights move in parameter space, this scalar norm does not directly control the structure of pairwise relationships among neurons, nor does it target the angular geometry that encodes semantics.

There are several reasons this Euclidean metric is incomplete as a preservation criterion. First, two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm but have substantially different pairwise angles among their neurons; small additive perturbations can change directions and thus alter semantic properties. Second, semantic preservation is more naturally expressed in angular terms (as argued above): the ability of a pretrained generative model to produce diverse, semantically coherent images is closely tied to how neurons are arranged on the hypersphere, not solely to their absolute vector positions. Third, because the Euclidean norm conflates magnitude and direction, it can overemphasize preserving magnitudes that may be less meaningful semantically while underemphasizing angular changes that have large semantic effect.

For these reasons, the paper advocates complementing or replacing a pure Euclidean closeness objective by a structural measure — hyperspherical energy — that directly captures the angular pairwise relationships one wishes to preserve during finetuning.

# Toy autoencoder study: magnitude vs angle information and semantic reconstruction

To make the role of angle versus magnitude concrete, the paper reports a toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During training the encoder–decoder used the ordinary inner product between convolutional kernels (neurons) and input patches to produce feature maps. At test time the authors compared three alternative ways of forming the feature map from a kernel $\bm{w}$ and a local input patch $\bm{x}$: (a) use the original inner product $\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}\rangle$ (the trained behavior), (b) use only the magnitude information $\|\bm{w}\|\|\bm{x}\|$ (i.e., discard angular information and keep magnitudes), and (c) use only angular information (i.e., use cosine similarity or normalize both vectors and take the inner product).

The reported outcome of this experiment is striking: using the angular information alone (c) almost perfectly recovers the input images, whereas retaining only the magnitude information (b) yields no useful reconstruction. Importantly, the network had been trained with inner products (a), not trained specifically on normalized/cosine features, yet the angle-only test reconstruction still retained semantics. This empirical observation illustrates the central claim that neuron directions contain most of the semantic content stored by the trained filters, while their magnitudes contribute little semantic information. The toy study therefore motivates focusing finetuning capacity on adjusting neuron directions (angles) rather than magnitudes if the goal is to alter semantic outputs without destroying pretrained knowledge.

# Invariance of hyperspherical energy under common orthogonal transformations

A core mathematical observation used by the paper is that hyperspherical energy is invariant when all neurons in a layer are subjected to the same orthogonal transformation. Let $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. Apply $\bm{R}$ to every column of a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0=[\bm{w}^0_1,\dots,\bm{w}^0_n]$ to obtain $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Orthogonality preserves vector norms and inner products:
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\| = \|\bm{w}^0_i\|,\qquad
\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i,\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\rangle = \langle \bm{w}^0_i,\bm{w}^0_j\rangle.
\]
Consequently, normalized neurons transform as $\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$, and pairwise Euclidean distances between normalized neurons are preserved:
\[
\|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}-\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j}\| \;=\; \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|
\;=\; \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\| \;=\; \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|.
\]
Because each pairwise distance is unchanged, every term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ in the hyperspherical energy sum remains identical after the orthogonal transform, hence
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) \;=\; \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
This invariance is the mathematical foundation for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposed in the paper: by restricting finetuning updates to layer-shared orthogonal transformations, one can change neuron directions in a controlled way while provably preserving the layer’s hyperspherical energy and therefore its angular relational structure.

The paper further notes that in the optimization viewpoint, minimizing the hyperspherical energy difference
\[
\big\| \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) - \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\big\|
\]
has an attainable minimum of zero whenever $\bm{W}$ differs from $\bm{W}^0$ by a global orthogonal factor, i.e., when $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Thus orthogonal updates constitute an exact solution to the hyperspherical-energy preservation objective.

# Intuition: Fourier magnitude vs phase analogy and semantic preservation

To provide further intuition for why angles are the right degree of freedom to manipulate while preserving semantics, the paper draws an analogy to the 2D Fourier transform of images. In Fourier decompositions, an image’s frequency representation splits into magnitude and phase spectra. Empirically it is well known that the phase (relative angles between the input and basis functions) carries the major portion of perceptual structure: replacing the magnitude spectrum with random values but keeping the original phase leads to an image that retains recognizable semantic content, whereas preserving the magnitude but scrambling phase destroys semantics.

Translating this analogy to neural weights and activations, the neuron directions (angles) play a role akin to the Fourier phase: they specify how inputs align to basis elements and thereby define semantic structure. The magnitudes of neurons are more similar to the Fourier magnitude spectrum — less crucial for identity of semantic content. This perspective reinforces the empirical toy-autoencoder finding and the theoretical preference for angular-preserving finetuning: if phase-like angular relations encode semantics, then finetuning should manipulate directions in a way that preserves global angular relationships unless deliberate semantic changes are intended. OFT operationalizes this by permitting orthogonal, layer-shared rotations of neuron directions — flexible enough to steer semantic outputs (by changing directions) but constrained to maintain the overall hyperspherical energy (thus preserving pretrained relational structure).

# Summary and implications for finetuning

Taken together, the geometric picture developed in the paper justifies why focusing on angular geometry and hyperspherical energy leads to a principled finetuning strategy. Angular similarity (cosine) aligns well with semantic measures observed in prior angular-margin work. Hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise normalized-neuron relations and hence quantifies a layer’s angular structure. Euclidean distance in parameter space, while useful as a generic regularizer, does not directly capture these relational properties and therefore can fail to prevent semantic degradation during finetuning. Empirical evidence from the toy autoencoder corroborates that directionality of neurons carries semantic information, while magnitudes contribute little. Finally, the invariance of hyperspherical energy under common orthogonal transformations provides a clean mathematical lever: by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms one can change neuron directions while guaranteeing the preservation of the layer-wise angular structure, which is the design principle underlying Orthogonal Finetuning. This geometric-semantic foundation explains both the motivation and the expected behavior of OFT in downstream text-to-image finetuning tasks.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Core Method and Intuition

# Formulation: W = R · W^0 with R orthogonal; forward pass change and identity initialization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) parametrizes the finetuned weight matrix of a layer as a left-multiplication of the pretrained weight matrix by a shared orthogonal matrix. Concretely, for a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\{\bm{w}^0_i\}_{i=1}^n$ are the neurons, OFT represents the finetuned weights as
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix satisfying
\[
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}.
\]
With this parametrization, the layer forward pass changes from the pretrained
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}
\]
to the OFT-forwarded
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the output vector of neuron responses.

Two implementation details are important. First, to ensure the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model (zero initial change), OFT initializes $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so that initially $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0$. Second, the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ is enforced during training using a differentiable parameterization (e.g., Cayley transform) or other orthogonalization strategies. Because a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be expensive when $d$ is large, OFT commonly uses a block-diagonal orthogonal $\bm{R}$ (with $r$ blocks) or shared blocks to reduce trainable parameters while preserving orthogonality within each block. This maintains the essential orthogonal property while keeping the finetuning parameter count manageable.

# Objective equivalence: minimizing hyperspherical energy difference to zero via orthogonal transforms

A central motivation for OFT is preserving the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, which the paper formalizes using hyperspherical energy. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ with column neurons $\{\bm{w}_i\}$, define the normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy (HE) of the layer is defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \big\| \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \big\|^{-1}.
\]
HE aggregates pairwise angular (hyperspherical) similarities among all neuron directions and therefore encodes the relational structure of neurons on the unit hypersphere.

The finetuning desideratum can be written as minimizing the difference between the HE of the finetuned layer and that of the pretrained layer:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \big\| \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) - \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \big\|.
\]
Because orthogonal left-multiplication preserves angles between vectors, any $\bm{W}$ of the form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. Thus the attainable minimum of the objective above is zero and is achieved exactly by these orthogonal transforms. In other words, enforcing $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ (with orthogonality on $\bm{R}$) is a principled way to preserve hyperspherical energy during adaptation. This equivalence gives OFT a provable guarantee: by construction, it maintains the layer-wise pairwise directional relationships among neurons that HE measures.

# Why OFT modifies directions (semantics) while preserving structural knowledge

Two linked ideas explain why OFT can change semantics without destroying pretrained structure. First, directional (angular) information in neuron weights carries most of the semantic content. The paper points to empirical observations and toy experiments showing that when convolutional neuron responses are recomputed using only angular information (ignoring magnitude), the network can still recover semantic content of inputs. This parallels how a phase spectrum in Fourier analysis preserves much of an image's structural semantics while magnitude affects contrast and intensity. Thus, changing neuron directions is a natural lever for modifying semantics in the network outputs.

Second, orthogonal left-multiplication rotates or reflects the whole set of neuron vectors in a layer in a coordinated way. This operation adjusts the directions of neurons (thus enabling semantic adaptation) but preserves pairwise angles between neurons and hence the relational structure learned during pretraining. Intuitively, OFT changes the coordinate frame in which the pretrained neurons are expressed, rather than independently rewriting each neuron. Because the pairwise angular geometry that encodes pretrained semantic relationships is preserved, OFT allows targeted semantic updates (via direction changes) while keeping the structural knowledge intact. This trade-off—directional flexibility with relational preservation—is the core reason OFT can adapt models for new tasks (subject-driven generation, adding control signals) without losing the general generative capabilities learned during pretraining.

The paper also notes a minor extension: re-scaled OFT augments the orthogonal transform with a diagonal positive scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ so that the forward pass becomes $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$. Rescaling neurons does not affect hyperspherical energy (because HE uses normalized neurons), so this extension increases flexibility without violating the HE preservation principle.

# Why OFT converges faster: optimization on a smooth hypersphere manifold; targeted semantic updates

There are two complementary reasons OFT tends to converge faster in practice. The first is that OFT focuses updates on neuron directions, which empirical evidence suggests are the most effective degrees of freedom for changing semantics. In the toy convolutional autoencoder experiments referenced in the paper, changing angular information of neurons sufficed to recover input structure, whereas modifying only magnitudes did not. Because OFT directly and efficiently adjusts directions, it performs the "most meaningful" kind of update for semantic adaptation.

The second reason is geometric: OFT constrains learning to a manifold of orthogonal transforms (equivalently constraining neuron directions through orthogonal rotations), which typically yields a smoother optimization landscape. Optimizing on structured manifolds (here the special orthogonal group or block-diagonal orthogonal groups) restricts the search space to transformations that respect the pretrained geometry, avoiding many pathological directions that can destabilize learning. The paper emphasizes that this constrained optimization behaves more stably and predictably than unconstrained finetuning or unconstrained additive updates, and it links to empirical results where OFT/COFT reach good controllability and subject preservation in fewer iterations and with smoother training dynamics than baseline finetuning methods (e.g., DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet).

Together, targeting the semantic-relevant subspace (directions) and constraining updates to an orthogonal manifold explain the observed faster convergence and improved finetuning stability of OFT.

# Why not minimize hyperspherical energy in finetuning (risk of destroying pretrained structure)

A superficially related alternative would be to drive hyperspherical energy toward some small value during finetuning (as prior work has done for training from scratch). However, the goals of finetuning a pretrained generative model and training a classifier from scratch differ. For classification, minimizing HE encourages neurons to spread uniformly on the hypersphere, reducing redundancy and improving separability. For finetuning a large pretrained generative model, forcing HE to decrease or to reach a new minimum can alter the intrinsic angular relationships that encode pretrained semantics, thereby destroying useful structure.

Therefore, OFT takes the opposite stance: instead of minimizing HE, it preserves HE exactly (up to numerical precision) by constraining updates to orthogonal transforms. Preserving HE keeps the pretrained neuron relational geometry intact, while still allowing coordinated directional changes that adapt generation behavior. This design choice—preserve rather than minimize HE—is pivotal to maintaining the pretrained model's semantic competence while enabling controlled adaptation.

# No inference overhead: folding R into W^0 after training

A practical advantage of OFT is that the orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights after finetuning, so there is no additional runtime cost or architectural change at inference. Concretely, after training has produced an orthogonal $\bm{R}$, one can compute the equivalent finetuned weight matrix
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0
\]
once and for all and then use the standard forward computations of the base model with $\bm{W}$ in place of $\bm{W}^0$. Because OFT does not require auxiliary modules or extra inputs at test time, the inference speed and memory usage match those of the original pretrained model. This contrasts with some adapter-style or conditional modules that must remain active during inference and therefore add cost. The paper highlights this deployment simplicity as a concrete benefit: OFT yields a deployable finetuned model without additional inference overhead.

Concluding summary

Orthogonal Finetuning is a principled finetuning strategy that enforces layer-wise orthogonal left-multiplication of pretrained weights to preserve hyperspherical energy. By doing so, OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (the relational geometry learned during pretraining) while enabling targeted directional changes that carry semantic effects. This design yields stable, fast-converging finetuning with no extra inference burden, and it forms the theoretical and practical backbone for the empirical gains reported in subject-driven and controllable text-to-image adaptation tasks.

Constrained and Re-Scaled Variants: COFT and Re-Scaled OFT

# COFT: adding a radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ to bound deviation from pretrained weights

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea with an explicit, tunable radius constraint that limits how far the learned orthogonal transformation may deviate from the identity. The OFT forward pass for a layer with pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ and learned orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ is
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
and OFT imposes $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ so that pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy) are preserved. COFT adds an additional constraint
\[
\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \le \epsilon,
\]
which explicitly bounds the operator (or chosen matrix) norm of the deviation between the finetuned and pretrained coordinate systems. The constraint is intuitive: it formalizes the commonly used finetuning heuristic of keeping the finetuned model "close" to the pretrained one, but does so in the orthogonal-transformation parameterization rather than by constraining the Euclidean difference of weights themselves.

Practically, COFT is initialized so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$; this corresponds to zero deviation and therefore exactly reproduces the pretrained network at the start of training. During optimization we update the orthogonal parameterization (described below) and project to satisfy the $\epsilon$ constraint so the learned transform never strays beyond the chosen radius. The primary purpose of COFT is to trade off flexibility and stability: a small $\epsilon$ enforces strong regularity and keeps the finetuned model behavior close to pretrained behavior, while a larger $\epsilon$ grants more capacity to adapt to the downstream task. Empirically, COFT yields improved finetuning stability compared to unconstrained OFT because the radius bound prevents large angular deviations that might otherwise degrade pretrained generative capabilities.

Because the constraint acts directly on the orthogonal transform, it simultaneously enforces a bound on angular changes across neurons while preserving the hyperspherical energy invariance that orthogonality guarantees. In other words, COFT enforces two complementary regularizations: (1) orthogonality, which preserves pairwise normalized directions, and (2) a controlled magnitude of rotation/reflection, which limits how much those directions can change in aggregate.

# Cayley transform and Neumann series insight: moving the $\epsilon$-constraint to a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ and using projected gradient

To maintain orthogonality during gradient-based optimization we need a differentiable and efficient parameterization of orthogonal matrices. COFT uses the Cayley parameterization: represent an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, i.e. $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. The Cayley map guarantees orthogonality and (as implemented here) produces matrices in the special orthogonal group with determinant $+1$; this practical restriction does not harm performance in the reported experiments.

The radius constraint $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$ is not directly a simple constraint in terms of $\bm{Q}$. However, for small $\bm{Q}$ one can use the Neumann series (an operator-series expansion) to approximate the Cayley transform. When $\bm{Q}$ has small operator norm, we may expand $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ as a series and obtain the first-order approximation
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]
Under this approximation the deviation satisfies approximately $\bm{R} - \bm{I} \approx 2\bm{Q}$, so controlling $\| \bm{Q} \|$ controls $\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \|$. The paper therefore replaces the inconvenient constraint on $\bm{R}$ by imposing a simpler constraint on the Cayley parameter: enforce
\[
\| \bm{Q} \| \le \epsilon',
\]
for some suitably chosen $\epsilon'$. In practice $\epsilon'$ is treated as its own hyperparameter rather than being a fixed analytic function of $\epsilon$, because the approximation involves higher-order terms and depends on the matrix norm used. Enforcing $\| \bm{Q} \| \le \epsilon'$ is straightforward with projected gradient descent: after each gradient step on $\bm{Q}$, if the norm exceeds $\epsilon'$ we project $\bm{Q}$ back to the admissible ball (for example by scaling $\bm{Q}$ to have operator norm $\epsilon'$).

Two operational benefits come from moving the constraint to $\bm{Q}$. First, $\bm{Q}$ is the direct optimization variable, so the projection is simple to implement. Second, initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ corresponds exactly to $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, providing an identity initialization and consistent zero-cost start for finetuning. Together, Cayley parameterization plus projected gradient on $\bm{Q}$ yields a computationally efficient way to realize orthogonality with a radius bound.

# Effect of $\epsilon$: flexibility–stability trade-off and behavior as $\epsilon$ varies

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ (or equivalently $\epsilon'$ on $\bm{Q}$) governs a fundamental trade-off in COFT between flexibility to fit the downstream task and stability in preserving pretrained generative abilities. At one extreme, when $\epsilon$ is very small (close to zero), COFT behaves almost identically to the original pretrained model: the orthogonal transform is forced to be near the identity and only tiny angular adjustments are permitted. This yields maximal stability and minimal risk of destroying pretrained semantics, but it also limits the model’s ability to specialize to new subjects or to incorporate new control signals.

At the other extreme, when $\epsilon$ is large (effectively not constraining $\bm{R}$ beyond orthogonality), COFT reduces to OFT. In that regime the model retains the full orthogonality invariance but can perform arbitrarily large rotations (within the orthogonal group), giving the greatest flexibility to adapt neuron directions. Empirically, the paper reports that OFT (unconstrained) already provides markedly more stability than naive finetuning, but COFT with an appropriately chosen intermediate $\epsilon$ often improves stability further and simplifies hyperparameter selection (e.g., learning rate and number of iterations).

Between these extremes the behavior interpolates smoothly: increasing $\epsilon$ increases the space of reachable orthogonal transforms and therefore the finetuning expressivity; decreasing $\epsilon$ shrinks that space and enforces stronger regularity. The choice of $\epsilon$ is task-dependent. If preserving the pretrained model's generality and diversity is paramount (for example when only a small number of subject images are available), a smaller $\epsilon$ is advisable. If the downstream task demands more aggressive adaptation (for instance, to follow a very specific control signal), a larger $\epsilon$ provides the required flexibility. Importantly, COFT makes this trade-off explicit and tunable, rather than relying on implicit small learning rates or ad hoc regularizers.

# Re-scaled OFT: learning a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ in $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$; preserving hyperspherical energy under re-scaling

Re-scaled OFT extends OFT by giving each neuron an independent, learnable positive scaling factor. The forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{D} = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries $s_i > 0$. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is still learned (via Cayley parameterization), and $\bm{D}$ provides per-neuron magnitude adjustments.

Why introduce these learned magnitudes? The original motivation for OFT is that directional (angular) information among neurons encodes the principal semantic structure. Orthogonal transforms preserve all pairwise angles and thus preserve hyperspherical energy: if we denote normalized neurons by $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, hyperspherical energy depends only on these normalized directions, so any orthogonal transformation leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged. Rescaling the neurons by positive scalars (i.e. multiplying columns of the weight matrix by $s_i$) also does not affect hyperspherical energy after normalization, because normalization removes magnitude. Equivalently, the pairwise cosine similarities among columns are invariant to positive per-column scaling after re-normalization. Therefore, learning $\bm{D}$ augments OFT's flexibility without violating the hyperspherical-energy preservation principle: the angular relationships remain the primary preserved structure.

From a practical standpoint $\bm{D}$ is lightweight: it adds only $n$ scalar parameters for a layer with $n$ neurons. Typically one initializes $\bm{D}$ to the identity (all $s_i=1$), so the starting network exactly matches the pretrained weights. During training the $s_i$ values can adaptively amplify or attenuate neuron outputs, which may accelerate convergence or help fit signals that require amplitude changes in particular channels. Empirically the paper notes that re-scaled OFT generally improves flexibility and can yield better results than plain OFT while adding a negligible number of parameters.

One other operational point: the scaling $\bm{D}$ can be fused into the weight matrix at inference by computing the product $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}$ and using $\bm{W}$ directly. Thus, like OFT, re-scaled OFT introduces no extra test-time computational overhead once finetuning is complete.

# Parameter cost and practical benefits of re-scaling

The parameter cost of re-scaled OFT is minimal. For a layer with $n$ output neurons, the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ requires $n$ additional scalar parameters. In typical modern architectures this is negligible compared to the parameter count of $\bm{R}$ or the underlying pretrained model: $\bm{R}$ (even when block-diagonal) dominates the trainable parameter budget and $\bm{W}^0$ itself usually has far more entries. Therefore, adding $\bm{D}$ is an inexpensive way to increase representational flexibility.

The practical benefits are also straightforward. Per-neuron scaling gives the finetuning process an extra degree of freedom to modulate the contribution of each neuron without disturbing angular structure. This can be especially useful when the downstream adaptation primarily requires adjusting relative strengths of pre-existing feature detectors rather than changing their directions. Because $\bm{D}$ interacts multiplicatively with the pretrained weights, it can implement a wide range of effective additive or multiplicative adjustments to layer behavior while still allowing orthogonal rotations to reshape directions when necessary.

As with OFT, the re-scaled variant maintains the important deployment property of zero inference overhead: after finetuning one can compute the final weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ and use it directly in place of $\bm{W}^0$, so the runtime and memory profile during generation are identical to the original pretrained model.

# Errata note regarding the placement of $\bm{D}$ in the forward pass

A technical erratum is documented in the paper: in the NeurIPS camera-ready version the re-scaled OFT forward pass was mistakenly printed as
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{D}\,\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
but the original implementation and the results reported in the appendices use the correct ordering
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
The difference matters because matrix multiplication is not commutative: placing $\bm{D}$ on the left would scale rows (input channels) rather than columns (neurons/output channels). The intended and implemented design scales the columns of $\bm{W}^0$, i.e. the per-neuron magnitudes, which matches the theoretical claim that rescaling columns does not change hyperspherical energy after normalization. The correct forward pass should therefore always be taken as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$, with $\bm{D}$ initialized to the identity and constrained to positive diagonal entries during optimization.

This erratum clarifies that the experimental findings attributed to re-scaled OFT reflect the $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ parameterization; the mistakenly printed $\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ in the camera-ready text does not correspond to the reported results.

# Concluding remarks on COFT and re-scaled OFT

COFT and re-scaled OFT extend the core OFT idea along two complementary axes. COFT introduces an explicit radius bound on orthogonal deviation to improve stability and make the flexibility–stability trade-off tunable and interpretable; it implements this bound efficiently by pushing the constraint into the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ and enforcing it via projection. Re-scaled OFT augments OFT with inexpensive per-neuron magnitude parameters $\bm{D}$ that boost representational flexibility while preserving the angular invariants that underlie OFT’s preservation of pretrained semantics. Both extensions retain OFT’s practical advantages—identity initialization, preservation of hyperspherical energy, and the ability to fold learned transforms into the base weights at inference for zero run-time overhead—while addressing distinct practical needs that arise in finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization and Complexity

# Cayley parameterization: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} with skew-symmetric Q; determinant +1 and practical impact

A central ingredient of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to represent layer-wise orthogonal transformations in a differentiable and computationally practical way. The paper adopts the Cayley parameterization, which constructs an orthogonal matrix \(
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
\) from a skew-symmetric matrix \(
\mathbf{Q}\) via
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}.
\]
This formula guarantees orthogonality: \(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\). Because the Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) to orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\), we can train the entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\) with standard gradient-based optimizers and obtain valid orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) at each step.

The Cayley parameterization has one structural consequence: it only represents orthogonal matrices with determinant \(+1\). In other words, \(\mathbf{R}\) produced by this transform belongs to the special orthogonal group and corresponds to rotations (not reflections). The paper notes this restriction explicitly and reports that, in practice, the determinant-\(+1\) limitation does not degrade finetuning performance. Thus the Cayley parameterization is a practical and effective choice: it enforces the orthogonality constraint by construction, is differentiable, and avoids the direct use of expensive orthogonalization procedures such as Gram–Schmidt during training.

The Cayley form also offers analytical convenience. Under a small-\(\mathbf{Q}\) assumption, one can expand \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a Neumann series to obtain the first-order approximation
\[
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2).
\]
The paper uses this expansion not as an alternative implementation but as an analytic tool: it allows mapping a small-deviation constraint on \(\mathbf{R}\) (used in Constrained OFT) into a constraint on \(\mathbf{Q}\), since \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\approx 2\mathbf{Q}\) when \(\mathbf{Q}\) is small and the Neumann series converges.

# Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices: r blocks, optional block sharing across channels

A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) has \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters (more precisely \(d(d-1)/2\) independent degrees of freedom). For large neuron dimension \(d\) this becomes costly, both in parameter count and in the cost of computing the Cayley inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\). The paper addresses scalability by restricting \(\mathbf{R}\) to a block-diagonal structure:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r),
\]
so that \(\mathbf{R}\in\text{O}(d)\) but consists of \(r\) independent orthogonal blocks of size \(d/r\). Each block \(\mathbf{R}_i\) can be Cayley-parameterized from a skew-symmetric block \(\mathbf{Q}_i\), and the Cayley inverse is applied block-wise. Block-diagonalization drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters while preserving orthogonality within each block.

Block sharing is an additional parameter-reduction option the paper highlights: set \(\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{R}_j\) for all blocks, i.e., use a single block repeated \(r\) times. This further reduces parameter count and introduces structural regularity across channel groups. The block-diagonal parameterization also admits natural interpretations for convolutional layers: choosing the number of blocks to match the number of input channels produces a per-channel orthogonal transform (akin to depthwise operations), whereas shared-block settings apply the same orthogonal transform across channels.

The block-diagonal choice is a user-controlled regularization knob. Fewer, larger blocks (small \(r\)) yield more expressive orthogonal transforms at higher parameter cost. More, smaller blocks (large \(r\)) give stronger regularization and lower parameter cost. As the blocks remain orthogonal, the hyperspherical energy preservation property of OFT still holds within the transformed subspaces.

# Parameter complexity: O(d^2/r) without sharing and O(d^2/r^2) with sharing

Counting independent parameters clarifies the efficiency gains from block-diagonalization and sharing. A full orthogonal \(d\times d\) matrix has \(d(d-1)/2\) free parameters, i.e., \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). For the \(r\)-block diagonal case where each block is size \(d/r\), each block contributes \((d/r)((d/r)-1)/2\) parameters. Summing over the \(r\) blocks gives the total number of parameters
\[
\text{params}_{\text{OFT}}(r)
= r\cdot \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2}
= \frac{d\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2},
\]
which scales asymptotically as \(\mathcal{O}\bigl(d^2 / r\bigr)\). Thus increasing \(r\) reduces parameter count approximately inversely with \(r\).

If blocks are shared (all blocks equal), then instead of \(r\) distinct blocks we have one block repeated \(r\) times. The parameter count then equals the number of parameters of a single block:
\[
\text{params}_{\text{shared}} = \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2} \approx \mathcal{O}\bigl(d^2 / r^2\bigr),
\]
so sharing yields an additional \(1/r\) factor reduction beyond block-diagonalization. The paper highlights these two practical regimes: unshared block-diagonal OFT with complexity \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\), and shared-block OFT with complexity \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\).

For comparison, LoRA parameterizes additive low-rank updates and uses \(r'\) as its rank hyperparameter. For a weight matrix of shape \(d\times n\), LoRA adds \(r'(d+n)\) new scalars. Thus LoRA parameter complexity grows linearly with \(d\) and \(n\) given a fixed rank \(r'\), whereas OFT's parameter counts scale quadratically with \(d\) but can be reduced by choosing larger \(r\) and block sharing.

# Concrete parameter-count example (e.g., 128×128 layer) and comparison to LoRA

To make the counts concrete, the paper gives a worked example for a square layer with \(d=n=128\). Take OFT with block number \(r=8\) (no block sharing). The number of OFT parameters becomes
\[
\text{params}_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{128\bigl(128/8 - 1\bigr)}{2}
= \frac{128(16 - 1)}{2}
= \frac{128\cdot 15}{2} = 960.
\]
LoRA with rank \(r'=8\) for the same \(128\times128\) weight matrix uses
\[
\text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d+n) = 8(128+128) = 8\cdot 256 = 2048.
\]
Thus in this example OFT uses fewer trainable parameters (960) than LoRA (2048) with comparable hyperparameter values. This concrete comparison demonstrates how OFT’s block-diagonal orthogonality can be parameter-efficient in practice. The paper also emphasizes that these counting formulas depend on chosen hyperparameters: reducing \(r\) makes OFT more expressive (and more expensive), while increasing \(r\) reduces its parameter cost and expressive power.

# Computational considerations: matrix inverse cost, differentiability, and scalability

Cayley parameterization requires computing \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) to obtain \(\mathbf{R}\). A naive inversion of a full \(d\times d\) matrix costs on the order of \(\mathcal{O}(d^3)\) for direct methods, and the cubic cost can be a bottleneck for very large \(d\). The paper acknowledges this practical limitation: while Cayley is differentiable and enforces orthogonality, the matrix inverse can limit scalability.

Block-diagonalization directly mitigates the inversion cost: with \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r\), the inversion cost becomes \(r\) times the cost of inverting each block. A simple cost model yields blockwise inversion arithmetic cost scaling like
\[
r\cdot \mathcal{O}\bigl((d/r)^3\bigr) = \mathcal{O}\bigl(d^3 / r^2\bigr).
\]
Thus block-diagonalization reduces the inversion cost by a factor of approximately \(r^2\) compared to the full-matrix inverse, making Cayley practical for moderate-sized blocks. Block sharing does not change the per-block inversion cost, but sharing reduces the number of unique block parameters that need to be stored and updated.

Differentiability is preserved: the Cayley map is a differentiable function of \(\mathbf{Q}\) wherever \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) is invertible, so standard autodifferentiation frameworks can compute gradients through the matrix inverse. The paper contrasts Cayley with direct orthogonalization schemes (for example, Gram–Schmidt), observing that those methods can be differentiable but are often too expensive; Cayley gives an efficient differentiable alternative.

Scalability trade-offs are explicit in the design: choose \(r\) large enough to keep block sizes manageable for inversion, but not so large as to over-regularize the transformation and remove useful finetuning flexibility. The paper reports that the block-diagonal approach keeps OFT practical for realistic diffusion model layer sizes and that the determinant-\(+1\) restriction of Cayley did not harm empirical results.

Finally, the paper notes that Constrained OFT (COFT) adds a small extra computation: projecting \(\mathbf{Q}\) to satisfy a norm constraint (to keep \(\lVert\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\rVert \le \epsilon\) effectively mapped to \(\lVert \mathbf{Q}\rVert \le \epsilon'\) via the Neumann approximation). That projection is inexpensive (a norm clipping or projection) compared to matrix inversion and is done in \(\mathbf{Q}\)-space rather than modifying the Cayley inverse directly.

# Implementation checklist: initialization (R=I via Q=0), preserving orthogonality during training

Practical implementation of OFT / COFT follows a concise set of steps grounded in the paper’s design choices.

1. Choose block configuration and sharing policy. Decide on block count \(r\) and whether to share blocks across groups. This trading parameter controls expressiveness versus parameter/inversion cost.

2. Parameterize each orthogonal block via Cayley. For each block, maintain a skew-symmetric trainable matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) and compute the block orthogonal transform as \(\mathbf{R}_i = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}_i)(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1}\). Assemble \(\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r)\).

3. Initialize to identity. Set \(\mathbf{Q}_i=\mathbf{0}\) for all blocks at initialization. This yields \(\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{I}\) and ensures the finetuned model starts from the pretrained weights exactly (zero-change initialization).

4. Use the OFT forward pass. Replace the original layer forward computation \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\) with \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\). During training, only the \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) (and optionally diagonal rescaling \(\mathbf{D}\) in the re-scaled variant) are updated.

5. Enforce skew-symmetry and orthogonality. Maintain each \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) skew-symmetric (the Cayley map requires this). Compute \(\mathbf{R}_i\) via the Cayley formula; orthogonality is then enforced by construction. (The paper points to differential orthogonalization strategies in the literature as options, but Cayley is the chosen practical route.)

6. For COFT, enforce the deviation constraint. If a constrained variant is desired, map the desired constraint \(\lVert \mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\rVert \le \epsilon\) to a constraint on \(\mathbf{Q}\) using the Neumann-series approximation \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\approx 2\mathbf{Q}\), yielding \(\lVert \mathbf{Q}\rVert\le\epsilon'\). Enforce this via a simple projected gradient step (clip or project \(\mathbf{Q}\) back into the feasible norm ball after each update).

7. Training and inference. Train with standard optimizers on the task loss while updating the \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) parameters (and \(\mathbf{D}\) if re-scaled OFT is used). At inference one may precompute \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) and use the composed weight matrix directly, so OFT introduces no additional runtime overhead compared to the pretrained model.

These steps follow the paper’s design choices: initialize with identity (\(\mathbf{Q}=0\)), use Cayley to guarantee orthogonality, reduce parameter and computation costs by block-diagonalization and optional sharing, and, for COFT, constrain \(\mathbf{Q}\) via projection to stabilize finetuning.

*Summary.* The combination of Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal structure yields a practical, differentiable, and parameter-efficient implementation of OFT. The Cayley map enforces orthogonality by construction and limits orthogonal transforms to determinant \(+1\), a restriction the paper finds harmless in practice. Block-diagonalization and block sharing allow the designer to trade expressivity for parameter and computational efficiency, with parameter complexity scaling as \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) without sharing and \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) with sharing. A concrete 128×128 example shows OFT can use substantially fewer parameters than LoRA for comparable hyperparameter choices, and the implementation checklist above summarizes the practical choices required to train OFT and COFT reliably.

Applying OFT Across Architectures and Pipelines

# Where to finetune: attention layers in diffusion UNet for fair comparison to LoRA

When adapting Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) to a large text-to-image diffusion model, a practical and experimentally justified place to apply OFT is the attention weights inside the diffusion UNet. In the paper’s empirical comparisons with LoRA (a widely used parameter-efficient finetuning method), the authors applied OFT to the same attention layer(s) where LoRA was applied, so that comparisons would be fair and differences could be attributed to the finetuning parameterization rather than to which parts of the network were changed. Therefore, if you want a like-for-like evaluation against LoRA, start by applying OFT to the same transformer attention matrices used by LoRA in the UNet.

There is no architectural restriction in principle: OFT is model-agnostic and can be applied to any linear / weight matrix inside the model. However, attention layers are a natural first target because they are commonly adapted by other parameter-efficient methods and because they capture global interactions that are important for text-to-image conditioning. Applying OFT to attention weights also yields a clean experimental setup: initialize the orthogonal transform to identity so the finetuned model begins exactly at the pretrained weights, and optimize only the OFT parameters for the selected attention layers while holding the rest of the model fixed.

# Convolutional layers: interpreting block-diagonal R as depth-wise or shared channel transforms

OFT can be applied to convolutional layers as well as to fully connected or attention matrices. The paper emphasizes that the block-diagonal structure chosen for the orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) has useful interpretations when applied to convolutional kernels. Concretely, OFT parameterizes \(\bm{R}\) as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix:

\[
\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r)
\quad\text{with}\quad
\bm{R}_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)
\]

where \(d\) is the input dimension for the weight matrix and \(r\) is the number of blocks. Two limiting interpretations are particularly useful for convolutional layers:

- If you set the number of blocks \(r\) equal to the number of input channels, then each block \(\bm{R}_i\) acts on a single channel’s neurons. This is analogous to a depth-wise per-channel transform: each channel is rotated (orthogonally transformed) independently. This interpretation connects directly to depth-wise convolution concepts and yields a strong sparsity / per-channel regularization.

- If you share the same block across channels (i.e., \(\bm{R}_1=\bm{R}_2=\cdots=\bm{R}_r\)), then the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels. This corresponds to a channel-shared orthogonal change of basis and is a stronger regularization that reduces the number of trainable parameters further.

The trade-off is clear: more blocks (larger \(r\)) give finer-grained per-subspace flexibility but reduce the number of parameters per block and increase the structural bias; fewer blocks (smaller \(r\), down to \(r=1\)) make \(\bm{R}\) more expressive at the cost of more parameters. The block-diagonal choice therefore provides a practical knob that maps naturally to convolutional channel structure and allows you to trade flexibility for parameter efficiency.

# Integration into existing setups: DreamBooth for subject-driven, ControlNet/T2I-Adapter-style training for controllable generation

OFT integrates into standard finetuning pipelines with minimal changes to training code. Two concrete examples described in the paper are subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style) and controllable generation (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter-style).

- Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth): DreamBooth finetunes a pretrained diffusion model on a few images of a subject (with a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior-preservation loss). OFT can be dropped into the DreamBooth framework by replacing the usual weight updates on selected layers with the OFT parameterization. Practically, keep the same loss functions and optimization schedule used by DreamBooth, but change the forward pass for the targeted layers from \((\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\) to \((\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}\) (with \(\bm{R}\) initialized to identity). This preserves the DreamBooth training logic while enforcing hyperspherical-energy preservation via orthogonality. The paper shows OFT and its constrained variant (COFT) achieve faster, more stable convergence and better subject preservation than baseline DreamBooth finetuning or LoRA in this setting.

- Controllable generation (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter style): For adapting a diffusion model to accept additional control signals such as edges or segmentation maps, OFT can be used where ControlNet or an adapter would normally be attached. Unlike ControlNet (which introduces extra network branches that add inference overhead), OFT simply modifies selected pretrained weights by left-multiplying them with \(\bm{R}\); at inference time you can precompute \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) and use the model with the same architecture and the same computational cost as the original pretrained model. In the experiments, OFT and COFT were applied within the same training setups as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, and they required fewer parameters or less data while converging faster and producing stronger controllability.

In both integration cases, OFT preserves the original loss, conditioning, and sampling pipelines; it only changes the internal parameterization of the finetuned weights for the chosen layers.

# Hyperparameters: choosing r (blocks), block sharing, learning rate, optimizer, and \(\epsilon\) for COFT

Tuning OFT centers on a few structural and optimization hyperparameters that control flexibility, parameter cost, and stability.

- The block count \(r\): This determines the block-diagonal partition of \(\bm{R}\). Smaller \(r\) (e.g., \(r=1\)) makes \(\bm{R}\) a full orthogonal matrix and yields the greatest flexibility (and the largest number of OFT parameters). Larger \(r\) splits \(\bm{R}\) into more, smaller orthogonal blocks; this reduces the number of trainable parameters and increases regularization. The paper gives parameter-complexity expressions: a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix has \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) parameters (order \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\)), while an \(r\)-block diagonal orthogonal matrix has about \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) parameters. Choose \(r\) to balance expressivity and parameter budget: increase \(r\) to save parameters, decrease \(r\) to get more capacity.

- Block sharing: You can optionally share block matrices across blocks, i.e., set \(\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j\) for all \(i,j\). This further reduces parameters; the paper points out that sharing all blocks reduces the parameter complexity roughly to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) under a certain reporting convention. Block sharing is useful when you want a strong inductive bias and very few parameters.

- Learning rate and optimizer: The paper shows that OFT and COFT are less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count than naive finetuning; COFT particularly makes learning-rate and iteration choices “effortless” when \(\epsilon\) is set appropriately. The paper does not prescribe a single optimizer; what matters algorithmically is that you optimize the OFT parameterization (the Cayley/skew-symmetric parameter \(\bm{Q}\) or block matrices) with a standard gradient-based optimizer and, in the case of COFT, apply projection to enforce the deviation constraint. In practice, use the optimizer you would normally use for finetuning (e.g., an adaptive optimizer) but be aware that the training dynamics are generally smoother than for standard finetuning.

- COFT deviation bound \(\epsilon\): Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds a radius constraint to keep the orthogonal matrix near the identity:

\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon.
\]

The paper analyzes the Cayley parameterization \(\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) where \(\bm{Q}\) is skew-symmetric (\(\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}\)), and under small-\(\bm{Q}\) assumptions uses the Neumann-series approximation \(\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)\). That motivates enforcing a simpler constraint \(\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\) and applying projected gradient descent on \(\bm{Q}\) to respect the bound. Intuition for tuning \(\epsilon\): larger \(\epsilon\) makes COFT behave more like unconstrained OFT (more flexibility); smaller \(\epsilon\) keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained one (more conservative). The paper empirically shows \(\epsilon\) controls the trade-off between flexibility and stability.

- Re-scaled OFT: If you want modest extra flexibility without many additional parameters, consider the re-scaled OFT forward pass used in the experiments:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]

where \(\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)\) is a positive diagonal matrix learned alongside \(\bm{R}\). This preserves hyperspherical energy (angles) while allowing per-neuron magnitude changes and typically improves flexibility at negligible parameter cost.

Practical tuning recipe from the paper’s findings: if you want a parameter-efficient but powerful configuration, start with a moderate block count (e.g., choose \(r\) so that block size is a small multiple of typical channel sizes), consider block sharing if extreme parameter savings are necessary, initialize \(\bm{Q}=0\) (so \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\)), use COFT with a modest \(\epsilon\) to stabilize training, and increase flexibility (reduce \(r\), increase \(\epsilon\), or add \(\bm{D}\)) if control or subject preservation is inadequate.

# Stability tips: identity initialization, monitoring for collapse, selecting iteration counts

OFT includes several built-in practices that enhance stability; follow these concrete tips when training:

- Identity initialization: initialize the orthogonal transform to the identity. Practically this means initializing the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter \(\bm{Q}\) to the zero matrix so that the Cayley parameterization produces \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) at the start. Identity initialization guarantees that you begin exactly at the pretrained model, avoiding sudden distributional shifts that can destabilize finetuning.

- Enforce orthogonality: use the Cayley parameterization \(\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) where \(\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}\) to ensure \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal during optimization. This orthogonality is the mechanism that provably preserves hyperspherical energy and helps prevent destructive changes to learned semantics.

- Use COFT for extra safety: if you see instability or worry about overfitting / model collapse (as sometimes appears with unconstrained finetuning methods), turn on COFT by constraining \(\|\bm{Q}\|\) (or equivalently \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\)) with a small \(\epsilon\). The paper reports that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT because it explicitly limits deviation from the pretrained model.

- Monitor for collapse and performance metrics: during finetuning, regularly sample generated images and compute the same automatic metrics used in the paper to detect degradation or collapse. For subject-driven tasks monitor subject fidelity metrics such as DINO and CLIP-based image similarity (CLIP-I), prompt fidelity (CLIP-T), and diversity (LPIPS). For controllable tasks track control consistency metrics (for example IoU / F1 for edge-based control, mean IoU and accuracies for segmentation control, and mean \(\ell_2\) landmark distance for pose control). A sudden drop in these metrics or clearly collapsed images is an early warning to intervene.

- Iteration counts: OFT and especially COFT are less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning iterations than naive finetuning. The paper finds OFT converges faster and remains stable across a wide range of iterations, whereas other methods can suddenly collapse if you train too long. With COFT you can often pick a relatively large iteration limit without complex tuning; nevertheless, it is prudent to use validation samples and the above metrics to pick a practical stopping point.

- If training becomes unstable: check that \(\bm{Q}\) is kept reasonably small (project back to the \(\epsilon'\)-ball if using COFT), ensure numerical stability of the Cayley inverse \((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\), and consider increasing the number of blocks \(r\) (to add regularization) or reducing learning rate as a last resort.

# Compositionality potential: multiplying orthogonal matrices from different finetunings

One intriguing structural property of OFT is compositionality at the level of orthogonal transforms. Orthogonal matrices form a group under matrix multiplication: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Consequently, if you perform multiple independent OFT finetuning runs on the same base weights but for different downstream tasks, you obtain a sequence of orthogonal transforms \(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots\). Multiplying these gives another orthogonal transform \(\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}=\bm{R}_k\cdots\bm{R}_2\bm{R}_1\), and applying \(\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}\) to the pretrained weights yields a single set of weights that is syntactically an OFT transform of the base model.

This algebraic composability suggests the potential for compactly composing task-specific finetunings: rather than storing many separate finetuned full-weight matrices, you could store only the orthogonal transforms and multiply them as needed. However, the paper frames this as an open problem: whether such compositions preserve the knowledge and guarantees of each individual downstream finetuning (for example subject identity plus a control modality) remains to be studied. The group property guarantees the composed matrix is orthogonal, and thus hyperspherical energy is preserved, but whether the composed transform semantically combines the downstream behaviors without interference is not proven and is left as future work.

# Failure modes and troubleshooting

Understanding common failure modes helps you diagnose problems quickly and select corrective actions.

- Removing orthogonality: if you relax or remove the orthogonality constraint (i.e., allow unconstrained updates to \(\bm{R}\) parameters or directly update weights without orthogonality), the paper demonstrates that the optimization can fail dramatically. In a controllable generation experiment the authors show that OFT without the orthogonality constraint failed to produce realistic images or achieve control. If you observe such collapse, reintroduce the Cayley parameterization / orthogonality constraint or revert to COFT.

- Block choice and expressivity trade-offs: choosing a very large \(r\) (many small blocks) imposes a strong inductive bias and may underfit the downstream task—control or subject identity may not be achievable because the per-block transforms are too small in dimension. Conversely, choosing \(r\) too small (very expressive \(\bm{R}\)) increases parameter cost and can risk overfitting or make numerical operations heavier. Remedy: if control is weak, decrease \(r\) (fewer blocks) or add the re-scaling diagonal \(\bm{D}\); if you see instability, increase \(r\) or enable COFT.

- Numerical issues with the Cayley transform: the Cayley parameterization produces \(\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\). If \(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\) becomes poorly conditioned or nearly singular, numerical instability can occur. In COFT you typically bound \(\|\bm{Q}\|\) with projected steps which prevents \(\bm{Q}\) from growing too large and helps keep \(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\) well-conditioned. If numerical instability appears, reduce learning rate, shrink the \(\bm{Q}\) projection radius, or increase block count \(r\) so block inverses are smaller and cheaper.

- Overly large \(\epsilon\) in COFT: a too-large deviation bound makes COFT behave like unconstrained OFT, which can be desirable for flexibility but may reduce the conservatism you rely on to prevent catastrophic changes. If you detect semantic degradation due to large deviation, reduce \(\epsilon\).

- Block-diagonal bias: the block-diagonal parameterization trades off bias and efficiency. If you see systematic artifacts that look like channel-wise inconsistencies or repetitive patterns, it may be caused by inadequately expressive block structure. Try decreasing \(r\) (bigger blocks), removing block sharing, or enabling re-scaled OFT (\(\bm{D}\)).

- Insufficient control fidelity: if the model does not respect control signals, consider adding more OFT capacity (reduce \(r\), add \(\bm{D}\)), increasing the number of finetuning examples, or training slightly longer—note that OFT typically converges faster than baselines, but some tasks may require more capacity or data.

Finally, remember to inspect both automatic metrics (CLIP, DINO, LPIPS, IoU / F1 / mIoU, landmark \(\ell_2\) as appropriate) and qualitative samples to detect failures that metrics miss. The paper’s empirical results show OFT/COFT converge more stably and quickly than alternative finetuning approaches, but sensible monitoring and conservative initial hyperparameter choices (identity initialization, small initial \(\epsilon\) for COFT) are still prudent.

Experiments I: Subject-Driven Generation

# Setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5, DreamBooth loss, baselines (DreamBooth, LoRA), fair layer selection

The subject-driven generation experiments evaluate how well Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that the model can generate images of a specific subject given only a few reference images plus a text prompt. All experiments in this chapter use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained backbone. To make comparisons meaningful, every finetuning method in the study adopts the same DreamBooth training objective: the reconstruction loss on subject images combined with a class-specific prior-preservation loss that prevents overfitting the model to the few subject examples (this is the DreamBooth framework described in the paper).

The baseline methods are the original DreamBooth finetuning procedure (naïve weight updates from the pretrained model with small learning rate) and LoRA, which is a popular parameter-efficient adaptation method that adds a low-rank additive update to certain weight matrices. To ensure a fair comparison between OFT/COFT and LoRA, OFT and COFT are applied to precisely the same layer(s) where LoRA is applied in the baselines. This isolates the effect of the adaptation parameterization (orthogonal multiplicative transforms versus low-rank additive updates) from other differences such as which parts of the network are adapted.

Practically, OFT/COFT begin from the pretrained weights and initialize the orthogonal transform to the identity so training starts exactly at the pretrained model. COFT adds an explicit constraint that the orthogonal transform remain within a small neighborhood of the identity (formally \|R - I\| \le \epsilon for the learned layer-shared orthogonal matrix R), giving a knob that controls how far the finetuned model may drift from the pretrained weights. Both OFT and COFT are trained with the same DreamBooth objective and the same optimization choices as the baselines, except that only the orthogonal parameters (and any small auxiliary parameters) are updated.

# Convergence and stability: early (≈400 iters) vs late (≈2000 iters) behavior; collapse avoidance

Two empirical properties are critical in subject-driven finetuning: (1) how quickly a method converges to good subject control and prompt adherence, and (2) whether the method remains stable when training continues for more iterations. The paper reports behavior at early and late checkpoints to highlight these properties.

At early training (around 400 iterations in the reported runs), both OFT and COFT achieve visible and reliable control of the subject: the generated images begin to reflect the target subject identity while also following the text prompt. In contrast, LoRA often fails to preserve the subject identity at this stage; the finetuned model may already deviate away from the subject characteristics present in the reference images. The standard DreamBooth finetuning shows some preservation early on but its stability is more fragile.

At later training (around 2000 iterations), differences become dramatic. The paper reports that standard DreamBooth finetuning can start to generate collapsed images after prolonged training: outputs lose image quality and coherence. LoRA similarly shows failures at longer training: for example, intended clothing colors or materials may be replaced by incorrect textures (the paper gives the example of a requested yellow shirt becoming yellow fur). By contrast, OFT and COFT maintain stable generation even at 2000 iterations — they preserve subject identity and continue to follow prompts without collapsing. This demonstrates two important advantages of OFT/COFT: faster convergence to good control and robustness against collapse when training longer than strictly necessary.

A practical implication emphasized by the authors is iteration insensitivity. Because OFT and COFT preserve the pretrained layer-wise hyperspherical structure (angles between neurons) and restrict drift (COFT explicitly), they are forgiving with respect to the number of training iterations. Users can choose a relatively large iteration budget without careful tuning and still obtain stable behavior, whereas DreamBooth and LoRA require much more delicate tuning of how long to train.

# Quantitative metrics: DINO and CLIP-I (subject fidelity), CLIP-T (prompt fidelity), LPIPS (diversity)

Quantitative evaluation in subject-driven generation requires metrics that measure different aspects of the task: whether the generated images retain the subject identity, whether they match the text prompt, and whether the method still produces diverse samples. The paper uses the following automatic metrics.

DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity. DINO here refers to embeddings from a DINO ViT S/16 model; CLIP-I denotes image embeddings from CLIP. For both metrics, the paper computes the average pairwise cosine similarity between generated images and the real subject images. Given two vectors a and b, cosine similarity is

\[
\text{cosine}(a,b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|\|b\|}.
\]

Higher average cosine similarity indicates the generated images are closer to the reference subject images in the respective embedding space, which is interpreted as better subject fidelity.

CLIP-T for prompt fidelity. CLIP-T is the cosine similarity between the text prompt embedding and the embedding of the generated image (using the CLIP text encoder and image encoder). Higher CLIP-T means the generated images align better to the semantic content of the prompt.

LPIPS for sample diversity. The Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) is a perceptual distance metric that measures how different two images look to a convolutional neural network. In the experiments the authors compute average LPIPS (or LPIPS-based cosine similarity as stated) between generated images of the same subject with the same prompt. Larger LPIPS indicates greater perceptual diversity among samples, while very low LPIPS would mean the sampler is producing nearly identical outputs and thus low diversity.

By combining these metrics we obtain a multi-faceted picture: DINO and CLIP-I measure whether identity is preserved, CLIP-T whether prompt instructions are followed, and LPIPS whether finetuning has collapsed diversity.

# Multiple-seed evaluation to reduce randomness

Stochastic elements in sampling and finetuning (e.g., random initialization of added parameters, data ordering, sampling stochasticity) can result in significant variability in measured outcomes. To obtain robust comparisons, the paper repeats each finetuning experiment with the same pretrained model across multiple random seeds. Concretely, for the subject-driven experiments each method is finetuned with 30 different random seeds. Reported averages and comparisons are computed over these repeated runs to reduce the influence of chance.

This multiple-seed protocol serves several purposes. First, it shows whether a method is consistently stable or whether only a minority of seeds produce good results. Second, averaging increases confidence that differences between methods are not simply due to particular random draws. The paper reports that across 30 seeds OFT and COFT consistently outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in the key subject-fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I) and achieve at least comparable prompt fidelity and diversity.

# Key findings: superior subject preservation and prompt adherence; iteration insensitivity

From the experimental suite focused on subject-driven generation, the key findings are the following.

First, OFT and COFT provide stronger subject preservation than both DreamBooth and LoRA as measured by embedding-based fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I). The preservation holds across multiple random seeds, indicating that OFT/COFT are robust and repeatable.

Second, OFT and COFT maintain or slightly improve prompt adherence (CLIP-T) relative to baselines. In other words, these methods do not trade off the ability to follow the text prompt for the sake of preserving identity; they improve both aspects simultaneously.

Third, OFT (and especially COFT) exhibit fast convergence. Good subject-specific generations are obtained early (≈400 iterations), which makes them more practical in low-data or limited-compute settings.

Fourth, OFT and COFT are insensitive to the total number of training iterations: they do not require the careful stopping rules that standard DreamBooth or LoRA require to avoid collapse. This property is primarily attributed to the OFT principle of preserving hyperspherical energy (pairwise neuron angles) and COFT's additional radius constraint that explicitly bounds deviation from the pretrained weights.

Finally, COFT's deviation hyperparameter \epsilon provides a practical stability-control trade-off: smaller \epsilon makes the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained model (more conservative updates), while larger \epsilon grants flexibility akin to unconstrained OFT. This gives practitioners a convenient way to make finetuning more or less aggressive.

# Qualitative analysis: identity retention vs prompt following; representative successes and failures

Quantitative metrics are informative but incomplete; visual inspection is essential for subject-driven generation. The paper reports qualitative comparisons where images are randomly sampled from each finetuned model (using the same underlying finetuned checkpoint per method). These examples illustrate failure modes and successes that metrics may not fully capture.

Representative successes for OFT/COFT include images that both clearly depict the target subject and follow the text prompt (background, clothing, posture, or other prompt-specified properties). OFT and COFT tend to produce coherent, photorealistic images where the subject's identifying features (face, body shape, textures specific to the subject) remain recognizable across different prompt contexts.

Representative failures for baselines expose their weaknesses. LoRA sometimes loses the subject identity entirely in examples where OFT/COFT succeed — the paper cites an illustrative "bowl" example where LoRA-generated outputs do not preserve the subject identity. DreamBooth, while better at identity retention than LoRA in some cases, can fail to incorporate the prompt correctly: e.g., the model preserves identity but does not render the prompt-specified attributes accurately. Additionally, both DreamBooth and LoRA are sensitive to iteration count; with too many iterations they can collapse or produce semantically wrong textures or artifacts (yellow shirt turning into yellow fur is given as an example).

Qualitative inspection also confirms the iteration-insensitivity claim: OFT/COFT produce stable outputs across early and late training checkpoints, whereas baseline outputs degrade or change unpredictably as training continues. This stability makes OFT/COFT more user-friendly because they remove the need to find a narrow stopping window.

# Human evaluation summary and insights

Beyond automated metrics and qualitative examples, the authors conducted a human evaluation reported in the appendix to further validate the superiority of OFT. While the main paper does not reproduce detailed human-study numbers in the body, it states that human judgments corroborate the automatic metrics: raters prefer OFT-finetuned images for subject fidelity and overall quality compared to baselines. This human validation is important because perceptual aspects of identity and prompt adherence can be subtle and not always fully captured by embedding-based metrics.

The human evaluation supports the technical explanation behind OFT/COFT: by preserving pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) and restricting drift (COFT), these methods maintain the pretrained model’s generalization and semantics while enabling targeted adaptation. In practical terms, this translates to images that humans judge to be more faithful to the subject and better aligned with the prompts.

# Takeaways

The subject-driven generation experiments make a clear empirical case for Orthogonal Finetuning and its constrained variant. By focusing adaptation on layer-shared orthogonal transforms that preserve hyperspherical energy, OFT/COFT achieve faster convergence, stronger and more consistent subject identity preservation, and greater robustness to overtraining than both naive DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA. The constrained version, COFT, adds an explicit radius constraint that further stabilizes training and reduces sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate and iteration count. Quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS), multi-seed evaluation, qualitative examples, and human judgments together paint a consistent picture: OFT/COFT are effective, efficient, and practical choices for subject-driven finetuning of large text-to-image diffusion models.

Experiments II: Controllable Generation, Metrics, and Practical Guidance

# Tasks and datasets: Canny→Image (COCO), Segmentation→Image (ADE20K), Landmarks→Face (CelebA-HQ)

This work evaluates controllable generation on three concrete tasks that represent different kinds of control signals and visual domains. The first is Canny edge to image (C2I) using the COCO dataset. Here the control input is a Canny edge map derived from a natural photograph, and the task is to generate a photorealistic image whose edge structure matches the input while also satisfying a text prompt. The second task is Segmentation map to image (S2I) on ADE20K. The control there is a semantic segmentation map (pixel-wise class labels), and the objective is to generate an image with correct object classes, layout, and appearance corresponding to that segmentation. The third task is Landmark to face (L2F) on CelebA-HQ. In L2F the control signal is a set of facial landmarks describing pose and expression; the model must generate a face image that meets those landmark constraints while producing high-fidelity facial appearance.

These three tasks were chosen to cover a range of control granularities (sparse geometric cues in landmarks, mid-level structural cues in edges, and dense semantic cues in segmentation) and a range of datasets from general scenes (COCO, ADE20K) to the constrained face domain (CelebA-HQ). All methods in the comparison finetune the same pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 model for 20 epochs on each dataset, using the task-specific control inputs alongside text prompts. This unified setup allows a direct comparison of convergence behavior, final controllability, and sample fidelity across methods.

# Control consistency metrics: edge IoU/F1, segmentation mIoU/mean/overall accuracy, landmark L2 error

To quantify how well generated images adhere to their control inputs, the paper introduces a control consistency evaluation methodology: reconstruct the control signal from the generated image using an off-the-shelf estimator, and compare the reconstructed control to the original input. This gives a task-appropriate, interpretable metric for controllable generation rather than relying only on perceptual or text-alignment scores.

For the C2I task (Canny edges), the reconstructed control is an edge map estimated from the generated image; the comparison metrics are intersection-over-union (IoU) and F1 score between predicted and input edge maps. These metrics measure whether generated edges occur in the correct places and with correct coverage.

For S2I (segmentation maps), the evaluation uses standard semantic segmentation metrics: mean IoU (mIoU) across classes, mean class accuracy, and overall pixel accuracy. An off-the-shelf segmentation model produces the predicted segmentation from the generated image; these metrics then indicate whether objects and scene regions appear with the right semantic identity and spatial extent.

For L2F (landmarks to face), the control is a set of facial landmark coordinates. From a generated image we run a landmark detector and compute the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the detected landmarks and the original control landmarks. Lower mean $\ell_2$ error indicates more accurate geometric compliance to the requested pose and facial configuration.

Beyond being intuitively meaningful, these control consistency metrics have the practical advantage of being computable automatically during training and validation, so they can be used to monitor convergence and to select checkpoints that best trade off fidelity and controllability.

# Convergence comparisons: smooth, fast convergence of OFT/COFT vs sudden emergence in ControlNet

One of the most salient empirical findings is that orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) converge both faster and more smoothly than competing finetuning approaches such as ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. Concretely, in the L2F experiment the mean landmark $\ell_2$ error produced by OFT/COFT declines steadily across epochs and reaches strong accuracy far earlier: OFT/COFT attain the level that LoRA reaches at epoch 20 as early as epoch 8. This illustrates a substantially faster convergence in terms of epochs.

The dynamics of ControlNet are qualitatively different: controllability often “suddenly” appears after a specific epoch (the paper reports a sudden emergence around epoch 8 in some runs). That kind of abrupt transition makes hyperparameter selection and checkpointing more brittle—there is a risk of either stopping too early (before the control effect emerges) or continuing too long (risking overfitting or collapse). By contrast, OFT and COFT exhibit smooth, stable training curves where improvements are gradual and predictable. This smooth behavior simplifies practical use: fewer runs are needed to find good hyperparameters, and setting a larger number of iterations is safer because OFT/COFT are less sensitive to iteration count.

Data efficiency is also tied to convergence. The experiments show OFT can reach strong controllability using a fraction of the data used by other methods. For example, OFT can converge well with only 5% of the ADE20K training set in the segmentation-to-image task, indicating that the orthogonal transformation provides an inductive bias that reduces sample requirements.

# Results summary: OFT/COFT outperform ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA; data and parameter efficiency

Across the three control tasks, OFT and COFT consistently outperform the baselines on the control consistency metrics. In C2I, OFT/COFT achieve higher edge IoU and F1; in S2I they obtain superior mIoU, mean class accuracy, and overall accuracy; and in L2F they achieve lower mean landmark $\ell_2$ error. These quantitative gains are supported by qualitative examples where OFT/COFT generate images that both look realistic and adhere closely to control inputs.

Two efficiency aspects are particularly notable. First, parameter efficiency: OFT and COFT require a number of finetuning parameters comparable to LoRA and much fewer than ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. OFT achieves this by learning layer-shared block-diagonal orthogonal transforms rather than full extra networks or large adapters. Second, data/compute efficiency: because OFT/COFT converge faster and are more sample-efficient, they require fewer epochs and fewer examples to reach strong performance. The paper provides a specific comparison in the L2F task where LoRA requires about 20 epochs to match the performance OFT achieves by epoch 8, demonstrating that OFT reduces both wall-clock training and effective data needs.

Moreover, the experiments indicate that OFT’s performance continues to improve when the number of learnable orthogonal parameters is increased (e.g., by reducing block sharing or increasing block size), suggesting the method has not saturated its performance ceiling within the parameter regimes explored. This flexibility means practitioners can trade parameter count for higher fidelity when resources allow.

# Qualitative outcomes: fidelity, geometry, and precise adherence to control signals

Qualitatively, OFT and COFT produce generated images that strike a balance between high-fidelity appearance and accurate control adherence. In the S2I task, generated scenes show coherent object shapes and realistic textures while matching the segmentation layout; the paper reports that LoRA often fails at following the segmentation, producing images that do not respect the given semantic map, whereas OFT and COFT reliably respect class boundaries and object placements.

For C2I, OFT and COFT can hallucinate plausible textures and object details while preserving the coarse edge structure provided as input. This results in realistic images that match the user-specified structure even when the input edge maps are sparse or stylized. T2I-Adapter and LoRA perform worse in such cases, generating images that deviate from the intended edge shapes or present incoherent content.

In the L2F task, OFT/COFT produce faces whose pose and expression align closely with the landmark control; the mean $\ell_2$ errors corroborate the visual impression of precise geometric adherence. This precision is especially important in applications such as avatar generation or pose-conditioned synthesis, where small landmark deviations can produce significant perceptual differences.

Overall, the qualitative analysis shows that OFT and COFT do not achieve controllability at the expense of image quality. Instead, by preserving the pretrained model’s hyperspherical neuron relations and limiting deviation through COFT’s radius constraint, OFT-based finetuning retains the generative priors that produce photorealism while enabling the model to follow control inputs accurately.

# Practical guidance: picking r and ε, data requirements, iteration counts, and monitoring control metrics

The paper provides several practical insights for applying OFT and COFT in controllable generation. These recommendations arise from the architecture and empirical behavior described in the experiments.

Choosing block parameter \(r\). The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be parameterized as block-diagonal with \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r\). This block structure trades expressiveness for parameter and compute efficiency. Smaller \(r\) (fewer, larger blocks) gives more degrees of freedom and thus more flexibility; \(r=1\) is a full orthogonal matrix. Larger \(r\) (more, smaller blocks) reduces learnable parameters and biases the transform toward channel-wise or depth-wise style changes. The paper shows that increasing \(r\) reduces parameter complexity from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\), and sharing blocks further reduces complexity toward \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\). Practically, pick \(r\) to meet a target parameter budget: start with a moderate block count (e.g., the same order as a typical LoRA rank) and increase block size if more flexibility is needed.

Setting the COFT deviation radius \(\epsilon\). COFT constrains the Cayley-parametrized orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) by \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \epsilon\), which in practice is approximated inside the Cayley parameterization as a norm constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\). The hyperparameter \(\epsilon\) controls the finetuning flexibility: smaller \(\epsilon\) keeps the model behavior close to the pretrained weights and yields more stable training; larger \(\epsilon\) approaches unconstrained OFT and accepts greater change for higher adaptivity. The experiments show COFT improves finetuning stability compared to OFT, and that sensible \(\epsilon\) values let COFT be robust to learning-rate and iteration choices. Practically, begin with a small \(\epsilon\) to favor stability; increase it only if the model fails to achieve required control.

Data and iteration guidance. OFT/COFT are empirically data-efficient and converge faster. The paper shows OFT can produce good controllability with small subsets (e.g., 5% of ADE20K), and convergence milestones occur significantly earlier than for LoRA or ControlNet. Because OFT/COFT training curves are smooth, practitioners can set a generous iteration budget without severe risk of collapse. COFT especially reduces sensitivity to the exact number of iterations and learning rate. A practical workflow is: (1) start with COFT and a conservative \(\epsilon\) and learning rate, (2) run for a moderate number of epochs while monitoring the control consistency metrics, and (3) optionally relax \(\epsilon\) or increase block sizes if control remains insufficient.

Monitoring and checkpoint selection. Use the task-appropriate control consistency metrics during training to monitor progress and select checkpoints. For C2I monitor IoU and F1 on held-out validation edge maps; for S2I monitor mIoU, mean, and overall accuracy; for L2F track mean \(\ell_2\) landmark error. Because these metrics are computed automatically from generated images via standard estimators, they provide direct feedback on how well the model respects control signals. Prefer checkpoints that achieve the best trade-off between control consistency and perceptual fidelity (e.g., visual quality or CLIP alignment).

Parameter-budget trade-offs. If parameter budgets are tight, favor block sharing and larger \(r\) (more blocks) for efficiency; if fidelity and flexibility are paramount and computation allows, reduce \(r\) (fewer blocks) and possibly disable block sharing to let the orthogonal transform be richer. The experiments suggest OFT scales well with parameter increases, so there is room to improve performance by investing parameters into orthogonal blocks.

# Open problems: scalable orthogonalization (matrix inverse), compositionality across tasks, reducing bias from block structure

While OFT and COFT offer strong empirical advantages, the paper highlights several open problems that remain areas for research and engineering improvements.

Scalability of orthogonal parameterization. The Cayley parameterization used to guarantee orthogonality constructs \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top\). This requires computing a matrix inverse (or solving a linear system) during training which can be expensive for large \(d\). The block-diagonal parameterization mitigates this cost, but it also introduces inductive bias. Finding more scalable, differentiable orthogonal parameterizations or algorithms that avoid expensive inverses while preserving the theoretical invariance of hyperspherical energy remains an open engineering problem.

Compositionality across tasks. An intriguing property of orthogonal transforms is closure under multiplication: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. This raises the prospect of composing task-specific orthogonal matrices to accumulate multiple adaptations or to compress multiple finetuning solutions into a single deployable matrix. However, it is unknown whether multiplicative composition of OFT matrices trained on different downstream tasks will preserve the capabilities of each task in practice or how to order or regularize such compositions. Studying whether and how a set of orthogonal transforms can jointly encode multiple downstream behaviors while keeping generative quality is an open research direction.

Bias introduced by block-diagonal structure and parameter efficiency. The block-diagonal parameterization trades flexibility for parameter efficiency, but it also imposes structural bias that may limit the kinds of neuron rotations that can be represented. While this bias is practically useful, understanding its impact theoretically and designing better parameter-efficient orthogonal transforms that reduce bias (for example via sparse but structured parameterizations that do not overly restrict interaction across channels) is an open design problem. Closely related is the question of the best way to allocate a fixed parameter budget across layers and blocks to maximize controllability without sacrificing pretraining generalization.

Approximation limits from Cayley/neumann expansion. COFT enforces an $\epsilon$-deviation constraint by approximating \(\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)\) via the Neumann series and constraining \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\). Rigorously characterizing when this approximation is valid in practice, and its effect on the final model behavior and stability, is an analytical question remaining to be explored.

In summary, while OFT and COFT provide a principled, effective route to controllable finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models—offering faster, more stable convergence, stronger control adherence, and good parameter efficiency—they also open several promising technical directions where further algorithmic and theoretical advances can improve scalability, multi-task compositionality, and parameter efficiency without introducing restrictive biases.
