\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: Overview and Motivation

# Why text guidance alone is insufficient for fine-grained control

Large text-to-image diffusion models are remarkably capable at producing photorealistic images from natural language prompts, but text guidance by itself often leaves important aspects of the desired output underspecified. A written prompt can describe <|endoftext|> content ("a person sitting on a bench in a park") and even many fine-grained attributes ("wearing a red jacket, looking left, with a dog at their feet"), yet it still may fail to pin down exact identity details, precise geometry, or low-level structure that a <|endoftext|> application requires. The paper emphasizes two concrete failure modes that motivate more structured control: (1) preserving the appearance of a particular subject from only a few reference images, and (2) enforcing a pixel- or structure-level conditioning such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. In both cases, the plain language prompt is ambiguous or insufficient to guarantee the desired output.

This insufficiency arises because text prompts are <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> descriptors that do not directly constrain the internal representations a diffusion model uses to synthesize pixels. The <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> generative ability is broad and flexible, which is an advantage for <|endoftext|> but it means that small changes in the prompt or the <|endoftext|> weights can lead to large and semantically meaningful variations in the output. Therefore, to achieve fine-grained or subject-specific control, we need principled ways to adapt the <|endoftext|> model so that it reliably interprets prompts together with additional conditioning signals (images, masks, edges, landmarks) without losing the <|endoftext|> existing generative knowledge.

# Two target tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

The paper focuses on two <|endoftext|> tasks that exemplify different kinds of control needs.

Subject-driven generation. In this setting the goal is to generate images of a specific subject (for example a particular pet or person) in new contexts, given only a few example images of that subject plus a text prompt describing the new scene. The central challenge is to adapt the diffusion model so it preserves the identity-specific visual details of the subject—coloration, distinctive markings, face shape, accessories, etc.—while allowing the rest of the scene to vary according to the prompt. Existing approaches like DreamBooth finetune the model with reconstruction and prior-preservation losses, but they do so by updating weights directly (often with a small learning rate) and do not guarantee that the finetuning will preserve the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> general generative competence.

Controllable generation. Here the model should obey an additional structured control signal that is not text, for example Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The objective is to produce images that both respect the control signal <|endoftext|> the generated object aligns with edges or segmentation regions) and remain photorealistic and faithful to the text prompt. Recent methods such as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter accomplish this by adding or finetuning modules that inject the control information, but these adaptor-style approaches trade off between adding parameters and maintaining the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> behavior—without a guarantee about preserving internal structural relationships.

Both tasks share a common technical requirement: finetune the <|endoftext|> diffusion model so it gains new controllability while retaining its <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> and generative quality. The design of the finetuning method should therefore balance the need for expressive adaptation and the need to avoid destructive changes to learned representations.

# Desiderata for finetuning: training efficiency and generalizability preservation

The paper identifies two desiderata for any effective finetuning method for text-to-image diffusion models.

Training efficiency. Because diffusion models are large, a practical finetuning method should introduce only a small number of additional trainable parameters and converge in a small number of training steps or epochs. This reduces compute, memory, and the amount of training data required. Parameter-efficient finetuning is especially important when adapting models per subject (few-shot personalization) or per control type.

Generalizability preservation. Finetuning must avoid destroying the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> general generative ability, specifically its capacity to produce high-fidelity, diverse, and semantically coherent images across prompts. A finetuned model that specializes to a <|endoftext|> task but loses the broad capabilities acquired during pretraining is of limited utility. Thus a successful finetuning strategy should explicitly or implicitly constrain updates so that the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> useful structure is preserved.

These desiderata create a tension: more flexible updates give greater ability to fit a <|endoftext|> task quickly, but they increase the risk of catastrophic changes to the <|endoftext|> representation. Conversely, overly conservative updates may preserve <|endoftext|> but fail to capture new control behavior.

# Limitations of small-step finetuning and additive adapters

Two common approaches for adapting large <|endoftext|> models are (1) small-step, direct finetuning of weights with low learning rates, and (2) adapter-style additive parameterizations that inject a small reparameterized component (for example LoRA's <|endoftext|> additive updates). The paper examines limitations of both.

Small-step finetuning. Many works simply continue training the <|endoftext|> weights with a very small learning rate. This practice is predicated on the intuition that small <|endoftext|> deviations from the <|endoftext|> weight matrix \(\bm{M}^0\) imply preservation of <|endoftext|> performance; formally this is equivalent to implicitly minimizing \(\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|\) during finetuning. While this often reduces catastrophic changes, <|endoftext|> closeness alone is not a reliable proxy for preserving the <|endoftext|> structure stored by the neural network. A small <|endoftext|> distance can nonetheless rearrange internal geometries that are crucial to the <|endoftext|> behavior, and conversely a larger <|endoftext|> change that respects certain structural invariants might preserve <|endoftext|> better.

Additive adapters <|endoftext|> LoRA). Adapter approaches add a reparameterized, typically <|endoftext|> additive matrix \(\Delta\bm{W}\) so the new weight is \(\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0+\Delta\bm{W}\) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\Delta\bm{W})\) <|endoftext|> to be small. This reduces the number of trainable parameters while preventing unstructured, large updates to <|endoftext|> However, additive <|endoftext|> updates do not provide a principled guarantee about preserving pairwise relationships among neurons (angles, relative alignments), and they do not directly target the internal <|endoftext|> structure that underlies <|endoftext|> behavior. As a result, adapter methods can still produce undesirable changes in generation <|endoftext|> or require careful hyperparameter tuning to avoid collapse.

In summary, neither small-step finetuning nor additive adapters offers a principled measure or constraint that directly captures the preservation of the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> internal relational structure among neurons. The paper argues this gap motivates finding a structural invariant to constrain finetuning.

# The core idea of OFT: preserving hyperspherical structure via orthogonal <|endoftext|>

Central observation: the paper builds on the empirical insight that angular relationships (directions) between neurons carry important <|endoftext|> information. Prior work has shown that angular similarity (for example cosine similarity) often better captures <|endoftext|> relations than raw magnitudes. Motivated by this, the authors adopt hyperspherical energy as a way to measure the pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer.

Hyperspherical energy. For a weight matrix \(\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), where each column \(\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d\) represents a neuron, define the <|endoftext|> (unit) neuron vector
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
The hyperspherical energy of the layer \(\bm{W}\) is then
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Intuitively, \(\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})\) is a <|endoftext|> that aggregates pairwise angular (hyperspherical) similarities among neurons: lower values reflect more uniform angular separation, and changes in \(\mathrm{HE}\) measure changes in the overall pairwise geometry of the neuron set on the unit hypersphere.

Key invariance under orthogonal <|endoftext|> A crucial mathematical property motivates OFT: if we apply the same orthogonal transformation \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (so that \(\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\)) to every neuron in the layer, then pairwise angles and distances between <|endoftext|> neurons are preserved. Concretely, if \(\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) then for any \(i,j\),
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i,
\]
because orthogonal <|endoftext|> preserves norms. Hence
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|,
\]
and therefore \(\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) = \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\). In words, a shared orthogonal transform leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Motivated by this invariance, OFT proposes to adapt a <|endoftext|> weight matrix <|endoftext|> by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix <|endoftext|> and replacing the forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
with
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},
\qquad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
Because <|endoftext|> is shared across all neurons in the layer, OFT rotates (or reflects) the set of neurons as a rigid body in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). By construction, this leaves the hyperspherical energy invariant. The paper frames OFT as solving a principled objective that aims to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned weights and the <|endoftext|> weights:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \big\| \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) - \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \big\|.
\]
Since orthogonal <|endoftext|> achieve equality of hyperspherical energy, OFT realizes this minimization by restricting updates to the orthogonal group.

Why this is a good <|endoftext|> bias. There are two intuitive reasons offered in the paper. First, angular information of neurons tends to <|endoftext|> essential <|endoftext|> structure: experiments in the paper show that replacing magnitude information by angular (phase-like) information preserves much of the visual <|endoftext|> in an autoencoding toy. This suggests that meaningful <|endoftext|> modifications of the model are often achieved by rotating neuron directions rather than changing magnitudes arbitrarily. Second, orthogonal <|endoftext|> are a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity: they allow expressive changes (rotations/reflections) to neuron directions while preserving the pairwise angular geometry that appears crucial for <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> behavior. Empirically, OFT finetunes faster and more stably than unconstrained or additive-update methods.

How OFT addresses the earlier desiderata and limitations. OFT directly targets the preservation of internal relational structure via hyperspherical invariance, avoiding the brittle reliance on small <|endoftext|> distance alone. Because it learns a compact orthogonal transform per layer (and can use block structure), OFT is parameter-efficient and tends to converge quickly in practice. It therefore aims to achieve both training efficiency and generalizability preservation simultaneously: it is expressive enough to adapt neuron directions to capture new control signals, yet regularized in a way that provably preserves pairwise angular relationships central to <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|>

Summary. In short, OFT rethinks finetuning as a problem of transforming neuron directions in a way that preserves the geometry of the set of neurons on the unit hypersphere. By learning layer-shared orthogonal matrices, OFT preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, providing a principled structural constraint that helps the model gain new controllability while maintaining the <|endoftext|> generative qualities the <|endoftext|> tasks require.
