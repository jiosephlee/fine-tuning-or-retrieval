# Problem setup and training data

Subject-driven generation asks a text-to-image diffusion model to produce images of a specific subject (for example, a person, pet, or object) in new contexts, using only a small set of reference images of that subject and a text prompt describing the desired scene. In the setting used in the paper, the <|endoftext|> model is Stable Diffusion v1.5 and the finetuning follows the DreamBooth paradigm: a few images per subject are provided (typically on the order of only a handful), and the finetuning goal is to <|endoftext|> the subject into the model so that subsequent text prompts containing a dedicated token (or otherwise referring to the subject) produce images that preserve the subject identity while varying context, pose, background, etc.

The available training data therefore contains two kinds of examples used during finetuning. First, the subject-specific images (the few-shot images of the target subject) are used to teach the model a reconstruction mapping for that subject: conditioning on a prompt that includes the subject token, the model should be able to reproduce the subject image. Second, class-level images (images of the broader class to which the subject belongs) are used to regularize the finetuning so the model does not overfit or forget the class prior. This two-part dataset is the same structure employed by DreamBooth: subject-instance examples to learn the new identity, plus class examples to enforce a prior that preserves diversity and prevents <|endoftext|>

Because the available subject images are scarce, the finetuning procedure must be sample-efficient and must carefully balance adaptation to the new subject against preservation of the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> broad generative capabilities. The OFT approach is applied in this same few-shot training setup so that comparisons to DreamBooth and adapter-based methods (such as LoRA) are performed under the same data regimen and loss structure.

# Loss functions and prior preservation (DreamBooth framework)

DreamBooth finetuning uses two <|endoftext|> loss terms. The first is a reconstruction loss that encourages the finetuned model to reproduce the subject images when conditioned on prompts containing the subject token and corresponding conditioning inputs. Concretely, for a subject image with prompt containing the special subject token, the diffusion denoising objective trains the model so that sampled denoising trajectories map back to the reference image under that prompt.

The second critical component is the class-level prior preservation loss. Because finetuning only on the few subject images tends to overfit and cause the model to forget the general appearance of the class (leading to degenerate or collapsed outputs), DreamBooth augments the subject-specific training with class photographs and a "prior-preservation" loss that penalizes the model if its outputs <|endoftext|> from the class distribution when prompted with generic class prompts. This term helps retain generative diversity and prevents the model from memorizing the few subject images to the exclusion of other class instances.

OFT is integrated into this same loss framework: the training objective remains the DreamBooth objective (reconstruction on subject examples plus class prior preservation on class images). What changes is not the loss, but how the model parameters are adapted during optimization. Instead of performing unconstrained weight updates on the <|endoftext|> weights, OFT replaces the update mechanism for selected weight matrices by a learnable orthogonal transformation applied <|endoftext|> to the <|endoftext|> weights. This lets OFT minimize the DreamBooth loss while explicitly preserving inter-neuron angular relationships (hyperspherical energy) and thereby helping to maintain the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> capabilities.

# Stability across iterations and insensitivity to training length

A notable practical property reported in the paper is that OFT (and its <|endoftext|> variant COFT) yields much more stable finetuning dynamics across the number of iterations than standard unconstrained finetuning or adapter methods like LoRA. Empirically, the authors observed that OFT converges quickly and remains robust even if one uses a relatively large number of training steps; this reduces the burden of precisely tuning the number of finetuning iterations for each subject.

Illustratively, the experiments show that after about 400 iterations both DreamBooth and OFT variants can achieve reasonable control of the subject identity. However, when training continues to many more iterations (for example, 2{,}000 iterations), DreamBooth can begin to produce collapsed images and LoRA can meaningfully degrade in subject fidelity or produce semantically wrong outputs (the paper gives the example of LoRA generating "yellow fur" instead of a "yellow shirt"). In contrast, OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to produce subject-preserving, semantically coherent images even at high iteration counts.

# Metrics: DINO, CLIP-I for subject fidelity; CLIP-T for prompt fidelity; LPIPS diversity

A careful evaluation for subject-driven generation must quantify (1) how well the generated images preserve the subject identity, (2) how well they follow the text prompt, and (3) how diverse the generated samples are. The paper uses the following metrics to capture these aspects.

DINO: DINO is a representation produced by a self-supervised ViT (specifically ViT-S/16 in the paper). For subject fidelity, the authors compute the average pairwise cosine similarity between the DINO <|endoftext|> of generated images and the corresponding real subject images. Higher DINO similarity indicates better preservation of subtle subject identity cues captured by DINO features.

CLIP-I (image fidelity): CLIP-I denotes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP image <|endoftext|> between generated images and real images of the subject. Because CLIP <|endoftext|> align visual features to <|endoftext|> textual concepts, CLIP-I measures whether the generated images are close to the subject in the multimodal <|endoftext|> space.

CLIP-T (prompt fidelity): CLIP-T computes the average cosine similarity between the CLIP text <|endoftext|> of the prompt and the CLIP image <|endoftext|> of the generated image. This measures how well the generated images align with the <|endoftext|> content of the text prompt.

LPIPS (diversity): To measure diversity among samples generated for the same subject-prompt pair, the authors use LPIPS (a perceptual metric). Specifically, they compute the average LPIPS (the paper reports an LPIPS-based cosine similarity variant) between generated images that share the same prompt and subject. Higher LPIPS-based diversity indicates the model can generate varied renditions while preserving the subject identity.

Together these metrics quantify subject fidelity (DINO, CLIP-I), prompt fidelity (CLIP-T), and intra-prompt diversity (LPIPS). For robustness, the experiments repeat finetuning with multiple random seeds (the paper reports 30 different random seeds for each method) so that reported averages are not dominated by one favorable seed.

# Quantitative and qualitative findings vs DreamBooth and LoRA

Quantitatively, the paper reports that both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by a considerable margin on subject-fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I). At the same time, OFT/COFT achieve slightly better or comparable scores on prompt-fidelity (CLIP-T) and sample diversity (LPIPS). These results indicate that OFT not only better preserves the subject identity but does so without sacrificing the ability to follow the text prompt or to produce diverse samples.

The repeated-seed evaluation (30 finetuning runs per method) demonstrates that this improvement is consistent and not an artifact of lucky <|endoftext|> The superior subject-fidelity scores reflect the central hypothesis behind OFT: preserving inter-neuron angular structure (hyperspherical energy) during finetuning helps retain the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> capacities that are important for identity preservation.

Qualitatively, OFT and COFT produce images that clearly retain subject-specific attributes—facial features, clothing details, and other idiosyncratic aspects—while placing the subject in novel contexts that match the text prompts. The paper shows examples where LoRA fails to preserve identity (for instance, replacing clothing with fur or otherwise altering the subject), and cases where DreamBooth, although it can preserve identity, sometimes fails to follow the prompt precisely. OFT/COFT achieve a better balance: they both preserve identity and more faithfully render the requested textual attributes.

A further practical advantage is that OFT's performance is robust to the number of iterations: users can finetune for more iterations without needing to stop early to avoid collapse, and COFT adds an explicit radius constraint that reduces sensitivity to both learning rate and iteration count. The paper also notes that OFT converges faster and with fewer training steps than competing methods; for example, OFT reaches good control in far fewer iterations, and it is more sample-efficient.

Finally, the paper includes additional qualitative material and a human evaluation (reported in the appendices) that corroborate the automated metrics: human raters preferred OFT/COFT outputs for subject fidelity and overall quality in many cases. Taken together, these quantitative and qualitative findings show that integrating OFT into a DreamBooth-style finetuning pipeline yields practical benefits: stronger identity preservation, better prompt adherence, faster and more stable convergence, and less sensitivity to hyperparameter choices.
