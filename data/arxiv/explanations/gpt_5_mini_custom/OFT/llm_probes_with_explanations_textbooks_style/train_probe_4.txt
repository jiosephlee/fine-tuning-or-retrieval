Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: Preserving Pretrained Behavior While Enabling Control

# Background: Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Large text-to-image diffusion models can synthesize high-fidelity images from text prompts, but downstream tasks often require finer control than text alone can provide. Two such tasks are subject-driven generation, where the model must preserve a subject’s identity across contexts from only a few example images, and controllable generation, where the model must follow an additional control signal such as canny edges or segmentation maps in addition to text. In both cases, fine-tuning a pretrained model is the central mechanism for injecting the new behavior without sacrificing the model’s broad, high-quality generative ability.

Conventional finetuning updates weights directly with small learning rates to maintain proximity to the pretrained model in Euclidean norm. More structured methods such as LoRA introduce low-rank updates to further regularize the change. While these strategies limit the magnitude of the weight change, they do not address the deeper question of what structural properties of the pretrained model should be preserved to retain its semantic generation ability. The paper proposes that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons—summarized by the hyperspherical energy—play a central role in encoding semantic structure. Building on this idea, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is designed to preserve hyperspherical energy exactly by restricting updates to layer-shared orthogonal transformations. This preserves pairwise neuron angles while allowing controlled semantic shifts by rotating or reflecting a layer’s neuron directions.

However, even with preserved hyperspherical energy, OFT can still drift from the pretrained model’s behavior if the learned orthogonal transform deviates too far. This motivates the constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds an explicit constraint to keep the learned transform within a small neighborhood of the identity, anchoring the finetuned model closely to the pretrained one.

# Hyperspherical Energy and Why Angles Matter

The approach begins from the observation that angular relationships capture semantics effectively. Empirical studies have shown that angular features correlate with semantic similarity, and training with neurons normalized on a unit hypersphere can preserve capacity and generalization. A toy experiment in the paper illustrates this clearly. A standard convolutional autoencoder is trained with standard inner products. At test time, three feature extraction variants are compared: the original inner product, magnitude-only, and angle-only (cosine similarity). The angle-only variant nearly perfectly reconstructs input images, while the magnitude-only variant fails to retain useful information. Although training did not use the cosine activation, the angles alone retained the semantic content, suggesting that neuron directions encode the essential semantic information.

To operationalize this idea for finetuning, the paper uses hyperspherical energy to characterize the pairwise relational structure among neurons. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the $i$-th neuron. Define the normalized neurons as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is
$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$.
This energy aggregates pairwise hyperspherical similarities, capturing how uniformly neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere. The paper hypothesizes that preserving this energy between the finetuned and pretrained models protects the pretrained semantic structure.

A naive regularizer to minimize $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ lacks guarantees. Instead, the paper leverages an invariance: pairwise angles are preserved under a shared orthogonal transformation. If $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ orthogonal, then the pairwise angles (hence the hyperspherical energy) are unchanged. This insight motivates updating each layer by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix, preserving hyperspherical energy exactly while rotating or reflecting neuron directions to inject the new control behavior.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Formulation and Intuition

In a fully connected layer, a forward pass computes $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}$ for input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. OFT replaces the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, yielding
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}$,
subject to $\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^{\top}=\bm{I}$. Initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ recovers the pretrained model at the start of finetuning. Learning $\bm{R}$ rotates or reflects all neurons in the layer by the same transform, thereby changing neuron directions in a controlled way while preserving all pairwise angles.

The objective can be viewed as achieving the ideal minimum in
$\min_{\bm{W}}\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|=\min_{\bm{W}}\left\|\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0-\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|^{-1}\right\|$,
which equals zero when $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Thus OFT obtains the strongest possible guarantee of preserving the measure that the paper identifies as critical for retaining pretrained semantic ability.

This design strikes a balance between flexibility and regularity. Standard finetuning is highly flexible but can destabilize pretrained capabilities, especially in large models. Low-rank updates (e.g., LoRA) restrict changes to a low-dimensional subspace. OFT instead enforces a structural constraint at the level of neuron geometry, preserving angles and hence the hyperspherical energy. This preserves semantic structure while enabling semantically meaningful shifts by reorienting the neuron basis via $\bm{R}$.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

To be practical, the orthogonal constraint must be enforced efficiently and differentiably. Direct orthogonalization (e.g., Gram–Schmidt) is typically too expensive. The paper adopts the Cayley parameterization:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^{\top}$. This yields an orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$, which the paper finds sufficient in practice. To further reduce parameter and computation costs, a block-diagonal structure is used:
$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$,
with $\bm{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r)$. This reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and optionally to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ if blocks are shared. Despite these reductions, $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, and thus hyperspherical energy is still preserved exactly.

This parameterization renders OFT both principled and efficient: it enforces the core geometric constraint while offering tunable parameter efficiency via the number of blocks and block sharing.

# The Primary Risk in Plain OFT: Unconstrained Deviation

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, it does not by itself prevent large deviations from the pretrained model in Euclidean terms. Intuitively, there exist many orthogonal transforms far from the identity that preserve pairwise angles but substantially change how the model behaves on inputs. In other words, preserving angles constrains the geometry of neuron directions relative to each other but not their absolute orientation in the space. If $\bm{R}$ can move arbitrarily on the orthogonal group, OFT may still drift from the pretrained behavior in ways that degrade generative fidelity or destabilize finetuning.

This risk is particularly salient in large models, where small changes can have amplified effects, and in tasks requiring both precise control and retention of general-purpose generation ability. The paper emphasizes a practical desideratum: preserving generative performance while enabling control. Relying solely on angular preservation may be insufficient to guarantee closeness to the pretrained model’s behavior throughout training.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): Adding an Explicit Neighborhood Constraint

COFT addresses this risk by adding an explicit deviation constraint that keeps the finetuned model within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model. The forward pass remains
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}$,
but now with constraints
$\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^{\top}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$.
Here, $\epsilon$ controls how far the learned transform is allowed to move from the identity. This directly limits the deviation from the pretrained weights while retaining the strict preservation of hyperspherical energy. The result is a principled pairing of two constraints:
- Minimal hyperspherical energy difference (achieved exactly by orthogonality), preserving the pretrained model’s internal semantic geometry.
- Bounded deviation from the pretrained model (via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$), preserving behavioral proximity during finetuning.

Crucially, COFT makes explicit what is often only implicit in standard finetuning practices. In conventional setups, small learning rates are used to keep updates near the pretrained weights, but this proximity is not explicitly enforced or measured. COFT formalizes this proximity as a hard constraint on the learned transform.

# Enforcing the COFT Constraint via Cayley and Neumann Approximation

Imposing $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ directly on the Cayley-parameterized $\bm{R}$ is nontrivial. To resolve this, the paper uses a Neumann series approximation to relate the constraint to the underlying skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$,
under the assumption that the series converges in operator norm. This suggests that constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ can be approximately achieved by constraining $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$, for a suitably chosen $\epsilon'$. Since $\bm{Q}$ lives in a linear space of skew-symmetric matrices, this constraint can be enforced with projected gradient descent. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ ensures $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start, preserving exact identity initialization.

This construction provides a practical way to implement the COFT constraint while maintaining the efficiency benefits of the Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal structure. It preserves the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ and thus the invariance of hyperspherical energy, while controlling the transform’s distance from the identity.

# Flexibility–Regularity Trade-off and the Role of $\epsilon$

COFT makes explicit the fundamental trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning. A larger $\epsilon$ allows the transform to move farther from the identity, increasing flexibility to fit downstream tasks, and in the limit recovers the behavior of OFT. A smaller $\epsilon$ tightens the neighborhood around the pretrained model, increasing regularity and preserving more of the pretrained behavior. The paper reports that varying $\epsilon$ visibly modulates this behavior: as $\epsilon$ grows, COFT’s results resemble OFT; as $\epsilon$ shrinks, COFT behaves increasingly like the pretrained model.

This explicit control is valuable in practice. It reduces sensitivity to training schedules and iteration counts: with an appropriately chosen $\epsilon$, training can proceed for many iterations without risking collapse or severe drift, because the constraint limits how far the model can move. The paper’s subject-driven generation experiments show that COFT maintains stability over long finetuning runs, in contrast to standard finetuning, which can collapse after extended training. In controllable generation, COFT exhibits smooth and predictable convergence, avoiding the sudden emergent controllability observed in some adapter-based methods.

# Why COFT Improves Stability Over Plain OFT

Both OFT and COFT preserve hyperspherical energy, which the paper identifies as central to preserving semantic structure. However, OFT alone allows the learned orthogonal transform to range widely over the orthogonal group. This can potentially produce large behavioral shifts even while maintaining pairwise neuron angles. The explicit neighborhood constraint in COFT prevents such shifts by anchoring the transform near the identity.

Empirically, this leads to several benefits reported in the paper:
- Greater finetuning stability across iterations, reducing the risk of model collapse in subject-driven generation.
- Faster, smoother convergence in controllable generation tasks, with improved ease of hyperparameter selection.
- Retention of pretrained generative fidelity while achieving strong controllability, demonstrated quantitatively and qualitatively.

In short, COFT directly addresses the primary risk that plain OFT might still allow: deviation from pretrained behavior. By bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$, COFT ensures that preserving angular structure translates into preserving functional behavior to a controllable degree.

# Connections, Extensions, and Practical Considerations

The paper situates OFT and COFT among existing finetuning methods. Compared to low-rank additive updates such as LoRA, OFT performs a multiplicative, full-rank update constrained by orthogonality, which can be rewritten as
$(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}$.
If $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank and $\bm{W}^0$ is full-rank, OFT can be seen as inducing a low-rank effective update, though its parameter efficiency arises from block-diagonal sparsity rather than rank constraints. COFT adds an orthogonality-preserving proximity constraint not present in LoRA.

The paper also notes a re-scaled variant that learns a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ in addition to $\bm{R}$, using
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^{\top}\bm{x}$,
which leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged because normalization removes magnitudes. While the main experiments focus on plain OFT and COFT to highlight the effect of orthogonality alone, the re-scaled variant provides additional flexibility with minimal parameter cost.

Finally, a practical advantage emphasized in the paper is that there is no additional inference overhead. After training, one can precompute $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and use $\bm{W}$ directly at inference. This preserves the original inference speed of the pretrained model, unlike some adapter-based methods that introduce extra structures at test time.

# Experimental Evidence: Stability and Control

The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on subject-driven generation and controllable generation, comparing against standard finetuning (e.g., DreamBooth), LoRA, and adapter-based methods such as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter.

In subject-driven generation, COFT and OFT exhibit stable convergence and strong subject identity preservation across many iterations, whereas standard finetuning can collapse and LoRA often fails to preserve identity. Quantitatively, COFT and OFT surpass baselines on subject fidelity metrics while matching or exceeding prompt fidelity and diversity.

In controllable generation, COFT and OFT converge faster and achieve stronger control according to the paper’s control consistency metrics. Training dynamics are smooth and predictable, contrasting with the sudden convergence behavior observed in some adapter-based baselines. Qualitative results show that COFT and OFT produce high-fidelity images with accurate adherence to control signals across tasks such as edge-to-image, segmentation-to-image, and landmark-to-face.

These findings support the central claims: preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal finetuning protects pretrained semantic structure, and adding the explicit $\epsilon$-deviation constraint in COFT effectively mitigates the primary risk of drift that plain OFT might allow, yielding superior stability and controllability in practice.

# Summary and Outlook

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments Orthogonal Finetuning by adding an explicit neighborhood constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ alongside orthogonality. Orthogonality preserves hyperspherical energy and thus pairwise neuron angles, maintaining the pretrained model’s internal semantic geometry. The explicit $\epsilon$-deviation constraint keeps the learned transform close to the identity, preventing large functional deviations from the pretrained model. Implemented efficiently via the Cayley parameterization and a Neumann series approximation, COFT offers a principled, computationally practical way to finetune text-to-image diffusion models with strong stability, fast convergence, and no inference overhead.

The paper highlights open challenges, including further improving scalable, differentiable orthogonal parameterizations, exploring compositionality of orthogonal transforms across tasks, and advancing parameter efficiency beyond block-diagonal structures. Nonetheless, within the scope of the presented results, COFT provides a clear, principled solution to the primary risk remaining in plain OFT: it explicitly constrains deviation from pretrained behavior while preserving the angular structure essential for semantic generation.
