Chapter: Orthogonal Finetuning for Convolutional Layers: Channel-wise Transformations and the Depth-wise Analogy

# Background: Orthogonal Finetuning and Hyperspherical Energy

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach for adapting pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks while preserving their semantic generation ability. The method is built on the observation that angular information among neurons is central to encoding semantics, and that preserving the pairwise angular relationships (measured via hyperspherical similarity) stabilizes finetuning. Concretely, OFT enforces invariance of hyperspherical energy during finetuning by applying a shared orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer.

For a fully connected layer with weights represented by a matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a neuron, the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized neuron. The central idea of OFT is that if the finetuned weights $\bm{W}$ are related to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ via an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$,
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0, \quad \text{with } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
then the pairwise angles among the neurons are preserved, and thus the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. This yields a finetuning strategy that directly targets semantic preservation by constraining updates to lie on a rotation/reflection of the pretrained neuron space. The forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is the layer input.

To make OFT computationally practical, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized efficiently via the Cayley transform and further structured as block-diagonal, providing both guaranteed orthogonality and parameter efficiency.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization: Block-diagonal Structure

A naive orthogonalization method is often too expensive in practice. OFT therefore constructs the orthogonal matrix using Cayley parameterization, 
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization guarantees orthogonality with determinant $1$, which suffices in practice.

Even with Cayley parameterization, the number of parameters for a dense $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ scales as $d(d-1)/2$. To further reduce trainable parameters while preserving the orthogonality constraint, OFT adopts a block-diagonal structure:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)\in \text{O}(d),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in \text{O}(d/r)$ is orthogonal and the full matrix $\bm{R}$ remains in $\text{O}(d)$. With $r$ blocks, the parameter complexity drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Further parameter sharing across blocks, such as $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$, reduces the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ while maintaining orthogonality.

This block-diagonal parameterization is key to OFT’s balance between regularity and flexibility: it restricts the transform to act within subspaces, which enhances stability, yet retains sufficient expressiveness to adapt neuron directions. Crucially, because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal regardless of block structuring, the hyperspherical energy of the transformed layer is still preserved.

# OFT for Convolutional Layers: Interpreting Blocks as Channel-wise Transforms

While the presentation above uses a fully connected layer for clarity, OFT is a model-agnostic finetuning approach and applies equally to convolution layers. In convolutional architectures, layer inputs are naturally organized into channels. The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ admits a particularly intuitive interpretation when aligned with this channel structure.

Let the input to a convolutional layer have $c$ channels. If we set the number of blocks $r$ in the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to equal the number of input channels, i.e., $r=c$, then
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_c),
\]
and each block $\bm{R}_i$ operates on the subspace corresponding uniquely to the $i$-th input channel. In effect, the orthogonal transformation decouples across channels: every channel is transformed by its own orthogonal matrix, independent of the others. Because the blocks are orthogonal, each per-channel transform preserves the pairwise angular relations among neurons within that channel’s subspace, and hence preserves hyperspherical energy within each subspace. The full-layer transform remains orthogonal and therefore preserves the hyperspherical geometry of the entire layer.

This structured action implies that OFT, when configured with $r=c$, performs channel-wise transformations. No cross-channel mixing occurs during the transform, which makes the update analogous to operations that act separately and exclusively on each input channel.

# The Depth-wise Convolution Analogy

The observation above leads to a clear analogy: when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. In standard convolutional processing, depth-wise convolutions apply separate filters to each input channel independently, without mixing channels. OFT’s block-diagonal transform with $r=c$ exhibits the same structural independence: each block $\bm{R}_i$ is dedicated to the $i$-th channel, and its action does not couple with other channels.

Formally, with $r=c$,
\[
\bm{R}=\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{R}_1 & & \\
& \ddots & \\
& & \bm{R}_c
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and the transformed weights become
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\cdots,\bm{R}_c)\bm{W}^0.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal, the product $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ applies $\bm{R}_i$ to the portion of $\bm{W}^0$ associated with the $i$-th channel, leaving the other channels unaffected. This structural property mirrors depth-wise convolution’s per-channel specialization: each channel’s representation is modified by a channel-specific transform, independent of other channels. In OFT, the transform is orthogonal, so it adjusts neuron directions (angles) within the channel while preserving their pairwise angular relationships, consistent with the goal of semantic preservation via hyperspherical energy invariance.

The analogy is informative in two complementary ways. First, it clarifies how OFT can be naturally aligned with convolutional architectures by respecting their channel structure. Second, it highlights the regularizing effect of channel-wise independence: by preventing cross-channel mixing during the finetuning transform, OFT narrows the degree of freedom in a controlled manner, which stabilizes optimization while preserving the pretrained model’s learned semantic organization.

# Variants: Shared Blocks and Their Implications

OFT also admits a variant where all blocks are shared, i.e., $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$. In the convolutional interpretation, this corresponds to applying the same orthogonal transform to every channel:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\underbrace{\bm{R}_\star,\bm{R}_\star,\cdots,\bm{R}_\star}_{c \text{ times}}),
\]
with a single learnable block $\bm{R}_\star\in \text{O}(d/c)$ shared across channels. This further reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/c^2)$, strengthening regularity by constraining all channels to be transformed identically. Even under this stronger constraint, orthogonality is preserved, ensuring hyperspherical energy invariance.

The contrast between per-channel blocks ($r=c$) and shared blocks underscores an important design axis in OFT: flexibility versus regularity. Per-channel blocks provide greater flexibility by allowing distinct transforms per channel, closely analogous to learning separate depth-wise kernels. Shared blocks provide stronger regularization, potentially improving stability and parameter efficiency at the cost of reduced per-channel expressiveness.

# Practical Considerations and Stability

The channel-wise interpretation of block-diagonal OFT offers practical benefits for finetuning diffusion models:

1. It aligns the finetuning transform with the natural modularity of convolutional layers, using per-channel orthogonal transforms to adjust neuron directions where they most directly encode semantics.

2. It preserves hyperspherical energy within each channel’s subspace and across the whole layer, ensuring that finetuning preserves the pretrained model’s relational structure among neurons, which supports semantic preservation.

3. It introduces no additional inference overhead: once trained, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weights, yielding $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and inference proceeds exactly as in the original model.

Moreover, OFT’s block-diagonal structure is one of two complementary routes to parameter efficiency discussed in this framework. Whereas low-rank updates (as in LoRA) reduce parameters by exploiting rank constraints, OFT reduces parameters by exploiting sparsity via block-diagonal orthogonality. Both routes limit the finetuning capacity in controlled ways, but OFT’s orthogonal constraint brings the unique benefit of provably preserving pairwise angles among neurons.

Finally, a constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), adds an explicit deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ on top of orthogonality, implemented by bounding the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ via $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$. This further stabilizes finetuning by keeping the transform close to identity, improving robustness across iterations and reducing sensitivity to hyperparameters. While COFT is not specific to convolutional layers, its stabilizing effect complements the regularity induced by channel-wise block-diagonal structures.

In summary, when the number of blocks in OFT’s block-diagonal orthogonal matrix equals the number of input channels, each block acts on a unique neuron channel. This yields a channel-wise, independent transformation that is directly analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels, while preserving hyperspherical energy and introducing no inference-time cost.
