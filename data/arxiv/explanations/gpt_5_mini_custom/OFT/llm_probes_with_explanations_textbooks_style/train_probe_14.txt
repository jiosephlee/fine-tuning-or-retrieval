Controlling Flexibility in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: The Role of the Parameter $\epsilon$

# Background: Orthogonal Finetuning and Hyperspherical Energy

Text-to-image diffusion models achieve high-fidelity generation but are difficult to finetune for downstream tasks without degrading their pretrained capabilities. A central challenge in finetuning is balancing flexibility—so that the model adapts to the new task—and regularity—so that the model’s pretrained semantic competence is preserved. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) provides a principled approach to this trade-off by preserving hyperspherical energy, a global measure of the relational structure among neurons in a layer.

Hyperspherical energy captures the pairwise relational structure among neuron directions on the unit hypersphere. For a fully connected layer with weights represented as columns of a matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, the hyperspherical energy is defined as
$\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$,
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized neuron. This measure is sensitive to pairwise angles and therefore encodes neuron uniformity and angular structure on the unit hypersphere. The guiding principle behind OFT is to finetune while preserving hyperspherical energy, thereby maintaining the pairwise neuron angles that encode pretrained semantic structure.

OFT achieves this preservation by transforming pretrained weights with a layer-shared orthogonal matrix. If $\bm{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights and $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix, then the OFT forward pass is
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$.
This transformation preserves all pairwise angles among neurons, and thus exactly preserves hyperspherical energy. In effect, OFT changes neuron directions through a global rotation or reflection in each layer while maintaining the intrinsic pretrained angular relationships that are hypothesized to store semantic knowledge.

# From OFT to COFT: Adding a Radius Constraint

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy, it still allows potentially large global rotations of neurons. To further stabilize finetuning, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) introduces an explicit radius constraint that limits the deviation from the pretrained model. COFT modifies the forward pass to
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ subject to $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$.
Here, $\epsilon$ is a nonnegative scalar that bounds the distance of $\bm{R}$ from the identity matrix in a chosen matrix norm. This constraint explicitly keeps the finetuned model within a hyperspherical neighborhood centered at the pretrained weights. In other words, COFT preserves hyperspherical energy (by orthogonality) while also constraining how far the global orientation of neurons may rotate from their pretrained directions.

This two-part constraint can be understood as combining two desiderata: preserving semantic structure through angle preservation, and preserving proximity to the pretrained model by limiting the size of the deviation. The former is enforced by orthogonality, the latter by the radius constraint.

# The Parameter $\epsilon$ as a Flexibility Control

The parameter $\epsilon$ directly controls the flexibility of COFT. Geometrically, the set of admissible orthogonal transformations is restricted to those within an $\epsilon$-ball around the identity matrix. This has immediate consequences for how much the neuron directions may deviate from their pretrained configuration.

When $\epsilon$ is small, $\bm{R}$ is forced to remain close to $\bm{I}$, and thus $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$ remains very close to $\bm{W}^0$. In this regime, COFT behaves increasingly like the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model: the finetuning step has limited capacity to alter neuron directions, and consequently exerts a conservative influence on the model. This yields strong stability and minimizes the risk of semantic drift, but restricts the degree to which the model can adapt.

When $\epsilon$ is large, the constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ becomes weak, and the optimization can explore a broader subset of the orthogonal group. In the limit of large $\epsilon$, the additional constraint becomes effectively inactive, and COFT reduces to standard OFT. In this regime, the finetuning process enjoys greater flexibility to reorient neurons, potentially enabling stronger adaptation to the downstream task while still preserving hyperspherical energy.

This monotonic relationship between $\epsilon$ and flexibility is explicitly observed in the COFT analysis: “$\epsilon$ controls the flexibility of finetuning. With larger $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model resembles the OFT-finetuned model. With smaller $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model behaves increasingly similar to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model.” Thus, $\epsilon$ is the knob that governs how far the model is allowed to move along the manifold of orthogonal transformations away from the identity.

# Cayley Parameterization and Enforcing the Constraint

Enforcing orthogonality during learning can be computationally demanding with naive methods. COFT adopts the Cayley parameterization to efficiently construct orthogonal matrices:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization ensures $\bm{R}$ lies in the special orthogonal group (determinant $1$) while maintaining differentiability and efficiency. Although it cannot produce reflections (determinant $-1$), this restriction is empirically unproblematic.

The radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ is not directly simple to impose in the Cayley form. COFT addresses this using a Neumann series approximation:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$
under suitable conditions for convergence in operator norm. This approximation links the deviation $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ to the magnitude of $\bm{Q}$. Consequently, the constraint can be moved inside the Cayley transform and enforced on $\bm{Q}$ instead: $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$ for a suitably chosen $\epsilon'$. This inner constraint can be implemented with projected gradient descent. Identity initialization of $\bm{R}$ corresponds to initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$.

In practice, scalability is improved by using a block-diagonal orthogonal structure:
$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\ldots,\bm{R}_r)$ with each $\bm{R}_i\in \text{O}(d/r)$.
This reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and can be further reduced with block sharing to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Importantly, these efficiency strategies maintain orthogonality and therefore preserve hyperspherical energy, while also giving additional implicit regularization.

# Why $\epsilon$ Controls Flexibility: A Geometric and Operational View

From a geometric viewpoint, OFT optimizes over the manifold of orthogonal matrices. COFT restricts this search to an $\epsilon$-neighborhood of the identity, which is equivalent to finetuning on a smaller patch of that manifold. As $\epsilon$ shrinks, the feasible region becomes a small local neighborhood where only slight rotations are allowed, and the model’s behavior remains close to the pretrained model. As $\epsilon$ grows, the feasible region widens, allowing larger rotations and thus larger semantic adjustments to neuron directions, approaching the flexibility of OFT.

Operationally, this manifests in training behavior and outcomes. The COFT analysis reports that smaller $\epsilon$ values yield models that “behave increasingly similar to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model,” reflecting low flexibility, while larger $\epsilon$ values allow behavior to “resemble the OFT-finetuned model,” reflecting high flexibility. The parameter $\epsilon$ therefore governs the strength of the deviation constraint, with direct and predictable effects on adaptation capacity.

This interpretation aligns with the underlying motivations of OFT and COFT. OFT is designed to target the most semantically effective degrees of freedom—neuron directions—while preserving angular relations. COFT further moderates those degrees of freedom by limiting how far the model can move away from its pretrained configuration. The single scalar $\epsilon$ is the explicit embodiment of this moderation.

# Practical Consequences for Stability and Convergence

The explicit deviation constraint in COFT has notable practical consequences. First, it improves finetuning stability by constraining deviations to remain within a bounded region around the pretrained model. The paper observes that “COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.” Second, the same mechanism simplifies hyperparameter selection, because the training becomes less sensitive to the number of iterations and learning rate when $\epsilon$ is properly set. In particular, smaller $\epsilon$ produces behavior closer to the pretrained model, reducing the risk of collapse over longer finetuning schedules.

At the same time, the fact that larger $\epsilon$ allows COFT to behave like OFT ensures that the method does not cap the attainable performance too strictly. When stronger adaptation is needed, increasing $\epsilon$ relaxes the constraint, unlocking more of the transformation space while continuing to preserve hyperspherical energy. This leads to a principled and interpretable control over the flexibility-regularity trade-off.

# Relating COFT’s $\epsilon$ to Standard Finetuning and LoRA

Standard finetuning informally constrains deviation by using a small learning rate, aiming to keep $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$ small throughout training. LoRA constrains the form of the update by imposing a low-rank structure on the increment, i.e., $\text{rank}(\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0)=r'$. OFT instead constrains the transform to be orthogonal, thereby preserving hyperspherical energy and pairwise neuron angles. COFT combines OFT’s invariance with an explicit bound on deviation, $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$.

This comparison highlights that COFT’s $\epsilon$ plays a role analogous to an explicit, principled radius constraint that standard finetuning applies only implicitly via learning-rate control. Unlike LoRA’s structural constraint on the update, COFT constrains the space of allowable transformations by directly bounding the radius of movement on the orthogonal manifold. As $\epsilon$ increases, the constraint weakens and the method recovers the flexibility of OFT; as $\epsilon$ decreases, COFT approaches the pretrained model’s behavior.

# Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning preserves the pretrained model’s hyperspherical energy, thereby maintaining the pairwise angular relationships among neurons that encode semantic structure. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning augments OFT with a radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ that explicitly limits deviation from the pretrained model. The parameter $\epsilon$ primarily controls flexibility: larger $\epsilon$ permits broader rotations and makes COFT behave like OFT, while smaller $\epsilon$ restricts rotations and makes COFT behave like the pretrained model. This clean interpretation of $\epsilon$ provides a principled and intuitive handle on the finetuning trade-off between adaptation capacity and stability, enabling predictable and robust control over downstream finetuning dynamics.
