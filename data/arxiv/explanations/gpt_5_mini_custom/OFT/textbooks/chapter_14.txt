\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 14: Limitations, Extensions, and Open Problems

# Computational cost of Cayley inverse and scaling to very large layers

A central practical building block of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in the paper is the Cayley parameterization of an orthogonal matrix:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). Cayley has the convenient property of producing orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (the special orthogonal group), and it admits a simple identity initialization by setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning.

Despite these advantages, the Cayley form requires computing an inverse of the matrix $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$, which is the core computational bottleneck when $d$ (the neuron dimension) is large. A naive dense inverse costs roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ arithmetic operations and $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ memory for a $d\times d$ matrix, and this cost must be paid inside the training loop if we compute or differentiate through the inverse. The paper explicitly notes that this inverse "slightly limits the scalability of OFT" and uses block-diagonal parameterization as a mitigation strategy: partitioning $\bm{R}$ into $r$ independent blocks reduces the inversion to $r$ inversions of size $d/r$, lowering per-step cost roughly toward $\mathcal{O}(d^3/r^2)$ in the dense case and the parameter footprint from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or lower if block sharing is applied.

Two important constraints determine how far this block strategy can be pushed. First, the block-diagonal decomposition reduces computational cost but also reduces the expressivity of the allowed orthogonal transforms; small blocks impose a strong inductive bias. Second, the Cayley inverse remains present for each block, and if blocks are still moderately large (as is common inside large transformers or deep convolutional layers), the per-step cost may remain an obstacle for large-scale training.

The paper raises the open engineering and algorithmic question of how to "speed up this matrix inverse in a differentiable way." Practically, this suggests research directions such as (a) developing approximate but differentiable solvers for $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ that trade a controlled amount of accuracy for computational efficiency, (b) designing orthogonal parameterizations that avoid explicit dense inverses while preserving the desirable properties of Cayley, and (c) examining mixed strategies (smaller blocks in some layers, larger blocks in others) that align computation budgets with the layers that matter most for control. Any such strategy must preserve the identity initialization and maintain orthogonality (or a controlled relaxation thereof) through training so that hyperspherical-energy invariance still holds or is approximately held.

# Compositionality of orthogonal transforms across tasks

The paper observes an intriguing algebraic property: orthogonal matrices form a group under multiplication. Concretely, if a finetuning for task A produces $\bm{R}_A$ and task B produces $\bm{R}_B$, then the composed transform $\bm{R}_{B}\bm{R}_{A}$ is still orthogonal. This algebraic compositionality suggests a natural idea: store downstream-task adaptations as orthogonal factors and combine them by matrix multiplication to compose or switch behaviors.

However, the paper highlights this as an open problem: "Whether this set of orthogonal matrices preserve the knowledge of all the downstream tasks remains an interesting direction to study." Several subtleties make this nontrivial. First, matrix multiplication is non-commutative in general: $\bm{R}_A\bm{R}_B\neq\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$, so the order of applying task-specific transforms matters. If we simply multiply transforms from multiple tasks, the resulting behavior may prioritize the last-applied task or produce an unpredictable mixture.

Second, even though $\bm{R}_A$ and $\bm{R}_B$ individually preserve pairwise neuron angles relative to the pretrained model, their product may map neurons into directions that neither task intended. Formally, if the original pretrained neurons are $\{\bm{w}_i^0\}$ and task A transforms them to $\{\bm{R}_A\bm{w}_i^0\}$ and task B to $\{\bm{R}_B\bm{w}_i^0\}$, the composed transform yields $\{\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A\bm{w}_i^0\}$. While each transform preserves hyperspherical relationships in its own coordinate frame, there is no immediate guarantee that the composed transform will preserve the distinct task-specific behaviors simultaneously or that the generated image distributions for both tasks will remain intact when combined.

Understanding when composition is benign versus when it causes catastrophic interference is therefore an important open theoretical and empirical question. The paper points toward plausible structural conditions that could make composition successful. For example, if different tasks act approximately on orthogonal subspaces (which can be induced by block-diagonal parameterization where different blocks are dedicated to different tasks), their transforms will commute on those subspaces and composition is more likely to preserve each task's effect. Conversely, if tasks require conflicting rotations of the same neurons, composition may be fundamentally incompatible.

A line of research suggested by the paper is to (a) characterize when products of task-specific orthogonal matrices preserve the performance of the constituent tasks, (b) develop protocols for orthogonal factor storage and retrieval that minimize interference (for instance, enforcing block sparsity per task), and (c) design validation methodologies that can predict compositional success before deploying composed transforms.

# Beyond block-diagonal parameterization for parameter efficiency

Parameter efficiency in OFT, as described in the paper, is obtained primarily via block-diagonal structure and optional block sharing. Block-diagonalization reduces the number of free parameters needed to represent $\bm{R}$ from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to approximately $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ when using $r$ blocks, and further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ if the $r$ blocks are shared. This is an effective and simple route to cut cost, but the paper cautions that relying on block structure "inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility."

The central limitation is that block-diagonalization constrains interactions across blocks: neurons in different blocks cannot be rotated into each other's subspaces. This restriction can be helpful when the desired finetuning is local to particular channels or subspaces (as might be the case in some convolutional kernels), but it can also prevent necessary global rotations when the downstream task requires coordinated changes spanning many neurons.

The paper therefore highlights improving parameter efficiency "in a more effective and less biased way" as an open problem. Concretely, future work could aim to (a) identify alternative orthogonal parameterizations that retain global coupling with fewer parameters, (b) study shareable or factorized orthogonal structures that adaptively allocate capacity where needed, and (c) find automatic ways to decide block granularity per layer and per task. While the paper does not prescribe specific constructions, it implicitly encourages exploration of richer structured parameterizations that strike a better trade-off between expressivity and parsimony than fixed block-diagonal decomposition.

# Potential for multi-task and modular finetuning via orthogonal composition

Building on compositionality and parameter efficiency, OFT presents an appealing framework for modular multi-task finetuning. The idea is to represent each downstream task (or control modality) by a small orthogonal factor (or set of factors), keep the pretrained backbone fixed, and switch or combine these factors to yield task-specific behavior. Because orthogonal transforms preserve hyperspherical energy, this modular approach could allow many task-specific behaviors without corrupting the backbone's global geometry.

The paper mentions this potential and frames it as an open direction: whether a set of orthogonal transforms can be combined to "preserve the knowledge of all the downstream tasks." A modular pipeline built on OFT would need to address several engineering and theoretical questions. How should factors be stored and indexed so that switching is efficient? How to detect and prevent conflicts when some tasks require incompatible rotations of the same neurons? Can tasks share blocks or parameters to reduce total storage while avoiding interference?

One promising structural design hinted in the paper is to dedicate disjoint blocks or subspaces to different tasks, so that task-specific transforms act largely independently and can be composed. Another approach is hierarchical factorization: learn small per-layer factors that mostly modify local geometric structure; composing many small factors could then approximate more complex global transforms while keeping each module compact. Importantly, the paper notes that orthogonal composition preserves algebraic structure (group closure), which is an enabling property for modular designs; formalizing the conditions under which modularity succeeds is an important line of future work.

# Integration with other control modalities and multi-conditioning

The experiments in the paper demonstrate OFT on two families of downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation (Canny edges, segmentation maps, landmarks). OFT is attractive in this context because it introduces no inference-time overhead: the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weights to yield an equivalent dense weight matrix for deployment.

A natural direction is to extend OFT to richer multi-conditioning settings where several modalities must be satisfied simultaneously (for example: a personalized subject token + a segmentation map + textual attributes). The paper suggests OFT is compatible with such combinations because it is model-agnostic and can be applied to the same layers used by other adapter-style approaches. But practical multi-conditioning raises new challenges: different control signals may impose conflicting demands on the model's representations, and balancing these can require more flexibility than a single shared orthogonal factor affords.

The paper also introduces a re-scaled OFT variant in which a diagonal magnitude matrix $\bm{D}$ is learned in addition to $\bm{R}$:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x},
\]
and notes that re-scaling does not change hyperspherical energy but increases flexibility. This suggests a pragmatic route for integrating multiple controls: combine an orthogonal component (to preserve angle relationships) with a small, task-specific magnitude or adapter (to modulate strengths where needed). The re-scaled variant thus points to an architectural principle: keep angular invariants stable while allowing limited, structured amplitude changes to accommodate multiple conditioning signals.

The open research problems are how to (a) allocate orthogonal and non-orthogonal degrees of freedom across modalities, (b) discover training curricula and loss terms that balance fidelity to each control signal, and (c) measure and certify that adding modalities does not destroy the pretrained model's generality. The paper's experiments—where OFT is applied to several control tasks and compared favorably to heavier adapters—indicate promise, but systematic study across many simultaneous controls remains to be done.

# Bridging theory and practice: formalizing semantic preservation guarantees

One of the conceptual anchors of OFT is the use of hyperspherical energy (HE) as a structural surrogate for semantic generation ability. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, the hyperspherical energy used in the paper is:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
A central, provable invariance exploited by OFT is that applying the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to all neurons preserves all pairwise inner products and hence preserves HE:
\[
\langle \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i, \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j \rangle = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\, \hat{\bm{w}}_j = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j.
\]
Since the pairwise cosines are unchanged, any HE functional that depends only on pairwise angular relationships (for instance via chord distances on the unit sphere) is invariant under the shared orthogonal action. The paper leverages this fact to motivate OFT as a method that preserves a geometry believed to encode semantic structure.

But this invariance is only a first step. The deeper theoretical question is to formalize the relationship between HE (or related geometric quantities of neurons) and the downstream semantics of generated images. The paper hypothesizes and empirically observes that preserving HE helps preserve pretrained generative ability, but a formal quantitative guarantee is not provided. Bridging this gap is an important open problem.

Possible theoretical directions suggested by the paper's perspective include deriving bounds that relate changes in HE (or other neuron-angle-based metrics) to changes in the model's forward mappings or in distributions over generated images. For example, one could attempt to bound perturbations in denoising network outputs in diffusion models as a function of angular perturbations of weights, perhaps by exploiting layerwise Lipschitz constants or local linearizations. Another promising direction is to make precise when HE preservation implies preservation of certain classes of downstream behaviors (e.g., semantic content as measured by particular perceptual embeddings). The paper implicitly points toward studying such connections empirically and theoretically.

The paper already provides a useful theoretical baseline: orthogonal transforms provably preserve all pairwise neuron inner products and HE. Building on that, the open theoretical agenda is to convert these structural invariances into operational guarantees about semantic fidelity, sample diversity, and control fidelity, or else to identify counterexamples that delineate the limits of what HE preservation can promise.

# Concluding summary of open problems

The OFT framework presents a conceptually simple but powerful constraint: finetune by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy) remain unchanged. This yields strong empirical benefits in preserving generative semantics, improving finetuning stability, and accelerating convergence, as demonstrated in subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.

At the same time, OFT opens several concrete lines of future work that the paper explicitly identifies or strongly suggests:

- Computational scalability: speeding up or approximating the Cayley inverse (or finding alternative orthogonal parameterizations) in a differentiable and efficient manner, especially for very large layers.
- Compositionality: understanding when and how products of task-specific orthogonal transforms can be composed to preserve multiple downstream behaviors without destructive interference.
- Parameter efficiency beyond block-diagonalization: finding representations that are both compact and expressive without imposing overly strong inductive biases.
- Multi-task and modular designs: exploiting the orthogonal-group structure to build switchable, composable task adapters while controlling task interference.
- Multi-conditioning integration: combining OFT with additional small amplitude modulations or adapter mechanisms (for example, the re-scaled variant) to handle multiple simultaneous control signals.
- Theoretical foundations: formalizing quantitative guarantees that link hyperspherical-energy preservation to preservation of semantic generation abilities and to controlled perturbations of diffusion outputs.

Each of these directions balances algorithmic design, practical engineering, and theoretical analysis. The paper provides both the motivating empirical evidence and the basic mathematical invariances; pursuing the open problems above is a natural next step to broaden OFT's applicability and to turn its empirical benefits into provable and scalable tools for controlling large generative models.
