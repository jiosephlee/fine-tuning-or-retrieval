\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: Existing Finetuning Strategies and Their Limits

# Full finetuning with small learning rates

One of the most straightforward approaches to adapt a large pretrained generative model to a downstream task is to update (some or all of) its weights directly via gradient descent. In practice, practitioners commonly use very small learning rates and a modest number of gradient steps. The small learning rate is an implicit regularizer: by moving weights only a small Euclidean distance from their pretrained values the finetuning procedure hopes to retain the pretrained model’s general generative abilities while enabling task-specific adaptation.

Mathematically, this implicit objective can be thought of as encouraging a small value of the Euclidean norm
\[
\| \mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^0 \|
\]
where $\mathbf{M}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights and $\mathbf{M}$ denotes the finetuned weights. The intuition is simple and intuitive: large changes in the parameter vector are risky, so keep updates small to avoid destroying what the pretrained model has learned.

However, the paper documents important limits of this strategy in the context of text-to-image diffusion models. First, small-step full finetuning can be unstable over longer runs: the authors observe that methods using this naive finetuning strategy (for example, variants of DreamBooth-style weight updates) can behave well early in training but may collapse or lose fidelity with additional iterations. Empirically, DreamBooth-style finetuning in the paper initially achieves reasonable subject control but, after extended finetuning (e.g., thousands of iterations), begins to generate collapsed or degraded images. Second, full finetuning is often computationally expensive: updating many parameters requires more compute and storage during training and can be impractical when repeated for many personalization tasks. Finally, the restriction to “small Euclidean changes” is only an implicit regularization and gives no guarantee that the semantic relational structure embedded in the pretrained model is preserved; the Euclidean proximity metric is agnostic to the geometry of neuron relationships that may actually encode semantics.

# Adapter-based methods (e.g., LoRA, Adapters)

Adapter-based finetuning methods introduce a small number of extra parameters and leave the majority of the pretrained model fixed. A representative approach is LoRA, which models the finetuning update to a weight matrix as an additive low-rank matrix. Concretely, for a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, LoRA parameterizes an additive update of rank $r'$ and the number of trainable parameters for that update is on the order of
\[
r'(d+n).
\]
This low-rank additive reparameterization is attractive because it drastically reduces the number of learned parameters, making finetuning cheap in memory and often effective in practice.

Adapters trade off flexibility for parameter efficiency: by restricting the update to a low-rank subspace, they prevent arbitrary large or high-complexity changes to the pretrained weights. This usually helps preserve much of the pretrained model’s behavior while enabling task adaptation. But the paper highlights practical and conceptual limitations. Empirically, LoRA sometimes fails to preserve identity or semantics in subject-driven generation tasks; in examples reported by the authors, LoRA could lose subject identity (e.g., generating the wrong subject attributes) even when tuned carefully. In controllable generation tasks, LoRA converges more slowly and reaches a lower performance ceiling compared to the orthogonal finetuning approach introduced in the paper.

Conceptually, adapter-style methods still rest on the assumption that a restricted additive change (e.g., low-rank) is an appropriate structural prior. That prior can be effective, but it is a different inductive bias: it favors updates that are representable as small-rank additions. Such a constraint does not explicitly preserve angular or relational structure among neurons; consequently, it provides no formal guarantee that certain geometric properties of neuron directions are maintained.

# Task-specific controllers (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter)

For controllable generation—where the model must respect supplementary signals such as edges, segmentations, or pose maps—researchers have also developed specialized controller modules. Two concrete examples discussed in the paper are ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. These methods finetune and add modules that process the control signal and inject the conditioning into the diffusion model. In practice they enable high-quality conditional generation that follows the external control inputs closely.

Despite their capabilities, task-specific controllers have notable drawbacks highlighted by the authors. First, many controller-based solutions add a substantial number of parameters and auxiliary computation, often increasing inference-time complexity. ControlNet, for example, involves nontrivial additional components that increase model size and computational cost. Second, the paper reports that adapter-style controllers tend to converge slowly and can exhibit abrupt learning dynamics: the controllability metric sometimes suddenly improves (“sudden emergence”) after several epochs rather than improving smoothly. This makes hyperparameter tuning and reliable training more difficult in practice.

Finally, while controllers excel at the specific control task for which they are designed, they introduce a heavier architectural change compared to lightweight finetuning approaches. The extra modules and their integration points add engineering complexity and inference overhead that may be undesirable when the goal is to produce a compact, deployable finetuned model.

# Assumption of Euclidean proximity to pretrained weights

Across the full-fineturning and many adapter-based strategies there is an implicit or explicit assumption: keeping the finetuned weights close in Euclidean distance to the pretrained weights is a good proxy for preserving the pretrained model’s semantic and generative capabilities. This assumption motivates small learning rates, proximal regularizers, and low-magnitude additive updates.

However, the paper argues this assumption is incomplete and sometimes misleading. Euclidean proximity does not directly capture relational structure among neurons—information encoded in directions (angles) on a high-dimensional sphere. Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm yet have very different angular configurations among their column neurons; conversely, a nontrivial orthogonal rotation can change the Euclidean coordinates substantially while preserving pairwise angles exactly. Thus Euclidean distance alone can either over-restrict updates (preventing useful adaptations) or under-characterize harmful changes (allowing semantic disruption that a mere small L2 shift does not detect).

The practical consequences of relying solely on Euclidean proximity are visible in training instability and model collapse: even when updates are bounded in norm, the semantic structure needed for the pretrained model’s general generation capability can be altered, producing artifacts or loss of identity. The paper documents such phenomena in subject-driven and controllable finetuning baselines.

# Need for a structural preservation measure beyond Euclidean distance

To address the gap left by Euclidean proximity, the paper motivates and defines a structural preservation criterion grounded in neuron relational geometry. The key insight is that angular relationships—how neuron weight vectors point relative to each other on the unit hypersphere—encode critical semantic information. Prior empirical observations (summarized in the paper) show that hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity between neuron vectors, correlates with semantic properties in representations. The paper demonstrates intuitively and empirically (e.g., a toy autoencoder experiment) that angular information of neurons can reconstruct semantic content while magnitudes often carry little of that structure.

The paper formalizes a scalar measure called hyperspherical energy (HE) for a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}=\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Let normalized neurons be $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$. Then the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \| \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \|^{-1}.
\]
HE captures the global pairwise angular relationships among neurons (it is a function of pairwise hyperspherical distance or cosine similarities). The authors propose that a desirable finetuning outcome is to keep the finetuned model’s hyperspherical energy close to that of the pretrained model, i.e., to minimize
\[
\left\| \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0) \right\|.
\]
A crucial mathematical fact that the paper leverages is that hyperspherical energy is invariant to orthogonal transformations applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer. If $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$) and $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, then the normalized columns of $\mathbf{W}$ are rotated versions of those of $\mathbf{W}^0$, and therefore $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)$. This observation motivates a new finetuning parameterization: instead of adding small Euclidean perturbations or low-rank additives, learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and replace the forward pass of a layer from $(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$ to
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
\]
Such orthogonal finetuning (OFT) provably preserves hyperspherical energy and therefore maintains the pairwise neuron-angle structure that the paper argues is closely tied to semantic generative performance.

Beyond pure orthogonality, the paper also motivates variants that explicitly combine structural and Euclidean considerations. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit bounded-deviation constraint $\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \| \le \epsilon$ so that the learned orthogonal transform remains close to the identity, thereby limiting Euclidean deviation as an additional safety measure. Re-scaled OFT further allows learnable positive scalings per neuron (a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D}$) so that the forward pass becomes $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}$; these scalings do not change hyperspherical energy (they are normalized out) but give extra flexibility to adapt magnitudes when appropriate.

Taken together, these structural preservation ideas point to why existing finetuning recipes—small-step full finetuning, low-rank adapters, and task-specific controllers—leave an important gap. They either lack an explicit mechanism to preserve the angular neuron relationships shown to encode semantics, or they introduce additional complexity and inference overhead. The orthogonality-based approach in the paper is presented as a principled middle ground: it enforces preservation of hyperspherical energy (a structural geometric invariant tied to semantics) while providing a constrained, computationally efficient parameterization for effective adaptation.
