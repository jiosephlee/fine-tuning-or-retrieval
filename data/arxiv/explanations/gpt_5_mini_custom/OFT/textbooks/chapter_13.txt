\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 13: Connections, Ablations, and Theoretical Insights

# OFT as multiplicative vs LoRA as additive low-rank update

One of the clearest ways to understand Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to contrast its parameterization with the popular adapter-style method LoRA. LoRA models a finetuned weight as an additive update to the pretrained weight:
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta,\qquad \Delta \approx \bm{A}\bm{B}
\]
where \(\Delta\) is implemented or constrained to be low-rank (e.g., \(\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}, \bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r'\times n}\)). The low-rank assumption explicitly limits the degrees of freedom in the additive change, which acts as a regularizer: the pretrained weights remain the dominant term and the learned correction is compact.

OFT takes a fundamentally different route: it treats the finetuned weights as a multiplicative transformation of the pretrained weights,
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\]
with \(\bm{R}\) constrained to be orthogonal (\(\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\)). This is not an additive correction but a learned change of the canonical coordinate system in which neurons (columns of \(\bm{W}^0\)) are expressed. Because orthogonal transformations preserve angles on the unit hypersphere, OFT explicitly preserves pairwise neuron angular relationships (and therefore the hyperspherical energy) while still permitting directional changes of neurons that can alter semantic behavior. Initializing \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) ensures the finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained model.

Viewed another way, additive updates (LoRA) restrict the amount and pattern of change by constraining \(\Delta\), whereas OFT restricts the geometric nature of change — only those changes that can be implemented by an orthogonal coordinate transformation are allowed. This multiplicative constraint trades off some flexibility (orthogonality is a strong structural constraint) for a different and often practically useful regularity: pairwise neuron angles are preserved exactly, which the paper argues is tied to preserving pretrained generative capability.

# When OFT induces effective low rank via \((\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\)

Although OFT is multiplicative at its core, it can be interpreted in an additive form as well. Expanding \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) around the identity gives
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
\]
The additive term is \((\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\). If \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\) is low-rank, then the additive correction is low-rank even when \(\bm{W}^0\) is full-rank. This is important for two reasons.

First, many of the desirable properties of low‑rank adapters (parameter efficiency, limited capacity to deviate from pretrained behavior) can be recovered within OFT by controlling the structure of \(\bm{R}\). For instance, if \(\bm{R}\) is block-diagonal with small blocks or otherwise constrained so that \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\) has small effective rank, then the effective additive update inherits low-rank structure. Thus OFT can mimic the “small corrective” behavior of LoRA while still preserving hyperspherical angles exactly.

Second, because \(\bm{W}^0\) is typically full-rank in practice, the rank of \((\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\) is upper-bounded by the rank of \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\). In other words, the capacity of the additive correction induced by OFT is governed primarily by the structure of \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\), not by \(\bm{W}^0\). This means that by designing \(\bm{R}\) (block sizes, sharing, or explicit low-rank parameterizations of deviations from identity) we can directly control the effective rank of the change, and hence trade off between parameter efficiency and finetuning flexibility.

The paper makes this concrete by using block-diagonal and shared-block parameterizations of \(\bm{R}\). Those parameter choices reduce the number of free parameters and encourage structured, low-complexity deviations from identity, which in turn produce effective low-rank additive updates while retaining the exact hyperspherical invariance of the columns of \(\bm{W}\).

# Convergence on hypersphere manifolds and smooth optimization landscapes

A central conceptual point in the paper is that OFT finetunes neuron directions by applying orthogonal transforms; this can be interpreted as optimizing on a hyperspherical manifold rather than in unconstrained Euclidean space. Constraining directions to a manifold tends to produce smoother optimization landscapes: the feasible set is compact and well‑structured, gradients respect the geometry of the manifold, and the learning dynamics avoid certain degenerate directions that would correspond to uncontrolled scaling or collapsing of neuron directions.

Intuitively, when directions (angles) are the primary degrees of freedom carrying semantic information, restricting updates to rotations and reflections focuses the optimization on the parameters that matter for semantic change while avoiding destructive deformations that alter magnitudes or pairwise angular relationships arbitrarily. The paper notes that this yields faster and more stable convergence in practice: empirical comparisons show OFT and its constrained variant COFT reach good control performance in fewer training iterations and with smoother training curves than methods that permit unconstrained additive updates. The authors attribute faster convergence both to the fact that directional changes are the most semantically effective (drawing on the toy autoencoder experiment) and to the improved geometry induced by the orthogonality constraint.

The constrained manifold also reduces the tendency for collapse during finetuning. Because orthogonal transforms preserve neuron norms and pairwise angles, they prevent many pathways that would otherwise dramatically alter internal representations and break pretrained generative behavior. This geometric regularization manifests empirically as stable finetuning across a wide range of iteration counts and hyperparameters.

# Why not minimize hyperspherical energy in finetuning

Hyperspherical energy (HE) measures aggregate pairwise similarity among normalized neurons:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},\qquad \hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
In some contexts, such as learning classification networks from scratch, minimizing HE (pushing neurons toward uniform angular spacing on the hypersphere) can be beneficial because it reduces redundancy and promotes generalization. Indeed, prior work cited in the paper exploits orthogonal parameterizations to keep HE small during training.

However, for finetuning a pretrained generative model the objective is different: we wish to adapt the model to a downstream task while preserving the pretrained model’s rich semantic structure. Minimizing HE during finetuning would force neurons toward a uniform angular configuration, which risks destroying the specialized angular relationships that encode the pretrained model’s generative knowledge. In essence, minimizing HE could erase or scramble the semantic wiring learned during pretraining, undermining generation quality or diversity.

OFT takes the opposite stance: rather than attempting to minimize HE, OFT explicitly preserves HE. An orthogonal transformation applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer leaves pairwise angles unchanged, so OFT guarantees the finetuned model has exactly the same HE as the pretrained model (up to numerical precision). This preservation is purposefully chosen because the paper hypothesizes — and empirically observes — that HE captures important semantic structure that should be retained when adapting large diffusion models to downstream control tasks.

Thus the distinction is conceptual and practical: minimizing HE is a training objective for certain problems (e.g., to induce neuron diversity in classification); preserving HE is a stability objective for finetuning pretrained generative models so that the adaptation is constrained to meaningful, angle-preserving transformations.

# Ablations: removing orthogonality, varying epsilon, block sizes

The paper presents a set of ablation studies that probe which components of OFT are essential and how hyperparameters affect performance. These ablations illuminate the trade-offs between flexibility and stability.

Removing orthogonality. One striking ablation is OFT without the orthogonality constraint. In this variant the transformation matrix is unconstrained, so the optimizer can freely change neuron directions and magnitudes. Empirically (Figure referenced in the paper), removing the orthogonality constraint led to failed finetuning: the model produced unrealistic images and did not achieve the intended control effect. This supports the claim that orthogonality is not merely a mathematical convenience but a stabilizing constraint that prevents destructive updates to neuron geometry. The orthogonality constraint both preserves hyperspherical energy and curbs degenerate changes that additive unconstrained updates might induce.

Varying \(\epsilon\) in COFT. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments OFT with an explicit radius/deviation constraint:
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon.
\]
In practice, COFT is implemented by parameterizing \(\bm{R}\) via the Cayley transform \(\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\bm{Q}\) skew-symmetric, approximating \(\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)\), and constraining \(\|\bm{Q}\|\) during optimization. The ablation varying \(\epsilon\) reveals a continuous trade-off: small \(\epsilon\) yields very conservative finetuning that stays close to the pretrained model (high stability but limited task adaptation), while large \(\epsilon\) approaches the flexibility of unconstrained OFT (stronger adaptation but greater potential to deviate). The paper shows that intermediate \(\epsilon\) values give a desirable sweet spot: sufficient capacity to adapt to downstream control while retaining stability and insensitivity to the precise number of training iterations. Importantly, COFT makes the implicit “stay close to pretrained model” constraint explicit and tunable, easing hyperparameter selection.

Varying block sizes in \(\bm{R}\). To control parameter complexity, OFT uses block-diagonal orthogonal matrices:
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
with each \(\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)}\). The block size (or equivalently the number of blocks \(r\)) is an important ablation axis. Smaller blocks (larger \(r\)) reduce parameter count and impose stronger structure: each block can independently rotate a subset of neuron coordinates. Sharing blocks across channels further reduces parameters. Empirically, block parameterization preserves hyperspherical invariance while trading off some flexibility. The ablations indicate that judicious block sizing yields parameter-efficient models that still adapt effectively; making blocks too small can over-regularize and reduce final performance, whereas making blocks too large increases parameter count and can reduce the implicit low-rank behavior that helps preserve pretrained structure. The paper’s experiments pick block sizes to balance these considerations and demonstrate improved performance and sample complexity compared to baselines.

Collectively, these ablations show orthogonality is essential for stability, \(\epsilon\) controls flexibility versus conservatism, and block structure is the practical handle for parameter efficiency versus representational capacity.

# Channel-wise interpretations for convolution (depthwise-like behavior)

Although much of the OFT exposition uses fully connected layers as the running example, OFT is applicable to convolutional layers. The block-diagonal parameterization of \(\bm{R}\) admits a natural channel-wise interpretation in convolutional settings.

When the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block corresponds to a transformation applied to a single input channel. In that regime, OFT effectively applies a per-channel orthogonal transform on the channel-wise neuron vectors; this is analogous to learning depthwise convolutions or channel-wise linear transforms that change how spatial filters combine channel information while keeping cross-channel mixing constrained. This per-channel (depthwise-like) perspective makes OFT particularly parameter-efficient for convolutional layers, since each block operates on a lower-dimensional subspace (the per-channel filter dimensions) and requires far fewer parameters than a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix.

On the other hand, if all blocks are shared across channels (block-sharing), OFT applies the same orthogonal transformation across channels. This produces a different bias: instead of independent per-channel rotations, the model applies a global rotation that treats channels homogeneously. Which choice (per-channel, shared blocks, or intermediate block sizes) is best depends on the task and desired balance between expressivity and regularity. The paper reports preliminary experiments showing that these convolutional interpretations make sense empirically and allow OFT to be deployed in convolutional parts of diffusion models with favorable parameter and compute trade-offs.

The channel-wise view also clarifies why OFT can be expressive enough to control generation: rotating per-channel neuron directions can change the semantic function implemented by a set of convolutional filters without changing their pairwise angular relationships, enabling content-preserving but semantics-adjusting transformations that manifest as controlled changes in generated images.

# Summary: trade-offs, compositionality, and practical guidance

This chapter has tied together theoretical insights and empirical ablations to clarify why OFT works and how it compares to alternatives. OFT’s multiplicative, orthogonality-constrained parameterization preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, which the authors argue is crucial for retaining pretrained generative capabilities while enabling directional changes that implement control. In its additive guise \((\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\), OFT can induce effective low-rank corrections when \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\) is structured, reconciling some of the benefits of low-rank adapters with the geometric invariance OFT enforces.

Empirically, orthogonality is essential: removing it breaks stability and produces poor images. COFT’s \(\epsilon\)-constraint offers a practical knob for trading flexibility against preservation of pretrained behavior, and block-diagonal parameterizations let practitioners control parameter cost and induce channel-wise inductive biases reminiscent of depthwise operations in convolutions.

A few practical takeaways follow from the paper’s insights and ablations. If you want conservative finetuning that is robust to hyperparameter choices and fewer epochs, favor COFT with a modest \(\epsilon\). If you need extra flexibility, increase block sizes or \(\epsilon\) at the cost of some risk. When applying OFT to convolutional layers, consider per-channel blocks for parameter efficiency and intuitive depthwise-like behavior. Finally, OFT’s orthogonal matrices compose: multiplying orthogonal matrices yields another orthogonal matrix, suggesting potential compositional uses (e.g., sequentially applying multiple task-specific OFT transforms), though whether composed transforms preserve all downstream tasks’ knowledge remains an open question the paper highlights.

Together, these theoretical and empirical pieces explain the stability, sample efficiency, and controllability improvements observed with OFT in the paper’s subject-driven and controllable generation experiments.
