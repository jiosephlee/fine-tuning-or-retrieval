\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 4: Geometry of Neural Representations: Hyperspheres and Energy

# Neuron normalization and cosine similarity

A convenient and revealing geometric viewpoint of neural representations is to consider each neuron (or filter, or weight vector) as a point on a unit hypersphere. Concretely, given a neuron represented by a weight vector $\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its normalized direction is
\[
\hat{\bm{w}} := \frac{\bm{w}}{\|\bm{w}\|},
\]
which places the neuron on the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Measuring relationships between neurons then becomes a question about angles on that hypersphere. The standard measure of angular similarity is cosine similarity:
\[
\cos\theta_{ij} = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j,
\]
which depends only on the directions of the two neurons and not on their magnitudes. This decoupling—direction versus magnitude—lets us focus on the angular geometry that, as the paper argues, carries semantic information.

Normalizing neurons to the sphere (either explicitly in a model or implicitly by analyzing normalized weights) highlights angular structure: two neurons with small angular separation (large cosine similarity) respond in similar directions in feature space, and neurons that are nearly orthogonal encode complementary directions. Because cosine similarity is invariant to positive rescaling of vectors, analyses that rely on cosine remove the confounding effect of neuron magnitude and concentrate on the geometry of directions. This focus is particularly useful when we want to reason about semantic preservation during finetuning: changes that rotate neurons on the hypersphere alter the angular relationships, whereas rescaling leaves them intact.

# Hyperspherical energy: definition and interpretation

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar summary that captures the global pairwise angular relationships among all neurons in a layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, define normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy used in the paper is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Because $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is a function of the angle between $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$, HE summarizes pairwise angular proximity: pairs of neurons that are close on the hypersphere (small geodesic/Euclidean distance) contribute large terms to HE.

There is an equivalent way to read HE in terms of cosine similarity. Using the identity
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2(1 - \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j),
\]
each summand $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ increases as the cosine similarity $\hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j$ approaches $1$. Thus HE penalizes highly aligned neuron pairs and, in aggregate, measures how clustered or unevenly distributed the neurons are on the sphere.

HE is an interpretable global statistic: a large HE indicates there exist many pairs of neurons that are very close in direction (redundancy or clustering), whereas a small HE indicates the neurons are more uniformly spread over the hypersphere. Because HE depends only on normalized vectors, it is insensitive to rescaling of individual neurons—only directions matter.

# Energy, uniformity, and semantic preservation

The notion of uniformity on the hypersphere is tightly linked to hyperspherical energy. If neurons are uniformly distributed on the sphere, they maximize mutual separation and thus minimize HE. In certain learning contexts (for example, classification networks where diversity among features can help generalization), minimizing HE towards uniformity has been proposed as a useful inductive bias. However, the paper carefully distinguishes two different goals: learning from scratch versus finetuning a pretrained model.

When finetuning a large pretrained generator such as a text-to-image diffusion model, the objective is not to re-arrange neurons into the most uniform layout, but rather to preserve the pretrained model's generative knowledge while enabling controlled adjustments for downstream tasks. The paper argues that the pretrained model encodes semantic generation ability in the pairwise angular relationships among neurons; therefore, drastically changing HE during finetuning risks destroying that semantic structure.

A central invariance motivates the chosen finetuning strategy: orthogonal transformations preserve angles. If $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$), and we apply the same $\bm{R}$ to every neuron in a layer, producing $\bm{W}'=\bm{R}\bm{W}$, then norms and inner products are preserved:
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\| = \|\bm{w}_i\|,\qquad (\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j)=\bm{w}_i^\top\bm{w}_j.
\]
Consequently, the normalized neurons are rotated by the same orthogonal map and pairwise angles (and therefore HE) remain unchanged. This invariance is the foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): by restricting adaptations to layer-shared orthogonal transformations, one can alter neuron directions in a structured way while provably preserving HE and thus maintaining the pretrained angular geometry that encodes semantic ability.

At the same time, the paper recognizes that preserving HE exactly may be too permissive: an arbitrary orthogonal transformation can rotate neuron configurations in ways that, while preserving angles among neurons, create an overall model that deviates significantly in Euclidean terms from the pretrained weights. To reconcile these needs, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds a radius (deviation) constraint that keeps the orthogonal map close to the identity, trading some flexibility for improved stability while still preserving hyperspherical energy.

# Empirical evidence: angular features capture semantics

Several empirical observations in the paper motivate focusing on angular (directional) information rather than magnitudes. Prior empirical results referenced in the work indicate that angular features (for example, normalized features or cosine similarity) often correspond well with semantic relationships. The intuition is that when neurons or features are normalized onto a hypersphere, their directions capture the salient discriminative and semantic structure, while magnitudes are less informative or act as scale factors.

Within the paper's experiments, the authors report that manipulating directions (angles) of neurons is an effective way to modify semantics in generators, while unconstrained changes in weights (including magnitudes) are more likely to damage pretrained generative performance. Orthogonal transformations directly act on directions, rotating the entire coordinate system for neurons in a layer. This provides a natural mechanism for introducing task-specific semantic edits (such as inserting a new subject identity or conditioning on an extra control map) without breaking the pretrained angular geometry.

The paper also presents comparative finetuning evidence: methods that do not control angular geometry—standard finetuning or certain additive low-rank updates—can lead to unstable convergence or collapse, whereas OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably, and preserve image semantics better. This empirical behavior supports the hypothesis that preserving angular relationships (as summarized by HE) is important for maintaining the pretrained model's semantics during finetuning.

# Toy autoencoder study: angle vs magnitude for reconstruction

To make the role of angle versus magnitude concrete, the paper describes a small toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During training the network uses the usual inner product between convolutional kernels and input patches to form feature activations. At test time the authors compare three ways of computing the feature map from the same learned convolutional kernels and the same input:

1. The original inner product (the same computation used during training).
2. Using only magnitude information of the kernel outputs.
3. Using only angular information (i.e., cosine of the angle between kernel and patch).

Although the network was trained with inner products and not with cosine activation explicitly, the empirical outcome is striking: using angular information alone almost perfectly reconstructs the input images, whereas using only magnitudes yields little useful information. That is, replacing the inner product with a cosine-like measure preserves most of the semantic content required for reconstruction, while keeping only scalar magnitudes loses it.

This toy study supports the claim that directions of neurons encode the bulk of semantic information: the geometry of how weight directions align with inputs determines the meaningful part of the representation. Thus, finetuning methods that act on directions—in a controlled fashion—are well positioned to change semantics where desired while retaining learned capabilities elsewhere.

# Fourier phase vs magnitude analogy for semantics

The paper draws an instructive analogy between neuron direction versus magnitude and the phase versus magnitude decomposition of a signal in the Fourier domain. In a 2D Fourier transform of an image, the complex spectrum can be decomposed into amplitude (magnitude) and phase components. Empirically, the phase spectrum carries much of the structural and semantic content of the image: swapping magnitudes while preserving phase still yields an image that retains recognizable structure.

Translating this intuition to neural representations, the paper suggests that the angular (phase-like) information of neurons plays the key role in encoding semantics of the generator. Magnitudes behave like a separate degree of freedom that is less central to semantic content. Orthogonal Finetuning is therefore designed to modify the angular part of neuron weights (the analogue of phase) while preserving pairwise angular relations that underpin pretrained semantic generation. Re-scaled OFT (an extension discussed in the paper) even allows learning per-neuron magnitude scaling after orthogonal rotation; because HE only depends on directions, adding such diagonal scaling does not affect hyperspherical energy, and thus magnitude adaptation can be incorporated without breaking the angular geometry.

This phase/magnitude analogy supplies an intuitive rationale for OFT: to achieve controlled semantic change, adjust the "phase" (directions) in a structured, energy-preserving way, and optionally adjust "magnitudes" afterwards for fine-grained calibration. The empirical results in the main experiments back up this theoretical intuition: methods that preserve angular geometry (OFT/COFT) produce stable, semantically faithful finetuning for subject-driven generation and controllable generation, converging faster and with fewer failures than unconstrained alternatives.

---

Together, these concepts present a geometric framework for thinking about neural finetuning: view neurons as points on a hypersphere, quantify their global arrangement via hyperspherical energy, and design finetuning updates that respect or controllably alter that angular geometry. This perspective yields practical algorithms (OFT and COFT) and explains why angular-preserving transformations can maintain pretrained semantics while enabling targeted adaptations.
