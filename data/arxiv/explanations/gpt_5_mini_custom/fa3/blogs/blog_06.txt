\title{Blogs about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

# Reading the Benchmarks: 740 TFLOPs/s, 1.2 PFLOPs/s FP8, and What That Means for You

## TL;DR
FlashAttention-3 (FA3) rethinks the attention kernel to exploit asynchronous hardware (Tensor Cores + TMA) and FP8 Tensor Core support on Hopper (H100). The headline results from the paper:

- FP16 forward performance: 1.5–2.0× faster than FlashAttention-2, reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s (≈75% of H100 theoretical Tensor FLOPs).
- FP8 forward performance: approaches 1.2 PFLOPs/s on H100.
- Numerical stability: FP8 FA3 with block quantization + incoherent processing reduces FP8 attention error by ~2.6× versus a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling.
- Compared to standard (materialize-intermediates) attention, FA3 can be 3–16× faster depending on sequence length and configuration, and for medium/long sequences it even outperforms a vendor-optimized cuDNN implementation.

Below I unpack what these numbers mean in practice, why FA3 achieves them, and how to think about FP16 vs FP8 choices for long-context workloads.

## Where FlashAttention-3 wins — and why
FA3 gains its speed from three core ideas that are tightly matched to H100-style hardware:

1. Warp-specialized producer-consumer asynchrony
   - On Hopper, both data movement (TMA) and matmul (WGMMA / Tensor Core) are asynchronous units. FA3 splits CTAs into producer and consumer warp groups: producers stream blocks of Q/K/V into shared memory while consumers run the compute kernels. This hides memory latency and keeps Tensor Cores fed.
2. Overlapping softmax with GEMM via intra-CTA pipelining
   - Softmax has relatively few FLOPs but uses special functions (exponential), which on H100 are much lower throughput than Tensor Core matmuls. FA3 pipelines two GEMMs and the softmax across iterations (a 2-stage pipelining, with a possible 3-stage variant), so the exponential and reductions execute while the Tensor Cores are busy on the next block. This increases Tensor Core utilization (e.g., 570 → 620–640 TFLOPs/s in one reported case).
3. Targeted FP8 support with layout fixes and accuracy tricks
   - H100 FP8 WGMMA requires operands be k-major; FA3 uses in-kernel transposes (LDSM / STSM) and register permutations so FP8 Tensor Cores can be used without expensive global-memory transposes. To control FP8 quantization error, FA3 applies block quantization (one scale per block) and incoherent processing (multiply Q and K by a structured random orthogonal transform such as diagonal ±1 times Hadamard) to "spread out" outliers. These preserve correctness of QK^T while dramatically improving per-block quantization error.

Taken together, these make FA3 much closer to the hardware peak than FA2. The paper reports FP16 reaching ~75% utilization on H100 and FP8 approaching ~1.2 PFLOPs/s — showing the advantage of combining asynchrony + FP8.

## How head dimension and causal masking affect performance
Two common variables significantly change throughput and relative advantage:

- Head dimension (per-head hidden size): the paper reports experiments for head dims 64, 128, and 256.
  - Smaller head dims (e.g., 64) have less arithmetic per tile and therefore can be more memory-bound / more sensitive to kernel overheads. For some settings FA3 FP8 was ahead at head dim 64.
  - Larger head dims (128, 256) produce larger GEMMs where the Tensor Cores dominate; here FP16 FA3 consistently shows strong wins. For FP8, FA3 is competitive for many large-sequence cases but can be behind cuDNN in some causal-masked small-sequence settings (this is partly due to FP8 FA3 not having a persistent kernel/load-balancing optimization implemented for benchmarks).
- Causal masking (autoregressive attention) roughly halves the amount of computed entries (triangular attention), so memory/layout and load balancing become more important. FA3 still offers large speedups, but:
  - For small sequence lengths with causal masking, FP8 FA3 can be less favorable against highly-optimized vendor kernels, because the FP8 implementation in the paper lacks a persistent kernel and certain small-seq optimizations. For large sequence lengths (1k+ tokens) FA3 tends to outpace cuDNN in FP16 and be competitive in FP8 on many head-dim choices.

In short: FA3 shines for medium-to-large sequence lengths where the extra arithmetic per block lets the kernel amortize overheads and exploit pipelining. The per-head dimension alters the arithmetic-to-memory ratio; larger head dims generally favor higher Tensor Core utilization and therefore larger absolute gains.

## Throughput, cost, and energy — practical trade-offs for FP16 vs FP8

Throughput: FP8 can deliver nearly 2× raw Tensor Core peak throughput relative to FP16 on Hopper (hence the up-to ~1.2 PFLOPs/s number). FA3’s FP8 path realizes that hardware advantage while maintaining much better numeric behavior than naive per-tensor FP8.

Cost (time/money): if you are optimizing inference latency or cost per token:
- FP8 FA3 can cut runtime significantly on large-scale attention, provided you can accept the engineering work (or adopt the FA3 kernel) to integrate FP8 block quantization and transforms. For throughput-oriented inference (batching many queries), FP8 looks highly attractive.
- For training or sensitive inference workloads where small numeric differences matter, FP16 FA3 delivers large speedups and keeps intermediate softmax in higher precision (FP32 accumulators and FP16 storage where appropriate), which reduces numerical error to parity with FA2.

Energy: energy tracks runtime and utilization. Because FP8 delivers more FLOPs per cycle on Tensor Cores, a high-utilization FP8 kernel can be more energy-efficient per token if accuracy is sufficient. But be wary: if FP8 causes more numerical instability and you compensate with repeated computations or larger models, the energy advantage disappears. FA3 mitigates this by improving FP8 accuracy via block quantization and incoherent processing.

Numerical safety: The paper shows FP16 FA3 keeps the same numerical error as FA2 (both better than the naive standard implementation because FA variants keep softmax intermediates in higher precision). For FP8, FA3’s block-quantization + incoherent processing reduces RMSE by ~2.6× versus a per-tensor FP8 baseline under a synthetic outlier-heavy distribution. That means FP8 FA3 is considerably more robust to real LLM outliers than simple FP8 schemes.

When to pick which:
- If you need the safest numeric behavior and large gains vs prior software, choose FP16 FA3. It gives substantial speedups (1.5–2× vs FA2) while matching FA2 numerical behavior.
- If you prioritize maximum throughput/cost efficiency and can accept more numeric engineering (or you validate accuracy on your model/task), FP8 FA3 is compelling — especially for long contexts and large head dims where peak Tensor Core throughput matters.
- For short sequences or autoregressive small-batch scenarios, evaluate empirically: vendor kernels (cuDNN) or FA2/FA3 FP16 with persistent-kernel strategies may be better. The paper notes FP8 FA3 lacks a persistent kernel in the benchmarks, which explains some small-sequence regressions.

## Practical caveats and limitations highlighted in the paper
- FP8 FA3 in the paper does not use a persistent kernel and persistent-load balancing for the benchmarks; that limits small-sequence / causal-case performance. The FP16 implementation does have a persistent kernel and load balancing.
- Using FP8 requires additional engineering: in-kernel transposes, register permutations, and the quantization transforms (block scales and incoherent transforms). The paper details how to perform these efficiently (LDSM/STSM, byte permutes, Hadamard-based incoherent transforms fused with rotary embedding), but integrating into production frameworks may need careful implementation.
- Numerical validation in the paper used synthetic data with outliers to stress-test quantization. Actual model behavior should be validated end-to-end; FP8 benefits can vary by architecture and distribution of activations.

## Bottom line
FlashAttention-3 is an important engineering step: by aligning algorithm design with Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Core and TMA capabilities and by building a careful FP8 path, the authors push attention kernels close to the machine’s theoretical throughput. For long-context workloads and large heads, FA3 unlocks major practical throughput gains: FP16 gives reliable 1.5–2× speedups with solid numeric behavior, while FP8 can bring nearly double the FLOP throughput (approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s) if you adopt block quantization and incoherent preprocessing to control error.

If you run large long-context inference or training, FA3 is worth trying: FP16 FA3 is the lower-risk path for big speedups, while FP8 FA3 is the higher-reward choice when you can validate accuracy for your model and pipeline.
