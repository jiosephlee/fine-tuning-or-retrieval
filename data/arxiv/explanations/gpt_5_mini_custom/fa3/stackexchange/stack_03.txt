\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does bar.sync enforce the pingpong overlap of softmax and GEMM across warpgroups without race conditions?
Question:
The paper describes a "pingpong" schedule where two consumer warpgroups alternate: while one warpgroup is doing its softmax (MFU ops), the other is executing GEMMs (WGMMA), and vice versa. They mention using bar.sync to force GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before those of warpgroup 2 so the softmax overlaps with GEMMs. How exactly are the barriers placed to avoid deadlocks and races? What is the ordering constraint that guarantees: (1) GEMM0/GEMM1 of warpgroup 1 start before softmax of warpgroup 1, and (2) the softmax of warpgroup 1 runs while warpgroup 2 is in WGMMA, then they swap cleanly?
Answer:
Short answer: they use a small set of explicit, stage-indexed barriers tied to the circular SMEM buffer and to the two warpgroups' roles so that every data-producer commit and every consumer release is witnessed in a single global order. Concretely, the producer writes a tile into buffer stage $s$ and “commits” it; each consumer warpgroup waits on the stage’s barrier before using that stage. The pingpong is enforced by placing a barrier pair (one barrier for the GEMM launches and one for the softmax path) at the same program points in both warpgroups so that the hardware schedules the two warpgroups’ WGMMA launches before either warpgroup proceeds to the softmax work. That common, per-stage rendezvous (bar.sync indexed by the circular buffer slot and by a pingpong phase bit) yields the ordering guarantees and removes races while avoiding deadlock because the waits are always acyclic and matched by commits/releases from the producer.

Longer explanation (intuitive + how it maps to the paper)

Think of the system as three actors per CTA: a producer warpgroup that fills circular SMEM stages with $K$/$V$ tiles and two consumer warpgroups that repeatedly (1) launch the SS-GEMM ($Q K^T$) to produce the next score tile, (2) perform softmax on the current score tile (non-GEMM, slow), and (3) launch the RS-GEMM ($P V$) to accumulate outputs. The goal of pingpong is to make sure that when consumer A is doing its softmax (compute-bound on special-function units), consumer B is running the two asynchronous WGMMA instructions so Tensor Cores are busy. We achieve that by making the GEMM launches of one warpgroup deterministically occur before the softmax entry of the other warpgroup.

There are two basic synchronization primitives used in the paper’s pseudocode and implementation: (a) producer “commit” / consumer “wait” on a given SMEM buffer stage (this is a semaphore-like notification that a tile is ready), and (b) explicit CUDA barrier instructions bar.sync that provide a global rendezvous among warps in the CTA. The crucial properties used are (i) WGMMA (Tensor Core) calls are asynchronous from the issuing warpgroup’s viewpoint (you may commit them and continue), and (ii) the implementation can issue bar.sync at chosen program points to force a particular relative ordering of issued WGMMA operations across warpgroups.

How the barriers are placed so there are no races or deadlocks

1) Per-stage buffer semantics avoid races on data. The circular SMEM buffer is indexed by stage id $j \bmod s$. The producer never overwrites a stage until the consumer has “released” it; likewise the consumer never reads a stage until the producer has committed it. This is the standard $s$-stage producer/consumer pattern in Algorithm 1: the producer “commit to notify consumer” after TMA load; the consumer “wait for $K_j$ to be loaded” before reading. Those waits are explicit and tied to the stage index, so no consumer can consume before a matching producer commit. Because each stage has a well-defined ownership timeline (producer writes → commit → consumer reads → release → producer may reuse), there are no data races on SMEM.

2) bar.sync is used to impose a cross-warpgroup ordering of GEMM launches versus softmax execution. The paper explains (Section 3.1, “Pingpong scheduling”) that they “force the GEMMs (GEMM0 and GEMM1 of warpgroup 1) to be scheduled before the GEMMs of warpgroup 2, so the softmax of warpgroup 1 will be scheduled while warpgroup 2 is performing its GEMMs.” In practice this is realized by inserting the same barrier IDs (or the same program-point rendezvous) at two program points in both consumer warpgroups: right after issuing the asynchronous WGMMA(s) and right before starting the softmax work that depends on the completed WGMMA. Both warpgroups execute the same sequence of bar.sync calls in the same control-flow order, so bar.sync establishes a global ordering of issued WGMMA operations versus subsequent softmax code.

Put another way, each consumer loop iteration contains these ordered events (matching Algorithm 2 lines):
  - Issue WGMMA for $S_{\text{next}}$ (line referenced as code:first_wgmma) and commit it (do not wait).
  - Issue WGMMA for $P_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ (code:second_wgmma) and commit it (do not wait).
  - Wait for the first WGMMA to complete, then run softmax on $S_{\text{next}}$ (code:softmax).
  - Wait for the second WGMMA to complete, then finish the RS-GEMM result handling.

If you place a bar.sync between the two groups at the program point just after the WGMMA commits but before either group proceeds to the softmax work, then both warpgroups will reach that bar.sync. The hardware then guarantees that all warpgroups have reached that synchronization point before any proceed. By carefully ordering where bar.sync appears in each wargroup’s code, you force one set of WGMMA launches (from both warpgroups) to be seen by the GPU scheduler before the softmax execution of either warpgroup is allowed to proceed; in effect the barrier serializes the softmax entry relative to the pending WGMMA issuance order. The implementation chooses the barrier placement such that warpgroup 1 issues its WGMMA pair and reaches the bar.sync while warpgroup 2 is still behind in program order (or vice versa), creating the desired overlap. Because the WGMMA instructions are asynchronous, issuing them then waiting at the barrier lets Tensor Cores execute the matmuls during the other wargroup’s softmax.

Why this does not deadlock
Deadlock would occur only if there were cyclic waits with no progress (for example warpgroup A waiting for B and B waiting for A at different program points). The scheme avoids that by ensuring that all bar.sync/wait operations are always reached in the same static order by each participating warpgroup and that buffer-stage waits are matched by producer commits/releases. The circular buffer provides bounded asynchrony but no cyclic dependency because ownership of each stage moves strictly forward in the producer→consumer→release direction. The bar.syncs are used only to synchronize program points (not to wait for data that will only be produced after the barrier), so there is no cycle where A waits at barrier X for B while B waits at barrier Y for A. In other words, every bar.sync is a single rendezvous point that both warpgroups arrive at in the same loop order, so the waits are matched and acyclic.

Concrete ordering constraints that guarantee the two properties you asked about

(1) GEMM0/GEMM1 of warpgroup 1 start before softmax of warpgroup 1. This is enforced by issuing the WGMMA instructions for GEMM0/GEMM1 and immediately reaching a bar.sync (or using the circular-buffer stage commit semantics which make consumers wait until the producer has committed). Because the softmax code is placed after that bar.sync in the program order, no thread in that warpgroup will execute softmax until after the WGMMA has been issued and the bar.sync has allowed progress. The WGMMA is asynchronous and thus can run in hardware while the warps wait or run softmax, but the program order guarantee (issue WGMMA → bar.sync → softmax) guarantees the GEMMs have been launched before softmax attempts to proceed.

(2) Softmax of warpgroup 1 runs while warpgroup 2 is in WGMMA, then they swap cleanly. This is achieved by using the same per-iteration barrier layout in both warpgroups but offsetting their phase so that at the moment warpgroup 1 reaches the softmax code it has already passed the “issue WGMMA” barrier and warpgroup 2 is still in the phase that issued its WGMMA and is proceeding into the barrier wait. Because WGMMA execution is asynchronous, the Tensor Cores will be actively executing the GEMMs from warpgroup 2 while warpgroup 1 executes the softmax on the scalar units. Later iterations swap roles: warpgroup 2 will execute its softmax while warpgroup 1’s WGMMA runs. The circular buffer + per-stage bar.syncs ensure the phase offsets are consistent and there is no race on the buffer contents because release/commit semantics are observed at each stage.

Mapping to the paper’s pseudocode
Algorithm 1 shows the per-stage commit/wait semantics for producer/consumer over the $s$-stage buffer. Algorithm 2 (the 2-stage pipelined consumer) shows the exact program points where the WGMMA calls are committed without waiting (code:first_wgmma and code:second_wgmma) and where the softmax works (code:softmax) happen. The paper states that they “use synchronization barriers (bar.sync instructions) to force the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before those of warpgroup 2” and that these barriers are tied to the circular buffer. In implementation terms you can think of a small set of bar.sync indices per buffer stage and per pingpong phase; both warpgroups call the same sequence of barriers, so the order of issue vs. softmax is deterministically enforced while the buffer stage ownership prevents data races and the ordered, identical barrier sequence avoids deadlocks.

Summary
The combination of (a) stage-indexed producer/consumer commits and releases on the circular SMEM buffer to avoid data races, and (b) a small set of matched bar.sync rendezvous placed at the program points immediately after WGMMA issuance and immediately before softmax, guarantees the ordering you want: GEMMs are launched before the corresponding softmax, and one wargroup’s softmax executes while the other wargroup’s asynchronous WGMMA runs. Because every wait is matched by a corresponding commit/release and both warpgroups run the same barrier sequence in the same loop order, there are no cyclic waits and hence no deadlock.
