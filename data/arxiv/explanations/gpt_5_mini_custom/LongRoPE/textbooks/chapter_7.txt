\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 7: Progressive Extension to 2,048k

# Stage 1: Search for 128k and 256k on the pretrained model

The progressive extension procedure begins by searching for a high-quality, non-uniform RoPE rescaling on the original pretrained model for moderately large target lengths: first 128k, then 256k. The reason for working in these intermediate ranges is practical: directly searching or fine-tuning to a 2048k window introduces an enormous search and training burden, and the majority of the catastrophic positional artifacts appear when many previously unseen positions are introduced at once. By choosing 128k and 256k as intermediate targets we keep the per-step extension ratio manageable (e.g., 32× and 64× from a 4k base), which makes both the search and subsequent fine-tuning tractable.

Concretely, the search problem optimizes a per-RoPE-dimension rescale vector \(\{\lambda_i\}\) together with a token-threshold \(\hat{n}\) so that the modified RoPE
\[
\text{RoPE}(n) = \big[\ldots,\cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\,
\sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\cdot\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\ldots\big]
\]
minimizes next-token loss on samples of length \(L'\) (here \(L'\) is 128k or 256k). The indicator
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})=\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat{n},\\
1/\lambda_i & n\ge\hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
encodes the two non-uniformities discovered in the paper: (i) different RoPE dimensions can use different rescale factors \(\lambda_i\), and (ii) the first \(\hat{n}\) token positions may be left un-rescaled (i.e., extrapolated less) because they often receive large attention and are more sensitive to interpolation.

Because the space of possible \(\{\lambda_i\}\) is enormous, LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search with several practical design choices. The per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) are searched in a grid from \(1.0\) (no interpolation/extrapolation) up to \(s\times 1.25\) (where \(s=L'/L\) is the extension ratio), with a step of 0.01; \(\hat{n}\) is chosen from a small set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\). The search is seeded with known solutions (PI, NTK, YaRN) in the initial population so the evolutionary process starts from sensible baselines. To reduce wasted evaluations the method enforces a monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\), motivated by NTK reasoning that lower-frequency (higher-index) RoPE dimensions can tolerate more interpolation while higher-frequency (lower-index) dimensions should be changed less.

Empirically this targeted search on the pretrained model yields rescale factors that preserve important RoPE dimensions and the most-attended early positions, producing substantially lower perplexity at 128k and 256k than naive linear PI or prior group-wise heuristics. Importantly, the paper shows that these searched, non-uniform RoPE settings already enable an 8× extension in non-fine-tuning settings, which is why 128k/256k targets are suitable stepping stones on the path to 2,048k.

# Stage 2: Fine-tune to 256k efficiently (e.g., 400 steps at 128k RoPE, then switch RoPE and 600 steps at 256k)

After obtaining good non-uniform rescale factors for 128k and 256k from Stage 1, LongRoPE proceeds to fine-tune the model in a short, efficient manner. The guiding principle is to make the amount of fine-tuning minimal while still adapting the model weights to the newly exposed positional regimes. Instead of attempting to fine-tune directly at 256k for many epochs (which is costly and difficult because very-long training segments are scarce), the paper uses a two-phase fine-tuning recipe:

1. Fine-tune for 400 steps with the RoPE rescale factors targeted at 128k, using long segments (e.g., RedPajama chunks of length 128k for LLaMA2 in the experiments). This first phase adapts the model to a large but not extreme increase in context length and stabilizes the weights while operating under rescale factors that remain conservative compared to the final 256k target.

2. Replace the RoPE rescale factors with those searched for 256k, and then fine-tune a further 600 steps. This second, shorter adaptation step raises the context capacity to 256k while leveraging the already adjusted weights from the first phase.

For LLaMA2-7B the paper uses a learning rate of \(2\!\times\!10^{-5}\) with linear decay and global batch size 32. The two-step fine-tuning (400 then 600 steps) therefore totals about 1k fine-tuning updates while operating at training lengths within 256k, which is computationally feasible compared with direct training at multi-million token lengths. The empirical result is a model that behaves well at 256k: because the search provided a rescale initialization that minimizes the interpolation-induced information loss, the model converges quickly and robustly in these relatively few steps.

The rationale for the two-stage fine-tuning is both practical and empirical. By first adapting at a smaller intermediate rescale, optimization instability caused by exposing the model to a very different positional regime is reduced. The model needs fewer gradient steps to accommodate the larger positional range when starting from a checkpoint already adapted to 128k rescaling, compared to starting from the untouched pretrained weights and trying to learn a direct 256k positional mapping.

# Stage 3: Second search on the 256k-finetuned model to reach 2,048k without further fine-tuning

Once the model has been fine-tuned to operate reliably at 256k with the searched RoPE initialization, LongRoPE performs a second evolutionary search—but now on the fine-tuned 256k model—to find rescale factors that enable much larger extensions without additional weight updates. The key empirical observation exploited here is that the non-uniform RoPE found for an already fine-tuned extended model can support another substantial non-finetuning extension: in the paper, the second search extends the 256k-finetuned model to 2,048k (an additional 8×), resulting in an overall extension from 4k to 2,048k of \(512\times\).

Mechanically, this second search has the same structure as the first search: it optimizes per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) under the same monotonicity constraint and similar search ranges, but it evaluates perplexity on very long samples drawn from Books3 or Pile-Books3 validation splits to ensure the RoPE rescaling works across multi-million-token contexts. Because the underlying weights were already trained to handle much longer contexts (256k) and the new rescale factors are chosen to preserve critical low-dimension (high-frequency) rotation angles and early token encodings, the resulting 2,048k-capable model achieves very large effective context with no extra gradient updates beyond those used to reach 256k.

This staged sequence—(search on pretrained → light fine-tuning to 256k → search on fine-tuned model)—is the core progressive strategy that enables a 512× overall extension while keeping heavy fine-tuning limited to a manageable training length (256k) and step count (approximately 1k updates in the LLaMA2 example).

# Short-context recovery: re-search RoPE for 4k–8k to mitigate crowding and preserve original performance

A known side-effect of aggressive positional interpolation is “crowding” of positions inside the original short context window: when rescale factors compress large new positions into the pretrained angular range, the rotation angles used by tokens within the original range become more tightly packed, which can degrade the model’s original short-context performance. This is especially noticeable when the overall extension ratio is large (e.g., \(512\times\) from 4k to 2,048k).

To address this, LongRoPE performs a short-context recovery step: after the model is extended to 2,048k, the paper runs additional evolutionary searches that specifically target short evaluation contexts such as 4k and 8k. In these recovery searches the maximum allowable \(\lambda_i\) is deliberately reduced (i.e., the search is constrained to encourage less interpolation), and the objective is to find rescale factors that preserve performance in the short regime. The search still uses the monotonicity constraint and the same per-dimension parametrization, but with a tighter range for \(\lambda\) so that the rescaling applied when the runtime sequence length is small does not over-compress the original RoPE geometry.

Empirically, applying these short-length RoPE variants at inference recovers much of the original perplexity and benchmark performance on tasks that operate within the original context window. This recovery is done without changing model weights: only the per-dimension rescale factors used to compute RoPE are swapped in at inference time when short sequences are processed.

# Dynamic RoPE selection at inference based on sequence length

All of the preceding work—searches for multiple target lengths (e.g., 4k/8k, 128k, 256k, 2048k) and the monotonicity-aware rescale vectors—becomes operational at inference through dynamic RoPE selection. The paper’s practical inference policy is simple: at runtime, the model selects the RoPE rescale factors appropriate for the current input sequence length. If the incoming sequence length \(N\) is small (for example \(N<8\)k), the model uses the rescale factors obtained from the short-context recovery search; if \(N\) is in an intermediate range (e.g., between 8k and 256k) it uses the rescale vector searched for the corresponding target; and for very long sequences (up to 2048k) it applies the rescale factors found in the second search on the 256k-finetuned model.

Because the modifications are limited to the positional computation, the core model architecture and most optimizations remain unchanged. Switching RoPE parameters is cheap: it simply changes the rotation angles used to form the complex-valued or interleaved cosine–sine positional multipliers in attention. This per-input adaptation ensures the model is not forced to use the same compressed positional geometry for both very long and short sequences, thereby preserving the original short-context capabilities while enabling million-token inputs when needed.

# Summary of the progressive pipeline and its benefits

The Progressive Extension to 2,048k is a practical and empirically validated recipe that combines a search for fine-grained, non-uniform RoPE rescale factors with a short, staged fine-tuning schedule and inference-time RoPE selection:

- Stage 1: Use evolutionary search on the pretrained model to find non-uniform per-dimension rescale factors for 128k and 256k targets, including the token-threshold \(\hat{n}\) that leaves initial tokens less interpolated.
- Stage 2: Fine-tune for a small number of steps (the paper uses 400 steps at the 128k RoPE and then 600 steps after switching to the 256k RoPE for LLaMA2) to adapt model weights at manageable training lengths.
- Stage 3: Run a second evolutionary search on the 256k-finetuned model to produce rescale factors that extend usefully to 2048k without further fine-tuning, producing an overall extension ratio of \(512\times\).
- Recovery: Re-search RoPE targeted at short contexts (4k–8k) with reduced maximum \(\lambda\) to recover and preserve original short-context performance.
- Inference: Dynamically select the appropriate RoPE rescale set based on the current sequence length.

This progressive strategy addresses the three main practical obstacles highlighted in the paper—catastrophic new position values, scarcity/expense of extremely long training texts, and degradation at short contexts—by (a) providing a non-uniform, information-preserving positional initialization, (b) concentrating fine-tuning at feasible lengths and with few steps (roughly 1k updates in the LLaMA2 example), and (c) using dynamic RoPE selection to avoid crowding short-range positional encodings. The result reported in the paper is an LLM extended to 2,048k tokens while maintaining strong short-context behavior and requiring only modest fine-tuning resources.
