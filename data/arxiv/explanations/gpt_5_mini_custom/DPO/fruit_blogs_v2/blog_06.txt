# The Theory in Plain Terms: Preference Models, KL Constraints, and Policy Optimality

This post unpacks the key theoretical ideas behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model". If you want to understand why DPO can skip explicit reward fitting and RL and still recover the same optimal policy, this is the condensed, intuitive tour: equivalence classes of reward functions, how the Bradley–Terry preference model uses only reward differences, and why the KL‑constrained optimal policy admits a closed-form mapping to rewards. I’ll keep the math minimal and use it only where it clarifies the mapping between rewards and policies.

## 1) Preferences depend only on reward differences (the <|endoftext|> view)

A standard way to model human <|endoftext|> preferences is the <|endoftext|> (BT) model. Given a prompt \(x\) and two candidate responses \(y_1, y_2\), BT says the probability that humans prefer \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) is determined by the difference in their <|endoftext|> scores (rewards):

p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)}
= \sigma\big(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\big),

where \(\sigma\) is the <|endoftext|> function. The crucial point: BT depends only on reward differences \(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\). Any <|endoftext|> shift of the reward that depends only on \(x\) cancels out in that difference. That observation is elementary but central.

This leads directly to an under‑specification: many different reward functions can induce the exact same preference <|endoftext|> Those reward functions form equivalence classes.

## 2) Equivalence classes of reward functions

Formally, say two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are equivalent if they differ by a function of the prompt alone:

r'(x,y) = <|endoftext|> + f(x)

for some \(f\) that does not depend on \(y\). Under BT (and more generally Plackett–Luce models), such a shift does not change any <|endoftext|> preference probability because it cancels in the difference.

Two consequences of this equivalence relation are important:

- Many reward functions represent the same underlying preference model; recovering a single representative from that class is sufficient for matching human preferences.
- The optimal policy under a KL‑constrained reward maximization problem is also invariant across reward functions in the same equivalence class. That means picking a convenient representative in the class does not reduce the set of achievable optimal policies.

These simple facts let us reparameterize rewards in a way that makes the optimal policy tractable.

## 3) KL‑constrained reward maximization and its closed‑form optimal policy

RLHF typically optimizes a policy \(\pi\) to trade off expected reward against deviation from a reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) (usually the SFT model). Write the per‑prompt objective as

maximize_pi E_{y \sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)} [ <|endoftext|> ] - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid <|endoftext|> x)\big),

where \(\beta\) is a temperature/conservativeness parameter. This is the standard reward-maximization objective with a <|endoftext|> penalty that prevents the policy from drifting too far from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

A classic result (from control-as-inference / soft optimization literature) gives the form of the optimal policy for a fixed reward \(r\):

<|endoftext|> x) = \frac{1}{Z_r(x)} \, <|endoftext|> x) \, \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\big),

where \(Z_r(x)\) is the partition function (normalizer) that makes \(\pi_r(\cdot\mid x)\) a valid distribution. This says the optimal policy reweights the reference policy by \(\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r)\) and then renormalizes.

That closed form is the key structural link between reward functions and optimal policies.

## 4) Reparameterizing reward via policy — the DPO trick

The closed form can be inverted (up to the partition function) to express reward in terms of a policy:

<|endoftext|> = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z_r(x).

Plug this into BT, and the partition function cancels because BT depends only on reward differences. Concretely, the BT preference probability induced by the optimal policy \(\pi^*\) becomes

p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
= \sigma\Big(\beta \log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big).

So the BT model can be written entirely in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^*\) and the known reference \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The unknown partition function (and thus the <|endoftext|> ambiguity in rewards) vanishes from the likelihood. That gives a direct maximum likelihood objective for the policy itself, without ever fitting an explicit reward model or running RL.

DPO turns this into a training loss for a parameterized language model policy <|endoftext|>

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\right],

where \((y_w,y_l)\) are human-preferred and human-dispreferred responses in dataset \(\mathcal{D}\). <|endoftext|> this loss fits <|endoftext|> so that the induced <|endoftext|> reward \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) \triangleq \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) explains the preference data under BT. Because of the equivalence class argument above, this reparameterization is not losing expressiveness: every reward class consistent with BT can be represented as \(\hat r_\theta\) for some <|endoftext|> and choice of \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) (Theorem in the paper).

In short: instead of separately fitting a reward model and then running RL to obtain \(\pi_r\), DPO directly optimizes <|endoftext|> to satisfy the same preference model that would have been implied by \(\pi_r\).

## 5) Why this <|endoftext|> expressiveness (no loss of generality)

Two facts combine to justify that DPO is not “throwing away” possible reward functions:

1. Any two rewards that differ by \(f(x)\) produce the same BT preferences, so reward learning already has that degree of nonidentifiability.

2. For any reward \(r\), the KL‑constrained optimal policy \(\pi_r\) has the <|endoftext|> reweighting form given above. If you start from an arbitrary reward \(r\), you can compute its optimal policy \(\pi_r\), and there exists a reward in the same equivalence class that is exactly \(\beta\log(\pi_r/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\). Thus the DPO <|endoftext|> picks a canonical representative in each equivalence class: the member whose partition function is <|endoftext|> so that the <|endoftext|> reweighting integrates to one with respect to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

Therefore, for the purpose of matching human preferences under BT and recovering the corresponding KL‑constrained optimal policy, representing rewards implicitly via \(\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) is fully general.

## 6) A quick look at the DPO <|endoftext|> (intuition)

The DPO loss produces an intuitive <|endoftext|> when a pair \((y_w,y_l)\) is labeled with \(y_w\) preferred, the <|endoftext|> pushes the model to increase \(\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)\) and decrease \(\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\). Importantly, updates are weighted by

\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big),

where \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\). That term is large when the current <|endoftext|> reward mistakenly <|endoftext|> the dispreferred example a higher score than the preferred one, making the update stronger. This dynamic per‑example importance weight is a <|endoftext|> mechanism compared to <|endoftext|> unlikelihood-style updates; it prevents degenerate collapse by tempering updates when the <|endoftext|> reward ordering is already consistent with the data.

## 7) Practical implications and why DPO simplifies RLHF

Putting the pieces together:

- BT preferences only constrain reward up to an \(x\)-dependent <|endoftext|> term.
- The KL‑constrained optimal policy for any reward has a closed form as an <|endoftext|> tilt of \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).
- Plugging that closed form into the BT model causes the <|endoftext|> ambiguity to cancel, producing a likelihood that depends only on a policy \(\pi\) and the known \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).
- Maximizing that likelihood over policies is a straightforward cross‑entropy-style objective; it recovers the same optimal policy that the RLHF pipeline (fit reward + RL) would aim for, but in one stage and without sampling-in-the-loop RL.

So DPO <|endoftext|> the expressiveness of reward learning (no loss from the under‑specification inherent to BT) and directly targets the KL‑constrained optimal policy with a stable supervised-style loss.

## 8) What this does not claim

DPO shows that, under the BT/Plackett–Luce family and the KL-constrained objective, one can reparameterize and directly recover the optimal policy. This is a theoretical equivalence for the modeled setting and explains why training with the DPO loss empirically matches or surpasses PPO-based RLHF in the paper’s experiments. It does not claim that every conceivable form of preference annotation or every RL <|endoftext|> outside these assumptions will admit the same trick; the derivation <|endoftext|> the BT structure and the <|endoftext|> penalty. The paper also discusses practicalities <|endoftext|> \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), choice of \(\beta\), and stability) and shows empirical results across sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue.

## Takeaway

If your preference model is of the <|endoftext|> / Plackett–Luce type and your RL objective is the usual reward maximization with a <|endoftext|> penalty to a reference policy, then rather than fitting an explicit reward model and running RL, you can directly parameterize the policy and optimize a simple binary‑classification-style loss. The <|endoftext|> ambiguity in reward functions is exactly what makes this possible: it lets you pick a canonical representative reward that makes the optimal policy analytically accessible, and fitting the policy to preference data recovers that optimal policy directly.
