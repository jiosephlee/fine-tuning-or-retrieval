# Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model: The Core Idea Behind DPO

## TL;DR
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) observes a simple analytical fact: the optimal policy for a KL-constrained reward-maximization problem has a <|endoftext|> relationship to the reward. Rewriting the reward in terms of the policy yields a <|endoftext|> where the policy itself implicitly defines a reward up to an <|endoftext|> function of the prompt. In the common <|endoftext|> preference model this <|endoftext|> term cancels out, so human preference <|endoftext|> can be written purely in terms of the policy and a reference policy. That means you can train the policy directly from <|endoftext|> preferences with a standard binary cross-entropy loss — no explicit reward model and no RL loop required.

Typical RL from Human Feedback (RLHF) workflows have three pieces:

2. A reward model \(r_\phi(x,y)\) trained on human <|endoftext|> comparisons using a preference model like <|endoftext|>
3. An RL algorithm <|endoftext|> PPO) which optimizes the policy <|endoftext|> to maximize expected reward while staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), typically solving
   \[
   \max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[ r_\phi(x,y) \big] - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big).
   \]

This pipeline works in practice but is complex: separate reward fitting, <|endoftext|> from RL, value <|endoftext|> and sampling in the loop.

## The simple algebraic insight
Consider the <|endoftext|> optimization above for a fixed reward function \(r\). The optimal policy for that problem can be written in closed form (a standard result in KL-constrained or maximum-entropy RL):

\[
<|endoftext|> x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big),
\]
where <|endoftext|> is the partition function (normalizer) that makes \(\pi_r(\cdot\mid x)\) a distribution.

Take logs and rearrange to express the reward in terms of the policy:

\[
<|endoftext|> \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid <|endoftext|> x)} \;+\; \beta \log <|endoftext|>
\]

This is the key reparameterization: any reward that leads to an optimal policy \(\pi_r\) can be expressed as a <|endoftext|> log-ratio between policy and reference, plus an <|endoftext|> term \(\beta\log <|endoftext|> that depends only on the prompt \(x\).

## Why the <|endoftext|> term doesn't matter for <|endoftext|> preferences
Human <|endoftext|> preferences are commonly modeled with <|endoftext|> (a <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> model):

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\big( r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2) \big),
\]
where \(\sigma\) is the <|endoftext|> sigmoid. Critically, this model depends only on reward differences. Plugging the reparameterized reward into the difference <|endoftext|> causes the <|endoftext|> \(\beta\log <|endoftext|> to cancel:

\[
<|endoftext|>
= \beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big).
\]

Thus the <|endoftext|> preference probability becomes a function only of the policy and reference policy:

\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
= \sigma\!\Bigg(\beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big)\Bigg).
\]

This is the crucial cancellation: you no longer need an explicit reward model to map preferences to likelihoods — the policy itself <|endoftext|> (modulo the reference policy and scale \(\beta\)).

## From that identity to a training objective
If we have an offline dataset of human comparisons \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}\) where \(y_w\) is the preferred completion and \(y_l\) the dispreferred one, we can write a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy <|endoftext|> directly:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}} \Big[ \log \sigma\!\Big( \beta\big(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\big) \Big) \Big].
\]

<|endoftext|> this loss adjusts <|endoftext|> so that preferred responses get relatively higher probability than dispreferred ones, measured against the reference policy. That is DPO in a nutshell: directly optimize the policy likelihood ratio to match observed preferences with a simple binary cross-entropy objective.

## Intuition for the <|endoftext|> and why it’s stable
Differentiating the DPO loss yields an update that (1) increases the log-probability of preferred responses and decreases that of dispreferred ones, and (2) weights each <|endoftext|> update by

\[
\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big),
\quad\text{where}\quad
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid <|endoftext|> x)}.
\]

This weight is larger when the current <|endoftext|> reward \(\hat r_\theta\) ranks the pair incorrectly (i.e., when the model currently thinks the dispreferred response is better). That <|endoftext|> adaptive <|endoftext|> helps avoid the collapse issues a <|endoftext|> unlikelihood objective can produce, and it sidesteps many variance sources of actor-critic RL (no on-policy sampling, no learned value baseline, no reward <|endoftext|> tricks).

## What you gain (and what to watch)
- Simplicity: training is standard <|endoftext|> (binary cross-entropy) on <|endoftext|> comparisons. No separate reward network or RL loop.
- Theoretically grounded: DPO optimizes the same underlying KL-constrained objective as RLHF under the <|endoftext|> model, because the reparameterization is lossless up to the well-understood equivalence class of rewards (adding functions of \(x\) does not change <|endoftext|> preferences).
- Stability and computational efficiency: <|endoftext|> sampling loops and many RL hyperparameters; the <|endoftext|> weight reduces degeneracy seen in <|endoftext|> methods.

Limitations noted by the authors include questions about out-of-distribution <|endoftext|> relative to explicit reward-based approaches, the behavior of reward over-optimization under DPO, and evaluation/selection of the scale hyperparameter \(\beta\). Also, experiments in the paper were up to 6B parameter models; <|endoftext|> behavior to much larger models remains to be explored.

## Bottom line
By recognizing that the optimal policy for the usual <|endoftext|> reward objective can be inverted to express the reward as a <|endoftext|> log-probability ratio (plus an \(x\)-dependent constant), and <|endoftext|> that <|endoftext|> preference models only depend on reward differences (so that constant drops out), we can convert the <|endoftext|> “fit a reward, run RL” pipeline into a one-stage “fit the policy directly to preferences” procedure. That is the core idea of DPO: your language model can implicitly represent the reward, and you can train it directly from <|endoftext|> human preferences using a straightforward likelihood objective.
