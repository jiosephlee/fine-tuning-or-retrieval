# From Preferences to Policy: How DPO Works Step by Step

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a lightweight way to turn pairwise human preferences into a deployed language model policy — no explicit reward model, no RL loop. This post walks through the practical steps to implement DPO, explains the core loss, and highlights the knobs and pitfalls the paper emphasizes: how to build the preference dataset, how to choose or construct the reference model, what the beta parameter does, sampling/temperature considerations, and how to avoid degeneration.

## 1) What DPO optimizes (short version)

Instead of learning an intermediate reward model and then running RL, DPO re-parameterizes the reward so the policy itself represents the optimal policy for some reward in the usual KL-constrained RLHF objective. Given a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and a parametric policy <|endoftext|> DPO trains <|endoftext|> with a single cross-entropy style loss built from preference triplets \((x, y_w, y_l)\) where \(y_w\) is preferred to \(y_l\):

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
\]

where \(\sigma\) is the <|endoftext|> function and \(\beta>0\) is the <|endoftext|> that controls the effective <|endoftext|> penalty (more on \(\beta\) below). Intuitively, the loss increases the relative log-probability of preferred answers while penalizing drifting too far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

The DPO <|endoftext|> can be read as "increase \(\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)\) and decrease \(\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\)" with a <|endoftext|> weight that down-weights examples where the model already ranks them correctly. This dynamic weight is critical for stability.

## 2) Building the preference dataset

DPO uses the same offline preference data format typical in RLHF:

- Dataset \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N\}\), where for each prompt \(x\) you have two candidate completions and a human label indicating the preferred completion.
- How the pairs are generated in practice: sample completions from a base model (commonly the SFT model) for each prompt, present pairs to human raters, and store the chosen winner and loser.
- You can also reuse existing public preference datasets <|endoftext|> TL;DR, Anthropic HH) provided you match the reference model to how the data were sampled — see next section.

Practical notes from the paper:
- Preference labels are relative judgments <|endoftext|> / Plackett–Luce style). DPO derives its loss from that same preference model.
- If you cannot collect new labels, you can fine-tune a suitable reference model to align with the distribution of the preferred completions in the dataset (see reference model section).

## 3) Choosing or constructing the reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)

The reference model plays the role of the base policy that DPO resists drifting away from. The paper recommends:

- When available, use the SFT model used to generate the comparison completions as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). This is the simplest and most consistent choice because the preference data were sampled from that model.
- When the original SFT that generated the dataset is not available, construct \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions:
  \[
  \pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)].
  \]
  That is, fine-tune a model on the chosen preferred responses. This helps reduce distribution shift between the dataset and the reference used in the DPO objective.

Why this matters: the DPO loss compares <|endoftext|> to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) via the log-probability ratio. If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) does not reflect the generator used for preference collection, that ratio becomes less meaningful and optimization may be less stable or less effective.

## 4) The role of beta (\(\beta\))

- \(\beta\) controls the strength of the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> penalty. Larger \(\beta\) makes the policy more willing to change (i.e., weaker penalty per unit change), while smaller \(\beta\) encourages conservatism and keeps <|endoftext|> closer to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
- Formally, the DPO <|endoftext|> is derived from solving
  \[
  \max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \pi_\theta}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \text{KL}(\pi_\theta\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}),
  \]
  and the reparameterization yields the DPO loss in terms of <|endoftext|> and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
- In practice the authors sweep a small set of \(\beta\) values <|endoftext|> \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\)) and find DPO robust with minimal tuning. The right \(\beta\) depends on how much deviation you want from the reference and on dataset quality.

## 5) Training recipe (practical outline)

1. Prepare preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) as described above.
2. Choose or construct \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (use SFT if available; otherwise fit to preferred completions).
3. <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (standard practice).
4. For minibatches of preference triplets \((x, y_w, y_l)\), compute the <|endoftext|> scores
   \[
   s_w = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)},\qquad
   s_l = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
   \]
   and the BCE loss \(-\log\sigma(s_w - s_l)\).
5. Backpropagate and update \(\theta\) with a standard <|endoftext|> (Adam-style). No actor-critic infrastructure, sampling loop, or reward baseline is required.
6. Validate periodically on held-out preference data or by an external <|endoftext|> (human or automatic) to pick <|endoftext|> and tune \(\beta\).

The paper uses the same simple <|endoftext|> training loop for DPO that you would use for any cross-entropy objective; there is no inner RL loop or sampling-from-policy in training.

## 9) Why choose DPO vs PPO-based RLHF?

From a practical standpoint, DPO offers a few clear advantages noted in the paper:

- Simpler: No RL infrastructure, no sampling from the policy during training, and no learned value network or reward baselining required.
- Stable: The objective is a standard classification-style loss with <|endoftext|> weights that <|endoftext|> updates.
- Efficient: In the experiments DPO yields a better reward-vs-KL frontier than PPO, and matches or exceeds PPO on summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, with less hyperparameter tuning.
