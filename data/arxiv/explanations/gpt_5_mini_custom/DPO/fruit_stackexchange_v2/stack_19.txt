### What are the computational bottlenecks of DPO and how do they compare to PPO?
Question:
In practice, DPO requires two forward passes per pair $(x,y_w)$ and $(x,y_l)$ to compute $\log \pi(\cdot\mid x)$ and $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Are there tricks like caching $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ logits, shared encoders, or low-rank adapters that substantially reduce compute and memory? How does total wall-clock time compare to PPO for a fixed number of preference pairs?

Naïvely that looks like four forward passes of the full transformer per pair (two for <|endoftext|> two for \(\pi_{\rm ref}\)), plus two backwards through <|endoftext|> By contrast, <|endoftext|> RLHF needs (1) sampling or beam-search rollouts in the inner loop (i.e.\ dozens of sequential, unbatched decoder steps per prompt), (2) a separate reward-model forward-pass, (3) a value-network forward (and <|endoftext|> and (4) multiple <|endoftext|> steps with clipped objectives and value-function updates. In practice this turns into many more total transformer passes and a much more complex optimization.

There are three simple “tricks” that almost entirely erase the overhead of DPO beyond standard <|endoftext|> fine-tuning:

  •  Precompute and cache \(\log \pi_{\rm ref}(y \mid x)\) for your entire offline preference set once before training. Since \(\pi_{\rm ref}\) never changes, you can store those scalars (or even normalize them off-line) and never run the frozen model again in your DPO loop.

  •  Share as much of the computation as possible between the two completions. In most implementations you’ll pack \((x,y_w)\) and \((x,y_l)\) into the same batch so that the prompt tokens \(x\) are encoded only once at each layer; only the final few tokens differ.

  •  If memory or bandwidth is tight, you can freeze the bulk of <|endoftext|> and train only a small <|endoftext|> adapter (LoRA or similar). That cuts down peak <|endoftext|> memory in backprop and keeps virtually all of your compute in fast fused kernels.

With these in place, each DPO step costs you only two forward passes through the trainable part of the network plus two backwards (all batched), exactly the same order of magnitude as a single supervised‐learning <|endoftext|> step with twice the batch size. There is no sequential generation, no auxiliary <|endoftext|> network, and no reward-model backprop.

Empirically, on the kinds of preference datasets used in RLHF papers, this typically translates into a $2\times$–$4\times$ reduction in end‐to‐end wall‐clock time compared with a well-tuned PPO pipeline on the same number of preference pairs. (PPO not only has the extra sampling and value-function steps but also typically needs multiple “epochs” over each batch of transitions to <|endoftext|> the clipped-objective updates.)

In short, the main <|endoftext|> in DPO is just standard autoregressive forward/backward through your LLM, whereas PPO adds large amounts of extra compute for rollouts, reward‐modeling, and actor-critic <|endoftext|> Once you cache the frozen reference scores and share prompt encoding, DPO runs almost as cheaply as straight maximum‐likelihood fine-tuning.
