### Can DPO be applied to continuous action spaces or non-text modalities without modification?
Question:
The derivation seems general, relying on a density $\pi(y\mid x)$ relative to a reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$. For continuous actions (e.g., diffusion models for images or audio), do we need any special considerations to evaluate $\log \tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ reliably? Are there known successful applications of DPO-like objectives to non-text generative models?
Answer:
DPO’s derivation in the paper really only assumes that you have two distributions—the current policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference <|endoftext|> x)$—and that you can form the log-ratio

$$
\Delta(y\mid x) \;=\; \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

All of the magic of DPO is that, under the <|endoftext|> (or more general Plackett–Luce) model of “win-probabilities,” the partition functions cancel when you only care about differences of rewards. As a result the DPO training loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(\beta[\Delta(y_w\mid x)-\Delta(y_l\mid x)]\bigr)\Bigr]
$$

looks just like a binary-cross-entropy on preference pairs—with the only wrinkle that you weight each pair by the sigmoid of how “wrong” your current log-ratio ordering is.

Because of this, in principle DPO is modality-agnostic: if you can (exactly or approximately) evaluate both $\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and $\log <|endoftext|> x)$ for your generative model—be it images, audio, or continuous-action RL—then you can fine-tune it with the same one-stage cross-entropy objective.

— If your generator has a tractable density <|endoftext|> a normalizing flow or a VAE with a known decoder density), then nothing in the math changes: you literally replace the “log-prob of a token sequence” with “log-density of a continuous sample,” sample two candidates $y_1,y_2$, get human (or proxy) preferences, and run <|endoftext|> steps on $\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}$.

— If your model is implicit—like a diffusion model, which typically does *not* expose an easy log-density—then you must introduce some way to estimate or approximate that log-ratio. Common tactics include  
  • training a *density-ratio estimator* (for instance by learning a binary <|endoftext|> to discriminate samples from $\pi_{\theta}$ vs.\ $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and <|endoftext|> its logits to $\log \pi_{\theta}(y)-\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)$), or  
  • using <|endoftext|> guidance* or *energy-based guidance* that effectively gives you
    $$
      \nabla_{y}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\;-\;\nabla_{y}\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
    $$
    and then integrating that into your update.  

Either route adds an extra approximation layer compared to the language-model setting, but it doesn’t change the core DPO idea: you still solve a simple cross-entropy on <|endoftext|> comparisons of $\Delta(y\mid x)$.

To date there aren’t, to my knowledge, *published* “DPO for images” or “DPO for diffusion” papers yet—DPO itself has only been demonstrated on autoregressive LMs where log-probs are immediate. That said, there is a long body of work on *preference-based RL* in continuous and high-dimensional domains (contextual dueling bandits, PbRL in robotics, etc.) that all rest on essentially the same <|endoftext|> re-parameterization. DPO simply shows that when your policy has *tractable* densities you can completely fold reward learning *and* policy optimization into one stable maximum-likelihood step.

So in summary:  
1. The DPO *objective* is completely generic and only asks for log-ratios of densities.  
2. If your model gives you those densities natively (flows, VAEs, explicit continuous RL policies), DPO drops in with no further modification.  
3. If your model is <|endoftext|> (diffusion or other score-based models), you’ll need an auxiliary density-ratio estimator or classifier-guidance module to approximate the log-ratio before you can apply DPO.

But in all cases, once you have $\Delta(y\mid x)$, you can train with exactly the same binary-cross-entropy loss on preference pairs that the original DPO paper uses.
