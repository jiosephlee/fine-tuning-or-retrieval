\title{Facts in the paper: DPO}

Large-scale unsupervised language models learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, but achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods to gain steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised language model to align with these preferences.
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is often used to fine-tune language models to human preferences.
RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure that first fits a reward model reflecting human preferences and then fine-tunes the large unsupervised language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the estimated reward while avoiding drift from the original model.
The paper introduces a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form.
This new parameterization allows the standard RLHF problem to be solved using only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight.
DPO eliminates the need for sampling from the language model during fine-tuning and reduces the need for significant hyperparameter tuning.
Experiments show that DPO can fine-tune language models to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods.
Fine-tuning with DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generations.
DPO matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
Large unsupervised language models trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities.
These large language models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets.
Some human goals and skillsets present in the training data may be undesirable for the model to imitate.
When generating code, we may want a coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes but be biased toward producing high-quality code.
A language model can be aware of a common misconception held by many people without asserting that misconception as true in its responses.
Selecting the model's desired responses and behavior from its broad knowledge and abilities is crucial for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable.
Existing methods typically steer language models to match human preferences using reinforcement learning.
The RL-based objective used by existing preference-learning methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, which greatly simplifies the preference learning pipeline.
Preference learning is performed after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset.
The most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high-quality responses.
The most successful class of preference-learning methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF).
RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model.
The RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning because it involves training multiple language models and sampling from the model policy in the training loop, which incurs significant computational costs.
The paper shows how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning.
The authors propose Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms, namely reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint.
DPO is simple to implement and straightforward to train compared to existing RLHF methods.
The DPO update increases the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to dispreferred responses while incorporating a dynamic, per-example importance weight.
The dynamic, per-example importance weight in DPO prevents the model degeneration that the authors observe with a naive probability ratio objective.
DPO relies on a theoretical preference model, such as the Bradley-Terry model, to measure how well a given reward function aligns with empirical preference data.
Unlike existing methods that use the preference model to train a separate reward model and then train a policy to optimize that learned reward, DPO uses a change of variables to define the preference loss as a function of the policy directly.
Given a dataset of human preferences over model responses, DPO can optimize a policy using a simple binary cross-entropy objective and produce the optimal policy for an implicit reward function fit to the preference data.
The main contribution of the paper is DPO, a simple RL-free algorithm for training language models from preferences.
Experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF, for learning from preferences in tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue using language models with up to 6B parameters.
Self-supervised language models of increasing scale learn to complete some tasks zero-shot or with few-shot prompts.
Fine-tuning on datasets of instructions and human-written completions significantly improves downstream task performance and alignment with user intent.
Instruction-tuning enables large language models to generalize to instructions outside of the instruction-tuning set and generally increases their usability.
Relative human judgments of response quality are often easier to collect than expert demonstrations.
Researchers have fine-tuned large language models with datasets of human preferences, which improves proficiency in translation, summarization, story-telling, and instruction-following.
These methods first optimize a neural network reward function for compatibility with the dataset of preferences under a preference model such as the Bradley-Terry model.
After learning the reward function, researchers fine-tune a language model to maximize the learned reward using reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE, proximal policy optimization (PPO), or variants.
A related line of work leverages instruction-following models fine-tuned with human feedback to generate additional synthetic preference data for targeted attributes such as safety or harmlessness using only weak supervision from humans in the form of a text rubric.
The approaches that use human preferences represent a convergence of prior work on training language models with reinforcement learning for various objectives and work on general methods for learning from human preferences.
Fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning remains a major practical challenge.
This work provides a theoretically justified approach to optimizing relative preferences without using reinforcement learning.
Learning policies from preferences has been studied outside of language in both bandit and reinforcement learning settings, and several approaches have been proposed.
Contextual bandit learning using preferences or rankings of actions is known as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB).
In the absence of absolute rewards, theoretical analysis of CDBs replaces the notion of an optimal policy with a von Neumann winner, which is a policy whose expected win rate against any other policy is at least 50%.
In the CDB setting, preference labels are given online, whereas learning from human preferences typically uses a fixed batch of offline preference-annotated action pairs.
Preference-based RL (PbRL) learns from binary preferences generated by an unknown scoring function instead of using rewards.
Various algorithms for PbRL exist, and some methods can reuse off-policy preference data.
Most PbRL methods generally involve first explicitly estimating the latent scoring function (i.e. the reward model) and then optimizing it.
The authors present a single-stage policy learning approach that directly optimizes a policy to satisfy preferences.
The RLHF pipeline usually includes three phases: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) preference sampling and reward learning, and (3) RL optimization.
In SFT, RLHF typically begins by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model with supervised learning on high-quality data for downstream tasks to obtain a model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.
In the reward modelling phase, the SFT model is prompted with prompts $x$ to produce pairs of answers $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$.
These answer pairs are presented to human labelers who express preferences denoted $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ denote the preferred and dispreferred completion respectively among $(y_1, y_2)$.
The observed preferences are assumed to be generated by a latent reward model $r^*(y, x)$ that is not directly accessible.
The Bradley-Terry (BT) model is a popular choice to model preferences, and more general Plackett-Luce ranking models are also compatible with the framework when multiple ranked answers are available.
The Bradley-Terry model defines $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$.
The static comparison dataset is $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$.
A parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is estimated by maximum likelihood framed as binary classification using the negative log-likelihood loss $\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))]$.
The logistic function $\sigma$ is used in the reward model loss.
In language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is often initialized from the supervised fine-tuned model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$ with an added linear layer on the final transformer layer producing a scalar reward.
Prior works normalize rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$.
During RL fine-tuning the optimization objective is $\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)]$.
The hyperparameter $\beta$ controls the deviation from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which is the initial SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.
In practice the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is initialized to $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.
The KL constraint is important because it prevents the model from deviating too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate, maintains generation diversity, and prevents mode-collapse to single high-reward answers.
The RL fine-tuning objective is not differentiable due to discrete language generation and is typically optimized with reinforcement learning.
The standard practical approach forms a shaped reward $r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))$ and maximizes it using PPO.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) derives a simple policy optimization method that uses preferences directly and avoids an RL training loop by choosing a reward parameterization whose optimal policy can be extracted in closed form.
DPO leverages an analytical mapping from reward functions to optimal policies to transform a loss over reward functions into a loss over policies, thereby avoiding fitting an explicit standalone reward model while still optimizing under preference models like Bradley-Terry.
In DPO the policy network represents both the language model and the implicit reward.
The optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization objective takes the form $
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$.
The partition function is defined as $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$.
Estimating the partition function $Z(x)$ is expensive in practice even when using the MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$ of the ground-truth reward $r^*$.
Rearranging the optimal policy expression gives the reward reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$.
The Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on reward differences, i.e. $p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\big(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\big)$.
Substituting the reparameterization for $r^*(x,y)$ into the Bradley-Terry model causes the partition function to cancel, so the human preference probability can be expressed solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
Under this reparameterization the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies $\displaystyle p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}$.
Analogous reparameterizations and expressions can be derived under the more general Plackett-Luce models.
The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is $\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$. 
The method fits an implicit reward using an alternative parameterization whose optimal policy is $\pi_\theta$.
The procedure is equivalent to fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model.
Because it is equivalent to a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, the procedure enjoys theoretical properties such as consistency under suitable assumptions on the preference data distribution.
The gradient of the DPO loss can be written as $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\big)\big]$.
The implicit reward is defined by $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$.
The DPO gradient increases the likelihood of preferred completions $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of dispreferred completions $y_l$.
Training examples are weighted by $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ scaled by $\beta$, which gives higher weight when the implicit reward model rates the dispreferred completion higher than the preferred one.
Empirical results indicate that omitting this weighting term can cause the language model to degenerate.
The DPO pipeline first samples completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)$ for every prompt $x$ and labels them with human preferences to construct an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$.
The DPO pipeline then optimizes the language model $\pi_{\theta}$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ for the given $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and desired $\beta$.
In practice, one prefers to reuse publicly available preference datasets instead of generating samples and gathering new human preferences.
Because preference datasets are sampled using $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, the authors initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ whenever $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is available.
When $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is not available, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is initialized by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x,y_w)$, i.e. $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)]$.
Initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by likelihood maximization helps mitigate distribution shift between the unavailable true reference distribution and the $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used by DPO.
The paper provides a theoretical analysis that interprets DPO, gives theoretical backing, and relates DPO's advantages to issues with actor-critic algorithms used for RLHF such as PPO.
DPO can bypass both fitting an explicit reward model and performing reinforcement learning by using a single maximum likelihood objective.
The DPO optimization objective (Eq. main_eq) is equivalent to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward parameterization $r^*(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$.
Optimizing the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ under this reparameterization is equivalent to optimizing a reward model (Eq. reward_model) under a change of variables.
The authors claim the reparameterization does not constrain the class of learned reward models and allows for exact recovery of the optimal policy.
The theoretical development begins by defining an equivalence relation between reward functions.
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are equivalent iff $r(x, y)-r'(x, y)=f(x)$ for some function $f$.
The equivalence relation on reward functions partitions the set of reward functions into equivalence classes.
Under the Plackett-Luce, and in particular the Bradley-Terry, preference framework, two reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution.
Two reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy under the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
The Plackett-Luce family of models exhibits an under-specification issue that typically requires additional identifiability constraints to obtain guarantees on maximum likelihood estimates from the reward model.
Because all reward functions in the same equivalence class yield the same optimal policy, it suffices for the final objective to recover an arbitrary reward function from the optimal equivalence class.
Under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) models can be represented by the reparameterization $r(x, y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a given reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$.
The projection operator $f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x, y)=r(x, y)-\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ normalizes the reward function by the logarithm of the partition function of $\pi_r$.
The normalization term in the projection operator is only a function of the prefix $x$, so $f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x, y)$ belongs to the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$.
Applying the projection yields $f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x, y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, so the projection produces a member of the equivalence class of $r$ with the desired reparameterized form.
The DPO reparameterization selects the reward function satisfying $
abla_y: \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)=1$ for each prompt $x$.
Under the DPO reparameterization, $\pi(y\mid x)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$, so $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid distribution with positive probabilities that sum to 1.
The quantity $\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$ is the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward function $r(x,y)$.
The DPO algorithm imposes constraints on the under-constrained Plackett-Luce family of preference models, and on the Bradley-Terry model in particular.
These constraints preserve the class of representable reward models while making the optimal policy analytically tractable for all prompts $x$.
The authors' framework can be used to diagnose instabilities in standard actor-critic algorithms used for RLHF, such as PPO.
The framework draws connections to the control-as-inference formulation for the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
The approach assumes a parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\big[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid\mid\pi^*(y\mid x)\big]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy induced by the reward $r_{\phi}(y,x)$.
Minimizing that KL divergence leads to the actor-critic optimization objective $\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\big[ r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y)\right)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\big]$.
The objective described is the same objective optimized in prior works using the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class of $r_{\phi}$.
The normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function of the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$.
The normalization term does not affect the optimal solution of the objective.
Without the normalization term, the policy gradient of the objective can have high variance, which can make learning unstable.
One approach to accommodate the normalization term is to use a learned value function, but this learned value function can also be difficult to optimize.
Prior works have normalized rewards using a human completion baseline, which is essentially a single-sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the normalizing term.
The DPO reparameterization yields a reward function that does not require any baselines.
The experiments empirically evaluate DPO's ability to train policies directly from preferences.
In a well-controlled text-generation setting, the experiments ask how efficiently DPO trades off maximizing reward and minimizing KL-divergence with the reference policy, compared to common preference learning algorithms such as PPO.
The experiments also evaluate DPO's performance on larger models and more difficult RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue.
The authors find that with almost no tuning of hyperparameters, DPO tends to perform as well or better than strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and than returning the best of $N$ sampled trajectories under a learned reward function.
Additional experimental details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.
The experiments explore three different open-ended text generation tasks.
For all experiments, algorithms learn a policy from a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$.
In controlled sentiment generation, $x$ is a prefix of a movie review from the IMDb dataset and the policy must generate $y$ with positive sentiment.
For the controlled sentiment experiment, preference pairs are generated using a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$.
For supervised fine-tuning (SFT) in the controlled sentiment task, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence on reviews from the train split of the IMDb dataset.
In summarization, $x$ is a forum post from Reddit and the policy must generate a summary $y$ of the main points in the post.
The summarization experiments use the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset along with human preferences gathered by Stiennon et al.
For summarization, an SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written forum post summaries and the TRLX framework is used for RLHF.
The human preference dataset for summarization was gathered on samples from a different, but similarly-trained, SFT model.
In single-turn dialogue, $x$ is a human query and the policy must produce an engaging and helpful response $y$.
The single-turn dialogue experiments use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset, which contains 170k dialogues between a human and an automated assistant.
Each transcript in the dialogue dataset ends with a pair of responses generated by a large (unknown) language model along with a preference label denoting the human-preferred response.
Because no pre-trained SFT model is available for the dialogue setting, the authors fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model on only the preferred completions to form the SFT model.
The experiments use two different evaluation approaches depending on whether the ground-truth reward is available.
In the controlled sentiment generation setting, algorithms are evaluated by their frontier of achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy because the ground-truth reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available.
In real-world settings where the ground-truth reward is not known, algorithms are evaluated by their win rate against a baseline policy.
GPT-4 is used as a proxy for human evaluation of summary quality in the summarization setting and response helpfulness in the single-turn dialogue setting.
For summarization evaluation, the baseline is the reference summaries in the test set.
For dialogue evaluation, the baseline is the preferred response in the test dataset.
A human study was conducted to justify using GPT-4 for evaluation, and GPT-4 judgments correlate strongly with human judgments.
Human agreement with GPT-4 is typically similar to or higher than inter-human annotator agreement.
The methods evaluated include DPO, zero-shot prompting with GPT-J for summarization, and 2-shot prompting with Pythia-2.8B for dialogue.
The SFT model and Preferred-FT are evaluated, where Preferred-FT is fine-tuned with supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$ from either the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic language model (in single-turn dialogue).
The Unlikelihood method optimizes the policy to maximize the probability of $y_w$ and minimize the probability of $y_l$, using an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ on the unlikelihood term.
PPO is evaluated using a reward function learned from preference data, and PPO-GT is an oracle variant that learns from the ground-truth reward function in the controlled sentiment setting.
Two implementations of PPO-GT are used in the sentiment experiments: an off-the-shelf version and a modified version that normalizes rewards and further tunes hyperparameters to improve performance.
The same reward normalization and hyperparameter modifications used for PPO-GT are also applied when running PPO with learned rewards.
The Best of $N$ baseline samples $N$ responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and returns the highest-scoring response according to a reward function learned from the preference dataset.
The Best of $N$ method decouples the quality of the reward model from PPO optimization but is computationally impractical for moderate $N$ because it requires sampling $N$ completions for every query at test time.
The KL-constrained reward maximization objective used in typical RLHF algorithms balances exploitation of reward while restricting the policy from deviating far from the reference policy.
When comparing algorithms one must consider both achieved reward and KL discrepancy because achieving slightly higher reward with much higher KL is not necessarily desirable.
The paper reports the reward-KL frontier for various algorithms in the sentiment setting.
The authors executed multiple training runs per algorithm, sweeping policy-conservativeness hyperparameters including PPO target KL $\in \{3,6,9,12\}$, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT.
The hyperparameter sweep included 22 runs in total.
After each 100 training steps until convergence, each policy was evaluated on a set of test prompts by computing the average reward under the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL $\text{KL}\left(\pi \mid\mid \pi_{\text{ref}}\right)$ with the reference policy.
The sequence-level KL is defined as the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences.
DPO produces the most efficient reward-KL frontier, achieving the highest reward while still maintaining low KL.
DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, but DPO is notably more efficient and its reward/KL tradeoff strictly dominates PPO.
DPO achieves a better frontier than PPO even when PPO can access ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT).
The authors evaluated the fine-tuning performance of DPO on both summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks.
Automatic evaluation metrics such as ROUGE can be poorly correlated with human preferences.
Prior work found that fine-tuning language models using PPO on human preferences yields more effective summaries.
The evaluation on summarization was done by sampling completions on the test split of the TL;DR summarization dataset and computing the average win rate against reference completions in the test set.
Completions for all methods were sampled at temperatures varying from 0.0 to 1.0.
DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tuned the same GPT-J SFT model.
DPO achieved an approximate win rate of 61% at temperature 0.0.
PPO achieved an approximate win rate of 57% at its optimal sampling temperature of 0.0.
DPO achieved a higher maximum win rate compared to the best-of-$N$ baseline.
The authors did not meaningfully tune DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter, so the reported results may underestimate DPO's potential.
DPO was much more robust to sampling temperature than PPO, whose performance can degrade to that of the base GPT-J model at high temperatures.
Preferred-FT did not improve significantly over the SFT model.
In human evaluations, DPO samples at temperature 0.25 were preferred 58% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.0.
The single-turn dialogue evaluation uses a subset of the test split of the Anthropic HH dataset with one step of human-assistant interaction.
GPT-4 evaluations use the preferred completions on the test as the reference to compute the win rate for different methods.
There is no standard SFT model for this task, so the authors start from a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model.
The authors use Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the chosen completions so that completions are within the distribution of the model.
After training the reference model with Preferred-FT, the authors train the model further using DPO.
The authors compare against the Best of $128$ Preferred-FT completions and observe that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at $128$ completions for this task.
The authors also compare to a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model and find that DPO performs as well or better for the best-performing temperatures for each method.
An RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset from a known source was evaluated, but no prompt or sampling temperature gave performance better than the base Pythia-2.8B model.
Based on results from TL;DR and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, the authors consider Best of $128$ to be a rough proxy for PPO-level performance.
Overall, DPO is the only computationally efficient method reported that improves over the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset.
DPO provides similar or better performance compared to the computationally demanding Best of $128$ baseline.
DPO converges to its best performance relatively quickly according to Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}.
The PPO and DPO policies from the Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment were evaluated on news articles in the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset.
The evaluation on CNN/DailyMail used the best sampling temperatures from TL;DR, specifically 0 and 0.25.
GPT-4 win rates were computed against the ground-truth summaries using the same GPT-4 (C) prompt from Reddit TL;DR, with the phrase "forum post" replaced by "news article".
On the CNN/DailyMail distribution, the DPO policy outperformed the PPO policy by a significant margin.
This out-of-distribution experiment provides initial evidence that DPO policies can generalize similarly well to PPO policies despite DPO not using the additional unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts that PPO uses.
A human study was conducted to verify the reliability of GPT-4's judgments using results from the TL;DR summarization experiment and two different GPT-4 prompts.
The GPT-4 (S) prompt simply asks which summary better summarizes the important information in the post.
The GPT-4 (C) prompt asks which summary better summarizes the important information and which summary is more concise.
The GPT-4 (C) prompt was used because with the GPT-4 (S) prompt GPT-4 preferred longer, more repetitive summaries than humans did.
Three comparisons were performed in the human study using a highest-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), a lowest-performing method (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a middle-performing method (SFT, temp. 0.25), each compared against greedily-sampled PPO at its best-performing temperature.
With both GPT-4 prompts, GPT-4 tended to agree with humans about as often as humans agreed with each other, indicating GPT-4 is a reasonable proxy for human evaluations.
Because of limited human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons.
The GPT-4 (C) prompt generally produced win rates more representative of humans and was therefore used for the main results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}.
Additional details about the human study, including the rater interface and the list of human volunteers, are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.
The paper argues that learning from preferences is a powerful and scalable framework for training capable, aligned language models.
The authors introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple training paradigm for training language models from preferences without reinforcement learning.
DPO identifies a mapping between language model policies and reward functions that enables training a language model to satisfy human preferences directly with a simple cross-entropy loss.
DPO trains language models from preferences without using reinforcement learning and without loss of generality compared to RL formulations.
With virtually no hyperparameter tuning, DPO performs similarly or better than existing RLHF algorithms, including those based on PPO.
Because DPO is simpler and performs competitively, it meaningfully reduces the barrier to training more language models from human preferences.
The paper raises the open question of how DPO policies generalize out of distribution compared with policies learned from an explicit reward function.
Initial results in the paper suggest that DPO policies can generalize similarly to PPO-based models, but the authors state that more comprehensive study is needed.
The authors propose investigating whether training with self-labeling from the DPO policy can make effective use of unlabeled prompts.
The paper raises the question of how reward over-optimization manifests in the direct preference optimization setting and whether a slight decrease in performance observed in one evaluation is an instance of it.
The authors evaluated models up to 6B parameters and identify scaling DPO to state-of-the-art models orders of magnitude larger as an important direction for future work.
The authors find that win rates computed by GPT-4 are impacted by the evaluation prompt and suggest future work to study how to elicit high-quality judgments from automated systems.
The paper states that DPO has potential applications beyond training language models from human preferences, including training generative models in other modalities.
