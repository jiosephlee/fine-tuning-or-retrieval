The RLHF Pipeline and Its Pain Points

# Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for downstream tasks
The first stage in the standard pipeline begins by adapting a large, unsupervised language network to the specific downstream domain or task that one cares about. This adaptation, often called supervised fine-tuning, trains the network to predict high-quality human-written continuations or responses given a prompt. The objective during this stage is the usual likelihood maximization: the model is exposed to many prompt–completion pairs and its parameters are adjusted so that the probability it assigns to the human completions is increased. The result is a reference policy, which we may denote $\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)$, that already generates fluent and task-relevant outputs and that serves as the starting point for later stages.

SFT has two complementary roles. First, it imparts domain-specific style and task competence: for example, a model fine-tuned on conversation transcripts will produce more dialog-appropriate replies than a raw pre-trained model. Second, it provides a stable sampling distribution that future optimization steps can use as an anchor. Because the model has been trained on high-quality human examples, many RL-style procedures prefer to keep the policy close to this SFT reference rather than letting it drift freely. Practically, SFT is conceptually simple and inexpensive compared with later phases, yet it is essential: the SFT policy is typically the main source of sensible behavior prior to preference-driven shaping.

# Preference collection: prompts and pairwise comparisons
After SFT, the pipeline collects human judgments that express relative preferences over alternative completions. The most common format is pairwise comparisons: given a prompt $x$, two candidate responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ are presented and a human rater indicates which of the two they prefer for that prompt. Repeating this process across many prompts builds a collection of preference triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one.

A typical process to obtain these comparisons is to sample candidate continuations from the SFT reference policy for many prompts and then have human raters compare sampled pairs. This scheme focuses annotation effort on alternatives the model actually proposes, which is practical and helps ensure the collected judgments are relevant to the generator’s behavior. The resulting set of comparisons serves as the empirical signal used to steer the model: the learning procedure should increase the probability of completions that humans tend to prefer and decrease the probability of those they tend to reject.

Two practical points are important. First, relative judgments are often easier and cheaper to obtain than absolute-quality scores or full demonstrations: people find it simpler to choose which of two outputs is better than to write an ideal output themselves. Second, because comparisons are sampled from the model’s own output distribution, the collection reflects the model’s weaknesses and strengths; this has advantages for targeted improvement but also produces a particular distributional structure that later optimization must respect.

# Reward modeling via Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models
To convert preference comparisons into a scalar training signal that guides optimization, practitioners postulate a latent scoring function (a reward) that assigns a numeric value to each completion given its prompt. A widely-used probabilistic link between scores and pairwise choices is the Bradley–Terry model, a two-item instance of the broader Plackett–Luce family. Under Bradley–Terry, the probability that completion $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ for prompt $x$ is modeled as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)},
\]
where $r^*(x,y)$ denotes the unknown true scalar score for the prompt–completion pair. This logistic-form probability depends only on the difference $r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)$, which is a useful property but also implies an under-specification: adding any function that depends only on $x$ to all rewards leaves pairwise probabilities unchanged.

A common operational procedure is to parametrize a differentiable reward predictor $r_\phi(x,y)$ and fit it by maximum likelihood to the observed comparisons. Using the logistic link, the negative log-likelihood loss for a collection of comparisons $\mathcal{C}$ is
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi; \mathcal{C}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{C}}\bigl[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function. In practice this reward predictor is often built on top of the same transformer that produced the SFT policy: a scalar head is placed on the final hidden state and trained to match the preference comparisons. Because the link model cares only about score differences, practitioners sometimes center or normalize predicted scores to reduce variance, but the essential step is learning a scalar function that reflects the pairwise judgments.

Plackett–Luce generalizes Bradley–Terry to ranked lists: it assigns probabilities to permutations of multiple alternatives using exponentials of scores. The same key property holds: only relative differences in scores determine ranking probabilities, so there is an inherent degree of freedom in absolute score values as a function of the prompt.

# KL-regularized policy optimization and the role of a reference
Once a reward predictor is in hand, the next task is to obtain a policy that produces outputs with high expected reward. Optimizing purely for expected reward can cause the policy to wander into regions of output space where the predictor has not been validated, producing degenerate or repetitive outputs. To control this drift, the optimization is commonly regularized with a divergence penalty that keeps the learned policy close to a reference distribution. The standard formulation maximizes expected reward under the learned reward function while penalizing deviation from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT policy). Formally, write the objective for a candidate policy $\pi_\theta$ as
\[
\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{C},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta>0$ trades off reward improvement against staying near the reference. Conceptually, this is a constrained policy improvement objective: it seeks higher expected score while limiting the distributional shift from the initial policy. The KL term acts as a soft trust-region, discouraging adversarial or unlikely generations that might exploit flaws in the reward predictor.

This regularization has several practical uses. It helps keep generations fluent and diverse (because the reference carries the distributional mass of natural text) and reduces the danger of reward over-optimization when the predictor extrapolates poorly. Choosing $\pi_\text{ref}$ as the SFT policy makes the penalty meaningful: the model will only move away from the human-grounded reference when the reward signal consistently encourages it.

# PPO for RL fine-tuning: on-policy sampling, baselines, and tuning
Because the output space of language models is discrete and the expected-reward objective is not directly differentiable through sampling, practitioners typically optimize the KL-regularized objective using reinforcement-style updates. A mainstream choice is proximal policy optimization (PPO), a stable policy-update procedure that operates by collecting on-policy samples, estimating advantage-like quantities, and taking constrained updates designed to avoid large policy steps.

In the RL fine-tuning loop using PPO, the current policy is used to sample continuations for many prompts; those sampled continuations are scored by the learned reward predictor (possibly with a term that corrects for the KL to the reference), and those scalar values form the learning signal. To reduce variance of gradient estimates, a value estimator (a network that predicts expected future score) is trained alongside the policy and used as a baseline when forming the advantage. The PPO update itself contains a clipped surrogate objective that prevents single updates from changing action probabilities too drastically; this clipping is one of the mechanics that lead to stable learning in many domains.

However, PPO-style training in this setting has practical subtleties. Because sampling is done from the policy being optimized, training requires frequent generation of many sequences, which is computationally expensive for large networks. The value estimator and the policy must be tuned jointly; the baseline must be chosen or estimated carefully to control gradient variance. The clipping thresholds, batch sizes, frequency of policy updates versus value updates, and how rewards are normalized all influence behavior, producing a sizable tuning burden in practice. Moreover, the training loop involves interleaving sampling and parameter updates, which complicates engineering and increases wall-clock training cost relative to purely supervised procedures.

# Practical challenges: instability, computational cost, and engineering burden
Putting the stages together reveals several pain points that commonly arise in real RL-from-preferences workflows.

First, instability in fine-tuning is a recurring problem. The combination of a learned reward predictor and policy optimization can produce high-variance gradient estimates. If the predictor assigns large scores to out-of-distribution continuations, the policy can exploit those regions and produce degenerate text. To mitigate this, practitioners rely on the KL penalty, reward normalization, learned value baselines, and carefully tuned update schedules; each of these instruments adds sensitivity and more tuning knobs to manage.

Second, the computational load is substantial. The pipeline typically requires maintaining multiple networks simultaneously: the SFT reference, the reward predictor, the policy being optimized, and a value predictor for variance reduction. On-policy updates force generation from the current policy inside the training loop, so training consumes significant generation compute. When training large networks, this sampling cost is a major contributor to wall-clock time and resource usage.

Third, engineering complexity rises quickly. The loop that collects model samples, ships them for scoring, trains a reward predictor, and then runs RL-style updates across multiple components is more intricate than standard supervised fine-tuning. Debugging is harder because failure can stem from any of the stages: a miscalibrated reward predictor, an unstable value estimator, imprudent clipping settings, or subtle distributional shift between sampling and scoring. The need to manage many interacting parts increases the barrier to entry for groups seeking to reproduce or extend the pipeline.

Finally, human-preference collection itself is costly and must be designed carefully to yield reliable, well-calibrated signals. Since preference models like Bradley–Terry only identify score differences, practitioners must choose a suitable reference and possibly impose normalization to make numerical rewards amenable to optimization. All of these aspects combine to make the standard pipeline powerful but resource- and effort-intensive.

In summary, the three-stage RL-from-preferences procedure—supervised fine-tuning to set a strong reference, preference collection to obtain comparative human judgments, and reward-predictor-driven RL-style fine-tuning with KL regularization—provides a principled route to steer large language networks toward desired behavior. At the same time, its reliance on multiple learned components, on-policy sampling, variance-reduction machinery, and careful tuning produces instability, heavy compute demands, and substantial engineering overhead. Understanding these trade-offs clarifies why simpler or more direct procedures that preserve the benefits of preference learning while reducing the loop’s complexity are attractive directions for further study.
