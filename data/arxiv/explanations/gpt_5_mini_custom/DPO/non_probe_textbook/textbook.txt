Title: Textbook of Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

Motivation: From Knowledge to Preferred Behavior

# Capabilities of an unsupervised language model versus controllability needs
Large-scale unsupervised pretraining equips a single language model with a broad repository of factual knowledge, stylistic patterns, and some reasoning tendencies. Through exposure to massive corpora, the parameters of the network encode many ways to continue a prefix, imitate genres, and produce plausible completions for a wide array of prompts. This broad competence is powerful: a single pretrained instance can respond about many topics, perform arithmetic-like steps, and imitate diverse writing voices without task-specific supervision.

However, this general competence is distinct from the narrower objective of producing the particular kinds of responses a developer or end user wants in deployment. The pretrained model’s implicit priorities reflect the varied intents and quality levels present in its training material: some continuations reflect expert-level solutions, others reflect casual opinion or error. When the system is deployed it is often desirable to bias behavior toward a specific subset of the possible continuations—for example, high-quality technical answers, concise summaries, or responses that avoid certain unsafe content. That biasing requirement is what I call controllability: the ability to steer the model’s outputs toward preferred behaviors while retaining the underlying competence and fluency the pretrained instance provides.

Controllability imposes two linked constraints. First, the preferred behavior should improve the outputs on desiderata of interest: factuality, helpfulness, succinctness, safety, or task-specific quality. Second, steering must avoid catastrophic degeneration: the system should not collapse to a narrow set of repetitive replies nor wander into outputs that the preference signal cannot reliably evaluate. Practically, that means any mechanism to push the model in a preferred direction must also include a restraint that keeps the adapted policy close to the original distribution on which the model’s general capability is defined.

# Examples of misalignment: misconceptions, coding quality, safety
Concrete examples illuminate why this steering matters. A pretrained language model may have encoded a widely believed but incorrect claim alongside more accurate statements. If the deployment goal is to provide correct answers, the system must not present the common misconception as the dominant reply; instead it should prefer the higher-quality evidence-based response that may be less frequent in the training text. In other words, the goal is not merely to represent population-level priors, but to select the subset of knowledge one wants the model to express.

In code generation, the same distinction appears. A pretrained instance may have seen both idiomatic, well-tested code and snippets that contain subtle bugs or anti-patterns. When used as a coding assistant, the system should privilege the higher-quality patterns—clear, secure, and correct implementations—even if those appear less often in raw text. Without targeted steering, the model will produce an indiscriminate mixture of both kinds of completions.

Safety concerns provide further examples. The training data may contain toxic or unsafe language produced by some authors. While the model must retain the ability to discuss safety topics when appropriate, the deployment objective commonly requires that the model avoid generating harmful content or must respond with mitigations. Achieving that selective behavior again requires a mechanism to prefer some completions over others, beyond what unsupervised pretraining provides.

Across these cases the underlying challenge is not the absence of capability in the pretrained instance; rather, it is the absence of a reliable, controllable selector that chooses which of the many competent continuations are acceptable for the deployed context.

# Instruction tuning versus preference-based tuning
One widely used way to bias behavior is to adjust the pretrained parameters with examples of desired outputs. A typical procedure is to present many prompts together with human-written or otherwise high-quality completions and update the language model’s parameters to increase the probability of those target continuations. This process, often called supervised fine-tuning, can make the model more responsive to instructions and improve baseline usefulness. It teaches the network to map prompts to the style and content exemplified by the demonstration set.

However, supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations has limitations. High-quality demonstrations can be expensive to collect because they often require expert-level writing or domain expertise. In many settings, rather than crafting exact demonstrations, it is easier for people to make relative judgments: presented two alternative continuations for the same prompt, a rater can reliably indicate which they prefer without producing a new exemplar. This type of comparative signal captures preference at scale while being less demanding per judgment.

Preference-based tuning embraces that kind of feedback. Instead of training on explicit demonstrations, one gathers pairs of continuations for the same prompt and records which of the pair is preferred by a rater. The collected paired judgments give a dataset of comparisons that encode which responses should be assigned higher “score.” This relative format is often more scalable for large corpora of prompts, since producing two completions from a base policy is cheaper than authoring a high-quality completion from scratch for each prompt, and many more prompts can be judged in parallel by non-expert raters.

# Why relative judgments from people are scalable
Relative preference labels have three practical advantages. First, for many tasks people find it easier to compare than to produce: judging between two completions avoids the cognitive load of composing a whole response and lets a rater focus on quality differences. Second, generating candidate continuations can be automated by sampling from an existing reference policy, so the human time is spent only on judgment rather than production. Third, comparative judgments allow broad coverage: the same reference policy can be sampled across many prompts, and each sampled pair elicits information about the underlying scoring function that ranks completions. With a moderate number of comparisons per prompt, one can recover a robust ordering of candidate replies.

From a statistical perspective, pairwise comparisons connect naturally to probabilistic preference models like the Bradley–Terry family. Those models formalize the probability that a rater prefers one completion over another as a function of latent scores assigned to each completion. Fitting such models to batches of pairwise judgments recovers an estimate of the latent scoring function up to certain identifiability constraints. Crucially, the pairwise format transfers well to large-scale collection: many cheap comparisons can produce a dense picture of relative quality across a wide space of candidate outputs.

# Overview of RLHF and its complexity
A common end-to-end pipeline that uses pairwise preferences proceeds in three conceptual stages. First, the pretrained language model is adapted with supervised fine-tuning on a corpus of high-quality demonstrations to obtain a reasonable reference policy; this step helps the later stages by keeping sampled continuations coherent and diverse. Second, the reference policy is sampled to produce candidate continuations for prompts; pairs of such continuations are then presented to people who indicate which they prefer. Third, the collected comparisons are used to fit a scalar function that aims to assign higher numbers to preferred completions; this scalar is then treated as a surrogate reward that guides a subsequent policy optimization step. The policy optimization objective is typically to maximize the expected surrogate reward under the new policy while penalizing deviation from the reference policy, commonly with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence term that preserves coverage and prevents collapse.

Formally, letting \(x\) denote a prompt and \(y\) a completion, the Bradley–Terry preference model states that the probability an evaluator chooses \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) depends on a latent score function \(r^*(x,y)\) through
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) = \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)}.
\]
A parametrized scalar function \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is fitted to the paired comparisons by maximizing the binary log-likelihood. Then the policy \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) is optimized to maximize expected reward under \(\pi_\theta\) while controlling divergence from a reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\), typically solving
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\; y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\, r_\phi(x,y)\,\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big],
\]
for a scalar \(\beta>0\) that trades off reward improvement and proximity to the reference. Because the output \(y\) is discrete, direct differentiation through the sampling process is not available, so practitioners optimize this objective with policy-optimization procedures from the reinforcement literature. These procedures involve sampling trajectories, estimating gradients via Monte Carlo, and managing additional components such as value estimators or baselines. In practice this pipeline is effective but operationally complex: it requires training a separate reward regressor, rolling out a sampling loop during the optimization phase, and tuning many optimization hyperparameters to stabilize training.

# High-level idea of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Direct Preference Optimization offers a distinct route that bypasses fitting a standalone scalar regressor and avoids the explicit reinforcement-style optimization loop. The key insight is algebraic: under the KL-constrained objective above, for any given scalar reward function \(r(x,y)\) the optimal policy has a closed-form relation to the reference policy. Specifically, the optimal policy \(\pi_r\) that maximizes expected reward minus \(\beta\) times KL satisfies
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\big).
\]
Taking logarithms and rearranging yields an exact reparameterization of the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x),
\]
where \(Z(x)\) is the partition function. Crucially, many preference models—including Bradley–Terry—depend only on differences of scores for two completions, and the additive partition-function term cancels when taking those differences. That cancellation means the pairwise preference probability can be expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^*\) and the reference \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), without the intermediate scalar regressor:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big),
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))\) is the logistic function.

This identity suggests a direct fitting objective for a parametrized policy \(\pi_\theta\): take the collection of observed triples \((x,y_{\mathrm{win}},y_{\mathrm{lose}})\) and maximize the likelihood that \(\pi_\theta\) assigns higher implicit score ratios to the preferred completions. Concretely, the loss becomes
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\left[\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\right].
\]
Optimizing this objective updates the policy parameters so that the implicit reward
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;:=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
ranks preferred completions higher than dispreferred ones. The key benefits of this construction are practical and theoretical. Practically, the optimization reduces to a simple binary cross-entropy over pairs: no separate scalar regressor, no inner reinforcement-style sampling loop, and no need to estimate partition functions. Theoretical justification comes from observing that the reparameterization spans the same equivalence classes of reward functions relevant to pairwise preference models: adding a function of the prompt alone does not change pairwise probabilities or the optimal policy, and the DPO form picks a canonical representative of the equivalence class that makes the optimal policy explicit.

Intuitively, a DPO update increases the log-probability of the preferred completion and decreases that of the less preferred completion, but with a per-example weight given by the logistic of the implicit reward difference. This adaptive weighting prevents excessive pushes on examples where the current implicit reward already ranks the pair correctly and concentrates updates on misordered pairs, which helps avoid the collapse phenomena that a naive likelihood-ratio objective can cause.

In summary, Direct Preference Optimization turns the usual two-stage fitting process—first fit a scalar ranking function, then optimize a policy under KL control—into a single-stage procedure that directly adapts a parametrized policy to match observed pairwise preferences while maintaining an explicit proximity penalty to the reference policy. The result is a simpler, more stable method to transform the broad capabilities encoded by an unsupervised language model into the specific, preferred behaviors needed for deployment.

The RLHF Pipeline and Its Pain Points

# Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for downstream tasks
The first stage in the standard pipeline begins by adapting a large, unsupervised language network to the specific downstream domain or task that one cares about. This adaptation, often called supervised fine-tuning, trains the network to predict high-quality human-written continuations or responses given a prompt. The objective during this stage is the usual likelihood maximization: the model is exposed to many prompt–completion pairs and its parameters are adjusted so that the probability it assigns to the human completions is increased. The result is a reference policy, which we may denote $\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)$, that already generates fluent and task-relevant outputs and that serves as the starting point for later stages.

SFT has two complementary roles. First, it imparts domain-specific style and task competence: for example, a model fine-tuned on conversation transcripts will produce more dialog-appropriate replies than a raw pre-trained model. Second, it provides a stable sampling distribution that future optimization steps can use as an anchor. Because the model has been trained on high-quality human examples, many RL-style procedures prefer to keep the policy close to this SFT reference rather than letting it drift freely. Practically, SFT is conceptually simple and inexpensive compared with later phases, yet it is essential: the SFT policy is typically the main source of sensible behavior prior to preference-driven shaping.

# Preference collection: prompts and pairwise comparisons
After SFT, the pipeline collects human judgments that express relative preferences over alternative completions. The most common format is pairwise comparisons: given a prompt $x$, two candidate responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ are presented and a human rater indicates which of the two they prefer for that prompt. Repeating this process across many prompts builds a collection of preference triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one.

A typical process to obtain these comparisons is to sample candidate continuations from the SFT reference policy for many prompts and then have human raters compare sampled pairs. This scheme focuses annotation effort on alternatives the model actually proposes, which is practical and helps ensure the collected judgments are relevant to the generator’s behavior. The resulting set of comparisons serves as the empirical signal used to steer the model: the learning procedure should increase the probability of completions that humans tend to prefer and decrease the probability of those they tend to reject.

Two practical points are important. First, relative judgments are often easier and cheaper to obtain than absolute-quality scores or full demonstrations: people find it simpler to choose which of two outputs is better than to write an ideal output themselves. Second, because comparisons are sampled from the model’s own output distribution, the collection reflects the model’s weaknesses and strengths; this has advantages for targeted improvement but also produces a particular distributional structure that later optimization must respect.

# Reward modeling via Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models
To convert preference comparisons into a scalar training signal that guides optimization, practitioners postulate a latent scoring function (a reward) that assigns a numeric value to each completion given its prompt. A widely-used probabilistic link between scores and pairwise choices is the Bradley–Terry model, a two-item instance of the broader Plackett–Luce family. Under Bradley–Terry, the probability that completion $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ for prompt $x$ is modeled as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)},
\]
where $r^*(x,y)$ denotes the unknown true scalar score for the prompt–completion pair. This logistic-form probability depends only on the difference $r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)$, which is a useful property but also implies an under-specification: adding any function that depends only on $x$ to all rewards leaves pairwise probabilities unchanged.

A common operational procedure is to parametrize a differentiable reward predictor $r_\phi(x,y)$ and fit it by maximum likelihood to the observed comparisons. Using the logistic link, the negative log-likelihood loss for a collection of comparisons $\mathcal{C}$ is
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi; \mathcal{C}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{C}}\bigl[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function. In practice this reward predictor is often built on top of the same transformer that produced the SFT policy: a scalar head is placed on the final hidden state and trained to match the preference comparisons. Because the link model cares only about score differences, practitioners sometimes center or normalize predicted scores to reduce variance, but the essential step is learning a scalar function that reflects the pairwise judgments.

Plackett–Luce generalizes Bradley–Terry to ranked lists: it assigns probabilities to permutations of multiple alternatives using exponentials of scores. The same key property holds: only relative differences in scores determine ranking probabilities, so there is an inherent degree of freedom in absolute score values as a function of the prompt.

# KL-regularized policy optimization and the role of a reference
Once a reward predictor is in hand, the next task is to obtain a policy that produces outputs with high expected reward. Optimizing purely for expected reward can cause the policy to wander into regions of output space where the predictor has not been validated, producing degenerate or repetitive outputs. To control this drift, the optimization is commonly regularized with a divergence penalty that keeps the learned policy close to a reference distribution. The standard formulation maximizes expected reward under the learned reward function while penalizing deviation from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT policy). Formally, write the objective for a candidate policy $\pi_\theta$ as
\[
\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{C},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta>0$ trades off reward improvement against staying near the reference. Conceptually, this is a constrained policy improvement objective: it seeks higher expected score while limiting the distributional shift from the initial policy. The KL term acts as a soft trust-region, discouraging adversarial or unlikely generations that might exploit flaws in the reward predictor.

This regularization has several practical uses. It helps keep generations fluent and diverse (because the reference carries the distributional mass of natural text) and reduces the danger of reward over-optimization when the predictor extrapolates poorly. Choosing $\pi_\text{ref}$ as the SFT policy makes the penalty meaningful: the model will only move away from the human-grounded reference when the reward signal consistently encourages it.

# PPO for RL fine-tuning: on-policy sampling, baselines, and tuning
Because the output space of language models is discrete and the expected-reward objective is not directly differentiable through sampling, practitioners typically optimize the KL-regularized objective using reinforcement-style updates. A mainstream choice is proximal policy optimization (PPO), a stable policy-update procedure that operates by collecting on-policy samples, estimating advantage-like quantities, and taking constrained updates designed to avoid large policy steps.

In the RL fine-tuning loop using PPO, the current policy is used to sample continuations for many prompts; those sampled continuations are scored by the learned reward predictor (possibly with a term that corrects for the KL to the reference), and those scalar values form the learning signal. To reduce variance of gradient estimates, a value estimator (a network that predicts expected future score) is trained alongside the policy and used as a baseline when forming the advantage. The PPO update itself contains a clipped surrogate objective that prevents single updates from changing action probabilities too drastically; this clipping is one of the mechanics that lead to stable learning in many domains.

However, PPO-style training in this setting has practical subtleties. Because sampling is done from the policy being optimized, training requires frequent generation of many sequences, which is computationally expensive for large networks. The value estimator and the policy must be tuned jointly; the baseline must be chosen or estimated carefully to control gradient variance. The clipping thresholds, batch sizes, frequency of policy updates versus value updates, and how rewards are normalized all influence behavior, producing a sizable tuning burden in practice. Moreover, the training loop involves interleaving sampling and parameter updates, which complicates engineering and increases wall-clock training cost relative to purely supervised procedures.

# Practical challenges: instability, computational cost, and engineering burden
Putting the stages together reveals several pain points that commonly arise in real RL-from-preferences workflows.

First, instability in fine-tuning is a recurring problem. The combination of a learned reward predictor and policy optimization can produce high-variance gradient estimates. If the predictor assigns large scores to out-of-distribution continuations, the policy can exploit those regions and produce degenerate text. To mitigate this, practitioners rely on the KL penalty, reward normalization, learned value baselines, and carefully tuned update schedules; each of these instruments adds sensitivity and more tuning knobs to manage.

Second, the computational load is substantial. The pipeline typically requires maintaining multiple networks simultaneously: the SFT reference, the reward predictor, the policy being optimized, and a value predictor for variance reduction. On-policy updates force generation from the current policy inside the training loop, so training consumes significant generation compute. When training large networks, this sampling cost is a major contributor to wall-clock time and resource usage.

Third, engineering complexity rises quickly. The loop that collects model samples, ships them for scoring, trains a reward predictor, and then runs RL-style updates across multiple components is more intricate than standard supervised fine-tuning. Debugging is harder because failure can stem from any of the stages: a miscalibrated reward predictor, an unstable value estimator, imprudent clipping settings, or subtle distributional shift between sampling and scoring. The need to manage many interacting parts increases the barrier to entry for groups seeking to reproduce or extend the pipeline.

Finally, human-preference collection itself is costly and must be designed carefully to yield reliable, well-calibrated signals. Since preference models like Bradley–Terry only identify score differences, practitioners must choose a suitable reference and possibly impose normalization to make numerical rewards amenable to optimization. All of these aspects combine to make the standard pipeline powerful but resource- and effort-intensive.

In summary, the three-stage RL-from-preferences procedure—supervised fine-tuning to set a strong reference, preference collection to obtain comparative human judgments, and reward-predictor-driven RL-style fine-tuning with KL regularization—provides a principled route to steer large language networks toward desired behavior. At the same time, its reliance on multiple learned components, on-policy sampling, variance-reduction machinery, and careful tuning produces instability, heavy compute demands, and substantial engineering overhead. Understanding these trade-offs clarifies why simpler or more direct procedures that preserve the benefits of preference learning while reducing the loop’s complexity are attractive directions for further study.

Foundations: Preference Models and KL-Constrained RL

# Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce preference models

Many practical settings produce judgments not as numeric scores but as pairwise comparisons: given the same prompt or context, two generated continuations are presented and a rater indicates which of the two they prefer. A compact probabilistic model for such pairwise outcomes is the Bradley–Terry formulation. In this view each completion y for a prompt x is associated with a latent scalar score r*(x,y). The probability that completion y1 is preferred to completion y2 is a soft comparison of these scores. In symbols,

\[ p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}. \]

This expression depends only on the difference \(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\); adding any function of x to all scores for that prefix leaves the pairwise probabilities unchanged. That under-specification is a central structural property: only relative scores matter for pairwise comparisons.

The Bradley–Terry form is a special case of the Plackett–Luce family, which handles ranked lists of more than two alternatives by assigning selection probabilities via normalized exponentials of scores. Intuitively, Plackett–Luce views ranking as repeated draws without replacement from a softmax defined by the scores. For our purposes the key takeaways are (a) pairwise preference probabilities are determined by exponentiated scores, and (b) only score differences are identifiable from pairwise judgments.

# Maximum likelihood for reward functions and sigmoid-based negative log-likelihood

Given a collection of triples \((x, y_w, y_\ell)\) in which \(y_w\) denotes the completion preferred over \(y_\ell\) for prefix x, one can fit a parametric scorer \(r_\phi(x,y)\) by maximizing the likelihood implied by the Bradley–Terry model. Writing \(\sigma(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))\) for the sigmoid, the negative log-likelihood objective (to be minimized) takes the compact form

\[ \mathcal{L}_R(\phi) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_\ell)}\Big[ \log \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_\ell)\bigr)\Big]. \]

This objective increases the gap between the fitted score on the preferred completion and the fitted score on the dispreferred completion. Practically, implementations often initialize \(r_\phi\) by taking an existing sequence model and appending a small head that maps a final hidden representation to a scalar reward; the parameters of that head (and sometimes of the underlying model) are then adjusted to reduce \(\mathcal{L}_R\) on the collection of comparisons.

Two important structural points follow from the sigmoid formulation. First, because the preference probability depends on score differences, the objective does not pin down an absolute baseline for scores: a per-prefix constant may be added without changing pairwise likelihoods. Second, the sigmoid-based log-likelihood is convex in the score differences and thus provides a straightforward fit target for scalar-valued parametrizations.

# KL-regularized reward-optimization objective

A common next step is to take a fitted scorer and use it as feedback to modify a conditional sequence generator (a policy) so the generator produces continuations that receive higher scores. Rather than simply pushing the generator to choose high-scoring completions, practitioners typically impose a penalty that keeps the updated generator close to a reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (for example, the sequence model obtained from supervised fine-tuning). This results in an objective that trades off expected reward against a KL-type penalty relative to the reference. In compact mathematical form the per-prefix objective is

\[ \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\, r(x,y)\,\big] \;-\; \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big), \]

and an overall objective averages this expression over the prompts of interest. The scalar \(\beta>0\) controls how strongly deviation from the reference is penalized: small \(\beta\) places more emphasis on matching the reference, while large \(\beta\) permits larger shifts to increase expected reward.

The KL term acts as a regularizer that discourages mode collapse and helps ensure that the updated generator remains within the support where the scorer is reliable. In sequence settings the KL is typically computed as the expected sum over token-level log-ratio terms; this structure will be convenient in what follows.

# Deriving the optimal policy under the KL penalty

The optimization above is a convex variational problem in the space of distributions \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) for each fixed x. One can derive the analytic form of the optimizer by forming the Lagrangian that enforces that \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) sums to one. For a fixed prompt x write the objective to be maximized as

\[ J(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\,r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \lambda\Big(1 - \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\Big). \]

Differentiating \(J(\pi)\) with respect to \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and setting the derivative to zero yields

\[ r(x,y) - \beta\bigg( \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + 1\bigg) - \lambda \;=\; 0. \]

Rearranging and exponentiating gives the closed-form optimizer

\[ \pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big), \]

with the normalizing factor (partition function)

\[ Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big). \]

This expression has a clear interpretation: the optimal generator reweights the reference probabilities by an exponential tilt proportional to the scorer \(r\). When \(\beta\) is small the tilt is mild and the generator remains close to the reference; when \(\beta\) is large the generator more strongly prefers high-scoring continuations.

# Partition function and its intractability in sequence generation

The partition function \(Z(x)\) above is a sum over the entire space of possible continuations y for a given prefix. In simple discrete problems this sum can be computed exactly, but in sequence generation the space of possible sequences is astronomically large and direct computation is infeasible. Two common remedies are: Monte Carlo estimation by sampling from some proposal and importance-weighted approximations, or approximating \(Z(x)\) with a learned baseline term. Both techniques incur practical costs: sampling-based estimators incur variance that can destabilize optimization, and learned baselines introduce additional fitted components that must be tuned and can themselves produce bias.

The intractability of \(Z(x)\) therefore constitutes a key computational bottleneck when attempting to use the closed-form optimal policy directly from a fitted reward function. Any procedure that depends on explicit evaluations of \(Z(x)\) for many prefixes will either be very costly or will inherit the instability of high-variance estimates.

# Control-as-inference perspective and soft value functions

An alternative and illuminating way to view the KL-regularized objective is through the lens of probabilistic inference. The optimal form \(\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)\) can be seen as the posterior distribution that results from treating the reference as a prior and the exponential of the reward as a likelihood factor. From this viewpoint the partition function \(Z(x)\) plays the role of a soft value: it is the normalizer that converts the unnormalized quantity \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)\) into a proper probability distribution.

It is useful to rewrite the per-step objective used when updating a parametric policy \(\pi_\theta\). Substituting the expression for the optimal form back into a KL minimization gives an equivalent maximization

\[ \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\, r(x,y) - \beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\big) \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big]. \]

Here the term

\[ V_{\text{soft}}(x) \;:=\; \beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\big) \]

is the soft value associated with the reference prior and reward. It depends only on x (not on the sampled y) and hence acts like a baseline that reduces variance of gradient estimates. In practice, many procedures estimate or approximate \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) with a separate parametric component. That introduces a second fitted object and the need to coordinate its updates with the policy updater; mismatches and variance in that estimation can produce instability in the overall procedure.

Viewed from control-as-inference, the optimization balances three effects: increasing immediate expected reward, accounting for the normalizing soft value that captures the expected exponential reward under the reference, and penalizing departure from the reference through the \(-\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) term. The presence of the soft value baseline is essential for correct gradients but is the source of practical difficulty when it must be approximated or Monte Carlo–estimated in high-dimensional sequence spaces.

The closed-form relationship between reward functions and optimal policies summarized above yields two further observations. First, multiple reward functions that differ by a prefix-dependent additive constant produce the same pairwise preferences and, importantly, the same optimal policy under the KL-penalized objective. Second, if one can reparameterize the scoring function in terms of a policy as

\[ r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \text{(a function of }x\text{)}, \]

then the partition function cancels in pairwise comparisons and the optimization based on comparisons can be expressed directly in terms of policy ratios without needing to compute \(Z(x)\). That reparameterization is the foundation upon which single-stage procedures can be built that fit a policy directly from preference comparisons while avoiding separate partition estimates.

---

This chapter has presented the mathematical primitives that underlie preference-based control for sequence generation: probabilistic pairwise preference models that depend only on score differences, a sigmoid-based maximum likelihood objective for fitting scorers from comparison collections, the KL-penalized optimization that produces an exponential-tilt optimal policy, the intractable partition function that arises in sequence spaces, and the control-as-inference interpretation that identifies the soft value as the normalization term. These elements together explain both why fitting a separate scorer and then optimizing a policy is conceptually straightforward and why it is practically challenging in large discrete output spaces.

Deriving Direct Preference Optimization

# From optimal policy to reward: change of variables

We begin with the objective that is commonly used to train a generation policy to prefer high-scoring outputs while staying near a reference policy. Let $\pi(y\mid x)$ denote a candidate generation policy for outputs $y$ given prefix $x$, let $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ denote a fixed reference policy, and let $r(x,y)$ denote a scalar function that scores a completion. The constrained objective can be written as a tradeoff between expected score and divergence from the reference:

\[
\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\ \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\beta>0$ controls how strongly deviations from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ are penalized. This objective is a standard form that balances improving expected score against keeping the policy close to a known reference.

A key observation is that, for a fixed scoring function $r$, the solution of this optimization problem has a closed form. The policy that maximizes this objective for each prefix $x$ is a reweighted version of the reference policy:

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

where $Z(x)$ is the partition function that normalizes the right-hand side into a probability distribution over $y$. This form follows by taking functional derivatives with respect to $\pi$ and enforcing the normalization constraint; the exponentiated score reweights the reference distribution to favor higher-scoring completions.

Rearranging that expression gives an explicit relation from an optimal policy back to its scoring function. Taking logarithms and isolating $r(x,y)$ yields

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\ \log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\ \log Z(x).
\]

This is the change of variables that underpins the direct method: any scoring function (up to the additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$) can be represented by the log-ratio between its optimal policy and the reference, scaled by $\beta$. The additive term depends only on $x$, not on $y$, and this fact will be crucial in what follows.

# BT probability in terms of policy-to-reference log-ratio

Human or pairwise preference labels are often modeled using a logistic form known as the Bradley–Terry model. Under that model the probability that, for a given prefix $x$, a rater prefers completion $y_1$ to completion $y_2$ is

\[
p\bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\bigr) \;=\; \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\bigr)
\]

where $\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})$ is the logistic function and $r$ is the latent scoring function that explains preferences.

Using the reparameterization just derived, substitute the representation of $r$ in terms of $\pi_r$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ term cancels when you take the difference between two scores, because it appears for both $y_1$ and $y_2$ and depends only on $x$. After cancellation the pairwise preference probability becomes a function of the ratio of policy probabilities relative to the reference:

\[
p\bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\bigr) \;=\; \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr).
\]

Thus, the Bradley–Terry preference probability can be expressed entirely in terms of the log-ratios between the optimal policy and the reference policy. This representation removes any dependence on the intractable normalizer $Z(x)$ and permits working directly with policies in place of an explicit score function.

# DPO objective: logistic negative log-likelihood over policy log-ratio differences

Once pairwise preference probabilities are expressed as a function of a policy, one can fit a parametric policy directly by maximum likelihood on observed preference pairs. Suppose we have a dataset of triplets $(x, y_w, y_\ell)$ where $y_w$ is the completion preferred by a human annotator over the alternative $y_\ell$ given $x$. Parameterize a candidate policy by $\pi_\theta$. The negative log-likelihood of the data under the model above gives the training objective

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_\ell)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Biggl[ \log\,\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_\ell\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_\ell\mid x)}\Bigr)\Biggr].
\]

This objective is the logistic negative log-likelihood of pairwise preferences, written directly in terms of the candidate policy. Training minimizes this objective with respect to $\theta$, thereby fitting a policy whose implied scoring function $\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ explains the observed preferences under the Bradley–Terry model.

It is instructive to inspect the parameter gradient of this objective. Differentiating yields

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \;=\; -\beta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_\ell)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_\ell)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)\,\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_\ell\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr],
\]

where $\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ is the implicit score defined by the policy. The update therefore increases probability mass on preferred completions and decreases it on dispreferred completions; the magnitude of the per-example update is controlled by the logistic weighting factor, which reflects how strongly the current implicit score ordering disagrees with the observed preference. This adaptive per-example weight guards against na\"ive ratio-based updates that can otherwise push the policy into degenerate regions.

Because the objective is a standard likelihood objective on pairwise comparisons, implementation reduces to sampling preference pairs, computing log-probabilities of the two candidates under the current policy and the reference, forming the difference, applying the logistic transform and taking gradients — a procedure that avoids sampling from the policy inside the training loop as is required by many reinforcement-style procedures.

# Why the partition function cancels in pairwise differences

The cancellation of the partition function is a simple algebraic fact but conceptually important. The representation

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x)
\]

contains an additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ that depends only on the prefix $x$. When a preference model depends only on differences of scores, any component that is identical for two candidates will subtract out. In symbols,

\[
r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)},
\]

because the $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms cancel. Bradley–Terry and other ranking models in the Plackett–Luce family are functions of such pairwise or listwise score differences, so they are blind to additive functions of $x$ added to the scoring rule. That under-specification is not a shortcoming here; rather, it is the property that allows the scoring function to be expressed through a policy log-ratio without needing to compute $Z(x)$.

# Generalization from BT to PL listwise preferences

Bradley–Terry handles single pair comparisons. The more general Plackett–Luce family models preferences among lists or full rankings: the probability of an ordered list $(y_1, y_2,\ldots,y_k)$ under Plackett–Luce is

\[
p\bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \succ \cdots \succ y_k \mid x\bigr) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_i)\bigr)}{\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_j)\bigr)}.
\]

Each fraction uses exponentiated scores normalized over the remaining items. Substituting the reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$ into every numerator and denominator again produces factors of $Z(x)$ that cancel between numerator and denominator at each step. After cancellation, every factor can be expressed purely in terms of $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Thus the entire Plackett–Luce likelihood for listwise preferences becomes a function of the policy-to-reference log-ratios, just as in the pairwise case.

This means that the same change of variables that yields the pairwise DPO objective continues to apply when human data takes the form of ranked lists or full permutations: one can write the Plackett–Luce likelihood in terms of the candidate policy and fit it directly by maximum likelihood.

# Equivalence to optimizing KL-constrained reward maximization

One can view the direct fitting procedure as solving the original constrained objective implicitly. For any score function $r$, the optimal policy for the KL-constrained expected-score objective was shown above to be

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

Conversely, given any policy $\pi$, the formula

\[
\hat r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \text{function of }x
\]

gives a score function for which $\pi$ is optimal under the KL-constrained objective. The additive function of $x$ is inconsequential for preference likelihoods and for the optimality of $\pi$ because it cancels out in differences. Therefore the mapping between reward functions and their optimal policies is one-to-one up to the addition of an $x$-dependent offset, and any policy can be interpreted as optimal for some member of that family of rewards.

From a practical standpoint, this means that fitting $\pi_\theta$ by maximizing the preference likelihood written in terms of policy-to-reference log-ratios is equivalent to finding a policy that would maximize expected score subject to the KL penalty for some reward function in that family. Instead of first fitting a separate scoring function and then running a separate constrained optimization to turn that scorer into a new policy, the direct method fits the policy that is implicitly optimal for a compatible scoring function. In other words, the training target and the constrained optimization objective are consistent with each other under the change of variables.

A concise constructive argument clarifies the point. Given any scoring function $r$, define the transformed function

\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; r(x,y) \;-\; \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

The subtraction is an $x$-dependent normalization; $\tilde r$ differs from $r$ only by an additive function of $x$. Using the optimal-policy formula with $\tilde r$ gives

\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

so any member of the family of rewards that differ by an $x$-dependent offset admits a policy representation via the log-ratio. Hence fitting $\pi_\theta$ on preference data finds a policy that corresponds to some normalized version of a reward compatible with those preferences.

Taken together, these facts justify the direct procedure: by reparametrizing scores through the policy log-ratio and fitting the policy via logistic likelihood on preference pairs (or listwise likelihood for ranked data), one directly recovers a policy that is optimal for a KL-regularized expected-score problem for a compatible score function, avoiding the separate steps of reward fitting and reinforcement-style policy search.

--- 

Summary of the technical core. The change of variables from score function to policy log-ratio eliminates the need to compute the partition function when the preference model depends only on score differences. Writing the Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce likelihoods in terms of those log-ratios yields a simple maximum-likelihood objective for the policy parameters. The gradient of that objective has a clear interpretation as increasing preferred tokens and decreasing dispreferred ones, with adaptive per-example weighting that reflects current model disagreement with labels. Because every policy corresponds to some normalized score function, the procedure recovers a policy that is consistent with the KL-constrained expected-score optimization, so one may obtain the desired tradeoff between reward and divergence without a separate scoring-stage and without an inner loop that samples from the policy during training.

Mechanics of DPO: Intuition, Updates, and Tuning

# Implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \lambda \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$
Direct Preference Optimization replaces an explicit scalar scorer with a compact reparameterization that ties a policy to an implicit scoring function. The scoring function is defined by the policy's log-probability relative to a fixed reference policy. In symbols, for a policy $\pi_\theta$ and a chosen positive scalar $\lambda$, the implicit score used by the method is
$\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \lambda \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.
This expression has two parts: the log-ratio of probabilities compares how much the candidate completion $y$ is preferred by the current policy relative to the reference, and the multiplicative factor $\lambda$ sets the scale of those scores.

There are two useful ways to read this expression. First, it is a compact estimator of "advantage": it measures how much the current policy favors a particular completion over what the reference would produce. Second, because the reference appears in the denominator, the implicit score inherently encodes a regularizing preference for staying near the reference distribution. A completion that is assigned much higher probability than the reference will have a correspondingly large positive score, and vice versa. By making the policy itself carry the scores, the method avoids a separate step of fitting a standalone scalar regressor and then solving a separate constrained optimization to turn that regressor into a policy.

# Gradient analysis: upweighting misordered pairs via $\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)$
The training objective is a simple binary log-loss applied to pairwise preference records. Given a tuple $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ the dispreferred one, the loss for a batch of such comparisons is
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}\big[\log \sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\big]$,
with $\sigma$ the logistic function. Differentiating this loss with respect to the policy parameters yields the update term that practitioners will see during optimization:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)
= -\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
\]

This form is highly informative. Two effects are combined. The inner difference $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l)$ pushes probability mass toward the human-preferred completion $y_w$ and away from $y_l$. The multiplicative scalar $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ acts as a per-example importance weight: when the implicit scores already rank $y_w$ above $y_l$ by a comfortable margin, the sigmoid term is small and the update is downweighted; when the implicit scores wrongly prefer $y_l$, the sigmoid returns a value near one and the gradient push is strong. Thus the method focuses effort on examples where the current policy's implied ranking disagrees with the recorded preference. Finally, the overall factor $\lambda$ scales the magnitude of these corrections (see the next section for interpretation).

Intuitively, this prioritization prevents wasting gradient steps on examples that are already correctly ordered by the implicit scoring function, and concentrates updates on misordered pairs. It also produces a form of curriculum that is adaptive to the policy's current beliefs: easy examples receive small updates; hard or misranked examples receive larger corrections.

# Why naïve likelihood ratio / unlikelihood can degenerate
A simple, tempting alternative when one has pairwise preferences is to push up the likelihood of the preferred completion and push down the likelihood of the dispreferred completion with fixed weights. Concretely, one could minimize
$-\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) + \eta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$
with a constant multiplier $\eta>0$, or equivalently maximize the log-likelihood ratio $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\theta(y_l)}$ without further structure. This kind of unbalanced log-likelihood objective lacks the per-example, confidence-weighting mechanism described above.

Two failure modes appear in practice. First, with fixed negative pushes on many dispreferred completions, the policy can be driven to concentrate its mass in an unstable way: pushing down many candidates without an adaptive criterion encourages the policy to collapse onto a narrow set of tokens or completions that escape penalization. Second, without normalization tied to the reference, the log-ratio objective can produce runaway scaling: gradients can grow as the policy lowers the denominators and raises numerators, which destabilizes optimization and can encourage degenerate sequences that exploit quirks rather than satisfy true preference structure.

The DPO-style weighting using the logistic of score differences mitigates both problems. Because the per-pair weight depends on the current implied ranking, updates slow as the policy approaches an ordering consistent with the comparisons. Because the implicit scores are defined relative to a fixed reference, the method keeps an anchored notion of where probability mass should remain, reducing the tendency to collapse into pathological modes. Empirically, removing the adaptive sigmoid weight is shown to cause degeneration in generations; including it stabilizes optimization.

# Role of $\lambda$: trade-off between staying near the reference and update scaling
The scalar $\lambda$ plays a dual role. In the constrained objective that DPO is derived from, that same scalar controls the penalty on divergence from the reference policy: larger $\lambda$ corresponds to a stronger pull to remain close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, while smaller $\lambda$ allows larger departures to chase higher implicit scores. In the DPO gradients one also sees $\lambda$ appear multiplicatively, so it directly scales the size of parameter updates coming from each misordered pair.

These two effects mean that adjusting $\lambda$ has concrete operational consequences. Increasing $\lambda$ tends to make the learned policy more conservative: the implicit score differences must be larger to produce the same sigmoid weighting and the same tendency to change relative probabilities, so the learned policy will stay closer to the reference. Decreasing $\lambda$ makes the sigmoid more gradual and the policy freer to reallocate mass away from the reference. Because $\lambda$ multiplies gradients, it also interacts with the optimizer step size: if $\lambda$ is increased, one often reduces the optimizer learning rate to keep step magnitudes stable; if $\lambda$ is small, a larger optimizer step may be needed to make effective progress.

Two practical rules of thumb follow from the derivation. First, interpret $\lambda$ as the knob that encodes how much deviation from the reference is permitted: set it based on how conservative you want the final behavior to be. Second, tune the optimizer step size in tandem with $\lambda$ so that the effective parameter updates are in a reasonable range; otherwise changes in $\lambda$ can be absorbed inadvertently by the optimizer, making comparisons across settings difficult.

# Selecting and training the reference policy (SFT vs. Preferred-FT)
The reference distribution $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a crucial ingredient because all implicit scores are measured relative to it. The choice determines what "staying close" means, and it also affects how well the reparameterization matches the process that generated the comparison collection.

When a supervised fine-tuned policy (SFT) that originally produced the pairwise candidates is available, it is natural to use that SFT policy as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Doing so minimizes distribution mismatch: the comparisons were sampled from that SFT behavior, so using it as the denominator ensures the implicit scores reflect the same baseline that generated the comparisons. This makes the implicit reward well-calibrated and helps the optimizer avoid chasing regions of output space that were underrepresented in the sampled candidates.

If such an SFT artifact is not available, a pragmatic alternative is to construct a reference by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions themselves. This procedure, sometimes labeled Preferred-FT, fine-tunes a copy of the base generative model on the observed winners so that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assigns higher mass to the helpful responses. Using a reference trained on preferred completions reduces the mismatch that would otherwise be introduced if the denominator policy had little overlap with the observed winners. In short: choose a reference that reflects the distribution the comparisons came from; if that distribution is unknown, fit a compact reference to the preferred samples so the implicit ratios are meaningful.

The derivation also explains why the reference should be held fixed during the DPO minimization: the implicit reward is defined relative to a fixed denominator so that preference probabilities depend only on the learned policy. If one were to update both numerator and denominator jointly, identifiability issues would arise and the per-example weighting mechanism would be compromised.

# No LM sampling in the loop: stability and efficiency benefits
A major practical advantage of the DPO procedure is that the optimization reduces to a simple supervised binary classification-style loss applied to stored preference pairs. There is no need to roll out the current policy into its own sampling process during optimization, nor to estimate on-policy returns or fit auxiliary value estimators. Concretely, the training loop becomes: (i) collect or use an existing collection of comparisons $(x,y_w,y_l)$, (ii) evaluate log-probabilities of $y_w$ and $y_l$ under the current policy and the fixed reference, (iii) compute the logistic loss and its gradient, and (iv) update parameters with any standard stochastic optimizer.

This structure brings several operational benefits. First, variance is much lower than in classic policy-gradient loops because the updates rely on deterministic log-probabilities of a fixed set of sequences rather than noisy sampled returns. Second, there is no requirement to fit a separate scalar regressor or a baseline function to stabilize gradients, which removes a common source of instability and tuning burden. Third, the compute pattern is standard maximum-likelihood-style training with batched log-probability evaluations, which is easier to scale and to tune on modern accelerators than a sampled RL loop. Finally, because the optimization directly targets the policy that would be optimal under the implicit reward class, there is no mismatch between the fitted scalar scorer and the final policy optimization step, avoiding a two-stage training instability that otherwise appears when a separate scalar regressor is imperfect.

Taken together, these properties explain why the method can be both more stable and more computationally efficient than procedures that require sampling from the current policy at each update or fitting and using a separate value/baseline estimator.

Closing remarks
Understanding the mechanics of the DPO update clarifies why it behaves well in practice. By making the policy carry the implicit score, weighting updates by current misrankings, anchoring learning to a chosen reference, and offering a single supervised-style loss, the method combines the modeling flexibility of preference-based rewards with the simplicity and stability of standard supervised optimization. Thoughtful choices of the scalar $\lambda$ and of the reference policy, together with attention to optimizer step sizes, are the main practical levers one uses to shape final behavior.

Theoretical Properties and Comparisons to Policy–Value Methods

# Equivalence classes of rewards

When preferences are expressed as pairwise comparisons between candidate completions for a given prompt, the underlying scalar scoring function that gives rise to those preferences is not unique. Formally, consider two scoring functions r(x,y) and r'(x,y) defined for prompt x and completion y. We declare r and r' equivalent when they differ only by a term that depends on the prompt but not on the completion; that is, when there exists a function f(x) such that
\[
r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x)\quad\text{for all }x,y.
\]
Intuitively, adding the same offset to every completion for a fixed prompt does not change any pairwise differences r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2), and hence cannot change relative comparisons between completions for that prompt.

This notion partitions the space of scoring functions into equivalence classes. Each class contains all functions that give the same ordering (and same pairwise comparison probabilities under logit-style preference models) for completions of each prompt. Because most preference models depend only on differences of scores, working with classes rather than individual functions is the appropriate mathematical viewpoint: any inference or optimization based on pairwise labels can at most recover one element of the class, not a unique absolute scoring function.

# Same preferences and same optimal policy within an equivalence class

Two formal facts follow directly from the equivalence definition and from the structure of the common preference models used in practice.

Proposition 1 (same pairwise probabilities). Under a pairwise logit preference model (the pairwise comparative logit), the probability that completion y_1 is preferred to y_2 for prompt x depends only on the difference r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2). Thus any two scoring functions in the same equivalence class induce identical pairwise preference probabilities:
\[
\Pr(y_1 \succ y_2\mid x) = \sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr)
= \sigma\bigl(r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where \(\sigma\) denotes the logistic function.

Sketch of argument. Since r' = r + f(x), the difference r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2) equals r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2); the logistic link depends only on that difference, so probabilities match.

Proposition 2 (same constrained-optimum policy). Suppose the objective for choosing a stochastic generator (a policy) π(·|x) is to maximize expected score under a Kullback–Leibler penalty relative to a fixed reference distribution π_ref(·|x):
\[
\max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\; \mathrm{D}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).
\]
Any two scoring functions in the same equivalence class induce the same optimal π, for every prompt x.

Sketch of argument. Adding f(x) to r simply adds f(x) to the expected score term for any π, which does not affect which π maximizes the objective because this additive shift is independent of π. Thus the optimizer is unchanged.

These two propositions justify focusing on equivalence classes rather than absolute scores: a preference dataset and a KL-constrained optimization target collectively determine only a class of scoring functions and a single optimal stochastic generator per class.

# Representation theorem: any Plackett–Luce–consistent class via \(\beta \ln \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)

A key structural relationship links scoring functions, the KL-constrained maximizer, and an explicit reparameterization that expresses a representative of each class in terms of a stochastic generator. Start from the closed-form optimal policy that solves the KL-regularized expected-score maximization for a given scoring function r. For each prompt x the optimizer has the Gibbs form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z_r(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
where the partition function
\[
Z_r(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr)
\]
normalizes the distribution. This identity is purely algebraic and follows by writing the first-order condition for the constrained optimization problem.

Rearranging the previous equation yields an exact expression for r in terms of the optimal generator π_r and the known reference π_ref:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\,\ln\!\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\,\ln Z_r(x).
\]
Because the last term depends only on x, this shows that r is equivalent (in the sense of the equivalence class) to the function
\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;:=\; \beta\,\ln\!\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
The mapping r ↦ π_r and the reverse mapping π ↦ \(\tilde r\) therefore establish that every scoring function class consistent with the Plackett–Luce family has a representative of the explicit form \(\beta \ln(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\).

Theorem (representation). For any scoring function r that induces an optimizer π_r via the Gibbs relation above, the shifted function
\[
\tilde r(x,y) = \beta \,\ln\!\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
lies in the same equivalence class as r. Conversely, for any stochastic generator π the function defined by that formula induces π as the optimizer of the KL-regularized expected-score problem for the corresponding class.

This result is constructive: given a desired optimal π we can write down an explicit scoring representative whose Gibbs optimal policy is exactly π. Thus parameterizing the scoring representative by a policy does not reduce expressive power within the family of score-classes compatible with the preference model.

# Normalization condition selects a canonical representative

Because equivalence classes are defined up to additive functions of x, we can choose a canonical representative within each class by imposing a normalization constraint. Using the reparameterization
\[
\tilde r(x,y) = \beta \,\ln\!\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
the partition function associated with the original r becomes
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]
If we pick the representative r' that equals \(\tilde r\), then plugging r' into the definition of Z gives
\[
\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r'(x,y)\Bigr)
= \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\ln\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr)
= \sum_y \pi(y\mid x) = 1.
\]
Thus the normalization condition
\[
\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r'(x,y)\Bigr) = 1
\]
selects exactly the representative r' that corresponds to a valid probability distribution π. Imposing this condition turns the under-specified class into a unique canonical element, and that choice makes the mapping between scoring representatives and policies one-to-one.

From a practical perspective this is useful because many preference likelihoods (pairwise or ranking-based) depend only on score differences. By choosing the canonical representative above, the intractable partition function cancels out of the pairwise preference probabilities, which enables direct optimization of a policy parameterization without ever needing to compute or approximate Z(x).

# Control-as-inference view: removing centering terms via reparameterization

A common alternative viewpoint frames the KL-regularized expected-score objective as a form of probabilistic inference: one can think of the optimizer π as a posterior proportional to the prior π_ref times an exponential of the scaled scores. From this control-as-inference perspective, the normalization term Z(x) plays the role of a soft value function that depends on the scoring function. When attempting to optimize a parametric policy via sampling and stochastic gradients, that soft value appears inside expectations over the policy and hence must be estimated or compensated for to keep variance manageable.

Concretely, suppose a scoring estimator \(\hat r_\phi\) has been fit to comparison data and one attempts to find π_θ by directly maximizing
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[\hat r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}\bigl(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr).
\]
Rewriting in terms of the Gibbs relation for the class of \(\hat r_\phi\) yields an objective that includes the term
\[
f(x) \;=\; \hat r_\phi(x,y) \;-\; \beta\,\ln\!\Bigl(\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\hat r_\phi(x,y')\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
which depends implicitly on the entire distribution of scores for prompt x. This centering term must be estimated or removed to obtain stable gradients when optimizing π_θ by sampling from π_θ, because it influences the scale and sign of the reward signal inside the expectation.

The reparameterization to write the canonical representative as \(\beta \ln(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) eliminates the need to separately represent or estimate this centering term: substituting the representative into the pairwise preference likelihood causes the partition function to cancel, producing preference expressions that involve only the ratio \(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Thus a single-stage optimization that directly fits π_θ to maximize the reparameterized likelihood avoids the separate fitting of an explicit scoring estimator and the separate estimation of the soft value. In other words, the centering term is absorbed into the policy parameterization, so it never appears as an unstable sampling-dependent quantity during policy optimization.

# Why policy–value methods need centering estimates and can be unstable; why direct policy parameterization avoids this

Policy–value style procedures (those that maintain separate parameterizations for a stochastic policy and for a value-like function that centers returns) typically proceed in two stages: first fit a scoring estimator from pairwise comparisons, then run a sampled-gradient routine to improve the policy with respect to that estimator, using a learned value estimate (a control variate) to reduce gradient variance. The need for a learned centering quantity arises because the objective optimized by such routines contains the normalization term described above, and naive Monte Carlo estimates of gradients without subtraction of a suitable control variate tend to have large variance. Learning a stable centering estimator is itself a nontrivial subproblem: if it is poorly estimated, gradient estimates become biased or noisy, which can cause oscillation, premature convergence to degenerate policies, or reward overfit.

The direct policy parameterization described earlier sidesteps these issues for two reasons. First, the preference likelihood under the pairwise logistic model can be re-expressed solely in terms of the policy ratio \(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\); the partition function cancels from pairwise differences. This means training reduces to standard binary logistic regression where the inputs are log-ratios of policy probabilities, and no separate centering estimate appears anywhere. Second, the implicit scoring function \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\,\ln(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))\) moves together with the policy parameters, so there is no mismatch between a fixed reward estimator and a changing policy that needs to be corrected by an external value network.

A useful way to see the stabilizing effect is to examine the gradient of the direct preference objective. Let training data consist of triples (x,y_w,y_l) where y_w is preferred to y_l. Define the implicit score under π_θ by
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta\,\ln\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
The DPO-style loss for one triple is the negative log of a logistic comparing the two implicit scores, and its gradient with respect to θ evaluates to
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)
\;=\;
-\,\beta\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Bigl[
\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\,\bigl(\nabla_\theta \ln\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \ln\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr],
\]
where σ is the logistic function. Several stabilizing features are evident. The scalar weight before the score-gradient difference is between 0 and 1 and naturally downweights examples where the implicit ordering is already correct; conversely it upweights cases where the ordering is wrong. Because the gradients are expressed in terms of score gradients of the policy itself, no separate sampled return minus value estimate term appears. This avoids the delicate coupling that can make policy–value routines brittle: there is no added learned centering term to mis-estimate, and the per-example weighting is automatically adaptive.

In contrast, a two-stage procedure that first fits a separate scoring estimator will feed that estimator into a sampling-based optimizer; the optimizer must either learn a value estimate to serve as a control variate or rely on sample baselines. Both options introduce extra moving parts: a value estimator that must be trained and tuned, or noisy Monte Carlo baselines whose single-sample nature yields high variance. In practice this leads to extra tuning, potential instability, and the need for care when updating the policy so that it does not exploit imperfections of the scoring estimator.

Summary. The reparameterization that writes a canonical representative of each scoring class as \(\beta \ln(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) gives a principled way to eliminate a separate scoring stage and the centering/value estimation that policy–value routines require. By folding the scoring representative into the policy itself, one obtains a single-stage likelihood objective with naturally adaptive per-example weights and without sampling-dependent centering terms. This reduces variance sources that commonly produce instability in sampled gradient procedures, and it yields an optimization target that is both theoretically equivalent to the original KL-regularized expected-score objective and simpler to train in practice.

Implementing DPO: Inputs, Engineering, and Training

# Preparing preference collections (pairwise and listwise)
A DPO pipeline begins with a collection of preference judgments: for each prompt $x$ the collector provides two or more candidate completions together with a preference indication that one completion is judged better than another. The most common format is pairwise comparisons $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the chosen completion and $y_l$ is the rejected completion. When a rater ranks more than two candidates for a given $x$, those rankings can be reduced to multiple pairwise comparisons or handled directly via a Plackett–Luce style likelihood; the DPO math supports both pairwise (Bradley–Terry) and listwise preference models, but engineering is simplest when the storage format is pairwise tuples.

When assembling the collection, record for each entry: the prompt prefix $x$, the chosen completion $y_w$, the alternative completion $y_l$, and metadata such as the sampling policy that produced $y_w,y_l$ and any quality flags. Because the DPO objective uses the reference policy's probabilities for the same completions, it matters which generator produced the candidates; include an identifier for the generator that was used so you can choose the matching reference policy at training time. If completions were produced from different generators for different prompts, you should either train separate reference policies per generator or normalize across generators before computing ratios (see the section on surrogate reference policies).

For listwise judgments (full ranking of $k$ completions), you can convert each ranked list into $(k-1)$ pairwise “wins over” relations, or train directly with the Plackett–Luce likelihood. Converting to pairwise tuples is the simplest and compatible with the pairwise DPO objective; keep in mind that this increases the number of training tuples per prompt and may require deduplication or weighting to avoid bias from heavily ranked prompts.

# Tokenization and sequence-level log-prob computation
All probability ratios in DPO are sequence-level, so you must compute the log probability of an entire completion given its prompt. Under an autoregressive policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ the sequence-level log probability is the sum of conditional token log probabilities:

\[
\log \pi(y\mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]

Practically, compute this by tokenizing the concatenation of $x$ and $y$ and passing it through the policy network in a single forward pass. Use the network's per-token logits and the usual cross-entropy-style log-prob extraction: for each token position corresponding to $y_t$ take the log of the softmax probability assigned to the ground-truth token. Summing those per-token log-probs reproduces the sequence-level value above.

When doing this for both the training policy (the one being updated) and the frozen reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, ensure identical tokenization and the same handling of special tokens and end-of-sequence conditions. If the reference policy shares the same vocabulary and tokenizer as the training policy, you can reuse tokenization outputs and just run two forward passes (one for each frozen/learned network), or more efficiently run a single pass and use cached logits when the networks are identical in architecture but with different parameters.

# Efficient batching of (x, y_w, y_l) tuples
Efficiency matters because each tuple requires computing sequence log-probabilities for two completions under two policies (training and reference), yielding up to four forward-pass log-prob computations per tuple if implemented naively. The following engineering patterns reduce compute:

1. Concatenate prompt and completion into a single sequence per (x,y). Batch many such sequences together and pad to the maximum length in the batch. Use attention masks so padding tokens do not contribute to log-prob sums. This amortizes transformer costs across many completions.

2. If the prompt $x$ is shared among multiple completions in the same mini-batch, compute the prompt-only activations once and cache key/value tensors (or use prefix caching) to cheaply evaluate multiple suffixes $y$. Many transformer runtimes support a cached incremental decode API; reuse prefix activations when possible.

3. If the reference policy is identical in architecture to the training policy and you run both on the same device, consider stacking parameter sets and computing logits for both in a single fused kernel when supported by your runtime. Otherwise, do the frozen forward pass(s) at lower precision or with fewer devices to save resources.

4. Precompute and persist $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ for the entire preference collection when the reference policy is static and the collection is large. This avoids recomputing the same reference log-prob repeatedly across epochs. Store these sequence-level log-probs alongside tuples and reload when forming batches.

A typical per-batch computation looks like:
- Form a batch of $B$ tuples $(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})$.
- Tokenize the $2B$ sequences $x^{(i)}\!+\!y_w^{(i)}$ and $x^{(i)}\!+\!y_l^{(i)}$ into padded tensors.
- Compute $\log \pi_\theta(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}), \log \pi_\theta(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})$ in one batched forward pass.
- Compute (or load) $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}), \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})$.
- Assemble the per-example scalars used by the DPO loss (below).

# Numerical stability: log-prob clipping, sigmoid log-loss stabilization
Two numerical issues dominate: very large or small log-prob ratios, and unstable evaluation of the sigmoid- log-likelihood.

1) Working with log ratios. DPO uses an implicit reward estimate
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta\left(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\right).
\]
Both log probabilities are sums of many negative numbers (token log-probs), so their difference may be large in magnitude. To avoid overflow/underflow when exponentiating or when passing the difference into later functions, clip the log-ratio difference to a bounded interval $[-M, M]$ prior to multiplying by $\beta$ (or clip the scaled difference itself). Choose $M$ to be a modest constant (single-digit to low-double-digit nats); the exact setting can be tuned but clipping avoids runaway gradients when an example is far out-of-distribution.

2) Stable evaluation of the pairwise sigmoid log-likelihood. The DPO per-example contribution is
\[
-\log \sigma\bigl(\Delta\bigr),\qquad \Delta \;=\; \beta\bigl(\log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr).
\]
Directly computing $\log \sigma(\Delta)$ via $\log(1/(1+e^{-\Delta}))$ can overflow when $|\Delta|$ is large. Use the numerically stable identity
\[
\log \sigma(\Delta) = 
\begin{cases}
-\log\bigl(1 + e^{-\Delta}\bigr), & \Delta \ge 0,\\[4pt]
\Delta - \log\bigl(1 + e^{\Delta}\bigr), & \Delta < 0,
\end{cases}
\]
implemented with a single call to a stable log-sum-exp primitive when available. Many deep learning runtimes provide a fused stable-log-sigmoid operator; prefer that. This avoids NaNs and saturating gradients.

3) Gradient clipping. Even with the above, occasional batches can produce large gradients. Apply per-step gradient clipping (norm or value) to maintain steady optimization.

# Training loop and pseudocode for DPO
Below is a concise recipe for a single training iteration written in math and step form (no banned terminology).

1. Sample a mini-batch of $B$ tuples $\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^B$ from the preference collection.

2. For each tuple compute sequence-level log-probabilities under the current policy $\pi_\theta$:
   \[
   a_w^{(i)} \;=\; \log \pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr),\qquad
   a_l^{(i)} \;=\; \log \pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr).
   \]

3. Obtain sequence-level log-probabilities under the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:
   \[
   b_w^{(i)} \;=\; \log \pi_{\text{ref}}\!\bigl(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr),\qquad
   b_l^{(i)} \;=\; \log \pi_{\text{ref}}\!\bigl(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr).
   \]
   These can be loaded from cache or computed on the fly.

4. Form the scaled difference per example (optionally clip to $[-M,M]$):
   \[
   \Delta^{(i)} \;=\; \beta\bigl((a_w^{(i)}-b_w^{(i)}) - (a_l^{(i)}-b_l^{(i)})\bigr).
   \]

5. Compute stable per-example loss $\ell^{(i)} = -\log \sigma(\Delta^{(i)})$ using the stable log-sigmoid formulation.

6. Aggregate the mini-batch loss:
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_\text{batch} \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B \ell^{(i)}.
   \]

7. Backpropagate gradients through $\pi_\theta$ and take an optimizer step. Apply gradient clipping and any numerical safeguards.

8. Optionally update learning-rate schedule, track metrics, and save checkpoints.

This procedure requires only standard maximum-likelihood-style backprop mechanics and does not require on-policy sampling in the inner loop or explicit reinforcement-style rollouts.

# Choosing $\beta$, early stopping, and monitoring Kullback–Leibler divergence vs. win rate
The scalar $\beta$ controls the tradeoff between increasing reward (relative preference mass) and staying near the reference policy. Intuitively:
- Small $\beta$ makes the loss sensitive to modest log-ratio differences and keeps the trained policy close to the reference.
- Large $\beta$ amplifies differences, pushing the policy more aggressively toward high implicit reward completions.

Start with a conservative $\beta$ and sweep across a small grid to inspect the tradeoff frontier. Monitor two main diagnostics during training:

1. Estimated Kullback–Leibler divergence between the trained policy and the reference, per-prefix:
   \[
   D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
   \;=\;
   \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right].
   \]
   Approximate this expectation by sampling a small number of completions per monitored prompt or estimate sequence-level KL by summing tokenwise KLs over a sample. Track the mean KL across a validation set of prompts.

2. Win rate against a baseline. Use an external judge (automated judge such as a high-performing black-box evaluator or held-out raters) to compute the proportion of times the trained policy's completion is preferred to the baseline completion. This is the operational metric that matters in practice.

Plot the frontier of mean implicit reward (or win rate) versus mean Kullback–Leibler divergence as training proceeds or as you vary $\beta$. Early stopping rules can be framed as: stop when additional training yields only marginal win-rate improvement while Kullback–Leibler continues to grow, or when validation win rate plateaus or declines. Because the implicit reward is derived from the policy itself, watch for over-optimization where the policy improves its loss but external judge win rate does not improve; this signals potential misalignment between the implicit objective and the external assessment.

# Adapting when SFT is unavailable: training a surrogate $\pi_{\text{ref}}$
When no supervised fine-tuned reference policy is available, DPO recommends initializing the reference policy by maximizing the likelihood of chosen completions in the preference collection. Concretely, fit a frozen-or-finetuned policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by solving:
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{C}}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the collection of chosen completions. This is standard sequence maximum-likelihood fitting: fine-tune a generator on the chosen completions only. The resulting surrogate reference serves two purposes: it reduces distribution shift between the generator that produced the candidates and the $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used in the DPO ratio, and it provides numerically reasonable denominators for the log-ratio.

If you cannot or prefer not to fine-tune a separate reference, you can approximate $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by training a copy of the network with a smaller learning rate and early stopping on likelihood of chosen completions, or even initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from the same checkpoints used to sample candidates. The key desideratum is that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ matches the distribution from which $y_w,y_l$ were sampled to reduce bias in the log-ratio.

# Integration with existing reinforcement-from-preference frameworks
DPO can be slotted into many existing toolchains that already implement preference collection, reward fitting, and policy fine-tuning. The main changes to such a pipeline are:

- Replace the two-stage reward fitting + policy optimization phase with a single supervised-style optimization of the policy using the DPO loss. The code path that computes logits and sequence log-probs is largely identical to what RL-based fine-tune loops use for rollouts and scoring, so much of the infrastructure (tokenizers, sampling utilities, checkpointing) can be reused.

- If your codebase already computes a learned reward model from the preference collection, you can reuse that reward-fitting code to produce diagnostics, but you do not need to run a reinforcement-style optimizer (e.g., trust-region or clipping routines) once you adopt DPO.

- Keep any value or baseline networks that existing tooling uses for variance reduction if you want them for additional diagnostics, but they are not required by DPO. DPO uses only sequence log-probs under two policies.

Operational tips for integration:
- Reuse batching, tokenization, and caching code from the likelihood training path.
- If your framework supports mixed precision, run reference-policy forward passes at reduced precision to save memory, while keeping the training policy in the precision you use for gradients.
- Use existing checkpointing and experiment-tracking to log mean DPO loss, mean Kullback–Leibler divergence, validation win rate, and other safety checks.

# Practical checklist and common pitfalls
1. Tokenization parity: confirm identical tokenizer, byte-pair encoding, and special token treatment for both training and reference policies.

2. Caching reference log-probs when the reference is static: precompute to save runtime.

3. Numerics: implement the stable log-sigmoid and clip scaled log-ratio values to a bounded range before computing the sigmoid input.

4. Mini-batch composition: avoid grouping many tuples with the same prompt and the same completion repeatedly in a single batch unless you intentionally want that weighting.

5. Monitor both training loss and external win-rate: loss can decrease while external preference assessments do not improve.

6. Conservativeness knob ($\beta$): tune over a small set of values and examine the reward vs. Kullback–Leibler frontier. If you see mode collapse (the policy emitting near-deterministic completions), reduce $\beta$ or apply stronger regularization such as higher temperature sampling in diagnostics.

7. When reference log-probs are extremely small for some completions (very unlikely under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$), treat those tuples with care: they can produce large positive log-ratios that drive gradients. Clipping and filtering very out-of-distribution tuples helps.

Summary
This chapter described an end-to-end engineering blueprint for DPO: how to prepare preference collections, compute sequence-level log-probabilities, batch tuples efficiently, stabilize numerical computations, run the training loop, choose the conservativeness parameter $\beta$ and stopping criteria, construct a surrogate reference when needed, and integrate into existing preference-driven fine-tuning stacks. The core operational idea is simple: compute per-completion sequence log-probs under the trainable policy and under a fixed reference policy, form the scaled log-ratio difference for each preferred/rejected pair, apply a numerically stable sigmoid-log loss, and backpropagate. With attention to tokenization parity, caching, and numerical guards, DPO provides a lightweight and stable replacement for two-stage reward-fitting plus reinforcement-style optimization.

Empirical Results: Sentiment, Summarization, and Dialogue

# Tasks and datasets: movie reviews, Reddit TL;DR, and helpful/harmless dialogue
The empirical study evaluates the new training method across three open-ended text generation settings chosen to stress different aspects of preference learning.

The first setting requires controlling sentiment in completions given a prefix drawn from a movie-review corpus. For controlled evaluation, preference labels are synthesized: for each prompt the experimental pipeline generates pairs of candidate completions and a pre-trained sentiment classifier selects the completion with higher positive sentiment as the preferred item. This synthetic preference signal permits precise measurement of how well each method trades off reward attainment against staying near the starting distribution.

The second setting uses forum-post summarization from a popular social-news collection (the TL;DR task). Human preferences over candidate summaries were collected in prior work; those preference pairs are reused to train and evaluate policies. Because conventional surface metrics for summarization correlate poorly with human judgment in this domain, the experiments rely on human-style pairwise comparison judgments mediated by an automated high-quality judge, with a supplemental human rater study to validate that the automated judge is a reasonable proxy.

The third setting evaluates single-turn conversational responses on a large helpful/harmless dialogue collection gathered previously. Each example contains a user prompt and two candidate assistant replies with a human preference label. In this setting there is no standard supervised starter model available from the same source, so a reference model is constructed by fine-tuning a general pre-trained language model on the preferred completions from the preference set. All experiments work from an initial supervised-tuned model that serves as the reference distribution for subsequent preference-based tuning.

# Models and comparators: supervised fine-tuning, preferred fine-tune, PPO variants, unlikelihood, best-of sampling, and open-source LMs
Several competing methods are evaluated to place the new method in context.

The supervised fine-tuned reference is the model trained on high-quality demonstrations for the downstream task. A simple supervised mimic variant (referred to here as preferred fine-tune) further fine-tunes on the chosen preferred completions from the preference dataset: it learns to reproduce the human-preferred outputs in a maximum-likelihood manner. An unlikelihood-style method tries to raise probability of preferred completions while explicitly reducing probability of dispreferred completions by adding a penalty term on the latter; this approach includes a tunable coefficient to weight the penalty.

Policy-optimization methods are represented by a widely used proximal policy update procedure and an oracle variant of that procedure which has access to the true reward in the synthetic sentiment task. These procedures perform iterative policy updates to maximize expected reward under a constraint that discourages drifting too far from the reference model. The experiments also include a computationally intensive sampling strategy that draws many candidate completions at inference time and selects the one with the highest score according to a learned reward function; this “best-of-many” selection provides a strong upper-bound comparator for reward-aware selection even though it is costly at test time.

Finally, a variety of open-source large language models are used as zero- or few-shot baselines for certain tasks, and an automated high-quality judge (the same class of strong large model used in recent evaluation practice) provides pairwise judgments for summarization and dialogue when human labeling is not feasible at scale.

# Reward–KL frontier in sentiment control: the direct method dominates iterative policy optimization
The controlled sentiment experiment quantifies performance along two axes: average task reward measured by the ground-truth sentiment classifier, and divergence from the reference policy measured with sequence-level Kullback–Leibler. This frontier captures the core trade-off of the constrained optimization objective used in preference-based policy learning: increase task reward while remaining close to the distribution the reward evaluator was trained on.

Multiple runs were executed for each method with different conservativeness settings so that each method traces a reward–divergence trade-off curve. The new direct preference method achieves the most efficient frontier: for a given divergence budget it attains higher average sentiment reward than the competing iterative policy-optimization procedure, and it also outperforms the oracle variant of that iterative procedure which had access to the true reward. The dominance holds across the sweep of conservativeness settings that were tested. In practical terms, the direct training rule raises probability mass on preferred completions and lowers it on dispreferred ones in a way that respects the reference distribution, and that balance proves more effective than the sampled policy-gradient updates used in the iterative procedure in this controlled setting.

# Summarization: automated-judge win rates show a clear advantage for the direct method
For forum-post summarization, methods are compared by sampling model outputs at several sampling temperatures and computing pairwise win rates against reference test-set summaries as judged by the automated high-quality evaluator. The direct method yields a substantially higher win rate at conservative sampling behavior than the iterative policy-optimization comparator. At greedy or near-greedy sampling, the direct method attains roughly sixty-one percent wins over the test references according to the automated judge, while the iterative policy-optimization procedure attains roughly fifty-seven percent wins at its best sampling setting. The direct method also achieves a higher peak win rate than the best-of-many selection despite the latter’s expensive test-time sampling. Beyond peak performance, the direct method demonstrates relative insensitivity to sampling temperature: its win rate degrades slowly as sampling becomes more stochastic, whereas the iterative method’s performance degrades more sharply at high sampling randomness and can revert to the un-tuned reference behavior.

A human rater study supports the automated-judge results: the automated judge’s preferences align with human raters at a level comparable to inter-human agreement when prompts for the judge are chosen thoughtfully. Two styles of automated-judge prompts were evaluated — a simple prompt asking which summary better captures important information, and a concise prompt that also rewards brevity. The concise prompt produced automated judgments that better matched human choices in the study, and those judgments are the basis for the reported win rates. Overall, the evaluation evidence indicates that the direct training method produces summaries that humans prefer more often than those produced by the iterative policy-optimization method, and that this effect is robust across sampling settings.

# Dialogue: efficient improvement over preferred fine-tune and competitive with heavy sampling selection
In the single-turn conversational setting, the direct method is the only computationally efficient procedure that consistently improves over the preferred fine-tune model when judged by the automated evaluator. A large-sample selection strategy (the best-of-many approach with a very large sample count) represents a demanding comparator; it requires generating many candidates at inference time and selecting the highest-scoring reply according to a learned critic. The direct method attains similar or better judged quality than that expensive selection strategy while incurring modest computation both during training and at inference. Iterative policy-optimization runs available for this dataset did not yield clear gains over the starting model in the configurations tested, while the direct approach reliably lifts judged quality.

Convergence behavior shows that the direct method reaches its best performance relatively quickly during training, indicating practical advantages in training speed and resource usage compared with sampled policy-gradient methods that require careful tuning and can be more brittle.

# Sampling temperature sensitivity and robustness
A recurrent theme in the experiments is robustness to sampling temperature. Sampling temperature controls randomness in candidate generation at inference. The direct method exhibits stable performance across a range of sampling randomness: its judged quality varies gently as temperature increases. In contrast, the iterative policy-optimization method shows more pronounced sensitivity: at higher temperatures its outputs become more varied and its judged quality can drop to the level of the un-tuned supervised model. This robustness of the direct method reduces the need for careful hand-tuning of sampling parameters in deployment.

# Out-of-distribution generalization: news article summaries favor the direct method
To probe generalization, the models trained on the forum-post summarization preference set were evaluated on a different distribution of articles drawn from a news summarization corpus. Using the same automated-judge prompt as for the forum-post task (with only minimal wording changes to reflect the different input genre), the direct method maintained a lead over the iterative policy-optimization model. The direct-trained policy produced summaries that the automated judge preferred more often on the news-article test set, suggesting that the direct reparameterization approach can generalize at least as well as the iterative policy-optimization method when the input distribution shifts. This initial out-of-distribution result indicates that bypassing an explicit critic in favor of the direct reparameterization does not harm, and may improve, transfer behavior in these settings.

# Human versus automated-judge evaluations: prompt design, agreement, and reliability
Because human labeling is the gold standard but expensive, the experiments use a strong automated judge as a scalable proxy. The study includes a targeted human evaluation to validate this proxy. Two automated-judge prompts were compared: a simple prompt asking which completion best captures the important information, and a concise prompt also preferring brevity. Human raters evaluated a stratified set of model output pairs sampled from low-, middle-, and high-quality regimes. The findings show that automated-judge judgments, when using the concise prompt, agree with humans at roughly the same rate that humans agree with each other. That level of agreement lends credibility to using the automated judge for large-scale comparisons across many sampling temperatures and models.

The human validation also underscores a practical lesson: the wording used to instruct an automated judge materially affects its preferences, so careful prompt design is important when relying on automated evaluation. The concise prompt tended to downweight repetitive long summaries in favor of succinct and informative ones, aligning better with human raters’ judgments in this study.

# Summary of key empirical conclusions
Across all three experimental domains, the direct preference fitting procedure yields practical and empirical advantages. In the controlled sentiment task it attains a superior reward–divergence trade-off relative to iterative policy-optimization, including the version with oracle reward access. In summarization, judged by a high-quality automated evaluator validated by humans, the direct method secures higher win rates at conservative sampling and retains robustness to temperature. In single-turn dialogue, it offers efficient improvements over simple supervised fine-tuning and matches or exceeds the quality of expensive many-sample selection strategies. Initial tests on a news-article distribution show preserved or improved generalization relative to iterative policy-optimization. Finally, a human validation study supports the reliability of the automated judge when careful prompts are used, making the automated evaluations a defensible tool for large-scale comparative work.

Limitations, Extensions, and Future Directions

# Generalization and Reward Over-Optimization

A central promise of the procedure described in the paper is that a policy can be trained directly from pairwise comparisons of outputs, using a reparameterization that makes the implicit scalar objective a simple function of the policy and a reference distribution. In practice, however, the learned mapping from preferences to behavior can fail to generalize beyond the distribution of prompts and candidate outputs represented in the comparison collection. Two linked failure modes deserve particular attention.

First, distributional shift: if the set of prompts used to collect comparisons differs in systematic ways from prompts encountered at deployment, the implicit scalar that drives the policy via
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
may rank outputs correctly on the training support but mis-order them on novel inputs. The root cause is that the training comparisons constrain relative values only on pairs actually observed; the projection used in the reparameterization (which fixes a particular representative of an equivalence class of reward functions) does not in itself guarantee sensible extrapolation. Mitigations discussed in the paper, and that follow naturally from its theory, include choosing a reference distribution that closely matches the generation procedure used to collect comparisons, and augmenting the comparison collection with broader prompts or with additional forms of supervision (for example, demonstrations) so that the implicit reward receives signals across a wider input manifold.

Second, the familiar phenomenon of reward over-optimization (sometimes called reward hacking) appears in this direct framework as uncontrolled growth of the implicit scalar for certain outputs. Because the training objective literally raises relative log-probabilities of preferred outputs and lowers them for dispreferred ones, unconstrained updates may push the policy into regions that maximize the proxy objective but degrade practical usefulness or safety for other queries. The mechanism that helps prevent this in the original constrained formulation is the KL penalty to a reference distribution: it appears explicitly in the DPO gradient as the dependence of $\hat r_\theta$ on the ratio to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Nevertheless, over-optimization can still arise if the reference is weak or if the comparison collection lacks negative examples that capture the undesirable behaviors produced by extreme optimization. Practical precautions therefore include careful tuning of the trade-off parameter $\beta$, maintaining a strong and well-calibrated reference, and monitoring held-out prompts drawn from deployment-like distributions for signs of degeneration.

# Multi-Attribute and Listwise Preferences (Plackett-Luce family)

The paper develops the derivation under a pairwise logistic preference model and notes compatibility with the broader family of permutation models used for ranked data. When preferences concern multiple attributes (for example, usefulness, brevity, and safety) or when evaluators provide full rankings over several candidates rather than pairwise choices, two extensions are natural.

One route is scalarization: define a single scalar latent objective that combines attributes with fixed weights, and apply the same DPO reparameterization to that scalar. This keeps the training procedure unchanged, but it requires specifying or estimating the relative importance of attributes, which may be difficult in practice and may obscure trade-offs between goals.

A second route leverages the Plackett-Luce family: the paper’s math shows that, for preference models in this family, differences of reward terms determine ranking probabilities in a way that cancels terms that depend only on the prompt. For listwise feedback (full or partial rankings), likelihoods under Plackett-Luce can be expressed directly in terms of the implicit reward induced by the policy, enabling a natural generalization of the DPO loss to rank data. Concretely, for a ranking of k candidates the Plackett-Luce probability involves successive conditional choices; substituting the representation
\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + c(x)
\]
causes the additive prompt-dependent constant $c(x)$ to cancel in each conditional term, yielding an objective that depends only on the policy ratios. This preserves the paper’s benefit of avoiding an explicit scalar estimator and allows training on richer supervision.

However, practical issues arise: rank data is more expensive to collect, and multi-attribute judgments often contain internal trade-offs that vary across raters and contexts. When attributes are incommensurate or when raters provide noisy, inconsistent weights, it can be preferable to collect separate pairwise comparisons targeted at each attribute and either train multiple implicit scalars or learn a small parametric mapping from attribute-level scores to an overall preference. Any such extension should preserve the cancelation properties that make the DPO change-of-variables tractable.

# Active Preference Elicitation and Annotation Efficiency

The paper operates in an offline setting—comparisons are gathered first, then the policy is optimized—yet the DPO formulation is particularly amenable to active selection strategies that improve annotation efficiency. Because the DPO gradient weights updates by a logistic function of the implicit reward misordering, one can derive useful heuristics for which pairs to solicit next.

A practical active strategy is to sample prompts and candidate pairs where the current implicit scalar places the two outputs near equiprobability under the pairwise preference model. In math, for two candidates $y_1,y_2$ on prompt $x$, the uncertainty of the current policy about their ordering is large when
\[
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \approx \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}.
\]
Selecting such pairs for additional judgments yields high information gain per annotation because the loss gradient magnitude is greatest when the model’s ordering disagrees or is uncertain.

Other strategies include diversity-based selection over prompts, and targeted selection that focuses on attributes or subdomains where the implicit scalar is known to be unreliable (for example, safety-sensitive categories). Active elicitation must be paired with conservative update rules: if the policy is adapted continuously with newly collected comparisons, one should limit per-step change (analogous to the KL penalty) or accumulate comparisons in minibatches and re-fit stably to avoid oscillation.

# Self-Annotation and Iterative Improvement with DPO

A promising avenue discussed in the paper is self-labeling: letting the current policy propose candidate outputs for unlabeled prompts and then using some trusted judge—either people or an auxiliary automated evaluator—to produce preference signals for those candidates, thereby expanding the comparison collection without fully manual curation. The DPO reparameterization lends itself naturally to such iterative loops because the implicit scalar is directly available from the policy ratios.

There are notable benefits: the system can bootstrap itself, focusing annotation effort where it helps most, and can use its own generations to discover failure modes that were not present in the original collection. But there are also hazards. A self-labeling loop may amplify existing biases of the judge used to annotate self-generated pairs, and it may encourage confirmation bias where the policy and the judge collude to reinforce spurious correlations. To manage these risks, the paper suggests several practical controls: require a human or high-quality external judge to vet a fraction of newly labeled pairs, maintain diversity in candidate generation strategies (temperatures, decoding heuristics), and include periodic held-out prompts sampled from deployment scenarios to detect regressions.

From a theoretical point of view, iterative self-annotation can be seen as increasing support for the implicit reward’s estimates across input space; however, convergence guarantees are weak without assumptions on judge quality and on the stability of the policy update rule. Empirically, conservative update rules and human-in-the-loop checks are effective safeguards.

# Hybrid Use with Reinforcement-Based Fine-Tuning and Reranking at Inference

Although the paper argues that the direct objective can replace a two-stage scalar-estimate-plus-reinforcement pipeline, there are natural hybrid workflows that combine the strengths of both worlds.

One hybrid keeps the DPO-trained policy as the main production model but uses an explicit scalar estimator at inference time to rerank a small set of sampled candidates. This retains the computational simplicity of DPO during training while leveraging a possibly more flexible explicit scalar for final selection. The paper notes that selection-by-rank (best-of-$N$) can improve quality but is computationally expensive because it requires generating many candidates per prompt; combining DPO with a lightweight reranker (trained from the same comparisons) can reduce the number of samples required for comparable gains.

Another hybrid is to initialize a reinforcement-based fine-tuning pass (e.g., a proximal update procedure) from a DPO checkpoint. Because DPO already produces a policy aligned with the preference collection and because it avoids unstable value-estimator training during initial alignment, initializing reinforcement-based updates from DPO can reduce instability and sample complexity when those further updates are desired (for example, to incorporate auxiliary, non-pairwise costs or to handle sequential decision tasks with longer horizons). The theoretical connection in the paper—showing DPO optimizes the same constrained objective as the reinforcement-based procedure under the reparameterization—means such fine-tuning can be interpreted as refining an already consistent solution rather than exploring from a random initialization.

# Scaling to Bigger Language Systems and Other Modalities

The paper demonstrates the procedure on systems up to mid-sized parameter counts, and it discusses scaling as future work. Several practical and conceptual points arise when moving to substantially bigger systems or to non-text modalities.

From an engineering perspective, the DPO procedure is attractive for scale because it avoids on-policy sampling during training and eliminates the need to maintain and fit a separate scalar estimator and a value network. The primary bottlenecks remain standard fine-tuning costs: memory, throughput, and careful selection of the reference policy. For extremely large language systems, obtaining a reliable reference that represents the generation behavior used to collect comparisons is critical; misalignment between the reference and the true sampling procedure used at collection time can degrade the implicit reward’s calibration.

Extending to other modalities (image synthesis, audio, structured actions) is feasible in principle because the key identities used in the derivation rely only on the preference model family and on a reference distribution. In modalities with continuous outputs, some technical adjustments are needed to compute and backpropagate log-density ratios or their approximations. In structured generation tasks with combinatorial action spaces, enumerating candidates for pairwise comparison may be infeasible; instead, comparison collections must be gathered intelligently (for example, via constrained generation or beam-based proposals). The core DPO insight—the mapping from policy-density ratios to a representative scalar that cancels prompt-dependent constants—remains applicable.

# Assessment Challenges: Automated Judges and Prompt Sensitivity

A practical issue emphasized in the paper is that automated judges used to assess quality can themselves be sensitive to prompt wording, temperature, and scoring rubric. The paper used a powerful black-box automated evaluator as a proxy for people in several comparisons; results were shown to align reasonably with small-scale human checks, but the authors caution that assessment prompts materially affect which output is preferred.

Two implications follow. First, any deployment that relies on an automated judge for offline comparisons or for large-scale annotation should include calibration runs where a sample of judgments from the automated evaluator is compared against people across the target distribution. Second, automated judges may systematically favor certain attributes (verbosity, surface-level fluency) over others (conciseness, factual correctness) depending on the judgment prompt; therefore, it is advisable to use multiple prompts or multi-criteria prompts and to report sensitivity of measured win rates to these choices.

From a training perspective, one must also be cautious using an automated judge inside a self-labeling loop, since the policy may exploit idiosyncrasies of the judge. Auditing with held-out human-evaluated prompts and stress tests designed to probe known failure modes (e.g., adversarial prompts that elicit hallucination) mitigate this risk.

# Ethical Considerations and Safety-Oriented Preference Schemas

Preference-based procedures are powerful but also potentially perilous. The DPO framework shifts a scalar preference fitting problem into direct policy updates, which compresses many choices about values into the design of the comparison collection, the reference, and the choice of $\beta$. Ethical and safety-relevant considerations therefore centralize around how comparisons are gathered and what they are asked to encode.

First, the choice of raters, the instructions given to them, and the selection of prompts determine the implicit social priors encoded by the policy. Because “preferred” outputs reflect rater judgments that may differ across cultures, demographics, or roles, it is crucial to design schemes that either reflect a desired safety policy explicitly (for example, asking raters to prioritize non-harm and factuality) or to collect diverse opinions and then mediate among them with explicit aggregation protocols.

Second, safety-critical behavior often requires hard constraints rather than soft preference-driven improvements. The direct optimization paradigm must be augmented with constraint enforcement—either through guarded decoding, rule-based filters, or a separate verifier that enforces invariants—since preference maximization alone can allow outputs that are preferred under the comparison collection but still violate safety requirements. The paper suggests structuring preference tasks to emphasize safety attributes, but practical deployment should combine preference tuning with explicit safety checks.

Finally, auditability and transparency are important. Because DPO embeds the implicit scalar directly in the policy ratios, it is possible to inspect $\hat r_\theta$ for particular prompt-output pairs and to run targeted probes to reveal undesired incentives. Regular audits, adversarial red-teaming, and clear documentation of the composition of comparison collections and reference distributions are recommended best practices.

# Concluding Remarks

The reparameterization at the heart of the paper yields a lightweight, stable procedure for turning pairwise preference collections into aligned policies without an explicit scalar estimator or a reinforcement loop. That simplification opens many pragmatic possibilities—active elicitation, self-annotation loops, hybrid pipelines with reranking at inference, and extensions to richer ranking feedback—all of which must be pursued with attention to generalization limits, potential for over-optimization, and ethical safeguards. Future empirical work should stress-test the method across broader deployment distributions, evaluate long-run behavior under iterative self-labeling, and develop principled schemes for multi-attribute preferences and hard safety constraints so that the simplicity of the training procedure does not come at the cost of robustness or responsible behavior.
