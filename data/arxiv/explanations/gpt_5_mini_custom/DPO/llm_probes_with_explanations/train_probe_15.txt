According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', when increasing the relative probability of preferred responses, DPO's per-example importance weighting helps avoid model degeneration. This is because the DPO gradient scales the push–pull on each preference pair by a calibrated weight that reflects how wrong the model currently is about that pair: writing the implicit reward as $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, the per-example update is proportional to $\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)$, where $\sigma(\cdot)\in[0,1]$ grows large only when the dispreferred $y_l$ is erroneously rewarded more than the preferred $y_w$; thus, easy, already-correct pairs contribute tiny gradients, while hard, misordered pairs are emphasized, yielding bounded, self-calibrated step sizes. Because $\hat r_\theta$ encodes the KL trust region via $\beta$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the update effectively maintains the constraint in Eq. (3) without an explicit RL loop, preventing runaway increases in $\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$ that can collapse diversity or drift off-distribution. In contrast, a naïve probability-ratio or unlikelihood objective that uniformly increases $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decreases $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ lacks this data-dependent damping and can accumulate overly large, indiscriminate shifts in token logits, leading to degeneration—a failure mode the paper observes empirically when the weighting is removed.
