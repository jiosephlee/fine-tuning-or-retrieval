In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors warn that a naive probability-ratio objective can cause the model to concentrate its probability mass on single high-reward responses, a concise failure mode described as mode collapse. This is sensible because maximizing a raw difference of log-probabilities, such as maximizing a term like $\log \pi(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi(y_l\mid x)$ without a KL regularizer or a corrective weight, drives $\pi(y_w\mid x)$ toward 1 and $\pi(y_l\mid x)$ toward 0, which, via the exponential tilting form $\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$ (Eq. (4)), concentrates mass on whichever completion yields the largest effective reward, thereby collapsing diversity; the RLHF objective (Eq. (3)) explicitly includes the $-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]$ term to prevent this, and DPO’s gradient (Eq. (8)) further introduces a dynamic importance weight $\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)$ with $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ that downweights already-correctly ordered pairs and scales updates by $\beta$, avoiding the runaway push toward single responses; empirically, the authors note that a naive probability-ratio or unlikelihood-style objective without this weighting degenerates (Appendix Table), illustrating mode collapse in practice.
