From an identifiability perspective, imposing this normalization is analogous to fixing a gauge in physics: it does not change anything observable (preferences or optimal policy) but it gives a stable, well‑posed parameterization that is easier to work with in optimization.

The paper proves a useful constructive result: under mild assumptions, every equivalence class of reward functions that is consistent with a Plackett–Luce (in particular Bradley–Terry) preference model contains a representative of the special form
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
for some policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$. This is Theorem 1 in the paper, and it formalizes the claim that parametrizing the reward via the log ratio between a candidate policy and the reference policy does not lose representational power (up to the known equivalence class ambiguity).

In summary, actor‑critic/PPO approaches must contend with estimating baselines (soft values) to control gradient variance and stabilizing learning in the face of imperfect, learned rewards. DPO’s reparameterization algebraically removes the problematic normalization term, converts preference learning into a supervised logistic objective over policies, and introduces an intrinsic, example‑dependent weighting that targets remaining errors. These features collectively explain both the conceptual simplicity and the empirical stability advantages of DPO highlighted in the paper.

A short proof sketch captures the idea. Start from any reward $r(x,y)$ and form the policy $\pi_r$ that is optimal for that reward via
$\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$. Now define a new reward
\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; r(x,y) \;-\; \beta\log\!\biggl(\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y')\bigr)\biggr).
\]
The subtraction here is precisely the logarithm of the partition function for $r$, which depends only on $x$. Therefore $\tilde r$ lies in the same equivalence class as $r$. Rearranging the definition of $\pi_r$ shows that
\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
so $\tilde r$ has the desired log‑ratio form. Thus every equivalence class contains such a representative, and therefore parametrizing rewards via $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ covers the full family of reward classes that the Plackett–Luce model can represent.

DPO avoids this entire class of issues by turning the normalization into part of the parametrization. Because the DPO objective is derived by a change of variables that expresses preferences in terms of the policy log‑ratio with respect to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the effective reward is $\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x))$ and the preference likelihood involves only differences of these log‑ratios. Consequently, the training objective is a simple supervised/logistic objective over observed preference pairs, for which low‑variance minibatch gradient estimates are readily available. There is no separate learned critic, no sampling from the policy in the inner loop, and no need to estimate a partition function or soft value for each prompt. These features contribute to the empirical stability and simplicity of DPO reported in the paper.

If we replace $r$ by an equivalent reward $r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$, then
\[
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r'(x,y)\bigr)
=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)\cdot\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} f(x)\bigr).
\]
The multiplicative factor $\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} f(x)\bigr)$ is the same for every candidate $y$ for that prompt and therefore cancels out when the partition function normalizes the distribution. As a result $\pi_{r'}(y\mid x)=\pi_r(y\mid x)$ for all $y,x$. In words: adding a prompt‑dependent constant to the reward does not change the optimal policy under the KL‑constrained objective.

For any fixed reward $r$, the optimal policy under this objective has a closed form:
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z_r(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr),
\]
where $Z_r(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$ is the partition function that normalizes the distribution.

Intuitively, this result justifies switching perspective: rather than first estimating an abstract reward function and then solving for its optimal policy (an RL loop), we may parameterize and directly learn the policy $\pi$ while implicitly defining the corresponding canonical reward via a known closed‑form change of variables.

When we model human preferences with a latent scalar reward function $r(x,y)$ that assigns a score to each candidate completion $y$ given a prompt $x$, there is an important degree of under‑specification built into the usual preference models (such as Plackett–Luce and Bradley–Terry). Concretely, if we take any function $f$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ and form a new reward function
$r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$, then every pairwise difference in rewards remains unchanged:
$r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2)=r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. Since pairwise preferences in the Bradley–Terry model depend only on such differences, the two reward functions $r$ and $r'$ induce identical preference probabilities.

DPO’s reparameterization sidesteps this complication. By parameterizing rewards in the canonical log‑ratio form $r(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x))$ and then expressing the Bradley–Terry preference likelihood directly in terms of $\pi_{\theta}$ (see Eq. (7) in the paper), the normalization term cancels out algebraically. As a result, the training objective becomes a simple binary cross‑entropy (logistic) loss over pairwise preferences with no separate normalization constant to estimate:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\biggl[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\biggr].
\]
Because the DPO loss is written directly in terms of policy log‑probabilities relative to the reference, there is no separate value function or baseline to learn in order to make gradients well behaved.

Formally, we call two reward functions equivalent when they differ by an additive prompt‑dependent scalar:
$r'(x,y)-r(x,y)=f(x)$ for some function $f$. This relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so it partitions the space of reward functions into equivalence classes. Each class is a set of reward functions that are indistinguishable from the point of view of the Plackett–Luce family of preference models, because those models only depend on reward differences across completions for the same prompt.

Two consequences follow. First, every reward function in an equivalence class induces the same preference probabilities under the Plackett–Luce family (pairwise differences unchanged). Second, every reward function in the same equivalence class induces the same optimal policy under the RLHF objective (partition function normalization cancels the added prompt term). This is a formal statement of the intuition that the RLHF problem itself only determines the reward function up to an additive prompt‑only adjustment.

Policy‑gradient methods that operate on a learned reward model generally face two related practical difficulties: high variance in gradient estimates and potential instability due to errors in the learned reward. The high variance arises because gradients of expected reward under a sampling policy require Monte‑Carlo estimates; subtracting a learned baseline (a value function) or using an advantage estimator is a standard remedy. Actor–critic methods introduce a parametric critic to estimate this baseline, but training an accurate critic can be itself unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters, particularly when rewards are learned and therefore inaccurate or when the action space (language) is extremely large.

The construction in the previous section implicitly picks one canonical representative from each equivalence class by imposing the normalization condition
\[
\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr) \;=\; 1
\qquad\text{for every }x.
\]
This condition sets the partition function to one and is exactly the constraint used when deriving the reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x))$. Enforcing that partition function value selects the member of the equivalence class whose exponentiated (and ref‑weighted) rewards sum to unity.

Understanding these equivalence classes is crucial for two reasons. First, it explains why fitting a reward model from pairwise comparisons requires an additional identifiability constraint (for example, fixing a mean or anchoring at zero): without that constraint the MLE is only defined up to adding an arbitrary $f(x)$. Second, it opens the door to alternative parameterizations: rather than trying to recover an absolute reward function, one can choose a canonical representative from each class or parametrize rewards in a way that directly reflects the optimization objective we ultimately care about.

The paper analyzes this concretely in the RLHF context. When the reward model $r_{\phi}$ is used directly in an actor‑critic/PPO loop, the soft normalization term
\[
\beta \log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y')\bigr)
\]
acts like a prompt‑dependent baseline. If one ignores this term (i.e., uses raw unnormalized rewards) the resulting gradient estimator can have large variance and poor learning dynamics. Actor‑critic methods must therefore either learn to approximate this normalization (introducing another learning target and source of instability) or rely on noisy Monte‑Carlo baselines estimated from sampled completions.

Thus the control‑as‑inference viewpoint explains the operational benefit of the reparameterization: what would be a normalization (soft value) term in the actor‑critic formulation becomes an explicit part of the parameterization and cancels out of the DPO likelihood, removing the need to approximate it as a learned baseline.

Why is this useful? First, it removes the arbitrary degree of freedom in reward estimation without introducing any loss: because all members of a class yield the same preferences and the same optimal policy, we are free to choose a convenient canonical representative. Second, the chosen representative has a direct operational meaning: it is the unique reward in the class whose soft Bellman‑style normalization (the partition function) equals one, which makes the mapping between rewards and policies bijective under the imposed normalization. This bijection is what enables the change of variables that converts a likelihood over reward parameters (the usual reward‑modeling objective) into a likelihood over policies (the DPO objective).

$ \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[\,r(x,y)\,] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]. $

An additional practical advantage of DPO is a dynamic per‑example importance weight that appears in the gradient. Writing the DPO gradient (from the paper) yields:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)\;\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr],
\]
where $\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x))$ is the implicit reward induced by the current policy. The logistic weight $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))$ emphasizes examples where the current implicit reward ordering disagrees with the supervised preference label, and downweights examples where the model already orders them correctly with high confidence. This adaptive weighting helps prevent aggressive updates that could otherwise push the policy into degenerate regions — a failure mode observed when using naive unlikelihood or ratio objectives without such correction. Actor‑critic approaches do not automatically provide this per‑example correction; instead, they must rely on careful reward normalization, baselines, and hyperparameter tuning to avoid similar degeneration.

A standard way of viewing the KL‑constrained RLHF objective is through the control‑as‑inference lens. One can see the RLHF problem as minimizing the KL divergence between the learned policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the Boltzmann distribution induced by the reward, i.e. minimizing $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi^*]$ where $\pi^*$ is of the form $\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$. Rearranging the KL objective yields an expected objective of the following shape (as given in the paper):
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigl[\,r_{\phi}(x,y) \;-\; \beta \log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y')\bigr)\;-\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr].
\]
The middle term,
\[
\beta \log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y')\bigr),
\]
plays the role of a soft value function or normalization constant associated with the reward model $r_{\phi}$. Importantly, this term depends on $x$ only and not on the sampled action $y$. In Monte‑Carlo estimation of gradients, subtracting an $x$‑dependent baseline reduces variance; actor‑critic methods typically introduce and learn a value function to approximate exactly this kind of baseline. In other words, actor‑critic algorithms must grapple with estimating or approximating the partition/normalization term in order to obtain low‑variance gradient estimates.
