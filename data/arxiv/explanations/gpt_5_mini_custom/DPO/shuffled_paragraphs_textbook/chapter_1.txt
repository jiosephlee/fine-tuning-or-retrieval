Chapter 1: Motivation: From Knowledge to Preferred Behavior

Relative judgments—pairwise comparisons or partial rankings—have several practical advantages that make them attractive for aligning large models. First, annotators typically provide more consistent and reliable feedback when asked which of two completions they prefer, rather than assigning an absolute numeric score or writing an ideal completion. Second, generating two candidate completions can be automated by sampling a current model, so data collection can focus on annotation rather than generation. Third, comparisons capture human trade-offs implicitly: if an annotator prefers a shorter but slightly less detailed summary over a longer one, that preference is encoded directly in the comparison.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a different route: rather than first fitting an explicit reward model and then running RL, DPO directly parameterizes the optimal policy that would arise from some reward function in the RLHF objective and fits that policy by maximum likelihood to the preference data. The key analytical insight is that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained objective has a closed-form dependence on the reward:
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
where $Z(x)$ is a prompt-dependent normalizing constant. Rearranging, any reward in the same equivalence class can be written as
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Because pairwise preference models like Bradley–Terry depend only on reward differences, the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when forming preference probabilities. Substituting the reparameterized reward into the Bradley–Terry preference probability yields a model that expresses preferences directly in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr).
\]
This observation allows us to define a simple maximum-likelihood objective over policies, without ever explicitly learning $r_\phi$ or running an RL optimizer. The DPO loss can be written as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
Minimizing this loss directly adjusts the policy probabilities so that the modeled implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ places higher score on preferred completions. Importantly, the gradient has a per-example weight that downweights comparisons where the implicit reward already ranks the examples correctly and upweights comparisons where the model is wrong, which stabilizes learning compared to naive likelihood ratio objectives.

Two broad approaches have been used to increase the usability and alignment of language models after pretraining. Instruction tuning fine-tunes a model on datasets of instructions paired with human-written completions. This supervised fine-tuning (SFT) makes the model better at following a broad set of instructions and generally improves usefulness. Instruction tuning is effective because it directly exposes the model to high-quality examples of desired behavior.

Although effective in practice, RLHF introduces complexity and instability. It requires training multiple models (reward model and policy), estimating rewards for sampled trajectories, and running an RL optimizer such as PPO in a loop that samples from the model during training. The partition function in the soft-optimal policy is expensive to estimate, reward models can be brittle and under-specified up to additive functions of $x$, and actor-critic pipelines can be sensitive to hyperparameters and suffer from high-variance gradients. These factors make RLHF computationally expensive and often tricky to get right.

Preference learning takes a complementary approach: instead of requiring full demonstrations of ideal outputs, it uses human judgments that compare model outputs. Annotators are given two (or more) candidate completions for the same prompt and indicate which they prefer. These relative judgments are often easier and faster to collect than full demonstrations, because comparing examples can be simpler than producing the ideal output from scratch. Preference data can thus scale more readily across tasks and annotators with varying expertise.

In short, DPO reframes preference learning as a single-stage supervised-like optimization of the policy itself under a principled reparameterization. This removes the need for a separate reward model, sampling in the inner loop, and complex RL training, while remaining faithful to the original KL-constrained optimization objective used in RLHF. The result is a simpler, more stable, and computationally lightweight method to steer language models from knowledge toward preferred behavior.

Concrete examples clarify the gap between knowledge and preferred behavior. A model trained on broad text may “know” about common misconceptions that many people hold; it can therefore produce explanations that echo those misconceptions even when a correct explanation is desired. For instance, the model might repeat a widely held but incorrect claim when asked, because the training distribution contains many such claims. Similarly, consider code generation. A model may have seen many examples of both correct and buggy code; while it can reproduce and even diagnose frequent mistakes, when asked to generate code we prefer it to bias toward the rarer, higher-quality solutions rather than the average pattern in the data. Finally, safety concerns arise because the training data mixes benign and harmful viewpoints. Without additional alignment, the model can generate harmful, biased, or unhelpful content that is present in the corpus, even though such outputs are undesirable in deployment.

A common pipeline to leverage preference data is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The standard RLHF procedure in the literature (and in the paper) typically has three phases. First, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) optionally adapts the pretrained base model toward the target format and domain, producing a reference policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$. Second, the reward modeling phase fits a neural reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ to the preference dataset by maximizing the likelihood of the observed comparisons under a preference model such as Bradley–Terry. Concretely, reward modeling is often implemented as minimizing
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w) - r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $(y_w,y_l)$ denotes the preferred and dispreferred completions.

Third, the RL fine-tuning phase optimizes a policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ to maximize expected reward while constraining divergence from a reference policy (to avoid drifting too far from the distribution where the reward model is reliable). A typical formulation is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_{\theta}}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL penalty and $\pi_\text{ref}$ is often the SFT model.

Controllability refers to our ability to steer the model’s output toward the subset of behaviors we consider desirable for a given application: accurate answers, helpful tone, concise summaries, safe responses, or high-quality code. This steering is necessary because the model’s raw competence does not imply the model will choose the behavior we want. In other words, capability (what the model can say) is distinct from preference (what the model should say). Building useful and safe systems therefore requires additional training stages that take human judgments about desired behavior and incorporate those preferences into the model.

Large, unsupervised language models trained on massive corpora acquire broad world knowledge and surprising reasoning abilities. They learn to produce fluent text, to imitate many styles, and to solve tasks when prompted, often displaying competence that was never explicitly supervised. However, the objective used during unsupervised pretraining—predicting tokens—does not encode a single, human-aligned notion of what the model should say in every situation. The model's behavior is an amalgam of many different human goals and conventions present in its training data, and so the model ends up knowing more than we want it to imitate in all contexts.

The paper frames preference data probabilistically using a family of ranking models such as Plackett-Luce and its two-item special case, the Bradley–Terry model. Under Bradley–Terry, the probability that completion $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ for prompt $x$ is modeled in terms of an underlying (latent) scalar reward function $r^*(x,y)$:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\]
This simple probabilistic view makes it straightforward to convert preference datasets into losses for learning (e.g., a binary cross-entropy loss on the pairwise comparisons).

Preference learning is particularly valuable when it is costly or impractical to obtain expert demonstrations, or when we want to encode nuanced trade-offs (e.g., helpfulness versus brevity) that are easier to express as pairwise preferences than as absolute scores or handcrafted rules.

These examples illustrate that we cannot rely on unsupervised learning alone to select the “right” behavior from among the things the model knows how to say. We need methods that explicitly encode human judgments about which of the model’s possible outputs are preferred in context.
