# Tasks and datasets: IMDb sentiment, Reddit TL;DR, Anthropic HH
The empirical evaluation reported in the paper covers three open-ended text-generation tasks chosen to probe different aspects of learning from preferences: controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Each task uses an existing dataset and a preference dataset that pairs prompts with two candidate completions and a human (or synthetic) preference between them.

For controlled sentiment generation the prompts $x$ are prefixes of movie reviews drawn from the IMDb dataset. The goal is to steer a model to generate completions $y$ with positive sentiment. To make the evaluation controlled and repeatable, the authors generate preference labels automatically using a pre-trained sentiment <|endoftext|> for any sampled pair $(y_w,y_l)$ the <|endoftext|> satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$, and this <|endoftext|> is treated as the ground-truth reward function for the purpose of measuring true reward during evaluation. <|endoftext|> fine-tuning (SFT) in this task uses GPT-2-large trained on the IMDb reviews.

For summarization the inputs $x$ are forum posts from the Reddit TL;DR dataset (the Reddit TL;DR summarization corpus). The preference dataset used in experiments is the human preference set collected in prior work; the SFT model is a GPT-J model fine-tuned on human-written summaries (the CarperAI SFT <|endoftext|> cited in the paper). The evaluation compares generated summaries against reference summaries and uses an automatic judge (GPT-4) as a proxy for human preference to compute win rates.

For single-turn dialogue the data comes from the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) dialogue dataset, which contains transcripts where each prompt is followed by two candidate responses and a human preference over them. This dataset contains about 170k dialogues. No public SFT model matching this dataset was available, so the authors create a reference model by fine-tuning an off-the-shelf language model (Pythia-2.8B) on the preferred completions (Preferred-FT) and then use that as the reference for DPO training.

Across tasks the preference data are offline: each datapoint is a triple $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the human-preferred completion. The experiments aim to measure how effectively different algorithms transform this offline preference dataset into policies that (a) achieve high reward under the task-specific reward and (b) remain close to the reference policy as measured by <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|>

# Summarization: GPT-4 win rates; DPO ≈ 61% vs PPO ≈ 57% at best temps
On the Reddit TL;DR summarization task the authors evaluate methods by sampling completions at several temperatures (temperature range 0.0 to 1.0) and using GPT-4 as an automatic judge to compute win rates against the reference test summaries. The win rate is the fraction of examples for which a method's sampled summary is judged by GPT-4 to be better than the reference summary.

Using the GPT-J SFT <|endoftext|> as a common starting point for DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT, DPO attains a win rate of roughly 61% at temperature 0.0, exceeding the best PPO performance of roughly 57% (PPO’s optimal temperature in the experiments was also 0.0). DPO also achieves a higher maximum win rate than the Best-of-N baseline considered. The reported results are noteworthy because the authors did not heavily tune DPO’s single hyperparameter $\beta$, so these outcomes likely understate the method’s potential with careful tuning.

Two qualitative points emerge from the summarization experiments. First, DPO matches or exceeds PPO in absolute win rate against references on TL;DR, supporting the claim that the DPO objective is effective in practice for real preference datasets. Second, DPO is more robust to sampling temperature: while PPO’s performance can fall back to that of the base GPT-J model at higher temperatures, DPO degrades less with increased temperature, indicating more stable controlled generation across sampling regimes.

# Dialogue: DPO vs Preferred-FT, Best-of-128; DPO improves efficiently
In the single-turn dialogue experiments on the Anthropic HH dataset, the paper reports that DPO is the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions present in the dataset. Because no SFT model matched this dataset out of the box, the authors first train Preferred-FT (SFT on chosen completions) to form the reference model and then apply DPO. They also compare against Best-of-N sampling; Best-of-128 is used as a practical proxy for a strong but expensive baseline, and in preliminary exploration Best-of-N was observed to plateau by $N=128$.

The empirical findings are that DPO performs as well or better than Preferred-FT and Best-of-128 at their respective best sampling temperatures. # Sampling temperature sensitivity and robustness
Sampling temperature is a key <|endoftext|> hyperparameter that affects generation diversity and quality. The experiments sample completions for each method at multiple temperatures in the range $[0.0,1.0]$ and report win rates or reward/KL for each temperature. The paper <|endoftext|> emphasizes that DPO is more robust to the choice of sampling temperature than PPO. In summarization experiments PPO’s win rate deteriorates as temperature increases, sometimes falling back to the base SFT model’s performance; in contrast, DPO maintains stronger performance across temperatures, making it more forgiving to inference-time sampling choices.

While far from a definitive study of OOD robustness, the result provides initial evidence that DPO’s direct policy learning approach does not harm—and may help—generalization across related domains.

The human study had limited annotators, so the authors are careful to describe it as supporting evidence rather than a replacement for large-scale human evaluation. Still, these validation results justify using GPT-4 as a practical automatic proxy for human preference in their experiments.

# Synthesis and interpretations of the empirical findings
Across controlled sentiment, summarization, and dialogue tasks, the empirical story is consistent: DPO trains policies from preference data that are as good as or better than policies trained by standard RLHF (PPO) and simple <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> while being computationally simpler and more stable. The controlled sentiment experiments—where ground-truth reward is available—provide the cleanest evidence, showing that DPO achieves a strictly better reward-versus-KL trade-off than PPO (even PPO with ground-truth rewards). On real human-preference datasets (TL;DR and Anthropic HH), DPO reaches higher or comparable win rates against references and preferred completions, and is more robust to sampling temperature and to a modest domain shift (CNN/DailyMail).

The practical implications emphasized by the experiments are: DPO <|endoftext|> the complexity of fitting an explicit reward model and running an RL loop while still optimizing the same KL-constrained objective; it needs less hyperparameter tuning in the authors’ experience; and it is computationally efficient compared with expensive inference-time sampling schemes like Best-of-N. The validation of GPT-4 against human judges further supports the reported automatic comparisons.

Taken together, these empirical results make a compelling case that the DPO reparameterization and associated cross-entropy-like training objective provide an effective, stable, and simpler alternative to <|endoftext|> fine-tuning for learning from preferences in language generation tasks.
