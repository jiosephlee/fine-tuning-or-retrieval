There are several intuitions to draw from this expression.

- Directional effect: the bracketed difference \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\) is exactly the standard policy-gradient–style push: increase probability mass on preferred completions \(y_w\) and decrease it on dispreferred \(y_l\).

Thus DPO focuses its updates on misordered or uncertain pairs and down-weights pairs already handled satisfactorily.

- Global <|endoftext|> by \(\beta\): the overall factor \(-\beta\) scales the magnitude of updates. As discussed below, \(\beta\) has multiple interacting roles.

This weighted update is crucial: it is the mechanism that makes DPO behave sensibly and stably. Rather than <|endoftext|> pushing up preferred examples and down dispreferred ones, DPO modulates the strength of that push based on how strongly the current policy already prefers the right completion.

# Selecting and training the reference policy (SFT vs. Preferred-FT)
The reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is a crucial component of the DPO reparameterization: it anchors the <|endoftext|> reward and controls the <|endoftext|> neighborhood the algorithm prefers. The paper recommends two practical choices depending on dataset availability.

If a <|endoftext|> fine-tuned (SFT) model used to sample the preference dataset is available (common in RLHF pipelines), set \(\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}\). This is the natural choice because the preference dataset was generated from that SFT model and the intended <|endoftext|> penalty should measure deviation from the model that produced the candidate responses used for labeling.

If such an SFT model is not available (for example, public preference datasets were collected from an unknown or unavailable model, or you only have the preference pairs and no generator), the paper recommends training a reference policy by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions:
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\big].
\]
This "Preferred-FT" procedure fits a model to the chosen completions in \(\mathcal{D}\) so that \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) approximately matches the distribution of preferred outputs. Doing so helps <|endoftext|> distribution shift between the true sampling distribution used to create the labels and the \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that DPO uses as anchor. In experiments, Preferred-FT was used as the reference when no SFT <|endoftext|> was available.

Practical guidance from the paper: reuse existing SFT checkpoints whenever possible for \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Otherwise, prefer a <|endoftext|> fit on the preferred completions rather than using a generic pre-trained LM that is not aligned with the labeled data distribution.

# Practical recommendations and closing intuition
Summarizing the operational implications that follow from the mechanics above:

- Treat the policy as implicitly defining reward via \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\). You are optimizing the policy so that this <|endoftext|> reward explains the observed human <|endoftext|> preferences under a <|endoftext|> model.

- Use the DPO cross-entropy <|endoftext|> loss on preferred vs dispreferred pairs. The <|endoftext|> sigmoid weight focuses updates on misordered pairs, preventing the destructive uniform suppression that <|endoftext|> unlikelihood objectives can cause.

- Choose a reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that matches the data-collection generator when possible (SFT). If not available, fit \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) on the preferred completions (Preferred-FT) to reduce distribution shift.

- Pick \(\beta\) to reflect how conservative you want the fine-tuning to be: larger \(\beta\) keeps the policy closer to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and makes the preference <|endoftext|> crisper; smaller \(\beta\) allows more aggressive movement toward higher <|endoftext|> reward. In practice, the authors found DPO robust to coarse choices of \(\beta\).

- Benefit from greater stability and lower computational burden versus RLHF: no sampling in the inner loop, no separate reward network to optimize with an RL algorithm, no learned value baseline to tune.
