\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the normalization term in Eq. (10) relate to variance reduction in actor-critic and why is DPO more stable?
Question:
Equation (10) shows an objective with f(r_\phi, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta) that includes a log-sum-exp normalization term over y. The paper says omitting it creates high variance, motivating baselines/value functions in PPO. In DPO, this term doesn’t appear explicitly. Can someone connect the dots: is DPO implicitly incorporating this normalization by working with the log-density ratio as the ‘score’? Is there a formal variance comparison that explains DPO’s empirical stability without a learned baseline?
Answer:
Short answer: the log-sum-exp normalization term in the actor-critic / RLHF objective is the sequence-level “soft value” (a per-prompt baseline). If you drop it you turn a low-variance, expectation-based objective into a high-variance Monte‑Carlo policy gradient problem. DPO sidesteps this by reparameterizing rewards as the log-density ratio
$$r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},$$
so the partition function cancels in pairwise preference probabilities and never needs to be estimated or sampled. Practically this removes the need for a learned value/baseline and the single-sample normalization that causes much of the variance in actor-critic/RLHF, which explains DPO’s empirical stability.

Why the normalization term matters (intuitively + technically)
The RLHF / actor-critic formulation in the paper rewrites the constrained objective so the policy gradient involves the term
$$f(r_\phi,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log Z_\phi(x),$$
where
$$Z_\phi(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\right)$$
is the log-sum-exp / partition function. The important point is that the policy-gradient (or the KL-minimization view) requires expectations under the policy of the quantity $f(\ldots)$. Concretely (see Eq. (14) in the paper) you end up optimizing
$$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[ f(r_\phi,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big].$$
The partition function term depends on all possible completions $y$ for that prompt $x$; in practice you only have one (or a few) sampled $y$ from the policy at each update. Therefore you must either (a) estimate the partition function / value with a learned value network (actor-critic) or (b) use a single-sample Monte‑Carlo baseline (e.g. human baseline), both of which introduce variance and instability. Omitting the normalization entirely leaves a biased/noisy gradient with large variance, which is exactly why prior RLHF implementations rely on a learned value/baseline and careful tuning.

How DPO avoids this and why it reduces variance
DPO performs a change of variables: instead of fitting an explicit reward $r_\phi$ then sampling from the policy to estimate expectations, it parameterizes the reward class by policies via Eq. (6) / (9):
$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).$$
When you form pairwise preference probabilities (Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce), the additive per-$x$ term $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels, so preference likelihoods depend only on the log-density ratio
$$\hat r_\theta(x,y) := \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.$$
This means DPO never has to estimate the partition function or a value baseline. The DPO training objective (Eq. (10) / (11) in the paper) is a cross-entropy on pairwise comparisons:
$$L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big].$$
Its gradient (given in the paper) is an expectation over the offline comparison dataset (not an expectation over stochastic samples from $\pi_\theta$) of a weighted log-probability difference:
$$\nabla_\theta L_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta \,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].$$
Two variance-reducing facts follow immediately. First, this gradient is computed from the fixed labeled pairs in the dataset rather than from single-sample Monte‑Carlo returns drawn from $\pi_\theta$, so you avoid sampling noise caused by on-policy trajectory sampling. Second, the sigmoid factor acts as an adaptive importance weight: examples on which the current implicit reward misorders the pair get larger weight, while examples already correctly ordered get small weight. That adaptive weighting behaves like a control variate: it focuses updates where they matter and suppresses noisy, low-information gradients. Altogether, DPO replaces a noisy on-policy estimate of a global normalizer (or a learned value-baseline) with a deterministic log-density ratio computed from the model, so you avoid both baseline estimation error and single-sample Monte‑Carlo variance.

Formal variance comparison?
The paper does not give a formal theorem bounding variance between the actor-critic gradient and the DPO gradient. It does, however, give the exact algebraic connection (Eq. (13)/(14)) showing that the actor-critic objective contains the same normalization term that DPO cancels via reparameterization. From a standard variance-analysis perspective you can see why variance is lower for DPO: policy-gradient estimators that replace an unknown per-state value with a learned critic or a single-sample baseline carry additional estimation variance and bias. DPO sidesteps that estimator entirely, so the variance sources are reduced to (i) finite dataset sampling variance (offline comparisons) and (ii) usual SGD noise on the supervised-like DPO loss. If you want a precise, quantitative variance bound you would need to (a) choose a sampling model for trajectories under $\pi_\theta$, (b) pick a class and estimator for the value function used in actor-critic, and (c) compare the resulting estimator variances; that analysis is not carried out in the paper. The paper’s empirical experiments show the practical consequence: DPO’s reward–KL frontier strictly dominates PPO’s in the controlled experiments and DPO is more robust to hyperparameters and sampling temperature on real datasets.

In short: the normalization term is the source of the baseline/value problem in actor-critic RLHF; DPO eliminates the need to estimate that term by parameterizing the reward via the log-density ratio so the partition function cancels in pairwise preferences. This algebraic elimination of the partition function is the main reason DPO empirically avoids the high-variance behavior of PPO-style RLHF despite not using a learned baseline.
