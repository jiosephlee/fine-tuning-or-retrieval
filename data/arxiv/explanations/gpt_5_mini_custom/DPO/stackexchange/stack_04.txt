\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is the DPO sequence-level objective computed from token-level log-probabilities?
Question:
The DPO loss uses terms like \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} for a whole completion y. In practice, are these computed as sums over tokens, i.e., \sum_t \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}? If so, does the logistic loss \sigma(\cdot) use the total sequence-level difference between preferred and dispreferred pairs, or is there any per-token weighting? How do length effects get handled so that longer preferred sequences do not automatically dominate?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — DPO uses the sequence-level log-probability ratio, which for an autoregressive LM is computed as the sum of token log-probabilities. The logistic loss in Eq. (9) (the DPO objective) takes the scalar sequence-level difference between the preferred and dispreferred completions; there is no extra per‑token weighting in the loss beyond the implicit contribution each token’s log-prob makes to the sequence log-prob. The paper does not introduce an explicit length-normalizer; length effects are controlled implicitly by (a) comparing completions sampled from the same reference policy (so lengths tend to be similar), (b) the KL/β regularizer which limits how far the policy can push up sequence log-prob, and (c) the fact that the Bradley–Terry model and the DPO reparameterization only depend on reward differences (the partition function cancels). If length differences are a concern in practice, common mitigations are to use paired examples with similar lengths or to work with average (per‑token) log-probs — but note the DPO paper itself reports and optimizes sequence-level KL (they define KL as the sum of per-timestep KLs) and presents the objective in terms of full-sequence log $\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.

More detail and intuition tied to the paper

For an autoregressive policy $ \pi(y\mid x) = \prod_t \pi(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, the sequence log-prob appears in the DPO objective exactly as you wrote:

$\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),$

and consequently

$\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = \sum_t \left[\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right].$

The DPO loss (Eq. (9) in the paper),

$L_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right]\right)\right],$

operates on the scalar difference between the two full-sequence log-ratios. So the logistic ($\sigma$) acts on the total sequence-level score difference; there is no additional per-token weighting in the formula. The gradient (displayed in the paper) decomposes into gradients of the sequence log-likelihoods, which further decompose into sums of token-level gradients — that is how each token contributes to the total update.

About length effects

Because sequence log-probs are sums over tokens, longer sequences can have larger magnitude log-probs (and therefore larger log-ratio magnitudes). The paper does not add an explicit length normalization term. Instead, the method relies on a few mechanisms that reduce pathological length bias in practice:

- The DPO formulation is founded on reward differences (Bradley–Terry style), and the partition function/normalizing term cancels when forming preference probabilities, so any additive per-sequence baseline that is the same for both completions (a function only of $x$) does not affect the comparison.

- In practice, preference pairs are sampled from the same reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, so sampled completions tend to have similar length/statistics; comparing such pairs reduces systematic length differences.

- The algorithm includes the KL-style regularization via the implicit $\beta$: the implicit reward is $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, and $\beta$ controls how aggressively the model can boost sequence probabilities. This limits runaway increases in probability that could otherwise amplify length effects.

- The paper also mentions practical choices that reduce distribution shift (e.g., initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from an SFT model or fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to preferred completions), which helps keep the comparisons well behaved.

If you have a dataset where preferred and dispreferred completions systematically differ in length (e.g., preferred ones are always much longer), then that can bias the DPO signal because the raw sequence log-ratio will partly reflect length. Common practical mitigations (not specific prescriptions in the paper, but consistent with its setup) are to sample or pair completions of similar lengths, or to compute per-token (average) log-probabilities rather than raw sums if you specifically want to remove length scaling. Be aware, however, that switching to per-token averages changes the reward parameterization (it changes what DPO is optimizing) and may require re-tuning $\beta$.

Summary

- Yes: compute sequence-level log ratios by summing token log-probs. The logistic uses the total sequence-level difference between the preferred and dispreferred completion. There is no extra per-token weighting other than each token’s contribution to the summed log-prob.

- The paper does not add an explicit length-normalizer; length effects are mitigated by sampling/pairing practice, the choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and the $\beta$ (KL) regularization. If you expect systematic length differences in your pairs, you should consider pairing by length or using a per-token normalization deliberately, while understanding that this changes the objective the model is optimizing.
