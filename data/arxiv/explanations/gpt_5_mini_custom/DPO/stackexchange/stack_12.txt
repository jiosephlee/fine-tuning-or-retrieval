\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### If DPO is maximizing likelihood under a preference model, where does the implicit exploration come from?
Question:
PPO performs on-policy sampling during training, which provides exploration over outputs y given x. DPO, as presented, avoids sampling in the loop and uses an offline dataset of preferences. Does this mean DPO is purely offline? If the preference data is limited or biased, is there a mechanism akin to exploration to improve coverage of the output space, or must one augment DPO with additional preference data collection or self-generated pairs?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO itself is an offline, supervised-style fit to a dataset of preference pairs and does not perform exploration during optimization. Any “exploration” or coverage of the output space must come from how the preference dataset was collected (or from additional data augmentation/self-labeling you add before or between DPO runs), not from the DPO gradient updates.

Why: DPO derives a maximum-likelihood objective for the optimal policy under a preference model (e.g. Bradley–Terry) and optimizes the policy parameters to maximize the likelihood of observed comparisons. The training objective is

$$
\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr],
$$

which is a pure offline likelihood objective on an existing dataset $\mathcal{D}$. The paper is explicit: the DPO pipeline assumes you already have (or collect) preference-labeled pairs $(x,y_1,y_2)$ sampled from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and then fits $\pi_\theta$ to that dataset. There is no sampling-from-the-policy-in-the-loop RL step during DPO fine-tuning.

What provides exploration, then? It is provided at data-collection time, not by the optimizer. In the standard RLHF pipeline the preference dataset is created by sampling completions $y\sim\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\cdot\mid x)$ (possibly at various temperatures or sampling schemes) and then labeling those pairs. DPO reuses that same offline dataset. Thus coverage of the space of plausible outputs—and the ability of DPO to move the policy toward better regions—depends on: (1) how diverse the candidate responses in $\mathcal{D}$ are (which is determined by the sampling strategy used to produce them), and (2) whether the dataset contains comparisons that expose desirable alternatives. The DPO optimization itself enforces a KL-style conservativeness (through the $\beta$ reparameterization and the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta = \beta\log\!\bigl(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)$), which helps prevent collapse but does not invent new outputs beyond what the model can represent.

Practical implications and remedies when preference data is limited or biased: because DPO is offline, you must improve the data if you want broader coverage or to correct bias. The paper notes several relevant directions (and limitations/future work): reuse public preference datasets where possible, initialize $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions when the true SFT model is unavailable, and consider techniques for collecting more or different preference pairs. Concretely, common strategies are:

- Collect more candidate generations (wider exploration at collection time): sample from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ with higher temperature, use nucleus sampling, or deliberately diversify decoding to cover more modes, then obtain preference labels for those candidates.
- Iterated/online data collection: alternate between (a) sampling from the current policy (or a mixture of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\pi_\theta$), (b) labeling new pairs, and (c) re-running DPO. This recreates the “on-policy” discovery loop used by RLHF but keeps DPO’s simple optimization step.
- Synthetic or automated labeling: generate candidate pairs and label them with an automated judge (e.g. a rubric-driven LLM or a trained reward model) to cheaply expand coverage; the paper cites synthetic preference generation as commonly used in practice.
- Self-labeling / active learning: have the model propose novel candidates for which you solicit labels for the most informative comparisons (e.g. pairs where the model is uncertain or where $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ disagree). The paper mentions this as an open question: “can training with self-labeling from the DPO policy similarly make effective use of unlabeled prompts?”
- Best-of-N / large candidate sets at inference: while computationally expensive at test time, Best-of-N (sample many candidates and pick the highest-scoring one under a reward) is a way to exploit a reward model or preference model to get better outputs; DPO can be combined with such selection strategies if you have an evaluator.

Relation to generalization: the experiments in the paper show initial evidence that DPO generalizes comparably to PPO in some OOD tests (e.g. Reddit → CNN/DM summaries), but this generalization is still contingent on the representativeness and diversity of the preference data used for training.

Bottom line: DPO removes the RL loop and implicit sampling from the optimizer, simplifying training, but it does not remove the need to design a data-collection strategy that provides sufficient coverage of outputs. If your preference dataset is limited or biased, you must expand or diversify the preference data (via more sampling, self-labeling, active collection, or synthetic labels) or adopt an iterative collection loop; DPO then straightforwardly ingests the expanded offline dataset and learns from it.
