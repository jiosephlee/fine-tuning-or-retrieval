Chapter: Turning RLHF into Classification via Direct Preference Optimization

# Motivation and Overview

Large language models pre-trained with unsupervised objectives acquire broad knowledge and useful capabilities, but exerting precise control over their behavior requires steering them toward human-preferred responses. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a successful approach: first fit a reward model to human preferences over pairs of model outputs, then optimize a policy to maximize that reward while staying close to a reference policy with a KL-divergence constraint. However, RLHF adds substantial complexity: it trains multiple models, requires sampling in the loop, and depends on reinforcement learning procedures that can be unstable and expensive.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) addresses this complexity by showing that the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective can be re-expressed and optimized exactly using a simple binary cross-entropy classification loss. The key insight is a change of variables: reparameterize the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy, and then use the structure of the preference model to eliminate intractable terms. With this transformation, the policy itself implicitly defines the reward, and optimizing the preference likelihood directly over policies recovers the same solution that RLHF targets.

This chapter develops the intuition, derivation, and implications of that change of variables. We begin by reviewing the RLHF pipeline and the Bradley–Terry preference model. We then present the KL-constrained optimization problem and its closed-form optimal policy. Next, we perform the crucial reparameterization that maps rewards to policies and show how the partition function cancels inside the preference likelihood. This yields the DPO loss, a clean classification objective. We conclude with a mechanistic view of the gradient, practical considerations, and theoretical context that explains why “your language model is secretly a reward model.”

# From Human Preferences to the RLHF Objective

Preference learning in RLHF starts with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of a language model on high-quality demonstrations, producing a reference policy $\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$. The model is then used to generate pairs of completions $(y_1, y_2)$ for prompts $x$, and human annotators express binary preferences $y_w \\succ y_l \\mid x$, indicating which completion they prefer. These preferences are modeled as noisy observations of a latent reward function $r^*(x, y)$.

A standard model of pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry (BT) model, which asserts that the probability of preferring $y_1$ to $y_2$ at prompt $x$ depends on the differences of their rewards:
$$
p^*(y_1 \\succ y_2 \\mid x)=\\frac{\\exp\\left(r^*(x, y_1)\\right)}{\\exp\\left(r^*(x, y_1)\\right) + \\exp\\left(r^*(x, y_2)\\right)}.
$$
Given a dataset of comparisons $\\mathcal{D}=\\bigl\\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\\bigr\\}_{i=1}^N$, one can fit a parametric reward model $r_{\\phi}(x, y)$ by maximizing the BT likelihood, which is equivalent to minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss
$$
\\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\\phi}, \\mathcal{D}) = -\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\\sim \\mathcal{D}}\\bigl[\\log \\sigma(r_{\\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\\phi}(x, y_l))\\bigr],
$$
where $\\sigma$ denotes the logistic function. In RLHF, this learned reward is then used to optimize a policy with a KL-divergence regularizer that anchors the policy to the reference $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ (typically $\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$). The resulting objective is
$$
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}}  \\mathbb{E}_{x\\sim \\mathcal{D}, y\\sim \\pi_{\\theta}(y \\mid x)}\\bigl[r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\bigr] - \\beta\\mathbb{D}_{\\textrm{KL}}\\bigl[\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)\\mid \\mid \\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\bigr],
$$
with $\\beta$ controlling how far the policy may deviate from $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$. This objective is typically optimized by reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO, incurring the practical burdens of sampling in the loop, value estimation, and algorithmic stability management.

# The KL-Constrained Optimal Policy and Its Structure

A central fact underlying DPO is that the KL-constrained objective admits a closed-form optimal policy when the reward is known. Specifically, the optimal policy for
$$
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}}  \\mathbb{E}_{x\\sim \\mathcal{D}, y\\sim \\pi_{\\theta}(y \\mid x)}\\bigl[r(x, y)\\bigr] - \\beta\\mathbb{D}_{\\textrm{KL}}\\bigl[\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)\\mid \\mid \\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\bigr]
$$
takes the exponential tilting form
$$
\\pi_r(y\\mid x) = \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x, y)\\right),
$$
where $Z(x) =\\sum_{y}\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x, y)\\right)$ is the partition function that normalizes the policy for each prompt $x$. This form highlights two essential components: the “reference prior” $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ and the exponential scaling of rewards with temperature $\\beta$. Intuitively, the optimal policy reweights the reference distribution toward higher-reward completions, with the KL regularizer controlling the strength of this reweighting.

This expression is elegant but not directly practical for optimization over policies, because $Z(x)$ depends on the reward and is costly to estimate. DPO’s change of variables sidesteps this obstacle by reparameterizing the reward in terms of the policy itself.

# The Change of Variables: Reward in Terms of Policy

Starting from the optimal policy expression, take logarithms and rearrange to solve for the reward:
$$
r(x,y) =\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_r(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)} + \\beta \\log Z(x).
$$
This identity holds for any reward $r$ and its optimal policy $\\pi_r$. The crucial observation is that many preference models, including Bradley–Terry, depend only on differences of rewards between two completions $(y_1, y_2)$ for the same prompt $x$. Under BT,
$$
p^*(y_1 \\succ y_2 \\mid x) = \\sigma\\bigl(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\\bigr).
$$
Substituting the reparameterized rewards into this difference cancels the partition function:
$$
r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_1\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_1\\mid x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_2\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_2\\mid x)}.
$$
Therefore, the preference probability can be written directly in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy, with no dependence on $Z(x)$:
$$
p^*(y_1\\succ y_2 \\mid x)=\\frac{1}{1 + \\exp\\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_2\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_2\\mid x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_1\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_1\\mid x)}\\right)}.
$$
This change of variables is the pivotal step: it transforms a reward-modeling problem into a policy-modeling problem by expressing the preference likelihood in terms of $\\pi$ rather than $r$. Because the partition function cancels in the BT likelihood, the intractable term that complicates direct use of the optimal policy disappears.

# From RLHF to a Simple Classification Loss: The DPO Objective

Once the preference likelihood is parameterized by the policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ and the fixed reference $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$, we can perform maximum likelihood estimation over policies exactly as one would over rewards. Plugging $\\pi_{\\theta}$ into the preference model yields the DPO loss:
$$
\\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}(\\pi_{\\theta}; \\pi_\\text{ref}) = -\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\\sim \\mathcal{D}}\\left[\\log \\sigma \\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_w\\mid x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_l\\mid x)}\\right)\\right].
$$
This objective is a binary cross-entropy classification loss over preference pairs, with the logit equal to a scaled difference of log probability ratios between the learned policy and the reference policy. Optimizing $\\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}$ fits an implicit reward of the form
$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y \\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y \\mid x)}.
$$
In other words, the policy itself, relative to the reference, parameterizes the reward class that is consistent with the BT preference model. By minimizing this single classification loss, DPO recovers the same optimal policy targeted by the KL-constrained RLHF objective, but without explicitly modeling rewards or running reinforcement learning.

This is the essence of the change of variables: express the latent quantity of interest—differences in reward—through a tractable function of the policy and the reference, and then optimize the likelihood over policies directly. The resulting algorithm is computationally lightweight, sampling-free during training, and empirically stable, yet optimizes the same constrained objective as PPO-based RLHF.

# Mechanistic Understanding: What the DPO Update Learns

To build intuition, consider the gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the policy parameters $\\theta$:
$$
\\nabla_\\theta \\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}(\\pi_\\theta;\\pi_\\text{ref}) = -\\beta\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \\sim \\mathcal{D}} \\bigg[\\sigma\\bigl(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w)\\bigr)\\Bigl(\\nabla_\\theta\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_w \\mid x) - \\nabla_\\theta\\log\\pi_\\theta(y_l \\mid x)\\Bigr)\\bigg],
$$
where $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y \\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y \\mid x)}$. This gradient reveals two important behaviors.

First, the update increases the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion $y_l$, exactly as one expects from a classification loss over preference pairs.

Second, the update is adaptively weighted by $\\sigma\\bigl(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w)\\bigr)$, which is large when the implicit reward ranks $y_l$ above $y_w$ and small when it already ranks $y_w$ higher. This per-example weighting acts like an importance factor that focuses learning on the most misordered preferences while tempering updates on already-correct pairs. Empirically, this weighting mitigates degeneracy that can occur with naive probability-ratio objectives, preserving diversity and stability while still enforcing preference orderings.

# Practical Pipeline Under the Change of Variables

The DPO pipeline mirrors RLHF at a high level but omits explicit reward modeling and RL fine-tuning. One constructs the preference dataset $\\mathcal{D}$ by sampling pairs $(y_1, y_2)$ from a reference policy $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ for each prompt $x$ and annotating which completion is preferred. Then, initialize the learnable policy $\\pi_\\theta$, typically from the same SFT model used as $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$, and optimize $\\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}(\\pi_{\\theta}; \\pi_\\text{ref})$ for a chosen $\\beta$.

When an off-the-shelf SFT model used to generate the preference pairs is available, one simply sets $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}=\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$. If that precise reference is unavailable, $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ can be constructed by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions alone,
$$
\\pi_\\text{ref} = \\arg\\max_{\\pi}\\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \\sim \\mathcal{D}}\\left[\\log \\pi(y_w \\mid x)\\right],
$$
which helps match the distribution of the preference data and stabilize the subsequent ratio terms in the DPO objective. Crucially, the training loop requires no on-policy sampling and no value estimation; it is standard supervised optimization of a classification loss over pairs, yet implicitly optimizes the original KL-constrained objective.

# Why the Reparameterization Works: Rewards as Policy Ratios

The theoretical foundation of DPO’s reparameterization relies on an equivalence relation over reward functions. Two rewards $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if $r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ that depends only on the prompt. Under Bradley–Terry and more general Plackett–Luce models, rewards in the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution because only reward differences between completions for the same prompt matter. Moreover, under the KL-constrained RL objective, equivalent rewards yield the same optimal policy.

Given any reward $r$, its KL-constrained optimal policy has the exponential form with partition function $Z(x)$. Projecting $r$ by subtracting $\\beta\\log Z(x)$ yields an equivalent reward
$$
f(r; \\pi_\\text{ref}, \\beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \\beta\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x, y)\\right),
$$
and substituting the optimal-policy identity shows that
$$
f(r; \\pi_\\text{ref}, \\beta)(x, y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_r(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)}.
$$
Thus, every reward equivalence class consistent with BT/Plackett–Luce can be represented by an element of the form $\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)}$ for some policy $\\pi$. In this sense, the policy parameterization implicitly spans the same reward classes that are identifiable from preferences, and the normalization constraint that probabilities sum to one selects a particular representative within each class. This is why fitting preferences over policies via the DPO loss recovers the same optimal policy targeted by RLHF without constraining the class of learnable rewards.

# Implications and Connections

DPO’s change of variables removes the need to estimate or sample the partition function $Z(x)$ because it cancels inside the preference likelihood. It also avoids the instability associated with actor–critic optimization of the KL-constrained objective, where the normalization term functions like a soft value baseline that is difficult to approximate accurately. By reparameterizing rewards as log policy ratios, DPO yields a reward that does not require learned baselines or Monte Carlo normalization and can be optimized with a single, supervised objective.

Practically, this transformation means a policy can be trained from pairwise preferences with a binary cross-entropy loss and a reference-anchored logit, rather than through explicit reward modeling and RL. Conceptually, it means the language model policy is also an implicit reward model: $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y)$ is defined by how much more likely the model makes $y$ relative to the reference for the same prompt. Empirically, this simplicity comes with strong performance, stable training, and efficient computation, matching or surpassing PPO-based RLHF while greatly simplifying the pipeline.

In summary, the DPO change of variables turns the RLHF objective into a tractable classification problem by expressing preference likelihoods directly in terms of policy and reference probabilities. This makes it possible to learn aligned behavior from preferences without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning, while preserving the theoretical guarantees and practical advantages of KL-constrained optimization.
