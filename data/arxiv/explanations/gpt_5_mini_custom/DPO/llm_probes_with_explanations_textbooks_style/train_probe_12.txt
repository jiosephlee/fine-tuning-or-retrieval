Chapter Title: Per-Example Weighting in Direct Preference Optimization: Focusing Learning Where It Matters

# From RLHF to Direct Preference Optimization

Large-scale language models are typically aligned with human preferences through a three-stage reinforcement learning from human feedback pipeline. First, a supervised fine-tuning stage produces a reference model, denoted $\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$. Second, the reference model generates pairs of candidate completions for prompts, and human annotators provide binary preferences indicating which completion is better. Third, a reward model is trained to fit these preferences under a preference model such as Bradley–Terry, and the language model is then fine-tuned with reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward while staying close to the reference model via a KL-divergence constraint.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) removes the complexity of explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning by reparameterizing the objective in terms of the policy itself. Rather than learning a separate reward function and then performing reinforcement learning to optimize it, DPO derives a loss over policies directly from the preference model. This yields a simple binary cross-entropy objective that is equivalent to optimizing the same KL-constrained reward maximization objective, but without the instability or computational burden of reinforcement learning.

# The DPO Loss and the Implicit Reward

The DPO approach begins with the observation that the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization objective takes the form
$$
\\pi_r(y\\mid x) \\,=\\, \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\,\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x,y)\\right),
$$
where $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ is the reference policy (typically the SFT model), $\\beta$ controls the strength of the KL constraint, and $Z(x)$ is a partition function ensuring normalization. Rearranging this expression yields a change of variables that writes the reward in terms of the optimal policy and reference policy:
$$
r(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_r(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y\\mid x)} \\, + \\, \\beta\\log Z(x).
$$

Under the Bradley–Terry preference model, the human preference probability depends only on differences of rewards. Substituting the above reparameterization into the preference model cancels the partition function and produces an expression for preferences entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$. This leads to the DPO training objective over a parametric policy $\\pi_\\theta$:
$$
\\mathcal{L}_{\\text{DPO}}(\\pi_{\\theta}; \\pi_{\\text{ref}}) \\,=\\, -\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\\sim \\mathcal{D}}
\\left[\\log \\sigma\\!\\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_w\\mid x)} \\, - \\, \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_l\\mid x)}\\right)\\right],
$$
where $\\sigma$ is the logistic function, $y_w$ is the preferred completion, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred completion. This objective implicitly fits a reward of the form
$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y\\mid x)}.
$$
Crucially, the language model $\\pi_\\theta$ simultaneously represents a policy and an implicit reward under this reparameterization, which is the foundation for DPO’s simplicity.

# The Gradient of the DPO Loss

To understand how DPO updates the model, it is helpful to examine the gradient of $\\mathcal{L}_{\\text{DPO}}$ with respect to $\\theta$. Differentiating the objective yields
$$
\\nabla_\\theta \\mathcal{L}_{\\text{DPO}}(\\pi_\\theta;\\pi_{\\text{ref}}) \\,=\\, -\\beta\\,\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \\sim \\mathcal{D}} \\Bigg[ \\sigma\\!\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)\\big)\\Big(\\nabla_\\theta\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_w \\mid x) \\, - \\, \\nabla_\\theta\\log\\pi_\\theta(y_l \\mid x)\\Big)\\Bigg],
$$
with
$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y\\mid x)}.
$$
This gradient has three components: a global scale $\\beta$, a per-example importance weight $\\sigma\\!\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)\\big)$, and a direction that increases $\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_w\\mid x)$ while decreasing $\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_l\\mid x)$. The direction term encodes the basic preference learning action: push up the likelihood of the preferred completion and push down the likelihood of the dispreferred completion. The per-example weight modulates how strongly that update is applied.

# The Per-Example Weight: What It Measures

The per-example weight
$$
w(x,y_w,y_l) \\,=\\, \\sigma\\!\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)\\big)
$$
is a logistic transform of the difference between the model’s implicit rewards for the dispreferred and preferred completions. This difference quantifies how incorrectly the model currently orders the pair. If $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l)$ is higher than $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)$, the model is currently misranking the pair, and the argument of $\\sigma(\\cdot)$ is positive. In that case, $w$ is closer to $1$, and the update is applied strongly to correct the mistake. Conversely, if the model already rates the preferred completion much higher than the dispreferred one, $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)$ is a large negative number, and the logistic function returns a value near $0$.

In other words, DPO automatically focuses learning on the “hard” or misranked examples and deemphasizes examples that the model already handles correctly. This behavior emerges directly from the structure of the loss and the implicit reward; no heuristic sampling or explicit hard example mining is required.

# Limiting Behavior: When the Weight Goes to Zero

Consider the regime in which the model already rates the preferred completion substantially higher than the dispreferred completion. Formally, suppose
$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w) \\gg \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l).
$$
Then the difference $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)$ is a large negative quantity. Because $\\sigma(z)$ decays to $0$ as $z\\to -\\infty$, we have
$$
\\sigma\\!\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)\\big) \\approx 0,
$$
and thus the contribution of this example to the gradient is negligible. Intuitively, the model is already ordering the pair correctly with a large margin, so further pushing on this pair yields diminishing returns. DPO’s weighting naturally implements this diminishing-return behavior.

In contrast, when the model is confidently wrong, with
$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) \\gg \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w),
$$
the argument of $\\sigma(\\cdot)$ is a large positive number, and $\\sigma(\\cdot) \\approx 1$. In this case, the update is applied with near-maximal strength to correct the misordering: increase the log-probability of $y_w$ and decrease the log-probability of $y_l$. Between these extremes, the weight takes values in $(0,1)$ that reflect the degree of confidence and correctness of the current ranking.

# Why This Weighting Matters

The per-example weighting is not a cosmetic detail; it is essential to stable training. The gradient direction always seeks to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ and decrease the likelihood of $y_l$, but without appropriate weighting, repeatedly applying large updates to pairs that are already correctly ordered can lead to undesirable behavior. The model may overemphasize already-high-probability responses and underemphasize diversity, drifting away from the reference distribution beyond the regime where the preference model is faithful. The paper notes that a naive probability ratio objective without this weighting can cause the language model to degenerate, and presents experimental evidence to this effect.

By scaling updates based on how incorrectly the model orders each pair, DPO focuses optimization on genuinely informative errors while protecting well-ordered examples from unnecessary updates. This mechanism also interacts meaningfully with the KL constraint controlled by $\\beta$. Because the implicit reward is $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y\\mid x)}$, the margin between preferred and dispreferred completions is measured in units that incorporate both the policy and the reference. Consequently, large margins that already respect the KL constraint are further deemphasized, whereas misrankings are corrected efficiently.

# The Role of $\\beta$ in Weighting and Updates

The parameter $\\beta$ appears both outside the expectation, scaling the overall gradient, and inside the implicit reward. As a global scale, larger $\\beta$ yields larger updates per example, all else equal. As part of $\\hat{r}_\\theta$, $\\beta$ modulates the sensitivity of the implicit reward to differences in the ratio $\\frac{\\pi_\\theta}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}}$. Increasing $\\beta$ stretches the implicit reward differences, which can make the per-example weight saturate more quickly for strongly misranked or strongly well-ranked pairs. Conversely, smaller $\\beta$ compresses the implicit reward differences, yielding more moderate weights across pairs.

This dual role ensures that $\\beta$ governs both how far the policy is allowed to move from the reference distribution and how aggressively DPO focuses on correcting misranked examples. It is therefore a central knob controlling the trade-off between exploitation of preferences and adherence to the reference behavior.

# Practical Consequences for Training Dynamics

The weighting behavior has several practical consequences for optimization:

First, DPO naturally allocates computation to the most informative parts of the dataset. Examples that are already correctly ordered with large margins receive near-zero weight and contribute minimally to parameter updates. This prevents over-optimization on easy pairs and helps maintain diversity by not collapsing probability mass onto already-preferred completions.

Second, DPO mitigates instability without auxiliary baselines or value functions. In actor-critic approaches, normalization terms or baselines are introduced to reduce variance and stabilize learning. In DPO, the reparameterization yields an implicit reward that already incorporates the KL structure, and the per-example weighting attenuates updates where the model is correct, reducing gradient variance and preventing runaway optimization on uninformative examples.

Third, DPO’s focus on misranked examples improves sample efficiency. Because the strength of the update is largest where the model is most wrong, fewer updates are wasted on pairs that do not change the policy meaningfully. The paper’s experiments demonstrate that DPO achieves a favorable reward–KL frontier compared to PPO, even when PPO uses ground-truth rewards in a controlled setting, underscoring the effectiveness of this training dynamics.

# Summary

Direct Preference Optimization replaces the multi-stage RLHF process with a single, maximum-likelihood objective over policies derived from a preference model. Central to its stability and effectiveness is the per-example importance weight in the gradient,
$$
\\sigma\\!\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_w)\\big),
$$
which quantifies how incorrectly the current model ranks the dispreferred and preferred completions. When the model already rates $y_w$ much higher than $y_l$, this weight goes to $0$, and the example contributes negligibly to the update; when the model is confidently wrong, the weight approaches $1$, and the update is applied strongly. This behavior focuses learning on hard examples, reduces instability, and helps DPO achieve strong empirical performance while remaining simple and computationally lightweight.
