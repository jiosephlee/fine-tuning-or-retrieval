Direct Preference Optimization: Eliminating Reinforcement Learning by Closed-Form Policy Extraction

# From RLHF to Directly Optimizing Preferences

Modern language models exhibit broad knowledge and emerging reasoning capabilities after large-scale unsupervised pre-training, but aligning these models to desired behaviors requires an additional stage that incorporates human preferences. The commonly used pipeline, reinforcement learning from human feedback, proceeds in three phases: supervised fine-tuning to obtain a reference policy, reward modeling from pairwise human preferences, and reinforcement learning to optimize the policy under a Kullback–Leibler (KL) constraint relative to the reference. This approach has been successful in practice, yet it is operationally complex and computationally demanding. In particular, the final reinforcement learning phase requires sampling from the policy during training, learning auxiliary components such as value functions or baselines, and careful hyperparameter tuning to avoid instability.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) introduces a conceptual simplification of this pipeline. The central idea is to reparameterize the reward learning problem so that the optimal policy for the standard KL-constrained objective is expressed in closed form. With this reparameterization, the policy can be trained directly from preference data using a simple binary cross-entropy objective, obviating the need for reinforcement learning. The fact we will make precise in this chapter is that, once DPO extracts the optimal policy in closed form and trains with a classification loss over preferences, reinforcement learning becomes unnecessary for solving the same constrained reward maximization problem optimized by prior RLHF methods.

# The RLHF Objective and Its KL Constraint

To ground the discussion, consider the standard RLHF objective for a parametrized policy \( \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \), a learned reward model \( r_{\phi}(x, y) \), and a reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \) provided by the supervised fine-tuned model:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] \ - \ \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)\,\middle\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\right].
\]
The KL term controls how far the updated policy can drift from the reference policy, preventing over-optimization in regions where the reward model may be unreliable and maintaining diversity. However, because the action space is discrete and sequence-valued in language modeling, this objective is typically not differentiable with respect to the sampling distribution, leading to reinforcement learning algorithms such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) to optimize it. This procedure introduces sampling in the loop, reward normalization, and sometimes value function learning, all of which complicate training and can lead to instability.

DPO reconsiders this objective by leveraging its optimality conditions. Prior work shows that the optimal policy for such a KL-regularized objective assumes the following exponential-family form:
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x, y)\right),
\]
where \( Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right) \) is the partition function. This expression makes explicit that the optimal policy is a KL-projection of an exponentiated reward onto the neighborhood of \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). However, directly using this formula requires estimating \( Z(x) \), which is generally intractable for a large output space. DPO’s key insight is to invert this relation to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy, then design a training loss that depends only on policy ratios for paired comparisons, in which the partition function cancels.

# Modeling Human Preferences with Bradley–Terry

Preference data consists of tuples \( (x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \) where \( y_{w} \) is preferred to \( y_{l} \) for prompt \( x \). A commonly used probabilistic model of pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry (BT) model, which posits that preferences are generated by a latent reward \( r^{*}(x, y) \) via
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x) \ = \ \frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1})) + \exp(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))} \ = \ \sigma\!\bigl( r^{*}(x,y_{1}) - r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\]
where \( \sigma \) is the logistic function. Standard RLHF first fits a reward model \( r_{\phi}(x, y) \) to this dataset by maximum likelihood, optimizing the negative log-likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) \ = \ -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \sigma\bigl( r_{\phi}(x, y_{w}) - r_{\phi}(x, y_{l}) \bigr) \right].
\]
After this step, reinforcement learning is used to optimize the KL-regularized objective with the learned reward \( r_{\phi} \).

DPO departs from this two-stage design by recognizing that the BT likelihood can be equivalently written in terms of the optimal policy ratios when substituting the closed-form relation between rewards and the optimal policy.

# Closed-Form Reparameterization: From Rewards to Policy Ratios

Begin with the closed-form optimal policy for a given reward \( r \):
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y) \right).
\]
Taking logarithms and rearranging yields an expression for the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
r(x,y) \ = \ \beta \log\frac{\pi_{r}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \ + \ \beta \log Z(x).
\]
If we plug this reparameterization into the Bradley–Terry model for a pair \( (y_{1}, y_{2}) \), the partition function \( \log Z(x) \) cancels because the BT probability depends only on the reward difference \( r(x,y_{1}) - r(x,y_{2}) \). The resulting preference probability depends only on a difference of policy-to-reference log-ratios:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left( \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2} \mid x)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1} \mid x)} \right)}.
\]
This equation shows that the distribution of human preferences can be written directly in terms of the optimal policy \( \pi^{*} \) and the known reference \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \), without explicit rewards or partition functions. The conclusion is profound: if we parameterize a policy \( \pi_{\theta} \) and fit it by maximum likelihood under this model, the resulting \( \pi_{\theta} \) is precisely the optimal policy for a reward function implicitly defined by the ratio \( \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \).

# DPO’s Objective: A Simple Classification Loss Over Pairs

By replacing the explicit reward parameterization with the policy-ratio parameterization in the BT likelihood, DPO trains \( \pi_{\theta} \) via the maximum likelihood objective
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) \ = \ -\, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w} \mid x)} \ - \ \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l} \mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
This is a binary cross-entropy (classification) loss on pairwise comparisons, where the logit is the difference of policy-to-reference log-probabilities scaled by \( \beta \). Crucially, no reinforcement learning is needed: we are directly maximizing the likelihood of observed preferences under a model whose parameters are those of the policy. Sampling from the policy inside the training loop is unnecessary; instead, one computes likelihoods of observed completions. Thus, the reinforcement learning phase of RLHF is replaced by supervised learning with a classification-style loss, while still optimizing the same underlying KL-regularized objective.

To clarify the mechanics, define the implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \ = \ \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
\]
The gradient of \( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \) can then be expressed as
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) \ = \ -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l})} \left[ \sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{w})\bigr) \left( \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w} \mid x) - \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{l} \mid x) \right) \right].
\]
This update increases the likelihood of \( y_{w} \) and decreases that of \( y_{l} \), weighted by how severely the current implicit reward misorders the pair. The importance weighting provided by the logistic term stabilizes learning compared to naively pushing probability ratios without such weighting.

# Why Reinforcement Learning Becomes Unnecessary

The essential reason reinforcement learning is no longer needed is that DPO transforms the RLHF objective into a supervised learning problem over observed preference pairs. The transformation depends on two facts. First, in KL-regularized policy optimization, the optimal solution has a known exponential-family form relative to the reference policy, making it possible to solve for \( r \) or for \( \pi \) in terms of the other. Second, under the Bradley–Terry model, only differences of rewards matter, which implies that the unknown partition function cancels when forming preference probabilities.

Putting these together, DPO defines a direct maximum-likelihood objective over policies whose optimum coincides with the RLHF optimum. Where RLHF first learns \( r_{\phi} \) and then performs RL to optimize \( \pi_{\theta} \) against \( r_{\phi} \) under a KL penalty, DPO fits \( \pi_{\theta} \) directly via the Bradley–Terry likelihood expressed in policy-ratio space. In effect, the language model is “secretly” a reward model: the implicit reward \( \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \) induces the same ordering over completions that would be obtained by explicitly modeling \( r \) up to an additive function of \( x \). Preferences depend only on such differences, and so DPO’s reparameterization preserves the class of representable preference distributions pertinent to the problem.

Moreover, the DPO training loss requires only computing log-probabilities under \( \pi_{\theta} \) and \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) for observed responses, avoiding in-loop sampling, value function learning, or reward baselines. This eliminates the primary sources of instability in actor–critic methods used for RLHF while maintaining the same optimal solution class under the KL constraint.

# The Implicit Reward and Reward-Class Equivalence

DPO’s theoretical foundation rests on an equivalence relation among reward functions: two rewards \( r \) and \( r' \) are equivalent if \( r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x) \) for some function \( f \). Under Bradley–Terry or, more generally, Plackett–Luce models, equivalent rewards induce the same preference distribution because preference probabilities depend only on differences of rewards within the same prompt \( x \). Furthermore, within the KL-regularized objective, equivalent rewards induce the same optimal policy. Therefore, for policy optimization, we need only recover any representative of the correct reward class.

DPO chooses a canonical representative of each equivalence class by projecting rewards onto the subspace where the partition function matches that induced by the policy-to-reference ratio:
\[
r(x,y) \ = \ \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
\]
A constructive argument shows that for any reward \( r \), subtracting \( \beta \log Z(x) \) yields a reward in the same equivalence class that satisfies the above form, with \( \pi = \pi_{r} \) being the optimal policy corresponding to \( r \). Thus, DPO’s reparameterization does not restrict the set of reward classes that can be represented; it simply selects a particular representative for which the optimal policy is analytically tractable. Because the Bradley–Terry likelihood is optimized exactly in this parameterization, \( \pi_{\theta} \) trained with \( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \) recovers the optimal policy without requiring reinforcement learning.

# Practical Training: Reference Policies and Offline Preferences

In practice, preference datasets are collected by sampling pairs of completions from a supervised fine-tuned model \( \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}} \) and labeling which completion is preferred. DPO leverages this by using \( \pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}} \) whenever available. When the original reference is not available, one can train a surrogate \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions. This mitigates distribution shift between the unknown reference used to generate the dataset and the model used to compute policy ratios during DPO training.

The entire DPO pipeline comprises preparing the offline dataset \( \mathcal{D} \) of pairwise preferences and then optimizing \( \pi_{\theta} \) to minimize \( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \). Notably, the procedure requires neither reward model fitting nor reinforcement learning. The only hyperparameter directly controlling conservativeness is \( \beta \), which scales the policy-ratio logits in the classification loss.

# Stability Advantages Over Actor–Critic Approaches

DPO’s reparameterization also clarifies a source of instability in actor–critic methods like PPO when used for RLHF. When optimizing
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}} \left[ r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \right]
\]
up to an additive normalization term, the gradient variance can be high if the normalization (which corresponds to a soft value function) is not accurately estimated. Implementations often introduce reward baselines or learn value functions to stabilize training, but these introduce additional complexity and potential sources of error. By contrast, DPO’s loss corresponds to a maximum likelihood objective over observed pairs that requires no baselines and no policy-gradient sampling, thereby improving stability. Because DPO and PPO optimize the same KL-regularized reward maximization problem, DPO achieves comparable or superior solutions while using only supervised learning machinery.

# Empirical Evidence: Performance Without Reinforcement Learning

Empirical results demonstrate that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF across multiple tasks while being simpler and more computationally efficient. In controlled sentiment generation, where the true reward is known, DPO attains a superior reward–KL frontier relative to PPO, even surpassing PPO variants that optimize against the ground-truth reward. This directly validates that DPO optimizes the underlying KL-regularized objective effectively without reinforcement learning.

In summarization on the Reddit TL;DR dataset, DPO achieves higher win rates against references than PPO under comparable conditions, with substantially less sensitivity to sampling temperature. In single-turn dialogue using the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset, DPO improves over baselines that lack reinforcement learning and performs comparably to computationally intensive “best of \( N \)” selection strategies, all while avoiding PPO’s training complexities. These results confirm that DPO’s supervised learning approach, grounded in closed-form policy extraction and preference likelihood, is sufficient to reach or exceed the performance of reinforcement learning pipelines in these settings.

# Conclusion: Why DPO Makes Reinforcement Learning Unnecessary

The RLHF workflow traditionally relies on reinforcement learning to optimize a policy under a KL constraint with respect to a reference model, following a separate reward modeling phase. DPO shows that, under widely used preference models such as Bradley–Terry, the optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective admits a closed-form relation to the reward. This relation can be inverted to express preference probabilities directly in terms of policy-to-reference ratios, eliminating the partition function and enabling a simple maximum-likelihood objective over pairwise preferences.

As a result, DPO replaces reinforcement learning with supervised learning on a classification loss that operates on observed preference pairs. The language model itself implicitly parameterizes the reward through \( \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \), which faithfully represents the relevant equivalence class of rewards for policy optimization. Empirically, this approach is stable, lightweight, and performant, yielding equal or superior results to reinforcement learning-based methods while dramatically simplifying the training pipeline. Hence, once DPO extracts the optimal policy in closed form and trains with a simple classification loss, the reinforcement learning component becomes unnecessary.
