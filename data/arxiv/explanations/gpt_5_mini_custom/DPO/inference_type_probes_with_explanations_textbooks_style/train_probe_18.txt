Chapter: When the Optimal Policy Matches the Reference: Rewards up to Equivalence

# Background: Preferences, Rewards, and KL-Constrained Optimization

Large language models are often adapted to human preferences by first modeling those preferences with a latent reward function and then optimizing the model to maximize expected reward while remaining close to a trusted reference policy. In the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) pipeline, this optimization is formalized as a constrained objective that balances reward and divergence from a reference model. In the formulation reviewed by the paper, the language model policy is optimized according to the objective
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ is a learned reward function compatible with preference data and $\beta$ controls the strength of the Kullback–Leibler divergence penalty relative to reward maximization. This KL constraint is essential in practice for preventing the model from deviating too far from the distribution where the reward model is accurate and for maintaining diversity.

A key insight of the paper is that the optimal solution to this constrained objective admits a closed-form characterization for any given reward function. This characterization enables a change of variables: instead of explicitly fitting a reward model and then optimizing an RL objective, one can directly fit the policy that would be optimal under the preference model. This is the foundation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which replaces reinforcement learning with a simple maximum likelihood objective over preference-labeled pairs.

# The Optimal Policy and Its Reparameterization

The paper presents the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization objective. For any reward function $r(x,y)$, the optimal policy takes the exponential tilt form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\right),
\]
where the partition function
\[
Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\right)
\]
ensures normalization for each prompt $x$. This expression shows that the optimal policy is obtained by reweighting the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ with exponentiated reward values, followed by renormalization.

The same relation can be inverted to express the reward function in terms of the corresponding optimal policy:
\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]
This reparameterization reveals that, up to the additive term $\beta \log Z(x)$ that depends only on $x$, reward can be represented by the log-ratio of the optimal policy and the reference policy scaled by $\beta$. This identity is central to DPO: by modeling preferences directly in terms of the optimal policy, one can avoid fitting a standalone reward model while still remaining fully compatible with preference models such as Bradley–Terry.

# Preference Models and Reward Equivalence

Preference datasets are commonly modeled by the Bradley–Terry (BT) framework, in which the probability of preferring one completion $y_1$ to another $y_2$ given $x$ depends on the difference in their rewards:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)} = \sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Because only reward differences matter, the model is invariant to adding any function of $x$ to the reward. The paper formalizes this invariance by introducing an equivalence relation over rewards: two reward functions $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ are equivalent if $r(x,y)-r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$. Under both Plackett–Luce and Bradley–Terry preference frameworks, rewards from the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution.

This equivalence has two important consequences. First, it captures a well-known under-specification in preference models: absolute reward levels are not identifiable and need to be fixed up to a function of $x$ to ensure identifiability. Second, and crucially for optimization, rewards from the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective. Thus, for the purposes of learning a policy, it suffices to recover any representative of the equivalence class consistent with the preferences.

The paper further shows that every reward class consistent with the Plackett–Luce/Bradley–Terry models can be represented via the reparameterization
\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and given reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$. This representation selects a canonical member of each equivalence class by enforcing that the induced policy is a valid probability distribution, which in turn makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

# When $\pi^*(y\mid x)$ Equals $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$

We now analyze the special case highlighted by the fact: if the optimal policy equals the reference policy, what must be true of the reward?

Suppose $\pi^*(y\mid x) = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ for all $y$ and $x$. By the closed-form optimality condition,
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r^*(x,y)\right).
\]
Equating both sides and cancelling $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ wherever it is nonzero yields
\[
1 = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r^*(x,y)\right).
\]
This implies
\[
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r^*(x,y)\right) = Z(x),
\]
which is a quantity that depends on $x$ but not on $y$. Taking logarithms gives
\[
r^*(x,y) = \beta \log Z(x),
\]
so $r^*(x,y)$ is a function of $x$ only. In other words, whenever the optimal policy coincides with the reference policy, the reward function must be constant in $y$ for each fixed $x$.

This conclusion holds “up to equivalence” as defined by the paper. Since adding any function of $x$ to a reward leaves preferences and the optimal policy unchanged, any reward that differs from an $x$-only reward by an additive function of $x$ is equivalent. Therefore, the condition “$\pi^* = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$” implies that the reward lies in the equivalence class of functions that depend only on $x$. Equivalently, relative rewards across completions $y$ vanish, and the exponential tilt reduces to a uniform scaling of the reference policy that is absorbed by normalization.

# Intuition: Why $y$-Independent Rewards Preserve the Reference

The optimal policy expression shows that deviations from the reference are driven entirely by the relative weighting $\exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$ across different $y$ for a given $x$. If $r(x,y)$ depends on $y$, the tilt selectively strengthens or weakens certain completions, shifting mass away from the reference proportions. If $r(x,y)$ is constant in $y$ (for each $x$), then all completions are scaled by the same factor, and the renormalization cancels that factor. The policy remains exactly $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

From the perspective of preference models, a reward that depends only on $x$ produces zero differences $r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)$ for any pair $(y_1,y_2)$ at the same prompt $x$. Under Bradley–Terry, such a reward yields a preference probability of $\sigma(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, i.e., complete indifference between any pair of completions drawn from the reference. In that situation, there is no signal to move the policy away from the reference, and the KL constraint keeps it exactly in place.

Thus, “reward up to equivalence being a function of $x$” precisely characterizes when the optimal KL-constrained policy equals the reference, and it aligns with both the exponential tilt intuition and the invariances of the preference model.

# Implications for Direct Preference Optimization

Direct Preference Optimization exploits the mapping between rewards and policies to optimize preferences directly as a function of the policy. In DPO, the implicit reward defined by the policy and the reference is
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
Under this parameterization, a policy identical to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ corresponds to $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)$ being a function of $x$ only. Indeed, plugging $\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ into the definition produces $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log 1 = 0$, an $x$-only function after accounting for the equivalence relation. Consequently, the DPO objective will have no gradient signal to change the relative probabilities across $y$ at a given $x$ unless the observed preferences indicate differences that the current implicit reward fails to capture.

Moreover, the DPO gradient emphasizes correcting the ordering of completions when the implicit reward misranks a preferred and dispreferred pair. If the reward is $y$-independent, the implicit ordering is flat, and all observed preferences in the dataset will drive the policy away from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by increasing the likelihood of preferred responses and decreasing the likelihood of dispreferred ones. Conversely, if preference data indicate indifference across completions, the DPO loss is minimized by staying at the reference policy with an $x$-only implicit reward.

This analysis reinforces the core idea: the policy and reward are tightly coupled under the KL-constrained objective. When the policy equals the reference, the associated reward class collapses to functions of $x$; when preference data reveal $y$-dependent differences, DPO adjusts the policy to encode those differences via the implicit reward, guided by the Bradley–Terry likelihood.

# Conclusion

The paper establishes a closed-form relationship between rewards and optimal policies under a KL constraint and leverages it to train policies directly from preferences. Within this framework, if the optimal policy equals the reference policy, then the reward must, up to the paper’s equivalence relation, be a function of $x$ only. This follows immediately from the exponential tilt representation: any $y$-dependent reward would produce non-uniform tilts and thus deviate the optimal policy from the reference. The equivalence relation formalizes that adding an $x$-dependent term to reward does not affect preferences or the optimal policy, so the entire class of $x$-only rewards corresponds precisely to the case where $\pi^* = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
