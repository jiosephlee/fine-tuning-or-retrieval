Constrained Reward Maximization and the Role of the KL Penalty in DPO

# Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) introduces a simple and stable procedure for aligning language models to human preferences, avoiding explicit reinforcement learning loops. DPO rests on a central constrained optimization problem that balances two competing forces: increasing the reward assigned to model outputs that agree with human preferences, and maintaining proximity to a reference policy through a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty. This chapter explains how that balance is controlled by the parameter $\beta$, and why, in the limit of extremely large $\beta$, the learned policy $\pi_{\theta}$ converges to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, even if the reward model were perfectly accurate. We will build intuition for this outcome by reviewing the RLHF formulation, the closed-form optimal policy under the KL constraint, and the DPO reparameterization that connects preferences to policies directly.

# The RLHF Objective and Its KL Constraint

The standard formulation used for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) optimizes a policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ to maximize expected reward while penalizing deviations from a fixed reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$. The optimization problem is given by
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \ y \sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] \ - \ \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \, \| \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is a learned reward function intended to reflect human preferences and $\beta > 0$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. The KL term anchors the optimized policy to the reference policy, which is typically the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model that provides sensible behavior for the task domain. Intuitively, a larger $\beta$ makes deviations from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ more costly, thereby constraining exploration of high-reward completions that would push the model too far from the reference distribution.

The balance set by $\beta$ has concrete consequences. If $\beta$ is small, the optimization can aggressively chase reward, making large changes to the policy even if those changes depart from the behavior supported by the reference model. If $\beta$ is large, the optimization becomes conservative, preferring responses close to the reference policy even at the expense of reward. The central question is how this balance manifests in the exact form of the optimal policy, and what happens in limiting cases of $\beta$.

# The Closed-Form Optimal Policy Under KL-Constrained Reward Maximization

The RLHF objective admits a closed-form optimal solution when the reward function is fixed. Specifically, the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem is
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x, y)\right),
\]
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. This expression gives an explicit multiplicative adjustment to the reference policy, modulated by the exponentiated reward scaled by $1/\beta$. The reference policy serves as a baseline distribution, and the reward tilts it toward preferred outcomes. When the reward is positive for certain completions $y$, the ratio $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x, y)\right)$ boosts their probability relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$. When the reward is negative, the ratio diminishes their probability. The magnitude of this adjustment depends on $\frac{1}{\beta}$, which reveals how the KL penalty parameter $\beta$ controls the sensitivity of the optimized policy to the reward.

This formulation immediately yields intuition for extreme regimes. If $\beta$ is small, then $\frac{1}{\beta}$ is large, so $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ can significantly amplify or attenuate probabilities relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, enabling strong reward-seeking behavior. Conversely, if $\beta$ is large, then $\frac{1}{\beta}$ is small, and the exponential factor approaches $1$ for all $y$, meaning the reward contributes only a very mild tilt. In the limit of $\beta \to \infty$, the exponential factor becomes exactly $1$, and the partition function becomes $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) = 1$, leaving $\pi_{r}(y \mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$. Thus, regardless of how accurate or strong the reward is, a sufficiently large KL penalty forces the optimized policy to coincide with the reference policy.

# The DPO Reparameterization: From Rewards to Policies

DPO leverages the closed-form optimal policy to avoid explicit reinforcement learning. Starting from the optimal policy expression,
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x, y)\right),
\]
we take logarithms and rearrange to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
r(x, y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \ + \ \beta \, \log Z(x).
\]
Crucially, in preference modeling with Bradley–Terry (and more generally Plackett–Luce), only differences in rewards matter. Substituting the reparameterized rewards into the Bradley–Terry model cancels the partition function term, yielding a preference model that depends only on $\pi_{r}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \, \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2} \mid x)} \ - \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1} \mid x)}\right)}.
\]
This allows us to fit a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ directly to the preference data via a maximum likelihood objective, without explicitly modeling rewards:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) \ = \ -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log \sigma \!\left( \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w} \mid x)} \ - \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l} \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l} \mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
Here, $\sigma$ is the logistic function, and $(y_{w}, y_{l})$ denote preferred and dispreferred completions for prompt $x$. DPO thus trains the policy to increase the relative log probability of preferred responses against the reference, with a weighting induced by $\beta$.

This reparameterization can be viewed as selecting a representative from each equivalence class of rewards that differ only by an $x$-dependent additive constant. Under mild assumptions, any reward class consistent with the Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce models can be represented as
\[
r(x, y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)},
\]
for some policy $\pi(y \mid x)$. Consequently, optimizing the DPO objective learns a policy whose implicit reward matches the preference data, while remaining anchored to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ through the ratio $\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ scaled by $\beta$.

# Intuition for the Limit $\beta \to \infty$

The parameter $\beta$ appears in two intimately related places: the KL penalty in the RLHF objective and, via the closed-form solution, the scaling factor $\frac{1}{\beta}$ inside the exponential tilt of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Both perspectives lead to the same conclusion about the behavior of the optimized policy as $\beta$ becomes extremely large.

From the optimization perspective, a large $\beta$ enforces a strong penalty on deviations from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The optimizer can still gain reward by adjusting probabilities of certain completions, but any adjustment incurs a proportional KL cost multiplied by $\beta$. As $\beta \to \infty$, even small changes in the policy become prohibitively expensive, making the optimal choice to avoid changes entirely. Hence, the solution collapses to the reference policy.

From the closed-form policy perspective, the optimized policy is
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ \propto \ \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x, y)\right).
\]
If we imagine the reward function to be perfectly accurate and potentially large in magnitude, its influence on the policy is nonetheless filtered by $\frac{1}{\beta}$. As $\beta$ grows, the ratio $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ approaches $1$ uniformly over $y$, which neutralizes the reward tilt. The partition function in that limit reduces to $\sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) = 1$, leaving
\[
\lim_{\beta \to \infty} \ \pi_{r}(y \mid x) \ = \ \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x).
\]
This limit holds regardless of the accuracy or magnitude of the reward, because the scaling by $\frac{1}{\beta}$ suppresses the reward’s impact. In other words, the KL constraint can dominate the optimization so thoroughly that perfect reward accuracy becomes irrelevant to the final policy.

# Practical Implications for DPO Training

DPO’s training objective incorporates $\beta$ in the same way the RLHF objective does, but with an important simplification: the policy is trained by a binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs, avoiding sampling in the loop or fitting an explicit standalone reward model. Nevertheless, the fundamental trade-off controlled by $\beta$ remains the same. A small $\beta$ permits the model to significantly increase the relative log probability of preferred responses against the reference, potentially drifting farther from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as the learned policy prioritizes reward. A large $\beta$ attenuates those changes by shrinking the effective step size in the ratio $\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$, thereby keeping the learned policy close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

This trade-off is visible in experiments that characterize the reward–KL frontier. Algorithms that optimize the same constrained objective will trace a curve of achievable rewards versus KL divergence. DPO produces an efficient frontier, achieving high rewards at modest KL divergences, and strictly dominates PPO-based baselines in controlled settings. However, as $\beta$ increases, all methods face an inherent ceiling: the KL penalty suppresses the ability to deviate from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In the extreme, when $\beta$ is sufficiently large, the optimized policy will mirror $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ regardless of how well the reward captures human preferences. This is not a failure of reward accuracy, but rather an expected consequence of the optimization problem’s structure.

# Summary

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework central to RLHF and DPO encodes a principled trade-off between reward and fidelity to a reference policy. The closed-form optimal solution shows that the learned policy is a tilted version of the reference policy, where the tilt is governed by $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$. As $\beta$ grows large, this tilt vanishes, forcing the optimized policy to converge to the reference, even under a perfectly accurate reward model. DPO exploits this structure to fit policies directly from preferences with a simple maximum likelihood objective, while preserving the same control over the reward–KL trade-off. Understanding the limiting behavior of $\beta$ clarifies how to select it in practice: smaller values encourage reward-driven changes, while larger values emphasize stability and adherence to the reference policy.
