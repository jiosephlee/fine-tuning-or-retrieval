Chapter: KL Regularization in Direct Preference Optimization and Reward-Maximizing Policy Learning

# Introduction: Preference Learning and the Need for Control

Large language models acquire broad knowledge during unsupervised pre-training, but translating this knowledge into consistently desirable behaviors requires an additional alignment step. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) addresses this by learning from relative human preferences over model outputs. However, simply maximizing a learned reward can cause a policy to drift far from the behavior of a strong initial model, degrading diversity and leaving the regime where the learned reward is accurate. To counteract this, RLHF augments the reward objective with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to keep the policy close to a reference policy.

The central fact in this chapter is that, in the RL objective
\[
\max_{\pi} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi(y\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
the KL term conceptually acts to keep the learned policy near a fixed reference policy. This prevents excessive deviation from the distributional support where the reward is reliable and avoids degenerate behaviors such as mode collapse. We will unpack this idea thoroughly, connecting it to a closed-form characterization of the optimal policy, the derivation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and the observed reward–KL trade-offs in practice.

# The KL-Constrained RL Objective

The standard RLHF pipeline first fits a reward model from human preferences and then optimizes a policy to maximize this reward subject to a KL penalty from a reference policy. The reference is typically a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model. Formally, the objective is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
as given in Eq. (4). Here, \(\beta>0\) balances reward maximization against proximity to the reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

Intuitively, the KL term discourages the policy from drifting too far from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), which is crucial for two reasons highlighted in the paper. First, it maintains the distributional proximity on which the learned reward \(r_{\phi}\) is accurate; without it, the policy could exploit reward model idiosyncrasies or extrapolate into regions where the reward is unreliable. Second, it helps preserve generation diversity and prevents collapse to a few high-reward strings. Thus, the KL term operationalizes “stay near what already works,” allowing controlled improvement over a strong baseline.

# The Optimal Policy under KL Regularization

A key property of the KL-regularized objective is that its optimal solution admits a closed-form expression. Specifically, for any reward function \(r\), the optimizer of the KL-constrained objective takes the form (Eq. (5)):
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right),
\]
where
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right)
\]
is a partition function that depends only on the prompt \(x\).

This expression makes the role of the KL penalty explicit. The learned policy is a multiplicative tilt of the reference policy by an exponential factor of the reward scaled by \(1/\beta\). When \(\beta\) is large, the tilt is weak and \(\pi_{r}\) stays close to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). When \(\beta\) is small, the reward term dominates and \(\pi_{r}\) concentrates on high-reward regions, potentially moving far from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Thus, \(\beta\) directly tunes the strength of the KL-induced pull toward the reference.

Taking logs and rearranging yields an equivalent reparameterization of the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference (Eq. (6)):
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Although \(\log Z(x)\) is intractable to compute exactly, it cancels in preference models that depend only on reward differences, a fact that DPO exploits.

# From Rewards to Policies: Preference Models and the DPO Reparameterization

Human preference data is often modeled by the Bradley–Terry (BT) framework, which posits that the probability that \(y_{1}\) is preferred to \(y_{2}\) given prompt \(x\) is
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))+\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))} \;=\; \sigma\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\]
as in Eq. (1). Because only reward differences matter, the BT model is invariant to adding any function of \(x\) to the reward, a property that later underpins the DPO reparameterization.

Substituting the reparameterized form of \(r^{*}\) from Eq. (6) into the BT model cancels the partition function and yields a preference probability expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) and the reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) (Eq. (7)):
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\right)}.
\]
This identity enables a change of variables: instead of fitting a reward model and then running RL, one can directly fit a policy to maximize the likelihood of preferences under this induced model. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) defines a loss over a parameterized policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) by replacing \(\pi^{*}\) with \(\pi_{\theta}\) (Eq. (8)):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
\;=\;
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\log \sigma\!\left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\right)
\right].
\]
Here, the same \(\beta\) that governed the KL strength in the RL objective now scales a policy-logit margin relative to the reference. Thus, the KL term’s conceptual role—keeping the policy near \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)—is inherited in DPO via the implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
which is precisely the KL-induced reparameterization of reward differences.

# The Role of the KL Term and the Parameter \(\beta\)

The KL penalty enters the RL objective as \(-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}]\). Conceptually, this term enforces a proximity constraint that regularizes the learned policy toward the reference. The closed-form solution in Eq. (5) shows how this happens: the policy is formed by re-weighting \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) with \(\exp(r/\beta)\). In the limit of very large \(\beta\), the exponential factor approaches 1 and \(\pi_{r}\) approaches \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). In the regime of moderate \(\beta\), the policy leans toward higher-reward outputs but remains anchored by the reference distribution. In the small-\(\beta\) limit, the reference anchor weakens and the policy can concentrate on high-reward modes, risking degeneracy if the reward is mis-specified.

This trade-off is visible in the experimental analysis, where policies are evaluated along a frontier of achieved reward versus KL divergence from the reference policy. In the controlled sentiment setting, DPO produces an efficient reward–KL frontier that strictly dominates PPO, even when PPO has access to ground-truth rewards. These results illustrate that properly balancing reward improvement against KL proximity yields policies that achieve high reward without incurring excessive divergence from the reference, aligning with the conceptual role of the KL term.

In DPO, \(\beta\) plays the same conceptual role through the log-probability ratio relative to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The DPO loss increases the relative log probability of preferred completions over dispreferred ones, but the strength and scaling of these updates are governed by \(\beta\). Thus, the “stay near reference” effect is preserved: even though DPO eliminates the explicit RL inner loop, it still optimizes an objective whose optimal solution corresponds to a KL-regularized reward maximizer with respect to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

# Mechanistic View: The DPO Gradient and Stability

The gradient of the DPO loss clarifies how the KL-induced structure yields stable, preference-aligned updates. Writing
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) with respect to \(\theta\) can be expressed as
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
=
-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))
\left(\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\right)
\right].
\]
This update increases the likelihood of preferred completions and decreases that of dispreferred ones. Crucially, the examples are weighted more when the model’s implicit reward ordering is incorrect, as measured by \(\sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\). This importance weighting is scaled by \(\beta\), linking the strength of corrective updates to the same parameter that controls KL proximity in the original RL objective.

The paper notes that a naive probability-ratio objective without this dynamic weighting can cause degeneration, whereas the DPO weighting mitigates such issues. This provides an additional, mechanistic perspective on how the KL-induced reparameterization stabilizes learning: it ties the magnitude of updates to the margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs relative to the reference, rather than pushing probabilities unconditionally.

# Reward Equivalence Classes and Exact Recovery of the Optimal Policy

The Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models are under-specified up to additive functions of \(x\). Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) that differ by a function of \(x\) induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective. This observation motivates a reparameterization that selects a canonical representative within each equivalence class.

Theorem 1 formalizes that, under mild assumptions, any equivalence class of rewards consistent with Plackett–Luce preferences contains a representative of the form
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
for some policy \(\pi\) and given reference \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The proof constructs a projection that subtracts \(\beta\log Z(x)\), where \(Z(x)\) is the partition function from Eq. (5), yielding exactly the reparameterized form. This shows that the DPO parameterization does not restrict the class of representable rewards and that optimizing the induced preference likelihood over \(\pi\) can recover the optimal KL-regularized policy without ever computing \(Z(x)\). Thus, the KL term’s conceptual and mathematical role is preserved in DPO through the chosen reward–policy mapping.

# Actor–Critic Instabilities and the Normalization Term

Optimizing the KL-regularized objective with actor–critic methods such as PPO involves estimating baselines or value functions to control variance. Rearranging the optimization as minimizing \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi^{*}]\) yields (Eq. (10)):
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\Bigl[
r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta \log \sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr)
- \beta \log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\Bigr].
\]
The middle term can be interpreted as a soft value function of the reference policy. While it does not change the optimal solution, omitting or poorly estimating it can lead to high-variance gradients and unstable learning in practice. Prior approaches approximate this normalization via learned value functions or baselines such as human completion baselines.

DPO sidesteps this difficulty by choosing a reparameterization in which the problematic partition function cancels under the Bradley–Terry preference model. The result is a simple, stable binary cross-entropy objective in policy space that implicitly retains the KL-regularized structure through the \(\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\) terms.

# Practical Pipeline and the Reference Policy

DPO follows a simple pipeline. One gathers an offline dataset of preference pairs \((x,y_{w},y_{l})\) via sampling from a reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) and labeling preferences. One then optimizes \(\pi_{\theta}\) to minimize \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\). When an SFT model \(\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}\) exists, it serves naturally as \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). If not, the paper proposes fitting \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions, which helps mitigate distribution shift between the true but unavailable sampling distribution and the working reference model used in DPO.

In all cases, the conceptual function of the KL term—keeping the learned policy near the distribution on which preferences and rewards are reliable—is realized by anchoring the optimization around \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Even though DPO does not explicitly compute a KL penalty during training, its objective is derived from the same KL-regularized optimum and encodes proximity to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) through the log-ratio structure.

# Empirical Reward–KL Trade-offs

The paper evaluates methods along the frontier of achieved reward versus KL divergence from the reference in a controlled sentiment task where the ground-truth reward is available. DPO attains the most efficient reward–KL frontier, outperforming PPO, including a PPO variant with access to the ground-truth reward. These results corroborate the theoretical picture: anchoring to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) via the KL structure enables improved reward while maintaining proximity, and DPO’s reparameterization makes this trade-off easier to optimize. In summarization and dialogue, DPO achieves strong win rates against baselines while being robust to sampling temperature, again reflecting controlled improvements over the reference behavior without excessive drift.

# Conclusion

The KL term in the RLHF objective serves a crucial conceptual and practical role: it keeps the learned policy near a reference policy, preserving diversity and maintaining trust in the reward model’s validity. This role is made precise by the closed-form optimal policy, which exponentially tilts the reference distribution by the reward scaled by \(1/\beta\). Direct Preference Optimization leverages a reparameterization that absorbs this KL structure into a policy-only objective, replacing the RL loop with a stable, tractable classification loss while preserving the desired proximity to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Theoretical analysis shows no loss of generality in representable rewards or optimal policies, and empirical results demonstrate superior reward–KL trade-offs. In sum, the KL term is not merely a regularizer; it is the mechanism that ties reward maximization to controlled, reliable policy improvement, a property that DPO retains in a simplified and effective training framework.
