Chapter Title: Understanding DPO’s Preference Probabilities and the 50% Baseline

# From Preferences to Policies: The RLHF Setup
Language models trained with large-scale unsupervised objectives exhibit broad capabilities but lack reliable ways to steer their behavior toward specific, desirable responses. A widely used solution is reinforcement learning from human feedback, which fine-tunes a policy to align with relative human preferences. The conventional pipeline proceeds in three steps: supervised fine-tuning to obtain a reference model, training a reward model from pairwise human preferences using a preference model such as Bradley–Terry, and reinforcement learning to optimize the policy for high reward subject to a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence constraint that keeps the policy close to the reference distribution.

Formally, the reinforcement learning stage optimizes a policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ to maximize the expected learned reward while penalizing the KL divergence from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. This objective trades off reward maximization and conservatism with respect to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which in practice is the supervised fine-tuned model.

The conventional approach relies on a separate reward model $r_{\phi}$ trained from pairwise preferences $\bigl(x, y_{w}, y_{l}\bigr)$, where $y_{w}$ denotes the preferred completion and $y_{l}$ the dispreferred one. Under the Bradley–Terry preference model,
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x, y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x, y_{1})\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x, y_{2})\bigr)},
\]
the reward model is fit by maximizing the likelihood of observed preferences, yielding the well-known binary cross-entropy loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) \;=\; - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_{w}) - r_{\phi}(x, y_{l})\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
While effective, this pipeline introduces complexity and instability: one must fit a reward model and then solve a reinforcement learning problem, typically with approximate methods such as PPO.

# Optimal Policies and the Reward–Policy Bridge
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) removes explicit reinforcement learning by exploiting the analytic form of the optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective. It is straightforward to show that the optimal solution of the KL-constrained objective takes the Boltzmann form
\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x, y)\right),
\]
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. This expression exhibits a crucial structural relationship: the optimal policy is the reference policy reweighted by exponentiated reward.

Taking logarithms and rearranging isolates the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
This identity is the bridge that DPO uses to eliminate explicit reward modeling. Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on differences of rewards, the partition function term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels when taking differences $r(x, y_{1}) - r(x, y_{2})$. Consequently, DPO can express preference probabilities purely in terms of the policy–reference log-odds without ever computing $Z(x)$.

# The DPO Preference Model and Objective
Substituting the optimal-policy reparameterization into the Bradley–Terry model yields an expression for the human preference probability in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^{*}$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\right)}.
\]
This is precisely a logistic model over the difference of policy–reference log-odds. The DPO algorithm fits a parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ by maximum likelihood under this model, replacing $\pi^{*}$ by $\pi_{\theta}$:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}})
\;=\;
- \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\log \sigma\!\left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\right)
\right].
\]
Intuitively, DPO increases the relative log probability of preferred responses versus dispreferred ones, but does so through the lens of their deviation from the reference policy. The parameter $\beta$ modulates the strength of the KL regularization implicitly retained through the reparameterization.

The gradient of this loss elucidates the dynamics. Defining the implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)},
\]
the gradient takes the form
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}})
\;=\;
-\beta\,
\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l}) \sim \mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{w})\bigr)\,
\bigl(\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x) - \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr].
\]
Thus, the update increases the likelihood of $y_{w}$ and decreases that of $y_{l}$, weighted more heavily when the current policy ranks $y_{l}$ above $y_{w}$ in terms of the implicit reward $\hat{r}_{\theta}$. The reference policy appears throughout via the log-ratio, which stabilizes learning and encodes the preference model’s invariances.

# The 50% Preference Probability When Policy Equals Reference
The DPO preference probability reduces to a simple and revealing special case when the learned policy exactly matches the reference policy on a given pair. Consider the DPO preference model for a single comparison $(x, y_{w}, y_{l})$:
\[
p_{\theta}(y_{w} \succ y_{l} \mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}\right)}.
\]
Suppose that for this pair, $\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\text{ref}}$. Then each log-ratio equals zero:
\[
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)} \;=\; 0,
\qquad
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)} \;=\; 0.
\]
Consequently, the argument of the logistic function is zero, and we obtain
\[
p_{\theta}(y_{w} \succ y_{l} \mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{1 + \exp(0)}
\;=\;
\frac{1}{2}.
\]
Therefore, under DPO’s preference model, if the current policy does not deviate from the reference policy on the pair under consideration, the predicted probability that $y_{w}$ is preferred over $y_{l}$ is exactly $50\%$.

This result is a direct reflection of the reparameterized Bradley–Terry model’s reliance on differences in policy–reference log-odds. When the policy equals the reference, the implicit reward differences vanish because $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = 0$ for every $y$. The model is consequently indifferent between $y_{w}$ and $y_{l}$, and outputs the neutral probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

# Intuition: Neutrality at the Reference and Learning Signals
The equality $\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\text{ref}}$ can be interpreted as the policy having made no deviation from the baseline behavior encoded by the reference model. Because DPO measures preference probabilities only through the change from the reference, identical policies imply zero change in relative preferences across any pair. The Bradley–Terry structure mirrors this neutrality: the log-odds in favor of $y_{w}$ over $y_{l}$ collapses to zero, resulting in a symmetric $50\%$ prediction.

This symmetry has immediate implications for learning signals. In the DPO loss, pairs where the policy agrees with the reference yield a logistic input of zero and hence a probability of $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The gradient weighting term
\[
\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{w})\bigr)
\]
also becomes $\sigma(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ in this case. Thus, when the policy is identical to the reference on a given pair, the update neither strongly reinforces nor strongly penalizes either completion. In contrast, if the policy has drifted to favor $y_{l}$ over $y_{w}$ relative to the reference, then $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_{w})$ becomes positive, pushing the weighting closer to $1$ and generating a strong corrective signal. If the policy already agrees with the preference ordering and assigns higher implicit reward to $y_{w}$, the weighting moves toward $0$, diminishing the update.

This adaptive behavior arises from the DPO reparameterization, which retains the KL constraint implicitly by always measuring relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The $50\%$ baseline encapsulates the idea that, absent deviation from the reference, the model has no basis—within the DPO preference model—to favor one completion over the other.

# Why the Partition Function Cancels and What It Means
The fact that the partition function cancels in the DPO preference model is central to the simplicity of the method. In the optimal-policy expression,
\[
r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x),
\]
the additive term $\beta \log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ and not on $y$. Preference models like Bradley–Terry depend solely on reward differences $r(x, y_{1}) - r(x, y_{2})$, so this term disappears. As a result, DPO can write preference probabilities in terms of policy–reference log-odds without computing or approximating $Z(x)$, and without sampling from the policy during training.

This cancellation also clarifies the 50% neutrality. If $\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\text{ref}}$, then every implicit reward equals the same baseline function of $x$, namely $\beta \log Z(x)$, up to equivalence of rewards that add a function of $x$. When computing differences, these baselines cancel, making all pairs indistinguishable under the model and yielding the symmetric $\tfrac{1}{2}$ prediction.

# Broader Perspective Within DPO’s Theory
DPO leverages an equivalence relation over reward functions: adding any function $f(x)$ to a reward $r(x, y)$ produces an equivalent reward in the sense that it yields the same Bradley–Terry preferences and the same optimal KL-regularized policy. The DPO reparameterization selects a canonical representative of each equivalence class, namely one that satisfies
\[
r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)},
\]
thereby making the optimal policy analytically tractable. In that canonical form, the implicit reward differences become differences in policy–reference log-odds, and hence the special case $\pi = \pi_{\text{ref}}$ collapses all implicit reward differences to zero. The 50% preference probability is a precise statement of this canonical neutrality.

This perspective sheds additional light on why DPO avoids certain instabilities associated with actor–critic methods. In actor–critic formulations, normalizing terms that play the role of soft value functions must be estimated or controlled to stabilize gradients. By restricting attention to reward differences representable as policy–reference log-odds, DPO eliminates the need for explicit baselines or partition function estimation, while still recovering the same optimal solutions under the assumed preference model.

In summary, DPO’s preference probabilities are driven entirely by how much the policy’s probabilities diverge from those of the reference policy on the specific completions being compared. If there is no divergence, the model is indifferent, and the implied Bradley–Terry probability sits exactly at $50\%$.
