Chapter Title: The Role of β in Direct Preference Optimization and the Concentration of the Optimal Policy

# KL-Constrained Reward Maximization and the Optimal Policy

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is derived from the same constrained reward maximization objective that underpins reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The objective introduces a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to constrain a learned policy to remain close to a reference policy while maximizing reward. Formally, given a reward function $r(x,y)$ and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$, the objective is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta > 0$ is a coefficient controlling the strength of the KL divergence penalty with respect to the reference policy. The KL term is critical in practice: it prevents the learned policy from drifting too far from the distribution under which the reward is accurate and helps maintain diversity, avoiding collapse to a few very high-reward outputs.

A core result states that the optimal solution to this KL-constrained objective has the following closed form:
\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right),
\]
where the partition function is
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right).
\]
This expression enables direct analysis of how the learned policy trades off reward maximization and proximity to the reference policy through $\beta$.

# Reparameterizing Rewards in Terms of the Optimal Policy

The optimal policy expression can be rearranged to write the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
For preference learning under models such as Bradley–Terry, only reward differences between candidate outputs matter, so the unknown partition function $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels when comparing two outputs for the same prompt. This cancellation allows DPO to replace explicit reward modeling with a direct objective over policies while remaining consistent with the same preference model used in RLHF. In particular, DPO fits a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ by maximizing the likelihood of observed human preferences, where the implicit reward is
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
\]
Thus, the policy both realizes the learned behavior and implicitly encodes a reward consistent with the preference model.

# Interpreting β: The Strength of the KL Constraint

In the KL-constrained objective, $\beta$ modulates the balance between reward maximization and adherence to the reference policy. In the optimal policy,
\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right),
\]
$\beta$ scales the influence of the reward in the exponent. When $\beta$ is larger, the exponential factor $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$ varies more smoothly across outputs, so the reference policy exerts a stronger regularizing effect and the learned policy remains closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Conversely, when $\beta$ is smaller, differences in reward are amplified in the exponent, pushing the policy more aggressively toward outputs with higher reward.

This trade-off has two practical implications. First, a smaller $\beta$ allows the policy to prioritize reward maximization more strongly, potentially improving measured reward while increasing deviation from the reference. Second, an excessively small $\beta$ risks reducing diversity and can contribute to degeneration or mode collapse, motivating the inclusion of the KL term in RLHF and the per-example weighting that DPO introduces in its gradient to avoid naive, unstable updates.

# The Limit as β → 0: Concentration on the Highest-Reward Outputs

The optimal policy’s dependence on $\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)$ enables a precise characterization of the limit as $\beta \to 0$. Fix a prompt $x$ and consider any two outputs $y_1$ and $y_2$. The ratio of their probabilities under the optimal policy is
\[
\frac{\pi_r(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_r(y_2 \mid x)}
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y_1)\right)}
     {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y_2)\right)}
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)} \,
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, \bigl(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\bigr)\right).
\]
If $r(x,y_1) > r(x,y_2)$, then $r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2) > 0$, and as $\beta \to 0$, the exponential factor
\[
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, \bigl(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\bigr)\right) \to \infty.
\]
Hence the ratio $\pi_r(y_1 \mid x) / \pi_r(y_2 \mid x)$ grows without bound, regardless of the finite ratio $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)$. Symmetrically, if $r(x,y_1) < r(x,y_2)$, then the ratio goes to $0$ as $\beta \to 0$.

This pairwise comparison argument implies the following behavior. Let
\[
\mathcal{Y}_{\max}(x) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{y} \, r(x,y)
\]
be the set of outputs that achieve the maximum reward for prompt $x$. Then, as $\beta \to 0$, the policy $\pi_r(\cdot \mid x)$ places all of its probability mass on $\mathcal{Y}_{\max}(x)$. If the maximizer is unique, the policy concentrates on that single output. If there are multiple maximizers, the policy concentrates on that set, with the partition function normalizing the probabilities within the set. In all cases, the mass assigned to any output with strictly lower reward than the maximum tends to $0$.

To see this more explicitly, write the optimal policy as
\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)}{\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y' \mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y')/\beta\bigr)}.
\]
As $\beta \to 0$, the denominator is dominated by terms with the largest value of $r(x,y')$, and every term with lower reward becomes negligible relative to those maximizers. Therefore, probability mass concentrates on $\mathcal{Y}_{\max}(x)$, confirming that, in the small-$\beta$ limit, the optimal policy becomes concentrated on the highest-reward outputs.

# Implications for RLHF and DPO

Understanding the $\beta \to 0$ limit illuminates the role of the KL constraint and the stability properties discussed in the paper. Because a very small $\beta$ forces the policy to concentrate on the highest-reward outputs, it risks degeneration, such as reduced diversity or mode collapse. The paper underscores that the KL constraint is essential to prevent the model from deviating too far from the reference policy and to maintain diversity in generations. In practice, reinforcement learning implementations introduce baselines or value functions to stabilize updates, and DPO’s objective introduces a per-example weighting in the gradient that emphasizes corrections where the implicit reward ordering is incorrect. This weighting counteracts the instability that a naive, overly aggressive push toward higher-reward outputs would cause.

At the same time, the small-$\beta$ behavior clarifies the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference policy. Decreasing $\beta$ accentuates the influence of reward in the optimal policy, increasing the tendency to favor outputs that score highly under $r(x,y)$. Increasing $\beta$ softens that pressure, anchoring the policy more closely to the reference model. Because DPO optimizes the same KL-constrained objective in closed form via a classification-style loss, it inherits this trade-off without requiring an RL training loop. As a result, the choice of $\beta$ directly governs how sharply DPO’s learned policy prioritizes high-reward outputs relative to fidelity to the reference policy.

# Summary

The optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem has the form
\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right).
\]
This expression shows exactly how the coefficient $\beta$ balances reward exploitation against proximity to the reference policy. Analyzing the ratio of probabilities for two outputs reveals that, as $\beta \to 0$, the policy assigns vanishing probability to any output whose reward is strictly below the maximum, concentrating its mass on the set of highest-reward outputs. This limit behavior explains both the potential for degeneration at very small $\beta$ and the necessity of the KL constraint and stabilizing mechanisms highlighted in the paper. Through its reparameterization and preference-based likelihood objective, DPO optimizes this same trade-off directly and stably, while retaining the fundamental dependence of the learned policy on $\beta$.
