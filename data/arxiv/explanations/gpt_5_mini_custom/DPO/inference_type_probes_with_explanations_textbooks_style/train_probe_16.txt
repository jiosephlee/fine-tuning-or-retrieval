Chapter Title: The Role of Reward Differences and Partition Function Cancellation in Direct Preference Optimization

# Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is introduced as a simple, RL-free approach to training language models from human preferences. Its central insight is a change of variables that reparameterizes rewards in terms of the policy relative to a fixed reference model, allowing one to optimize a maximum likelihood objective over preferences without explicitly training a reward model or running reinforcement learning. A crucial algebraic step in this derivation relies on the fact that widely used preference models, such as the Bradley–Terry model, depend only on differences of rewards between two responses. This dependence causes a partition function term to cancel out, yielding an objective entirely in terms of the policy and the reference policy. In this chapter, we develop the intuition and mathematics behind this cancellation and explain why the derivation hinges on reward differences. We then consider a hypothetical alternative—preferences depending on absolute reward levels—and analyze why, under that assumption, the DPO simplification fails, specifically because the partition function would no longer cancel.

# RLHF Objectives, Preference Models, and the Partition Function

DPO starts from the RLHF setup, where one first fits a reward model from pairwise human preferences and then optimizes a language model to maximize expected reward while staying close to a reference policy via a KL constraint. Let the constrained optimization objective be
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, \ y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] \ - \ \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $r$ is a reward function, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference policy (often the supervised fine-tuned model), and $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL regularization. Under this objective, the optimal policy takes the form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right),
\]
with partition function
\[
Z(x) \ = \ \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right).
\]
This result expresses the optimal, KL-regularized policy in closed form in terms of the reward and the reference policy; however, the presence of $Z(x)$ makes direct use of this expression challenging. Estimating $Z(x)$ is computationally expensive in large, discrete output spaces typical of language models, which is one of the reasons RL-based optimization is commonly used in practice.

A central move in DPO is to rearrange the optimal-policy expression to solve for the reward in terms of the policy and the reference:
\[
r(x,y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \ + \ \beta \, \log Z(x).
\]
This reparameterization makes clear that the reward is only identifiable up to an additive, input-dependent constant $\beta \log Z(x)$. In general, without further structure, that constant remains an obstacle: it is unknown and depends on the entire output space via $Z(x)$. The key to eliminating it is how human preference models depend on rewards.

# Bradley–Terry Preferences Depend on Reward Differences

In the RLHF pipeline, human raters compare two candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)$ for a given prompt $x$ and select the preferred one. Under the Bradley–Terry model, these pairwise preferences are represented probabilistically in terms of reward differences:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \ = \ \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)} \ = \ \sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $r^*$ denotes the latent, true reward function that produces the preference distribution. This dependence on the difference $r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)$ is the structural property that enables DPO’s simplification.

Substituting the reward reparameterization
\[
r^*(x,y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \ + \ \beta \, \log Z(x)
\]
into the Bradley–Terry likelihood causes the partition function term to cancel:
\[
r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2) \ = \ \beta \left[\log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\right].
\]
The additive term $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both $r^*(x,y_1)$ and $r^*(x,y_2)$ and therefore vanishes when taking the difference. As a result, the preference probability can be written entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \, \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \, \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\]
This expression avoids the need to estimate $Z(x)$, providing a maximum likelihood objective over preferences that depends only on policy ratios and is thus practical to optimize directly:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) \ = \ - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \ \sigma \!\left(\beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \ - \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]
Here, the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$ consists of prompts $x$ and the human-preferred and dispreferred completions $(y_w, y_l)$. Optimizing this objective directly trains the policy, bypassing explicit reward modeling and policy optimization via RL.

# Why Cancellation Matters: Computational and Statistical Intuition

The cancellation of the partition function moves the problem from reward-space—where $Z(x)$ couples the entire output space—to policy-space ratios that are local to particular $(x,y)$ pairs. This yields a tractable objective that resembles a binary classification loss over preference pairs. Intuitively, the Bradley–Terry structure ensures that only relative quality matters to the preference likelihood. Any per-input additive shift to the reward function, such as $\beta \log Z(x)$, does not change the probability that one response is preferred over another. This is precisely the under-specification of rewards in the Plackett–Luce family: many reward functions differing by $f(x)$ induce the same preference distribution.

DPO leverages this under-specification constructively. By selecting the representative of the reward equivalence class given by
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
the algorithm imposes a constraint that makes the optimal policy analytically tractable. Because the Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on differences $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l)$, optimization can proceed with a simple cross-entropy loss, weighted in a way that emphasizes examples where the current implicit reward ranks the dispreferred response higher than the preferred one.

# A Hypothetical Shift: Preferences Depending on Absolute Rewards

Consider now a hypothetical variation in which human preferences depend on the absolute reward level $r(x,y)$, rather than on differences such as $r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)$. Under such a model, the likelihood of observing a preference would use $r(x,y)$ directly. Inserting the reward reparameterization
\[
r(x,y) \ = \ \beta \, \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \ + \ \beta \, \log Z(x)
\]
into a preference likelihood that depends on $r(x,y)$ alone would retain the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ inside the likelihood. Crucially, because there is no difference-taking operation, there is no cancellation. The partition function $Z(x)$ continues to appear, and it depends on a sum over all possible completions $y$ via
\[
Z(x) \ = \ \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right).
\]
This dependence reintroduces the core computational obstacle that DPO explicitly avoids: one must either estimate or otherwise handle $Z(x)$ to write down and optimize the likelihood. In large output spaces typical of language generation, that estimation is expensive and unstable, a reality that motivates RL-based optimization in standard RLHF.

Intuitively, when preferences depend on absolute rewards, the model must know the input-dependent reward baseline to correctly interpret the scale of $r(x,y)$. The $\beta \log Z(x)$ term exactly plays the role of a normalization constant for the optimal policy, and without cancellation, it cannot be factored out. Thus, the elegant reduction from reward modeling to direct policy optimization via a binary cross-entropy loss fails, because the objective cannot be expressed solely in terms of policy ratios. The simplification hinges on the Bradley–Terry structure; changing that structure to absolute, non-difference-based dependence blocks the path to DPO’s tractable objective.

# Implications for DPO’s Theory and Practice

Theoretical results in DPO highlight that reward functions are identifiable only up to an additive function of the input, and that equivalence classes of rewards under $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained RL objective. DPO’s projection selects a representative of each class of rewards that removes the unknown normalization by effectively embedding it into the policy ratio:
\[
f(r; \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)(x,y) \ = \ r(x,y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right) \ = \ \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This selection is useful precisely because the Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on differences; the cancellation keeps the optimization objective free of $Z(x)$.

If preferences depended on absolute reward levels, the projection would still define a valid representative, but the preference likelihood would not discard the $\beta \log Z(x)$ term. As a result, the maximum likelihood loss over preferences would involve $Z(x)$ explicitly, and the entire DPO derivation that yields a simple binary cross-entropy loss in terms of $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ would break down. One would face the same obstacles as in directly using the optimal policy form: either estimate $Z(x)$ or fall back on reinforcement learning procedures that do not require evaluating this partition function in closed form.

From a practical standpoint, the cancellation enabled by reward differences is what allows DPO to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight. It eliminates the need to sample from the policy in the loop, avoids the instability associated with estimating baselines for variance reduction, and produces gradients that increase the likelihood of preferred responses while decreasing that of dispreferred ones, weighted by how incorrectly the implicit reward currently ranks the pair. Without cancellation, these advantages would be lost, and the training pipeline would revert to the complexity of standard RLHF.

# Conclusion

DPO’s core derivation transforms a reward-modeling problem into direct policy optimization by exploiting a preference model that depends only on reward differences. This structure causes the partition function to cancel when expressing preference probabilities in terms of the policy and the reference policy. The resulting objective is a simple, tractable binary cross-entropy loss over pairwise preferences that avoids explicit reward modeling and RL. If, counterfactually, human preferences depended on absolute reward levels rather than differences, the cancellation would fail: the partition function would remain inside the likelihood, reintroducing the computational and statistical challenges that DPO is designed to circumvent. Thus, the dependence on reward differences is essential to the DPO simplification, and it underlies both the theoretical mapping between rewards and policies and the practical efficiency of the algorithm.
