Direct Preference Optimization and the Sample Inefficiency of PPO

# Introduction

Large language models trained with unsupervised objectives acquire broad capabilities, but steering their behavior toward desired responses requires a preference-learning stage. A widely adopted approach, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), fits a reward model from pairwise preference data and then optimizes a policy to maximize this reward subject to a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence constraint with respect to a reference policy. Although effective, this pipeline is complex and typically requires online sampling from the model during training, along with careful hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) uses a reparameterization of the reward that allows direct optimization of the policy from preferences with a simple classification loss, while implicitly solving the same KL-constrained reward maximization objective as RLHF.

A key empirical finding in the paper is that DPO outperforms proximal policy optimization (PPO) even when PPO is granted access to ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT). This result most directly indicates that PPO is sample-inefficient in this setting: despite optimizing the correct objective with the true reward function, PPO fails to reach the reward–KL frontier achieved by DPO. In this chapter, we develop the conceptual and mathematical foundations that explain this finding. We begin by detailing the RLHF objective and its optimal solution, then derive DPO’s loss via a change of variables that eliminates explicit reward learning and sampling. We analyze the mechanics of the DPO update, and connect actor–critic instability to PPO’s sample inefficiency. Finally, we interpret the empirical comparison on controlled sentiment generation, where DPO’s frontier strictly dominates PPO and PPO-GT, and discuss the implications.

# The RLHF Objective and Its Optimal Policy

The RLHF pipeline typically proceeds in three phases: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to obtain a capable base model, reward modeling from human preferences, and RL optimization to adjust the policy subject to a KL constraint. The constrained RL objective is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\,\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\bigr],
\]
where $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ is the learned reward function fit to preferences under a model such as Bradley–Terry, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a reference policy (e.g., the SFT model), and $\beta$ controls the penalty for deviating from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

A central analytical fact is that the optimal policy for this KL-constrained objective has the Boltzmann form
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right),
\]
with partition function
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right).
\]
Rewriting, the reward can be expressed as
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \,\log \frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \,\log Z(x).
\]
This representation reveals a bijective relationship (up to a normalization term in $x$) between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies under the KL constraint. However, naively using this relationship is intractable because computing $Z(x)$ is expensive. The insight behind DPO is that for preference models like Bradley–Terry, the partition function cancels when comparing two completions, enabling a direct maximum likelihood objective over policies.

# From Reward Models to Policy Optimization via DPO

Human preferences are commonly modeled by Bradley–Terry:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} \;=\; \sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1}) - r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $r^{*}$ denotes the latent ground-truth reward. Substituting the reparameterization
\[
r^{*}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \,\log \frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \,\log Z(x)
\]
into the Bradley–Terry model cancels the partition function, yielding
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left( \beta \,\log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \,\log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)} \right)}.
\]
This expression depends only on the optimal policy and the reference policy. Therefore, we can fit a parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ directly to preference data by maximum likelihood:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\left( \beta \,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
DPO thus optimizes the same KL-constrained reward maximization objective as RLHF, but without explicit reward modeling or an RL training loop. The policy network simultaneously represents the language model and an implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This change of variables preserves the class of representable reward functions under Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce. Specifically, rewards are only identifiable up to functions of $x$; two rewards differing by $f(x)$ induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy. DPO selects a representative within each equivalence class such that the induced policy is normalized, making the optimal policy analytically tractable.

# What the DPO Update Optimizes

To develop intuition, consider the gradient of the DPO objective:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[ \sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)\,\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x) \;-\; \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr) \right].
\]
The update raises the likelihood of preferred completions $y_{w}$ and lowers the likelihood of dispreferred completions $y_{l}$. Crucially, it scales each example by how incorrect the implicit reward ordering is, via $\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l}) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)$. When the model erroneously favors $y_{l}$ over $y_{w}$, the weight is high, emphasizing corrective updates; when the model already orders them correctly, the weight is low, preventing overcorrection and instability. This adaptive weighting, together with the KL structure encoded by $\beta$, is essential: naive probability-ratio objectives without this weighting can cause degeneration, whereas DPO remains stable and performant.

# Actor–Critic Instability and the Roots of Sample Inefficiency

PPO and related actor–critic methods optimize the same constrained objective, but they do so by sampling trajectories and estimating gradients using high-variance policy gradients. This process typically requires baselines or value functions to reduce variance, careful reward normalization, and multiple auxiliary losses to stabilize training. In the RLHF setting, one can view the normalization term
\[
-\beta\,\log \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r_{\phi}(x,y)\right)
\]
as a soft value function for the reference policy. While this term does not change the optimal solution, omitting or poorly estimating it leads to high-variance gradients. Practical implementations accommodate it with learned value functions or crude baselines (for instance, normalizing rewards using a single human completion baseline), both of which can be difficult to optimize reliably.

In contrast, the DPO reparameterization yields an implicit reward that does not require learned baselines or online sampling during fine-tuning. The partition function cancels in the Bradley–Terry likelihood, and the policy is trained with a simple binary cross-entropy objective over preference pairs. This analytic tractability removes variance-inducing components from the optimization loop, which are present in actor–critic pipelines. As a result, DPO is stable, computationally lightweight, and requires little hyperparameter tuning. These properties directly translate into more effective optimization of the reward–KL trade-off when measured empirically.

# Empirical Evidence: DPO’s Frontier Dominates PPO and PPO-GT

The most direct evidence of PPO’s sample inefficiency in this context comes from a controlled sentiment generation experiment. Here, the ground-truth reward is available from a sentiment classifier, allowing precise computation of each policy’s achieved reward and its KL divergence from the reference policy. The comparison evaluates a frontier of reward versus KL divergence by sweeping over conservativeness hyperparameters for each method and measuring the average sequence-level KL and average reward.

The results show that DPO produces by far the most efficient frontier: it achieves the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence, strictly dominating PPO’s frontier. Notably, DPO’s frontier also dominates PPO-GT, in which PPO optimizes with access to the true reward function. Since DPO and PPO optimize the same underlying KL-constrained objective, and PPO-GT is given the most favorable reward signal, the fact that PPO-GT underperforms DPO most directly indicates PPO’s sample inefficiency. Despite the availability of ground-truth rewards, PPO’s reliance on sampling, variance-prone gradients, and normalization challenges prevent it from efficiently navigating the reward–KL trade-off that DPO optimizes directly.

This finding is reinforced by broader experimental observations reported in the paper. DPO consistently matches or exceeds PPO on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue, often with greater robustness to sampling temperature and without extensive hyperparameter tuning. Moreover, DPO achieves similar or better performance than computationally demanding baselines like returning the best of $N$ sampled trajectories under a learned reward, while avoiding the impractical inference-time sampling cost. Together, these results demonstrate that DPO’s direct optimization yields policies that better satisfy preferences relative to their divergence from the reference policy, even compared to PPO equipped with ground-truth rewards.

# Interpreting “Sample Inefficiency” in This Context

“Sample inefficiency” refers to the inability of an algorithm to convert available training signal into effective policy updates without excessive sampling or variance. In the RLHF setting studied here, PPO’s sample inefficiency manifests as an inferior reward–KL frontier even when the reward model is perfect (ground truth). DPO outperforms PPO-GT because it eliminates the sources of variance and instability inherent in actor–critic optimization:

1. DPO’s change of variables reduces preference learning to maximum likelihood over the policy, avoiding an RL training loop altogether. The preference model depends only on differences of rewards, so the partition function cancels and no sampling is required during fine-tuning.

2. The implicit reward $\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ encodes the KL constraint directly into the loss, with adaptive per-example weights that focus updates where the model’s ordering is wrong. This mechanism reduces degeneracy and stabilizes training.

3. Actor–critic methods require estimating baselines or value functions to control gradient variance, and they rely on sampling to update both policy and critic. Even with true rewards, these estimators and sampling regimes introduce inefficiencies that degrade the reward–KL trade-off.

Given these factors, the empirical observation that DPO exceeds PPO-GT most directly indicates that PPO’s sampling and optimization apparatus is inefficient in this preference-learning regime, whereas DPO’s analytic reparameterization and simple classification objective translate preferences into effective policy updates.

# Implications and Outlook

The superiority of DPO over PPO, even with ground-truth rewards, has important implications for training language models from preferences. It demonstrates that adherence to human preferences in the KL-constrained RLHF framework can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs, without reinforcement learning or sampling in the training loop. This reduces computational complexity, eliminates instability associated with actor–critic baselines, and yields better reward–KL trade-offs in practice.

Beyond controlled sentiment generation, the paper reports that DPO matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue relative to PPO-based RLHF, with substantially simpler implementation and training. DPO is more robust to sampling temperature and requires minimal hyperparameter tuning. Initial evidence suggests that DPO generalizes well to new input distributions, outperforming PPO-trained policies on news summarization after being trained on forum posts. While more comprehensive study is warranted, these results support the conclusion that direct optimization from preferences is an effective and efficient alternative to RLHF with PPO.

In summary, the empirical finding that DPO outperforms PPO even with ground-truth rewards most directly indicates PPO’s sample inefficiency in this setting. The DPO framework explains why: by reparameterizing rewards in terms of policies and canceling intractable partition functions in pairwise comparisons, DPO transforms preference learning into stable, tractable maximum likelihood. This insight aligns with the theoretical under-specification of Plackett–Luce models and leverages the KL-constrained optimal policy’s structure, yielding a method that is both principled and practically superior.
