Chapter Title: Understanding the Normalization Term as a Soft Value Function in Direct Preference Optimization

# RLHF and the KL-Constrained Objective

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) typically optimizes a policy to maximize an estimated reward while constraining the policy not to drift far from a reference model. Concretely, the fine-tuning phase in RLHF is formulated as the following constrained optimization problem over a policy $\\pi_{\\theta}(y \\mid x)$:

\\[
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}}\\; \\mathbb{E}_{x\\sim \\mathcal{D},\\;y\\sim\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)}\\bigl[r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\bigr]\\;-\;\\beta\\,\\mathbb{D}_{\\mathrm{KL}}\\bigl[\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)\\mid\\mid\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\bigr].
\\]

Here, $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$ is a learned reward function fit from human preference data (often under the Bradley–Terry model), $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$ is a reference policy (commonly the supervised fine-tuned model), and $\\beta$ controls the strength of the KL-divergence regularization. This objective captures the central trade-off of RLHF: achieve high reward while remaining close to the distribution where the reward function is accurate and the model retains desirable diversity.

# The Optimal Policy and the Partition Function

A key observation is that the solution to the KL-constrained objective admits a closed form when the reward is fixed. The optimal policy $\\pi_{r}$ for any reward function $r(x,y)$ satisfies:

\\[
\\pi_{r}(y\\mid x) \\,=\\, \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\,\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x,y)\\right),
\\]

with the partition function

\\[
Z(x) \\,=\\, \\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x,y)\\right).
\\]

This form directly reflects the effect of the KL term: the optimal policy is an exponential tilting of the reference policy by the reward, tempered by $\\beta$, and normalized by $Z(x)$. Taking logarithms and rearranging yields an equivalent reparameterization of the reward in terms of the optimal policy:

\\[
r(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{r}(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)}\\; +\\; \\beta\\,\\log Z(x).
\\]

Crucially, $\\beta\\,\\log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$, not on $y$. This $x$-dependent term plays a central conceptual role later: it is the normalization needed to make $\\pi_{r}(\\cdot\\mid x)$ a valid probability distribution, and it will be identified with a soft value function of the reference policy.

# The Change of Variables and the DPO Objective

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) leverages the fact that preference models, such as Bradley–Terry, depend only on differences of rewards between completions. Under Bradley–Terry, the preference probability is

\\[
p^*(y_1 \\succ y_2 \\mid x) \\,=\\, \\sigma\\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\\,-\\,r^*(x,y_2)\\bigr),
\\]

where $\\sigma$ is the logistic function. Substituting the reparameterized rewards into the Bradley–Terry model, the $\\beta\\,\\log Z(x)$ term cancels because it is identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$. As a result, the preference likelihood can be expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$:

\\[
p^*(y_1\\succ y_2 \\mid x) \\,=\\, \\frac{1}{1\\,+\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi^*(y_2\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\\mid x)}\\,-\\,\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi^*(y_1\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\\mid x)}\\right)}.
\\]

This observation enables fitting a parametric policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ to preference data directly, without explicitly estimating a reward model or running a reinforcement learning loop. The DPO loss is the maximum likelihood objective over preferences:

\\[
\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}(\\pi_{\\theta};\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}) \\,=\\, -\\,\\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\\sim\\mathcal{D}}\\Bigl[\\log\\,\\sigma\\Bigl(\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\\mid x)}\\,-\\,\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\\mid x)}\\Bigr)\\Bigr].
\\]

DPO thus optimizes the same KL-constrained RLHF objective implicitly, but via a simple classification loss that increases the relative log-probability of preferred completions over dispreferred ones, with an importance weighting derived from the implicit reward.

# From Control-as-Inference to the Actor-Critic Objective

To analyze the behavior of actor-critic algorithms in RLHF, consider minimizing the KL-divergence to the optimal policy induced by a learned reward $r_{\\phi}$. This viewpoint leads to the following optimization objective for a parameterized policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$:

\\[
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}}\\; \\mathbb{E}_{\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)}\\Biggl[\\underbrace{r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\; -\\; \\beta\\,\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\\\phi}(x, y)\\right)}_{f(r_{\\phi},\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}},\\beta)}\\; -\\; \\underbrace{\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)}}_{\\text{KL term}}\\Biggr].
\\]

The term $f(r_{\\phi},\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}},\\beta)$ is precisely the reward composed with the normalization that enforces a valid partition function for the optimal policy tied to $r_{\\phi}$, as seen earlier. Importantly, the normalization component in $f(\\cdot)$ is

\\[
\\beta\\,\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\right),
\\]

which is the same $\\beta\\,\\log Z(x)$ term that appears in the optimal policy derivation. This term does not depend on the sampled action $y$, only on the state-like input $x$, and its role is fundamental to the stability and variance properties of actor-critic training.

# The Normalization Term as a Soft Value Function

Conceptually, the normalization term

\\[
\\beta\\,\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\right)
\\]

represents a soft value function of the reference policy $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$ at input $x$ under the learned reward $r_{\\phi}$. It aggregates the action-conditioned rewards $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$ by taking an expectation under a log-sum-exp form centered on $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$. The exponential weighting by $\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\phi}(x,y)\\right)$ and the tempering by $\\beta$ mirror how the optimal policy reweights the reference policy to prefer higher-reward completions, while preserving the constraint that probabilities remain normalized.

Intuitively, this soft value captures “how good,” in aggregate, the completions $y$ are under the reference policy for a given $x$, when measured by the learned reward and smoothed by the temperature $\\beta$. Since it depends only on $x$, it serves as a baseline-like quantity in the actor-critic objective: subtracting it from the instantaneous reward $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$ centers the objective and reduces variance in the policy gradient. In other words, the difference

\\[
r_{\\phi}(x,y)\\; -\\; \\beta\\,\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\right)
\\]

acts as a normalized advantage-like quantity, expressed in the DPO-equivalent reward class, that stabilizes learning by removing the $x$-dependent normalization needed to make the exponential tilting well-defined. Without this normalization, actor-critic updates can exhibit high variance and instability, especially when the reward scale or coverage of $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$ interacts poorly with the policy updates.

This interpretation connects directly to the earlier optimal policy derivation: the same partition function that ensures $\\pi_{r}(\\cdot\\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution also encapsulates the soft value of the reference policy at $x$. Thus, the normalization term both enforces proper normalization and provides a conceptual summary of the aggregate goodness of completions under $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$, tempered by $\\beta$.

# Practical Implications: Variance Reduction and Stability

Understanding the normalization term as a soft value function clarifies why its presence matters in actor-critic optimization for RLHF. In the actor-critic objective, dropping this term leaves the policy gradient exposed to large variance because the objective would then respond to raw rewards $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$ without subtracting the $x$-dependent baseline captured by the log-partition. Prior work has attempted to compensate by introducing baselines, such as learned value functions or heuristic normalizations (e.g., human completion baselines), but these can be difficult to optimize or noisy in practice.

By contrast, the DPO reparameterization selects a reward representative within the equivalence class that bakes the normalization into the transformation between reward and policy. As a result, DPO avoids the need for baselines altogether and yields a stable, low-variance maximum likelihood update that mirrors the constrained RL objective. The DPO gradient increases the likelihood of preferred completions and decreases that of dispreferred ones, with importance weights reflecting how incorrectly the implicit reward ranks the pair. This weighting emerges from the same analytical structure that gives rise to the soft value function in the actor-critic view, but DPO makes the training loop far simpler by eliminating explicit reinforcement learning, sampling in the loop, and baseline estimation.

# Summary

The KL-constrained RLHF objective admits an optimal policy that is an exponential tilting of a reference policy by the reward, normalized by a partition function. Rewriting the reward in terms of the optimal policy reveals a normalization term $\\beta\\,\\log Z(x)$ that depends only on the input $x$. In the actor-critic formulation, this term appears as

\\[
\\beta\\,\\log\\sum_{y}\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\!\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\right),
\\]

which conceptually represents the soft value function of the reference policy under the learned reward and temperature $\\beta$. It stabilizes policy optimization by serving as an $x$-dependent baseline that reduces variance, and it is intrinsically tied to the normalization required for the optimal policy. Direct Preference Optimization leverages this structure via a change of variables, turning the RLHF objective into a simple maximum likelihood preference loss that implicitly incorporates the same normalization effects—achieving stable, performant alignment to human preferences without explicit reinforcement learning.
