In the paper “Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model”, the authors report that DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO uses ground-truth rewards, which most directly indicates PPO’s sample inefficiency. This is sensible because DPO optimizes the same KL-constrained reward maximization objective as PPO (Eq. (1)) but does so via a change of variables that gives a closed-form characterization of the optimal policy and yields a simple maximum-likelihood loss over preferences (Eq. (8)). By substituting the optimal-policy form into the Bradley–Terry preference model (Eq. (2)), DPO eliminates the partition function and obtains a tractable loss that depends only on policy log-probability ratios with a reference, namely terms of the form \( \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \). As a result, DPO trains with a low-variance, offline binary cross-entropy objective over fixed preference pairs, avoiding the repeated on-policy sampling and trajectory-level variance that PPO relies on. Empirically, this translates into a superior reward–KL frontier: DPO increases reward while keeping \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) low, and it even dominates PPO-GT (PPO run with the true reward), which still depends on noisy policy-gradient estimates despite perfect reward signals.

The paper’s theoretical analysis further clarifies why PPO can be sample-inefficient relative to DPO. In the actor–critic view of the KL-constrained objective (Eq. (12)), PPO must estimate or baseline the normalization term \( -\beta \log \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y)\right) \), which acts like a soft value function; poor baselines or Monte Carlo estimates introduce high variance and instability in gradient updates. By contrast, DPO’s implicit reward \( \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \) selects a representative from the correct reward equivalence class and yields gradients that directly push up preferred completions and down dispreferred ones, weighted by how incorrect the current ordering is, i.e., \( \sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w})) \). This per-example, preference-informed weighting focuses learning where it matters most and stabilizes updates without any on-policy sampling loop. Consequently, even with access to \(r^{*}\), PPO still expends samples to tame variance and satisfy the KL constraint, whereas DPO achieves the same constraint through its log-ratio parameterization and optimizes preference likelihood directly—explaining why DPO can outperform PPO-GT and highlighting PPO’s sample inefficiency.
