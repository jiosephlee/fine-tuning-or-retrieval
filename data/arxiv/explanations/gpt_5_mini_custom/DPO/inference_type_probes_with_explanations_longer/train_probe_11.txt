According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', if the learned reward model were perfectly accurate but the KL penalty $\beta$ became extremely large, the optimized policy $\pi_{\theta}$ would converge toward the reference policy. This follows directly from the KL-constrained RLHF objective, which maximizes $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right]$ (Eq. (4)). As $\beta \to \infty$, the KL regularization term dominates the optimization and the only way to avoid incurring arbitrarily large penalty is to make $\pi_{\theta}$ match $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$; any deviation from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ yields a cost that outweighs any finite gain from the (even perfectly accurate) reward. Thus the optimizer must prioritize minimizing $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ over exploiting $r$, driving $\pi_{\theta}\to\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

The same conclusion arises from the closed-form expression for the optimal policy under the KL constraint (Eq. (5)): $\pi_{r}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right)$. When $\beta \to \infty$, the factor $\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right)\to 1$ for all $(x,y)$, and the partition function becomes $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=1$, yielding $\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$. DPO leverages this same mapping between rewards and policies via the reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ (Eq. (6)), so increasing $\beta$ strengthens the KL constraint in exactly the same way: in the large-$\beta$ regime, the optimal solution collapses to the reference distribution regardless of reward accuracy.
