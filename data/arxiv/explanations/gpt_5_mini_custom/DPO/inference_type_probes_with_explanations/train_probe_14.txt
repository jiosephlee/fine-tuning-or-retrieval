According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', when the model already rates $y_w$ much higher than $y_l$, the per-example weight in the DPO gradient, $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$, becomes near zero. This follows directly from the DPO gradient derivation, where the update for each preference pair $(x, y_w, y_l)$ is scaled by a logistic factor on the difference of the model’s implicit rewards $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. If the current policy strongly prefers $y_w$ over $y_l$, then $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)\gg \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$, so the argument of the sigmoid is a large negative number, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)\ll 0$, and hence $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))\approx 0$. Intuitively, DPO then applies almost no update, because the model already ranks the pair correctly under the KL-constrained implicit reward; conversely, when the ordering is wrong (or close), this term approaches 1/2–1, yielding larger updates that increase $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decrease $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$. This adaptive weighting, derived from the Bradley–Terry likelihood under the reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, focuses learning where it is needed and helps prevent degeneration observed with naive unweighted probability-ratio objectives.
