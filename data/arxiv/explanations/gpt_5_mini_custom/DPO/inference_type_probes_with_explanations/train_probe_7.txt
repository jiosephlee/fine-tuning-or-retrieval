In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors state that conceptually the quantity $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ in DPO corresponds to the model's implicit reward. This is sensible because under the KL-regularized RLHF objective, the optimal policy for any reward $r$ has the exponential tilting form $\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$, which rearranges to $r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$; since Bradley–Terry preferences and the induced optimal policy depend only on reward differences, the additive baseline $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels, placing all rewards that differ by an $x$-only term in the same equivalence class and making $\hat r_\theta(x,y)\!\triangleq\!\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ a valid representative of that class. The DPO objective is precisely the maximum-likelihood fit of this reparameterized Bradley–Terry model, and its gradient uses $\hat r_\theta$ to weight updates, increasing $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decreasing $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ in proportion to how much the implicit reward misorders the pair; furthermore, Theorem 1 shows that every reward class consistent with Plackett–Luce/Bradley–Terry can be represented as $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, so interpreting $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ as the model’s implicit reward is both derivationally exact (up to an $x$-dependent constant) and without loss of generality for the preference-learning problem.
