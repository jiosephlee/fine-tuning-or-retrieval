In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', if the optimal policy $\pi^*(y\mid x)$ equals the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ in Eq.~(7), then the reward must, up to equivalence, be a function of $x$. This follows directly from the closed-form optimal policy in Eq.~(7), $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$; setting $\pi_r=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ implies, for all $y$ with $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$, that $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)=Z(x)$, hence $r(x,y)=\beta\log Z(x)$, which depends only on $x$ and not on $y$. In the paper’s equivalence relation over rewards, $r\sim r'$ iff $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$, so any reward that makes $\pi^*=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is equivalent to the $y$-independent function $f(x)=\beta\log Z(x)$. Moreover, under Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce preferences the $y$-independent term cancels in differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, and by Lemma 2 in the paper rewards in the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy, so the only way for the optimal policy to match the reference everywhere is for the reward to be (up to equivalence) purely a function of $x$.
