In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', increasing the scalar $\beta$ typically allows the policy to deviate more from the reference policy, causing an increase in $\mathrm{KL}$. This follows from the DPO objective in Eq. (12), where the preference likelihood is $\sigma\!\left(\beta\big[\log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\big]\right)$, and from the gradient expression showing that updates are weighted by $\beta$ and push to increase the log-ratio gap between preferred and dispreferred responses; as $\beta$ grows, the argument to $\sigma(\cdot)$ and the update magnitude both scale up, driving larger changes in $\log \tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$ to better separate $y_w$ from $y_l$, which in turn increases $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}]$. Equivalently, under the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, a larger $\beta$ amplifies per-example reward differences and thus the pressure to move probability mass away from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to satisfy preferences, yielding higher deviation. Although in the RL Lagrangian of Eq. (4) a larger $\beta$ strengthens the explicit KL penalty, in DPO $\beta$ also sets the “temperature” of the preference model (Eq. (11)) and the aggressiveness of the policy updates that increase log-ratio separation, so in practice larger $\beta$ typically induces larger shifts in $\pi_\theta$ relative to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, manifesting as higher observed $\mathrm{KL}$.
