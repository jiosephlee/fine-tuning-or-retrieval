Chapter Title: Cayley Parameterization and the Invertibility Condition in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Setup

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapting pretrained models to downstream tasks by constraining the update to be an orthogonal transformation. Instead of adding an arbitrary update to a pretrained weight matrix, OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight as a product of an orthogonal matrix and the original weight. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the layer input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. The design choice of an orthogonal update is motivated by the desire to preserve the relational structure of neurons—specifically, pairwise angles—so that semantic knowledge from pretraining is retained during finetuning. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, and therefore offer a direct mechanism for maintaining the geometry learned during pretraining while still allowing expressive adaptation.

To make the method practical and stable, OFT enforces the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout training by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley transform. This parameterization allows the optimization to proceed over unconstrained variables while ensuring the resulting $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal.

# The Cayley Parameterization in OFT

OFT enforces orthogonality by employing the Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, and $\bm{I}$ is the identity. This form is used to guarantee that the learned transform $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal during the entire finetuning process, while allowing training to operate on the free parameters of $\bm{Q}$ instead of directly on $\bm{R}$.

The Cayley parameterization plays two crucial roles in OFT and its generalization:

1. It enables zero-deviation initialization. By initializing $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the parameterization produces $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, so the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly.

2. It provides a smooth, differentiable reparameterization from the unconstrained space of skew-symmetric matrices to the orthogonal group, ensuring that gradient-based optimization can be applied while maintaining orthogonality.

Within BOFT, an orthogonal butterfly-based generalization of OFT, the same Cayley idea is applied blockwise to many small factors that multiply to produce the overall $\bm{R}$. In both OFT and BOFT, the Cayley parameterization is the key mechanism that maintains orthogonality throughout training.

# The Implicit Invertibility Condition for Cayley Parameterization

The Cayley parameterization writes $\bm{R}$ as a product $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. This expression uses the inverse of $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$. For the expression to be well-defined, the inverse must exist. Therefore, the implicit mathematical condition that must hold for the Cayley parameterization to be valid is:
\[
\bm{I} - \bm{Q} \text{ is invertible.}
\]

This condition is inherent in the formula. The right-hand factor $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ is an inverse, and the existence of this inverse is what allows the mapping from the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ to the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to be computed. If $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$ were not invertible, then $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ would not exist, and the Cayley expression for $\bm{R}$ would be undefined.

This invertibility requirement aligns naturally with the standard training practice used in OFT and BOFT. At initialization, setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ yields $\bm{I} - \bm{Q} = \bm{I}$, which is invertible, and thus $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ is well-defined. During optimization, one continues to operate in the parameter space of $\bm{Q}$ while relying on the invertibility of $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$ to compute the forward map to $\bm{R}$. In short, the Cayley parameterization guarantees orthogonality so long as the computation remains well-defined, and the well-definedness is precisely the invertibility of $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$.

# Initialization and Training Stability through Cayley

A central desideratum for finetuning is to start from the exact pretrained model to avoid abrupt deviations. The Cayley parameterization naturally supports this through identity initialization: setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ produces
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}\bm{I}^{-1} = \bm{I}.
\]
This guarantees that the initial forward pass uses $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. The invertibility condition is trivially satisfied at initialization, because $\bm{I} - \bm{Q} = \bm{I}$.

Moreover, both OFT and its butterfly-based generalization BOFT exploit an important stability property of orthogonal left-multiplication: if $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is a singular value decomposition, then $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ has the same largest singular value as $\bm{W}^0$, since left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves spectral norms. As a result, enforcing orthogonality via the Cayley parameterization preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained layer, which is associated with training stability and favorable generalization behavior. The Cayley parameterization thereby supports both initialization fidelity and spectral preservation, provided that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ remains well-defined throughout training.

# From OFT to BOFT: Factorized Orthogonal Parameterization

While a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal $\bm{R}$ provides a simple and expressive mechanism for OFT, its naive parameterization involves $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom. To improve parameter-efficiency while still targeting dense transformations, the paper proposes BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly. The central idea is to compose $\bm{R}$ as the product of multiple sparse, structured, orthogonal matrices that together achieve dense connectivity:
\[
\bm{R}(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]
where each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block butterfly component with a carefully designed sparsity pattern and block size $b$. Each such factor is constructed to be orthogonal, and the product remains orthogonal as a consequence.

To ensure orthogonality for each component, BOFT applies the same Cayley mechanism locally to $2 \times 2$ or $2b \times 2b$ sub-blocks, where the skew-symmetric parameters are mapped to orthogonal blocks. The nonzero sparsity patterns of all higher-level butterfly components are permutations of the $2 \times 2$ or $2b \times 2b$ base pattern, so each factor can be parameterized orthogonally in the same way. In this factorized setting, the validity of the Cayley parameterization at the block level relies on the same implicit requirement: each use of $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ must be well-defined, i.e., the corresponding $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$ must be invertible in every block where Cayley is applied.

Through this factorization, BOFT achieves substantial parameter savings. For instance, with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ effective parameters to represent a dense orthogonal matrix, in contrast to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for an unconstrained dense orthogonal parameterization. When $m = 1$, BOFT recovers the block-diagonal OFT; when $m = 1$ and $b = d$, BOFT reduces to the original full OFT. Thus, BOFT subsumes OFT and offers a continuum between identity and full orthogonal expressivity, controlled by the factorization depth $m$ and block size $b$.

# The Information Transmission View and Its Relevance

The paper motivates sparse-to-dense orthogonal composition via an information transmission perspective. A dense matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ can be seen as establishing dense connectivity from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes. Factorizing $\bm{R}$ as a product $\bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ corresponds to relaying information over a $d \times (m+1)$ layered grid, where the sparsity pattern in each $\bm{B}_i$ defines which nodes can communicate between adjacent layers. To ultimately yield a dense $\bm{R}$, each source node must be able to communicate to every receiver node via some path across layers.

Under this view, one seeks sparse factors that jointly achieve dense end-to-end connectivity with as few trainable parameters as possible, while maintaining the orthogonality of each factor. The butterfly structure meets these desiderata, enabling $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ total nonzeros across factors to realize dense connectivity. In BOFT, each butterfly component is made orthogonal via blockwise Cayley parameterization; thus, at each factor where Cayley is used, the same implicit requirement applies: the inverse of $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ in the relevant block must exist. This blockwise invertibility condition is what ensures each orthogonal factor is well-defined and, by extension, that the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is well-defined.

# Practical Implications of the Invertibility Condition

The invertibility condition—$\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$ must be invertible wherever the Cayley parameterization is applied—has direct practical meaning in OFT and BOFT:

First, it guarantees that the parameterization produces a valid orthogonal matrix at every step of training. Without invertibility, the parameterization would be undefined, breaking the guarantee that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal.

Second, it aligns with identity initialization. Setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ yields $\bm{I} - \bm{Q} = \bm{I}$, which is invertible, and thus the initial $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ is valid and matches the pretrained model exactly.

Third, it applies uniformly whether one parameterizes a single dense orthogonal matrix (OFT) or multiple structured factors (BOFT). In BOFT, Cayley is applied locally to blocks; thus, each local $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ must be invertible for the associated block to be valid, ensuring the entire factor and its product remain well-defined.

Finally, this condition complements the broader benefits of orthogonal finetuning. By maintaining orthogonality through Cayley, OFT and BOFT preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weights under left multiplication and thereby contribute to training stability and generalization. The invertibility requirement is the minimal mathematical condition needed to realize these benefits via the Cayley map during optimization.

# Conclusion

Orthogonal finetuning updates pretrained weights via an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$, preserving geometric structure and spectral properties that are beneficial for stable and generalizable adaptation. The Cayley parameterization,
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \quad \text{with} \quad \bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
is the core mechanism that enforces orthogonality throughout training in both OFT and its butterfly-based extension, BOFT. The implicit mathematical condition that underpins this parameterization is that $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}$ must be invertible, ensuring the parameterization is well-defined at every step. Identity initialization satisfies this condition trivially, and the same requirement applies to each block where Cayley is used in BOFT’s factorization. With this foundation, BOFT composes many small, orthogonal factors into a dense orthogonal matrix using butterfly structures, achieving strong parameter-efficiency without sacrificing the orthogonality guarantees provided by the Cayley transform.
