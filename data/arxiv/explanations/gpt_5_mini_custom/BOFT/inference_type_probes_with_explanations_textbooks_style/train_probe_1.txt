Expressiveness and Regularity in Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled framework for adapting pretrained models by transforming neurons within a layer using an orthogonal matrix. The core idea is to preserve relational information within weight matrices, especially the pairwise angles between neuron vectors, so that semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining is retained during finetuning. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weights $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, OFT introduces a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ ensures that the pairwise angles among the columns of $\bm{W}^0$ are preserved after transformation, providing a geometric regularity that stabilizes finetuning.

To maintain orthogonality throughout optimization, OFT employs the Cayley parameterization. Specifically, $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. Identity initialization is achieved by setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ initially, so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and the finetuned model begins exactly at the pretrained model.

For parameter efficiency, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure: $\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ and $br=d$. This design reduces the effective number of trainable parameters by restricting transformations to independent blocks. However, this fixed sparsity pattern limits expressiveness and imposes an inductive bias based on indices, which cannot recover classic dense linear transforms and may hamper generalization for tasks requiring dense interactions across dimensions.

# From Expressiveness to Overfitting

Expressiveness in finetuning refers to the size and flexibility of the hypothesis class induced by the parameterization. A fully dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ yields $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and can represent any transformation in the orthogonal group, which is a highly expressive hypothesis class. In contrast, a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix significantly reduces the parameter space, constraining transformations to local blocks and thereby reducing expressiveness.

Increasing expressiveness without appropriate regularity commonly leads to overfitting during finetuning. The paper emphasizes that better expressiveness does not always translate to better adaptation performance, and notes that full finetuning—despite universal expressiveness—often yields unsatisfactory performance. This observation highlights the central trade-off: expressiveness determines how flexibly the finetuning can modify the model, while regularity constrains this flexibility to avoid fitting spurious signals from limited downstream data. Orthogonality itself is a powerful regularizer because it preserves key geometric properties of the pretrained weights. However, the absence of additional structural constraints can still allow the finetuning process to explore overly complex solutions that do not generalize well.

Within OFT and its extension, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), the parameterization explicitly tunes this trade-off. BOFT enlarges the parameter space beyond the block-diagonal OFT by composing $\bm{R}$ from multiple sparse orthogonal factors, yet it does so in a manner that encodes structured regularity. This design allows BOFT to increase expressiveness compared to block-diagonal OFT while keeping a strong regularization effect that mitigates overfitting.

# Orthogonal Butterfly: Controlling Expressiveness with Structure

BOFT composes a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse orthogonal matrices inspired by butterfly structures. With $d=2^N$, a butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is built recursively from butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, each of which is a block-diagonal arrangement of butterfly factors of size $k$. A butterfly factor is defined as
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
where $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ for all $i$. The butterfly matrix can be written as
\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is block-diagonal with butterfly factors of size $k$ along the diagonal. To guarantee orthogonality, each butterfly component is parameterized as an orthogonal matrix, which is straightforward because their non-zero patterns can be permuted to that of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, whose $2\times 2$ blocks can be made orthogonal using Cayley transform.

BOFT generalizes this construction with block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, where entries in butterfly factors are elevated from scalars to $b\times b$ matrices. The finetuning forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},\quad\text{with}\quad \bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
and each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal, i.e., $\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\bm{I}_d$. BOFT($m$,$b$) controls expressiveness through the number of components $m$ and the block size $b$. This yields $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a layer of size $d\times n$, offering a continuum between the highly regular block-diagonal OFT and more expressive dense parameterizations.

When $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters yet can produce a dense orthogonal matrix. In contrast, a full dense orthogonal parameterization in OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Crucially, BOFT can achieve density without requiring $b=d$, enabling finer control over expressiveness while embedding structural regularity at every multiplicative factor.

# Inductive Bias as Regularity

A central source of regularity in BOFT is its structured inductive bias. The butterfly structure, along with appropriate permutations, can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms, such as fast Fourier and Hadamard transforms. This property is significant: it defines a hypothesis class that is rich enough to model transformations commonly useful in signal and representation processing, while still being far smaller than the full orthogonal group. By constraining $\bm{R}(m,b)$ to such structured compositions, BOFT introduces an implicit bias that benefits generalization and prevents overfitting.

This stands in contrast to the block-diagonal structure used in OFT, which cannot recover these classic dense transforms and imposes an index-based sparsity pattern that may be misaligned with the intrinsic structure of the data. BOFT’s butterfly factorization embeds a smoother interpolation between full orthogonal transformations (maximal expressiveness) and identity matrices (maximal regularity), allowing practitioners to select a smaller, well-structured hypothesis class tailored to downstream tasks.

# Spectral Norm Preservation as Stability-Oriented Regularity

Orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which contributes to training stability and generalization. Let $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ be the singular value decomposition, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with singular values. Both OFT and BOFT left-multiply by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, yielding $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values, the largest singular value (i.e., the spectral norm) is preserved. This exact preservation is a strong regularity condition on the finetuning dynamics and has been shown to benefit training stability and generalization. In BOFT, this property holds component-wise because each butterfly component is orthogonal, and their product is orthogonal.

# Initialization and Dropout: Practical Regularization

Initialization and dropout are practical mechanisms that reinforce regularity during finetuning. BOFT uses identity initialization for all butterfly components by setting skew-symmetric parameters to zero under Cayley transform. This ensures the finetuned model starts at the pretrained model, preventing abrupt departures that may induce overfitting early in training.

Standard Dropout is not directly applicable because BOFT performs multiplicative updates via orthogonal matrices. To address this, BOFT introduces multiplicative Dropout that operates at the block level of butterfly components. Specifically, it randomly selects a fraction of butterfly components and a fraction of their diagonal blocks, then replaces the selected blocks with identity matrices during training. This enforces sparsity in the sequence of transformations, acts as a regularizer on the multiplicative structure, and reduces the risk of overfitting by limiting the effective complexity of $\bm{R}(m,b)$ during optimization.

# Information Transmission Perspective: Structured Control of Capacity

The information transmission framework reinterprets matrix factorization as sequential information exchange on a grid-structured graph. A dense matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ can be seen as full connectivity from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes, where non-zero entries in $\bm{R}$ indicate edges enabling information flow. Composing $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ corresponds to layered transmission across $d\times (m+1)$ nodes, and the sparsity patterns of the $\bm{B}_i$ define which routes are available.

Under this view, the desiderata are dense connectivity (each source reaches each receiver through some path) and minimum free edges (as few trainable edges as possible subject to orthogonality). Orthogonality imposes necessary constraints, such as requiring $d$ edges between adjacent levels to form a bijection, and introducing dependencies between zero patterns across entries. Butterfly graphs achieve dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges, providing an elegant way to reduce parameters while maintaining the ability to represent dense transformations. This reduction in free edges serves as a form of regularity: fewer trainable connections limit capacity and help prevent overfitting, while the structure of the connectivity preserves expressiveness where it matters.

# Kaleidoscope Hierarchy: Full Expressiveness and Its Risks

The expressivity of BOFT can be theoretically extended to represent the entire orthogonal group by composing multiple butterfly matrices in a kaleidoscope hierarchy. In particular, one can consider products of the form
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d),
\]
where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ are butterfly matrices. Generalizing BOFT, one can define
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2).
\]
When $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, this composition can represent the entire orthogonal group. While this establishes that butterfly-based constructions can attain maximal expressiveness, the paper cautions that better expressiveness does not necessarily yield better finetuning performance. Full finetuning—despite its universality—often performs unsatisfactorily, illustrating that unchecked capacity invites overfitting. BOFT’s value lies in offering a structured path to tune expressiveness while maintaining strong regularity, enabling a better trade-off for downstream adaptation.

# Practical Implications of the Expressiveness–Regularity Trade-off

In practice, the hyperparameters $m$ and $b$ in BOFT determine both expressiveness and regularity. Increasing $m$ and decreasing $b$ generally improves parameter efficiency and expressiveness while maintaining a structured inductive bias. For example, BOFT($\log d$, $2$) produces dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, balancing capacity and constraint. Identity initialization ensures that finetuning starts within a stable regime, and multiplicative Dropout further regularizes the multiplicative sequence, reducing the risk of overfitting. Together with spectral norm preservation, these elements define a coherent regularization strategy that tempers expressiveness and enhances generalization.

The paper also notes a modest training runtime overhead in BOFT compared to OFT due to the product of multiple orthogonal components. However, this does not affect inference latency, since the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained weights after finetuning. Finally, while butterfly networks are highly efficient for information transmission, the framework opens the door to exploring other network topologies that may further optimize the expressiveness–regularity trade-off.

# Concluding Perspective

Finetuning large pretrained models requires careful navigation of the tension between expressiveness and regularity. Orthogonal Finetuning provides a geometric regularity by preserving angles and spectral properties, and Orthogonal Butterfly extends this with structured compositions that increase expressiveness without sacrificing regularity. The butterfly structure brings an inductive bias aligned with classic transforms, reduces parameters through sparse factorization, and offers practical tools—identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout—to curb overfitting. The overarching message is clear: increasing expressiveness without appropriate regularity commonly leads to overfitting, and the design of BOFT is a principled answer to this challenge, enabling parameter-efficient, stable, and generalizable adaptation across diverse downstream tasks.
