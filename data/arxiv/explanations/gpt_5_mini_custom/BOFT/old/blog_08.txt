# Tuning the Dials: How to Choose m and b in BOFT

When applying Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) to a foundation model, two hyperparameters stand out: the depth $m$ and the block size $b$. Together, they control how expressive your finetuning transformation can be, how many parameters you need, and how much compute overhead you incur. In this post, I’ll walk you through what each of these dials does, how to think about their trade-offs, and some practical starting points and a checklist for choosing $(m,b)$ in your next project.

## Understanding BOFT’s Hyperparameters

Recall that BOFT replaces a single orthogonal matrix $\bm R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with a product of $m$ sparse “butterfly components,” each of which is block-structured with blocks of size $b$. Concretely, the finetuned weight is
$$
\bm R(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)},
$$
where each $\tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal block butterfly component. Two key facts follow:

1. **Parameter count**  
   BOFT uses 
   \[
     \tfrac12\,(b-1)\,d\,m
   \]
   effective trainable parameters per layer. When $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, this is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full orthogonal matrix.

2. **Compute overhead**  
   Each of the $m$ components must be multiplied into the activation, so the forward pass costs roughly $m$ times more matrix multiplications than a single dense orthogonal update.

Intuitively, $m$ controls *how many stages* of sparse mixing you apply, while $b$ controls *how big* each local mixing block is. Larger $m$ and smaller $b$ both push you toward a denser, more expressive transformation—but at the cost of more parameters (from larger $b$) or more compute (from larger $m$).

## The Trade-offs: Depth versus Block Size

Let’s spell out the main trade-offs you’ll see when adjusting $m$ and $b$:

Expressivity  
- Increasing $m$ adds more sparse factors in the product, allowing you to approximate a wider variety of orthogonal transforms.  
- Decreasing $b$ forces each block to be smaller, which also encourages more global mixing over the course of $m$ stages.  

Parameter efficiency  
- The total parameter budget scales linearly in both $m$ and $b$:  
  $$
    \#\text{params} \;=\;\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m.
  $$
- A naive full OFT (block size $b=d$ and $m=1$) uses $\sim\frac12d^2$ parameters. BOFT with $(m=\log d,b=2)$ uses only $\sim\frac12\,d\log d$.

Compute cost  
- Each added butterfly component adds one extra matrix multiplication on activations. So compute cost grows roughly in $m$.

Convergence and regularity  
- Smaller $b$ tends to act as a stronger regularizer: you restrict each update stage to simple rotations, which can make finetuning more stable.  
- Very large $m$ and very small $b$ may over-parameterize the butterfly expansion and suffer diminishing returns, especially on low-data tasks.

## Rules of Thumb and Defaults

Based on empirical studies and ablation simulations, here are some starting points:

- **Small models or scarce data**  
  Use a relatively *shallow* butterfly (small $m$, say $m=1\text{–}2$) with *moderate blocks* ($b=8\text{–}16$). This gives a parameter count of order $4d$ or $8d$ and keeps compute low, while still adding enough flexibility to adapt a pretrained model.

- **Medium models or balanced data**  
  Try $m=3\text{–}5$ with $b=4\text{–}8$. You’ll get $\tfrac12(b-1)dm$ parameters on the order of $6d\text{–}20d$ and a manageable compute overhead of 3–5 multiplies per layer.

- **Large models or high-complexity tasks**  
  For maximum expressivity, set $m\approx \log_2 d$ and $b=2$. This realizes the classic butterfly matrix with $\sim\tfrac12\,d\log_2 d$ parameters. It can approximate nearly any orthogonal map, at the cost of higher compute and parameter use.

- **Middle ground**  
  If you suspect the full butterfly is overkill, you can interpolate by using $b>2$ but still letting $m$ grow. For example, $b=4$ and $m=\frac12\log_2 d$ yields roughly the same param budget as $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d/2$, but more regularity per stage.

## Choosing m and b in Practice: A Checklist

Before you start finetuning, run through this quick decision guide:

1. **Estimate your parameter budget**  
   How many additional parameters can you afford? Compute $\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m$ to match your limit.

2. **Assess compute overhead**  
   Can you afford $m$ extra matrix multiplies per layer? If GPU time is tight, cap $m$ to 2 or 3.

3. **Match task complexity**  
   High-complexity tasks (e.g., style transfer, open-ended generation) benefit from higher expressivity: lean toward larger $m$ and smaller $b$.  
   Low-complexity tasks (e.g., fine-grained classification on small datasets) may overfit if you go too expressive—use smaller $m$ or larger $b$ for more regularity.

4. **Consider finetuning stability**  
   If training diverges or oscillates, try increasing $b$ (more parameters per factor but fewer factors) or decreasing $m$ to simplify each stage’s rotation.

5. **Validate and iterate**  
   Start with one of the rule-of-thumb presets above, monitor training loss and downstream accuracy, then adjust $m$ or $b$ by one step:  
   - If underfitting, increase $m$ or decrease $b$.  
   - If overfitting, decrease $m$ or increase $b$.

## Conclusion

BOFT’s $(m,b)$ hyperparameters let you smoothly dial between parameter efficiency, compute cost, and the expressivity of your orthogonal finetuning. By understanding how $m$ and $b$ trade off against each other—and by following simple rules of thumb and a decision checklist—you can tailor BOFT to your hardware constraints and your task’s complexity. With the right setting in hand, you’ll get the benefits of orthogonal finetuning—preserved spectral norms, stable convergence, and strong generalization—at a fraction of the parameter cost of dense OFT.
