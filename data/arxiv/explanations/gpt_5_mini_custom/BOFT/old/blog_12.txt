# BOFT on the Edge: Low-VRAM and On-Device Adaptation

Modern deep learning practitioners are often constrained by hardware: limited GPU memory on laptops, edge devices, or small workstations makes fine-tuning a large foundation model feel out of reach. Enter BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning), a parameter-efficient finetuning framework that slashes the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. In this post, we’ll walk through how BOFT’s butterfly parameterization unlocks on-device fine-tuning, how to budget your memory with mixed precision, how to combine BOFT with low-bit quantization (à la QLoRA), and finally how to merge the learned orthogonal transforms back into your base model for true zero-latency inference.

## Why $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ Matters for On-Device Finetuning

Traditional full finetuning updates every weight in a $d\times d$ matrix, costing $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters and commensurate GPU memory. Even block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning reduces this only to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters, where $b$ is block size, but still can be hefty for large $d$. BOFT exploits the butterfly structure—originally from the Cooley-Tukey FFT—to factorize a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal update matrix as the product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse factors, each with just $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros. In practice this means:

  * Fine-tuning head or adapter modules now costs $d\log d$ parameters instead of $d^2$.
  * On a $d=4096$ hidden dimension, BOFT uses roughly $4096\log_2(4096)\approx50{,}000$ parameters per layer, versus $16$ million in a full matrix.

That steep reduction makes updating a model on a single 8 GB laptop GPU entirely feasible.

## Memory Budgeting with Mixed Precision and Sparse Updates

Even with fewer parameters, you must squeeze activations, gradients, and optimizer states into VRAM. Here are practical tips when you finetune BOFT on-device:

  * **Half-precision (FP16) for activations and gradients.** Most modern GPUs achieve 2× memory saving by running in FP16. BOFT’s Cayley-based 2×2 rotation blocks in each butterfly component convert naturally to FP16, with little impact on numeric stability.
  * **Parameter-only sparsity.** BOFT’s sparse butterfly factors can be stored in compressed form, e.g. by storing only the non-zero blocks. You avoid allocating a full $d\times d$ buffer for each factor.
  * **Gradient checkpointing** for deep stacks of layers. If your model has dozens of transformer layers, recompute forward activations on the fly rather than storing them all.

Putting these together, a BOFT finetuning session on a 6 B–parameter vision transformer can run comfortably in 6–8 GB of VRAM—opening up on-device experimentation.

## Combining BOFT with Low-Bit Quantization

Quantization delivers another order-of-magnitude memory saving by storing pretrained weights in 4-bit or even 2-bit form. QLoRA popularized finetuning large language models with 4-bit weights plus LoRA adapters. You can similarly fuse BOFT into this workflow:

  1. **Quantize** the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to 4-bit using a method like GPTQ or BitCenter.
  2. **Attach BOFT modules**: your orthogonal butterfly factors remain in FP16 (or FP32 if you prefer).
  3. **Finetune**: perform forward and backward passes using 4-bit quantized weights plus $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ BOFT parameters.
  4. **Merge**: after training, absorb the learned butterfly factors into the quantized weight representations.

Even though the base model stays at 4-bit, the BOFT update lives in low-precision float, yielding high-quality task adaptation with minimal extra memory.

## Zero-Latency Inference via Merge-In

A common concern with adapters or extra modules is added inference cost. BOFT sidesteps this entirely. Because the final orthogonal update matrix $\bm{R}$ is just the product of your learned butterfly components, you can merge it into the base weights once and for all:

  * Compute the composite update $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ on the CPU or GPU.
  * Multiply your pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}$, storing the result as a single new weight tensor.
  * Discard the separate BOFT factors.

The result is a model indistinguishable from a fully-merged finetuned checkpoint, with zero extra layers or runtime overhead. You get the benefits of orthogonal finetuning with the simplicity of a drop-in single‐tensor update.

## Case Study: Finetuning a Vision Transformer on a Laptop GPU

Suppose you want to adapt a ViT-Base (768-hidden) model to a small custom image classification set on a 6 GB GPU. A naive adapter or LoRA plus 4-bit quantization might still hover around 5–6 GB usage, leaving little headroom. With BOFT:

  * Each layer’s BOFT update costs $\mathcal{O}(768\log_2 768)\approx768\times9\approx7{,}000$ parameters, or about 28 KB in FP16.
  * Across 12 transformer layers, that’s under 350 KB extra—negligible versus the 295 MB of 4-bit quantized weights.
  * Combined with mixed-precision activations and gradient checkpointing, total VRAM peaks around 5 GB.
  * After finetuning, you merge all BOFT factors into the quantized weights, shipping a single 4-bit checkpoint.

This workflow transforms your everyday laptop or edge GPU into a full fine-tuning rig, all thanks to BOFT’s $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter magic.

## Conclusion

On-device adaptation of large models no longer requires massive clusters or exotic hardware. By leveraging BOFT’s butterfly-based orthogonal finetuning, you cut trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, pair it with mixed precision and 4-bit quantization, and merge updates for inference with zero latency. Whether you’re iterating on a laptop, pushing updates to a fleet of edge GPUs, or building a mobile finetuning solution, BOFT brings large-model adaptation squarely into the hands of every practitioner.
