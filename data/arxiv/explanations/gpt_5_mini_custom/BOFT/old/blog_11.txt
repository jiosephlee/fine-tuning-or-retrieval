# Expressivity Without Bloat: What Butterfly Factorization Can Represent

In the world of large pretrained models, finetuning has become as crucial as pretraining itself. However, fully updating billions of parameters or naively adding even more can be prohibitively expensive in terms of storage, computation, and sometimes even inference latency. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offered an elegant solution by multiplying a fixed pretrained weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix. Yet a dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ still demands $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—hardly “lightweight.” Enter **butterfly factorization**, a structured approach drawn from the Fast Fourier Transform that lets us build very expressive orthogonal transforms in just $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In this post, we’ll explore how butterfly-parameterized finetuning (BOFT) achieves richer expressive power than the traditional block-diagonal approach, and how a simple extension called the kaleidoscope hierarchy can recover the *entire* orthogonal group without reverting to full finetuning.

## The Block-Diagonal Bottleneck

Before BOFT, a common trick to reduce the parameter count in OFT was to force the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ to be block-diagonal. If you split your $d$ neurons into $r$ chunks of size $b=d/r$, then
$$
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\bigr),\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}.
$$
This cuts the trainable parameters down to $r\cdot \tfrac12b(b-1)\approx\frac12bd$, but it comes at a price: neurons in different blocks never “talk” to each other. There’s no way to mix information across those block boundaries, which limits the kinds of rotations and reflections you can learn. In practice, this architecture often underfits tasks that require more global transformations of the feature space.

## Enter the Butterfly

Butterfly factorization is inspired by the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. In a nutshell, you repeatedly split your $d$-dimensional vector into two halves, apply simple $\!2\times2\!$ orthogonal “butterfly” transforms locally, and then shuffle and repeat. Graphically, this looks like a multi-stage network where each layer has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzero connections, yet information from *any* input coordinate can eventually reach *any* output coordinate after $O(\log d)$ stages.

Concretely, you represent
$$
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_1,
$$
where each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is a sparse block-permutation of simple 2×2 orthogonal rotations. By enforcing each $\mathbf{B}_i$ to be orthogonal (e.g.\ via a 2×2 Cayley or Givens parameterization), the product remains orthogonal. Because each stage has only $d$ or $O(d)$ free parameters and there are $m\approx\log d$ stages, BOFT uses just $O(d\log d)$ parameters. Yet the *effective* connectivity across all stages densely links inputs to outputs.

## More Expressive than Block-Diagonal

Why is this more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the *same* parameter budget? Think of a block-diagonal matrix as a set of local rotations on fixed chunks. No matter how you tweak those chunks, you can never couple different blocks. Butterfly factorization, on the other hand, weaves together local rotations in a multi-stage fashion so that each input coordinate is gradually mixed with every other. Empirically, when you try to **approximate** a random dense orthogonal matrix with either:

- a block-diagonal transform of size $b\times b$, or  
- a butterfly-style transform with the *same* number of parameters,

the butterfly family consistently achieves lower approximation error. In other words, BOFT can reconstruct richer, more global rotations without “bloating” the parameter count.

## Kaleidoscope Hierarchies: From Sparse to Complete

If you keep stacking butterfly factorizations and their transposes in alternation, you get what’s known as a **kaleidoscope hierarchy**:
$$
\mathbf{R}^G 
\;=\; (\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{d-1,2}^\top)\,
(\mathbf{B}_{d-2,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{d-2,2}^\top)\,\cdots\,
(\mathbf{B}_{1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{1,2}^\top).
$$
Here each $\mathbf{B}_{i,j}$ is a butterfly matrix of full depth. A classic result shows that by choosing enough alternating pairs—specifically $d-1$ such blocks—you can *exactly* recover any orthogonal matrix in $\mathrm{O}(d)$. The catch, of course, is that this “full coverage” scheme uses more parameters (scaling like $d(d-1)/2$ in total), which effectively becomes a dense parameterization again.

The magic, however, is that *we don’t need* full coverage to get strong performance on real tasks. In practice, a **shallow** BOFT with just $\log d$ stages (and small block size) often hits a sweet spot: it’s expressive enough to adapt the model effectively, but regularized enough to avoid overfitting. You get the best of both worlds—rich representational power **without** parameter bloat.

## Key Takeaways

- **Block-diagonal OFT** limits cross-block mixing, leading to under-expressive transforms if blocks are too small.  
- **Butterfly factorization** uses multi-stage sparse orthogonal factors to weave together global rotations in $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.  
- **Empirical studies** show BOFT approximates dense orthogonal matrices more faithfully than block-diagonals at the same parameter budget.  
- **Kaleidoscope hierarchies** can recover the entire orthogonal group but at the cost of a dense parameter count. In practice, a shallow BOFT strikes the right balance.  

By embracing butterfly structures, we can finetune large models in a parameter-frugal way without sacrificing the diversity of rotations, reflections, and permutations that orthogonal matrices can offer. This insight opens the door to more efficient and generalizable adaptation of foundation models—expressivity without bloat.
