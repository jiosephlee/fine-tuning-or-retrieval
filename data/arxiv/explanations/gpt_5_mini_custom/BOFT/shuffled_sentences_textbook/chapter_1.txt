Chapter 1: Motivation and Landscape of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning   Modern foundation models—large pretrained networks like GPT-3, large vision transformers, and diffusion models—have driven major advances across language, vision, and generative tasks. However, these models come with very large parameter counts (e.g., GPT-3 with roughly $175$ billion parameters), and training such models from scratch requires massive compute, long wall-clock time, and huge datasets. This family has the virtue of introducing no inference-time structural change: the same model architecture is used, often enabling zero additional inference latency. Full finetuning across billions of parameters remains compute- and memory-intensive, and it creates logistical burdens: storing separate full copies of the model for each task is costly; updating or rolling back models is heavy; and sharing task-specific variants becomes cumbersome. If $\bm{W}^0$ is a pretrained weight matrix, LoRA models the finetuned weight as $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where $\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ are small matrices and $r$ is the chosen low rank. This viewpoint makes it natural to seek sparse factorization topologies studied in computer networking and signal processing. Although OFT yields favorable generalization and training stability properties by preserving geometric relations, it encounters a practical challenge: a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, so representing a general orthogonal $\bm{R}$ is parameter-expensive. Adapter-based approaches typically require only a small number of new parameters per task and can be composed or shared across tasks, but they do increase inference-time compute and may require modifying the model architecture or inference pipeline. To reason about useful sparsity patterns for the factors, BOFT frames the problem as an information transmission task: viewing matrix multiplication as transmitting information from input coordinates to output coordinates across a layered grid, a good factorization must (i) enable dense connectivity from every input coordinate to every output coordinate (so the product can be dense) and (ii) minimize the number of trainable edges subject to orthogonality constraints. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a multiplicative perspective with a geometric motivation: preserve relational information among neurons (in particular, pairwise angles) learned during pretraining. Consequently, expressiveness is reduced: the block-diagonal orthogonal matrices cannot approximate many dense linear transforms (for example, global transforms that mix components across blocks) and may therefore limit the range of admissible adaptations. To ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal during optimization, OFT uses a parameterization such as the Cayley transform: express $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). Intuitively, OFT adapts the representation by rotating (and reflecting) the space of neuron coordinates rather than adding new components; this can lead to more stable training and better generalization by keeping much of the pretrained structure intact. - Generalization. In summary, BOFT is motivated by the desire to combine the stability and geometric preservation of orthogonal finetuning with a parameter budget far smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. Two influential reparameterization-based PEFT approaches illustrate different places in the design space: LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).
