The upshot is that \(\bm{R}^G\) gives a principled path from very compact structured finetuning (low \(m,\) moderate \(b\)) to the full, dense orthogonal parameterization (\(m\) large, \(b=d\)), with intermediate points that interpolate between regularity and expressiveness. The core idea is that different topologies privilege different practical trade-offs. In particular, pixelated or blockwise butterfly variants replace scalar diagonals in basic butterfly factors with \(b\times b\) blocks (this is exactly the block butterfly extension BOFT uses, \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\)), giving a richer local transform while keeping global sparsity. BOFT sits in a broader family of ideas that use structured, factorized, or sparse parameterizations to realize fast or compact linear transforms. The defining characteristics are (1) a recursive or layered sparsity pattern that mixes local dense interactions with permuted routing between stages, and (2) parameterization of the small dense units (e.g., \(2\times 2\) rotations) so the whole product remains efficient to store and apply. Permutations are a recurring ingredient: the same sparse block patterns permuted between layers allow the factorized product to mix information globally. Another mechanism the paper already introduces is multiplicative Dropout, which randomly replaces selected butterfly components or block entries by identity during training; multiplicative Dropout can be viewed as a crude stochastic exploration of substructures and could be extended into a structured pruning or selection routine where consistently identityized components are removed permanently. The paper leaves the systematic exploration of these alternatives as an open direction, arguing that the information transmission framework provides the conceptual bridge for applying networking topologies to orthogonal finetuning.
