The upshot is that \(\bm{R}^G\) gives a principled path from very compact structured finetuning (low \(m,\) moderate \(b\)) to the full, dense orthogonal parameterization (\(m\) large, \(b=d\)), with intermediate points that interpolate between regularity and expressiveness. The core idea is that different topologies privilege different practical trade-offs. In particular, pixelated or blockwise butterfly variants replace scalar diagonals in basic butterfly factors with \(b\times b\) blocks (this is exactly the block butterfly extension BOFT uses, \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\)), giving a richer local transform while keeping global sparsity. BOFT sits in a broader family of ideas that use structured, factorized, or sparse parameterizations to realize fast or compact linear transforms. The defining characteristics are (1) a recursive or layered sparsity pattern that mixes local dense interactions with permuted routing between stages, and (2) parameterization of the small dense units (e.g., \(2\times 2\) rotations) so the whole product remains efficient to store and apply. Permutations are a recurring ingredient: the same sparse block patterns permuted between layers allow the factorized product to mix information globally. Another mechanism the paper already introduces is multiplicative Dropout, which randomly replaces selected butterfly components or block entries by identity during training; multiplicative Dropout can be viewed as a crude stochastic exploration of substructures and could be extended into a structured pruning or selection routine where consistently identityized components are removed permanently. The paper leaves the systematic exploration of these alternatives as an open direction, arguing that the information transmission framework provides the conceptual bridge for applying networking topologies to orthogonal finetuning. The paper shows that this structured family can be further generalized by alternating products of different butterfly-based orthogonal factors and their transposes, giving the class \[ \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,\ell) = \bm{R}_{\ell,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{\ell,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2), \] where each \(\bm{R}_{i,j}(m,b)\) is a BOFT-style orthogonal operator built from butterfly components. BOFT instead applies a multiplicative update from the left, \(\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\), where \(\bm{R}\) is constrained orthogonal and parameterized with butterfly factors. Because butterfly products can efficiently realize many classic orthonormal transforms (e.g., Hadamard, Fourier, cosine-like transforms), they are useful in constructing fast structured features that approximate shift-invariant kernels or in building compact approximate embeddings. There is also an implicit link to kernel and random-feature approximations: prior works cited in the paper show butterfly-type factorizations employed in fast kernel approximations and structured random feature maps. From the perspective developed in the paper — information transmission and structured parameterization — hybrids are a natural extension: the butterfly family restricts a global permuted-sparse connectivity pattern, while LoRA concentrates capacity in a small affine subspace. This alternating (or kaleidoscopic) composition connects directly to known results about representing arbitrary orthogonal transforms. A central conceptual contribution of the paper is the information transmission viewpoint: representing a dense transform as the composition of sparse stagewise connectivities corresponds to a multi-level directed graph on which information must be routed from every input node to every output node. Alternatively, one could select depth \(m\) adaptively per layer by applying a validation-based early stopping on factor addition: start with a small number of butterfly components and incrementally add components until downstream performance plateaus. BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal parameterization has a conceptual connection to metric learning and bilinear similarity models. The paper emphasizes that these alternative structured parameterizations are conceptually close to BOFT and that the literature provides both inspiration and prior art for choosing block sizes, permutations, and factor counts. Concretely one can imagine finetuning with the composite form \[ \bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}, \] where \(\bm{R}(m,b)\) is a BOFT orthogonal transform and \(\bm{A}\bm{B}\) is a low-rank residual (LoRA-style). One could, for example, treat the sparsity pattern as a parameterized family (e.g., a parameterized distribution over permutations or over which blocks are active at each stage) and learn that distribution together with the block parameters. This connects to classical distance metric learning where one learns a bilinear or Mahalanobis metric \(M\) (often symmetric positive definite) to compare vectors; here \(\bm{R}\) plays the role of an orthogonal reorientation applied before the fixed pretrained geometry is used. The paper highlights that acting on the pretrained weights via \[ \bm{z}_i = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top (\bm{R}\bm{x}) \quad\text{or equivalently}\quad \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R} \bm{x} \] can be seen as learning a bilinear similarity \(\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R} \bm{x}\) between a neuron \(\bm{w}^0_i\) and the input \(\bm{x}\), where \(\bm{R}\) is constrained orthogonal. Mixing them is a straightforward way to tune the regularization-expressivity trade-off per layer or per model component, e.g., using BOFT for the bulk of layers where preserving pretrained geometry matters and small LoRA adapters in specific high-impact layers. A natural hybrid design, suggested by comparing these approaches, is to combine both multiplicative structured orthogonal transforms and additive low-rank corrections. structured multiplicative orthogonal updates. The butterfly graph is one efficient such topology drawn from the Cooley–Tukey FFT literature and is well-known in networking as a communication topology that enables all-to-all mixing in \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) stages. First, the alternating-product construction is a way to trade off between expressivity and parameter cost: increasing \(m_1,m_2\) and \(\ell\) increases expressivity, eventually reaching full expressiveness; reducing them yields a smaller, more regular hypothesis class. The orthogonality constraint enforces that \(\bm{R}\) is an isometry — preserving inner products and norms — so BOFT can be interpreted as learning an isometric change of basis that re-expresses pretrained neurons in a coordinate system better suited for the downstream task. LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix with an additive low-rank correction: typically \(\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}\) where \(\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) and \(r\) is small. Recall that a BOFT orthogonal matrix is constructed as a product of orthogonal butterfly components: \[ \bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}, \] where each component \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-wise butterfly orthogonal matrix and the product is taken left-to-right. BOFT’s use of blockwise orthogonal factors aligns with this literature: the same sparse, stagewise mixing that enables fast transforms also supports compact structured embeddings that serve as efficient bases for downstream similarity computations. Conceptually this architecture separates concerns: the orthogonal factor \(\bm{R}\) preserves spectral properties and neuron relational geometry (pairwise angles) and can perform coordinated reorientation of the feature space, while the low-rank additive term supplies targeted, high-capacity corrections in specific downstream directions. The extensions and connections discussed above remain faithful to the paper’s core messages: (1) alternating products of butterfly factors (kaleidoscope-like constructions) enhance expressivity and can reach the full orthogonal group; (2) butterfly-style sparse factorization sits naturally beside other structured families (low-rank, pixelated/monarch variants) and combining such families is a sensible hybrid strategy; (3) the information transmission framework invites exploration of alternative network topologies from networking theory; (4) learning or selecting structure automatically and connecting BOFT to metric learning and kernel approximation are promising directions that the paper highlights, leaving systematic empirical evaluation and algorithmic development for future work. The paper explicitly suggests that the information transmission framework makes it natural to look beyond the butterfly and borrow from the computer networking literature where network topologies are carefully designed for efficient broadcast, multicast, and all-to-all communication. In particular, the paper states that when the parameters are set to full depth and minimal block size (specifically \(m_1=m_2=\log d\), \(b_1=b_2=2\), and \(\ell=d-1\)), the generalized product \(\bm{R}^G\) can represent any element of the orthogonal group on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Any automatic selection method needs to preserve orthogonality constraints on retained blocks (for BOFT this is straightforward when blocks are parameterized by Cayley-transformed skew-symmetric matrices) and to account for the cost of multiplying additional sparse components during training. Translating those topologies into sparse factorization patterns yields alternative structured parameterizations: each topology induces its own schedule of sparse stagewise connectivities and therefore its own family of composable transforms. By situating BOFT in this conceptual neighborhood — between metric learning (bilinear similarities) and fast structured feature approximations — the paper suggests reasons why orthogonal multiplicative finetuning can lead to better generalization and training stability: preserving singular values stabilizes optimization and retaining structured mixing patterns can provide useful inductive biases analogous to those exploited by classical transforms in signal processing. Chapter 11: Extensions and Connections  A direct and important generalization of BOFT appears in the paper as a route to increasing expressivity while retaining structured parameter efficiency. This observation is summarized informally by Theorem 1 in the paper: by composing enough butterfly factors (and their transposes) one recovers universal coverage of the orthogonal group. One weakness of fixed structured parameterizations is the need to choose hyperparameters such as block size \(b\), number of stages \(m\), and the particular permutations or sparsity pattern. The paper presents BOFT as a principled step toward parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning, grounded in an information-transmission view and implemented via butterfly factorization. Thus, BOFT can be read as an instance of this broader design pattern with two specific choices: orthogonality enforced on each block (via Cayley transforms or small rotations) and the particular Cooley-Tukey style interconnection. Because BOFT preserves singular values of \(\bm{W}^0\), the multiplicative part maintains stable spectral norms; the low-rank tail can then adapt magnitudes where necessary without requiring \(\bm{R}\) to be highly expressive. The paper does not provide a concrete learned-structure algorithm, but frames such questions — how to search or learn sparsity patterns, how deep to make the factorization per layer, and how to allocate the parameter budget across layers — as promising directions. The paper cites prior work that learned fast linear transforms using butterfly-like parameterizations (e.g., DAO et al.'s kaleidoscope and related "pixelated" or "monarch" variants), and it distinguishes BOFT from attempts that reparameterize network weights directly for training-from-scratch. The BOFT construction explicitly relies on permuted block-diagonal factors (butterfly components) whose permutations enable dense final connectivity with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges when \(b\) is small. The information transmission view reframes this choice as selecting a multilevel directed graph that guarantees full source-to-sink reachability while minimizing the count of trainable edges subject to orthogonality constraints. From this perspective several automatic strategies naturally suggest themselves as extensions consistent with the paper's framework. A topology with smaller per-stage degree but more stages may yield slightly different parameter/speed trade-offs than the butterfly; a topology with more symmetric routing may provide desirable inductive biases for certain data modalities. BOFT and LoRA represent two distinct philosophies for parameter-efficient adaptation. Monarch and pixelated-butterfly style parameterizations follow the same high-level pattern: express a dense transform as a product of sparse factors, often incorporating permutations and small dense subblocks.
