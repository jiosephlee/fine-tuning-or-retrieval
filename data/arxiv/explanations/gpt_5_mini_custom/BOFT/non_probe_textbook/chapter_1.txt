Motivation and Landscape of Budget-Conscious Adaptation

# Why adapting foundation systems is hard: scale, resource demands, and limited labeled examples

Modern foundation systems are built at a scale that yields broad capabilities, but that very scale also creates friction when one wants to tailor them for a new objective. These systems contain huge collections of numeric weights that were set during a lengthy initial phase. Changing many of those numeric entries directly requires substantial computational effort and long runs on large clusters of processors. Beyond raw compute, adapting at large scale often requires curated labeled examples for the new task; in many practical situations only a modest pool of such examples is available, which raises the risk that the adaptation will over-specialize to the few examples and lose the broad behavior learned previously.

There are also deployment constraints that amplify the difficulty. Many applications demand that any changes made for a new task introduce little or no extra cost when the system is used in practice. Inference-time slowdowns, larger memory footprints, or the need for special runtime support can render an otherwise promising adaptation strategy impractical. Finally, the optimization dynamics when many numeric entries are allowed to move can be unstable: large updates can destroy the useful geometric relationships encoded in the original weight collections and lead to brittle final behavior. Taken together, these factors create a strong incentive to find ways to adapt large foundation systems that move far fewer numeric entries, preserve the useful structure of the original instantiation, and impose minimal deployment overhead.

# Three families of efficient adaptation: direct weight adjustment, plug-in modules, and input-prefix tuning

Practitioners have converged on three broad approaches for adapting large systems while keeping the number of adjustable quantities small and avoiding heavy runtime costs.

The first approach directly modifies a subset or a reparameterized form of the original weight arrays. The key idea is to leave the bulk of the original numeric content frozen and only expose a compact set of adjustable quantities that interact multiplicatively or additively with the frozen weights. Because the frozen bulk remains untouched, these adaptations can often be merged into the original weights after the adaptation process, producing no extra inference overhead.

The second approach inserts small auxiliary modules into the processing chain. These plug-in modules have few adjustable entries and are positioned so that, at inference time, their effect can be applied with minimal extra computation. Conceptually, one enriches the fixed backbone with a lightweight addendum that steers behavior toward the new objective. The modules are trained while the backbone stays fixed, and at inference they either remain as a small extra component or are merged into the backbone if desired.

The third approach leaves the internal numeric entries unchanged and instead prepends or appends a small set of exemplars or prefix vectors to the input stream. These input-side tunable items shift the internal activations and thereby change the system’s responses. Because only input-side artifacts are adjusted, this family can be extremely compact and, in many setups, requires no changes to runtime kernels.

Each family trades off where the adjustable footprint sits — inside the stored weights, as a separate small module, or at the input — and practitioners pick among them depending on constraints of compute, memory, and deployment.

# Design goals for efficient adaptation: small tunable footprint, stability, transfer performance, and zero added runtime cost

When designing an efficient adaptation method, several desiderata guide choices and trade-offs.

First, the footprint of adjustable quantities should be small compared with the size of the foundation system. A compact footprint reduces storage of task-specific artifacts, lowers the compute needed during adaptation, and limits the number of degrees of freedom that can overfit to scarce labeled examples.

Second, adaptation should preserve the favorable optimization properties of the initial system. Methods that maintain key spectral or geometric attributes of the original weight arrays tend to show more stable optimization trajectories and avoid sudden drops in performance. Stability here means that the optimizer can make progress without causing large, destabilizing shifts in how internal activations relate to each other.

Third, the adapted system should transfer well to unseen cases pertinent to the downstream objective. In practice this means the adaptation mechanism should act as a gentle inductive bias: expressive enough to capture the new behavior, yet constrained enough to discourage memorization of the small adaptation set.

Finally, it is highly desirable for an adaptation scheme to introduce no extra latency at inference time. Approaches that can be collapsed into the original stored weights, or require only trivial runtime overhead, are preferred in production settings where responsiveness and memory are critical.

A good method balances these goals rather than optimizing any single one in isolation.

# LoRA versus OFT at a glance: additive compressed pathway versus multiplicative constrained transform

Two influential reparameterization strategies exemplify contrasting philosophies about how to keep the tunable footprint small while adapting large systems.

One strategy injects a compact additive pathway into a weight array. In this picture, a small set of internal linear maps is trained and their product is added to the frozen weight array. The trained product has a narrow internal width, so the total number of adjustable entries is kept small. Operationally, this is an additive correction: the system’s forward computation uses the frozen weight plus a compact learned adjustment. This design is easy to implement, has low adaptation cost, and often yields strong empirical performance on many tasks with a modest number of adjustable entries.

The alternative strategy treats adaptation as a constrained multiplicative change to the frozen weights. Instead of adding a correction, an orthogonal transform is learned and multiplied with the frozen weight on the left. Because an orthogonal transform preserves directional relations among the column vectors of the frozen weight array, this multiplicative change tends to maintain the internal geometry that encoded useful behavior during the initial fit. That geometric preservation often translates into more stable adaptation and better transfer to held-out cases. However, unconstrained left-multiplication by a free orthogonal transform is expensive: an unconstrained orthogonal matrix in the full ambient size carries a large collection of adjustable entries, which defeats the goal of keeping the tunable footprint small.

Thus the two approaches differ in whether the adjustable content is an additive compact correction or a multiplicative structured transform. The additive compact pathway leans on compact internal width, while the multiplicative transform leans on structure that preserves geometry.

# Limitations that motivate BOFT: OFT’s large adjustable footprint and fixed partitioning biases

The multiplicative, geometry-preserving strategy has attractive stability and transfer properties, but it runs into a practical bottleneck when one attempts to make it compact. A naive orthogonal transform at full ambient size carries a vast number of adjustable entries. A pragmatic fix that has been used is to restrict the orthogonal transform to act independently on disjoint groups of coordinates: each group gets its own small orthogonal transform, and these groupwise transforms are arranged along the diagonal of a larger block matrix. This reduces the adjustable footprint dramatically because each small group transform has far fewer adjustable entries than a full-size transform.

While groupwise diagonalization saves adjustable capacity, it introduces a rigid inductive bias: coordinates are partitioned by their indices and there is no interaction across groups during adaptation. That rigidity can prevent the transform from approximating classic global linear mappings that are useful in many tasks, and it forces the adaptation to treat certain directions as forever independent. In other words, the very sparsity pattern that yields compactness also limits expressive reach and prevents the multiplicative strategy from recovering many sensible global transforms.

The desire to get the best of both worlds — the stability and geometric preservation of a multiplicative orthogonal change plus a compact adjustable footprint that still allows global interactions — motivates a different design. Rather than choosing a fixed partition, one can represent a dense orthogonal transform as a product of many sparse orthogonal factors whose sparsity patterns are arranged so that information can flow across coordinates over several multiplicative stages. By distributing interaction across stages, one trades extra multiplicative steps for far fewer adjustable entries in total. Inspired by fast structured transforms that efficiently route information across coordinates, this approach reduces the adjustable footprint while permitting global mixing of coordinates, thereby addressing the twin limitations of raw orthogonal transforms and rigid blockwise restrictions. This trade — more staged computation during adaptation in return for a smaller stored task-specific footprint and retention of desirable geometric properties — is the central idea that underlies the butterfly-inspired orthogonal factorization approach.
