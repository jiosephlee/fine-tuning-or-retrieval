Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles, Variants, and Limitations

# OFT forward pass and identity start

Orthogonal finetuning works by left-multiplying an existing linear mapping with a learned orthonormal operator before applying that mapping to an input. If the original, frozen linear mapping is written as $W_{\mathrm{init}}$ and an input is $x$, the modified forward computation produces an output $z$ via the compact expression
\[
z = \big(R\,W_{\mathrm{init}}\big)^\top x,
\]
where $R$ denotes the learned orthonormal operator and the superscript $\top$ denotes transpose. In words: the columns of the frozen mapping are first rotated by $R$, and then the rotated columns interact with the input in the usual way.

A key practical choice is how to start the finetuning process. OFT sets $R$ to the identity operator at the start, so that the initial model behavior is exactly the same as the frozen mapping. This identity start keeps the initial output unchanged and gives the learning procedure a conservative beginning: the system only departs from the original mapping as the finetuning signal accumulates.

This multiplicative approach contrasts with additive alternatives that modify the frozen mapping by adding a corrective term. The multiplicative left action has a geometric flavor: it changes how the frozen columns relate to one another while keeping their relative magnitudes intact at the outset.

# Cayley form and skew-symmetric operator

To maintain the orthonormal constraint on $R$ during optimization, one convenient analytic representation is the Cayley form. In this representation $R$ is expressed from an auxiliary skew-symmetric linear object $Q$ through the Cayley map:
\[
R = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
and $Q$ satisfies the skew condition
\[
Q^\top = -Q.
\]
Because of this skew condition, the Cayley map yields an operator $R$ that is orthonormal whenever the inverse exists. In practical finetuning, one works with $Q$ as the quantity that is adjusted by the optimizer; the Cayley formula then produces an $R$ that automatically satisfies the orthonormal constraint at every step.

The identity start is achieved simply by setting the initial auxiliary object to the null operator, so that the Cayley map returns the identity. During finetuning the adjustable entries of $Q$ evolve and induce a smooth path of orthonormal operators $R$. This approach has two attractive consequences. First, the orthonormal structure is enforced analytically rather than via a projection step, which simplifies constrained updates. Second, because the Cayley map is continuous around the identity, small changes in $Q$ lead to small, controlled changes in $R$, giving a stable trajectory early in finetuning.

# Partitioned-group OFT: structure, adjustable-values, and representational capacity

The full orthonormal operator acting on a space of dimension $D$ is highly expressive but also has a large count of adjustable scalar entries. To reduce that count, a common variant partitions the coordinate axes of the mapped space into a collection of groups and applies an independent orthonormal operator to each group. Concretely, imagine splitting the $D$ coordinates into several contiguous segments and letting a separate orthonormal operator act within each segment while leaving cross-segment coupling absent. This yields a structured operator that is the direct sum of small orthonormal operators, so the overall transformation can be written informally as a composition of independent transformations that act on disjoint coordinate subsets.

That structure sharply reduces the number of adjustable scalars: instead of having to specify on the order of $D^{\text{two}}$ values for an unconstrained orthonormal operator, the grouped variant requires only the sum of the squared sizes of the groups. If each group has size $S$ and the number of groups is $G$ so that $S\cdot G = D$, the total adjustable count scales like $G\cdot S^{\text{two}}$, which is much smaller when $S$ is modest. This trade produces a practical lever: by changing group size one can trade representational capacity against the total number of adjustable scalars to be optimized.

However, that economy comes at a representational cost. The grouped structure forbids any mixing of information across different groups: a column whose coordinates are split across groups cannot influence coordinates that lie in a different group under the learned orthonormal action. As a result, some global linear transforms that rely on coordinated mixing across many coordinates cannot be represented unless one of the groups spans the entire space.

# Structural prior and limits of partitioning

Viewing the grouped structure as a structural prior helps to see both its rationale and its limitations. On the positive side, the prior encourages conservative changes that alter only local coordinate relationships; this can reduce overfitting in settings where the downstream adjustments should be local refinements of the original mapping. Starting from the identity and allowing only small independent rotations inside each group makes the learning problem easier and often yields stable behavior.

On the negative side, fixing the partition by coordinate index is arbitrary with respect to the semantic structure of features. Important cross-coordinate interactions often do not respect simple contiguous partitions, so the grouped restriction may cut useful connections and thus limit final performance. Moreover, choosing a good partitioning is nontrivial in practice: there is no universal rule that tells which coordinates should be grouped together, and hand-crafted choices must be validated empirically. In tasks that require dense mixing across many coordinates, the grouped variant will be unable to reach the best transforms unless the grouping is relaxed to include larger groups, which reintroduces a higher adjustable count.

# Empirical trade-offs: OFT versus narrow-factor additive updates

Two families of light-touch finetuning stand out in practice. One family, represented by narrow-factor additive updates, modifies the frozen mapping by adding a small correction formed as the composition of two narrow linear maps. Conceptually, this is an additive, low-complexity adjustment: it augments the original mapping with a thin corrective term. The other family, orthonormal left-multiplicative finetuning, transforms the columns of the frozen mapping via an orthonormal operator and thereby preserves certain geometric properties of the original mapping.

Empirical studies show complementary strengths. The multiplicative orthonormal action has the appealing property that it preserves the singular values of the frozen mapping when applied on the left. In practical terms, the magnitude relationships among the original columns are retained under left multiplication by an orthonormal operator; this often yields more stable optimization trajectories and can help generalization because the overall scaling behavior of the mapping is unchanged. The grouped orthonormal variant retains some of that stability while reducing the number of adjustable scalars, which is helpful when compute or optimizer budget is tight.

Narrow-factor additive approaches typically require far fewer adjustable scalars for the same nominal memory budget and can be highly effective when the main adaptation needed is a low-complexity correction. They have shown strong empirical success across many tasks, especially when the downstream change can be captured by a thin correction. However, additive corrections can change the singular structure of the original mapping, and this change can sometimes lead to less stable learning unless care is taken.

Which approach is preferable depends on the task and available budget. If preserving the original mapping’s geometric structure and ensuring stable finetuning are primary concerns, the orthonormal multiplicative route is attractive. If extreme economy of adjustable scalars is required and the task can be solved with a thin additive correction, the narrow-factor additive route is often competitive. The partitioned orthonormal variant provides a middle ground: it keeps many of the orthonormal benefits while letting practitioners control the number of adjustable scalars through the choice of group granularity.

Closing remark: OFT’s core idea — conservative, geometry-preserving left-multiplicative adaptation — gives a clear conceptual alternative to additive low-complexity updates. The grouped variant makes that idea practical at constrained budgets, but the practitioner must weigh the representational sacrifices of fixed grouping against the stability and inductive advantages that orthonormal transformations bring.
