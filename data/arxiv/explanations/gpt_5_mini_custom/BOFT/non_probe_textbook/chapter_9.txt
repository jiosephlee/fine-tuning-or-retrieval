Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Benchmarks and tasks: large ViTs, LLMs, and text-to-image diffusion
The experiments in the paper span three broad categories of large pretrained systems: vision transformers for visual recognition, large autoregressive networks for language modeling, and diffusion-based generators for text-conditional image synthesis. For vision, the target tasks include standard classification and transfer recognition tasks where the pretrained vision transformer is adapted to new labels or domains and final classification accuracy is reported. For language, the targets include next-token prediction and handfuls of downstream language tasks where perplexity and task scores measure quality of the adapted network. For generative image work, the targets evaluate how well a base text-conditional generator aligns with new textual concepts or with held-out prompts; generative quality is measured with fidelity/diversity statistics and alignment-type scores specific to text-to-image benchmarks.

Across these settings the paper studies the same class of left-multiplicative transforms applied to frozen base weight matrices. That is, the original linear layer output $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ is replaced by $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal and has a compact factorized form. The experiments test how this approach behaves when the transform uses the butterfly-style factorization (BOFT) with various choices of stage count $m$ and chunk width $b$, and how it compares when the full left transform is updated directly or when alternative compact update strategies are used.

# Baselines: full-weight update, LoRA and variants, OFT partitioned-diagonal, inserted modules
The evaluations compare the proposed factorized orthogonal transform to several widely used adaptation strategies. One reference point is a full-weight update that lets all entries of the linear layers change; this serves as an upper bound on representational flexibility. A popular compact additive scheme that is also included is the low-rank additive update (LoRA), which augments a frozen weight by a product of two low-rank maps that are added to the base. The previously proposed orthogonal left-multiplicative approach with partitioned diagonal form (the original OFT) is evaluated as the orthogonal prior with a grouped diagonal pattern on $\bm{R}$. Finally, approaches that inject small extra modules into the network architecture (often called adapter inserts in the literature) are included for comparison as another way to reduce the count of adjustable scalars while still altering network behavior.

These reference strategies test complementary points along the trade-off: full updates maximize representational richness but carry a high cost in adjustable scalars; additive low-rank updates reduce that cost by restricting updates to a narrow subspace; partitioned-diagonal orthogonal transforms enforce angular preservation with grouped structure; and module inserts change architecture locally. BOFT is evaluated against all of these to show where it lands in the accuracy-versus-compactness trade-off.

# Metrics: accuracy / perplexity / FID and task-alignment measures
To compare methods across modalities the paper reports standard, interpretable metrics suited to each task family. For classification tasks the primary number is top-line classification accuracy on held-out sets. For language experiments the main metric is perplexity on validation or held-out text, complemented by task-specific scores when relevant. For image synthesis the paper reports common generative quality measures such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) along with alignment-type measures that quantify how well generated images match the guiding text prompts (these may include task-specific scoring protocols used in the text-to-image literature).

Beyond these task-level metrics, the authors also track optimization traces (loss curves and validation metric trajectories), and measure the match between target dense orthogonal matrices and their approximations in a controlled simulation. Where comparisons involve compact transforms, the plots use the count of adjustable scalars as the horizontal axis so that accuracy (or FID or perplexity) can be read against compactness. The formula that links design choices to adjustable-scalar count for BOFT is reported in the paper: the number of adjustable scalars for BOFT($m$, $b$) grows linearly in the ambient dimension $d$ and in the number of stages $m$, specifically as
\[ \text{adjustables} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m, \]
which makes the compactness comparison transparent when $d$ and $m$ vary.

# Results overview: BOFT’s accuracy–compactness trade-off improvements
The empirical picture is consistent across the three modality groups. BOFT occupies a favorable region of the trade-off curve: with far fewer adjustable scalars than a full left update it often matches or exceeds the task accuracy (or matches perplexity / improves FID) of other compact schemes. Against low-rank additive updates, BOFT typically yields better generalization when the same budget of adjustable scalars is used; this is attributed to the multiplicative orthogonal prior that preserves inter-neuron angular relations and spectral properties of the frozen base. Compared with the partitioned-diagonal orthogonal transform, BOFT attains dense, global left transformations while keeping a compact factor form; empirically this produces higher final scores for the same compactness budget, showing that the structured inter-stage mixing of info in the butterfly factorization pays off.

In generative image adaptation, BOFT achieves stronger alignment with textual guidance and often yields improved fidelity metrics relative to additive low-rank updates and to group-diagonal orthogonal updates, while using substantially fewer adjustable scalars than a full update. In vision transfer tasks BOFT reaches near-full-update accuracies at a small fraction of the adjustable-scalar count. For language perplexity, BOFT reduces validation perplexity under the same scalar budget compared to low-rank additive baselines. Across modalities, the paper emphasizes that BOFT tends to offer a smoother generalization profile as the compactness budget is varied: its curves interpolate gracefully from highly regular (close to identity) transforms to more expressive dense transforms as $m$ and $b$ are tuned.

# Ablations: effects of $m$, $b$, and multiplicative dropout
The paper studies the role of the two key design knobs in BOFT: the number of stages $m$ (how many sparse orthogonal stages are multiplied) and the chunk width $b$ (the inner grouping width used in the block-wise butterfly building blocks). Increasing $m$ deepens the factorization and allows more cross-dimension mixing; for fixed $b$ and dimension $d$, larger $m$ increases the number of adjustable scalars and hence representational richness. Increasing $b$ widens the per-stage groups, which also increases the per-stage degrees of freedom. In practical terms a small $b$ with moderate $m$ often gives the best compactness: the staged sparse mixing can approximate dense transforms while keeping the count of adjustables modest.

The multiplicative dropout scheme introduced for BOFT is also examined in ablation. Because BOFT applies a multiplicative, orthogonal left transform, conventional additive dropout is not directly applicable. The multiplicative dropout randomly replaces entire stages or a fraction of the per-stage groups with identity at each optimization step. The empirical effect is analogous to conventional dropout: it reduces overfitting in low-data or narrow-budget regimes and produces better validation performance. The paper reports that judicious choices of the stage-level and group-level replacement probabilities yield measurable improvements in generalization without harming final expressiveness when the compactness budget is increased.

# Stability analysis: optimization smoothness and convergence behavior
An important experimental finding is that BOFT inherits the favorable spectral properties of orthogonal left transformations. Multiplying the frozen layer on the left with an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values of the original weight block, so the adapted linear map keeps its spectral norm. This preservation stabilizes the optimization dynamics: loss and validation metric traces are smoother, gradients exhibit less extreme scaling, and optimization tends to avoid abrupt jumps that can occur when all entries are updated freely. Compared to low-rank additive updates, BOFT typically shows less variance in validation trajectories across random seeds and hyperparameter jitter, indicating more stable convergence.

The paper also notes a small overhead at optimization time for BOFT because the factorized orthogonal transform requires multiple sparse multiplies at each forward/backward pass. However, this cost is confined to the adaptation phase: after adaptation the left factors can be consolidated into the frozen base, producing no inference overhead. Thus, the optimization-time overhead is a one-time expense that buys compactness and stability during adaptation.

# Approximation study: 512\times512 orthogonal matrix fitting curves
To isolate representational questions the authors run a controlled approximation experiment: a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ with $d=512$ is sampled, and various compact forms attempt to approximate it. The objective is the Frobenius-norm error between the target dense orthogonal and the compactly represented orthogonal. Curves plot approximation error versus adjustable-scalar count. BOFT variants trace markedly better error-versus-budget curves than partitioned-diagonal orthogonal factorizations with the same per-stage group width. For example, a BOFT configuration with many stages and small $b$ attains the same approximation error as a partitioned-diagonal configuration with much larger per-group width while using far fewer adjustables.

This controlled fit study reproduces the intuitive trade-offs: increasing $m$ (more stages) reduces approximation error at fixed $b$; increasing $b$ reduces error at fixed $m$. Importantly, BOFT’s staged sparse mixing yields a pareto front that lies below the partitioned-diagonal front: for the same adjustable-scalar budget BOFT attains lower approximation error, which helps explain the downstream empirical gains seen in vision, language, and generative adaptation tasks.

# Synthesis and practical guidance
The multi-modal experiments support a consistent story. Enforcing an orthogonal left transform and representing it with a butterfly-style factorization yields a compact, stable, and expressive family of transforms that adapt large pretrained networks across different task families. Practitioners should view the two controls $m$ and $b$ as a compactness–richness dial: increase $m$ or $b$ if the task requires more expressive adaptation and computational budget during optimization allows it; keep $b$ small and raise $m$ for strong compactness with high mixing effectiveness. Multiplicative dropout is a practical regularizer in low-data regimes. Finally, although optimization has a modest extra cost during adaptation, the orthogonal factors can be merged with the base weights after adaptation to avoid any inference cost.
