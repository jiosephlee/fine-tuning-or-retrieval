BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly

# BOFT definition: $\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$

BOFT constructs a left-side orthogonal transform for a linear mapping by composing a sequence of structured orthogonal stages. Concretely, an orthogonal operator of dimension $d$ is written as
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m)}\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m-1)}\cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,1)}.
\]
Each factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is a partitioned orthogonal operator formed by tiling many small butterfly units along the main axis, so that every such factor is orthonormal in the sense that
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top=\mathbf{I}_d.
\]
The small building block used inside each partition is a compact two-by-two style orthonormal rotation (or its blockwise extension of size $2b\times 2b$ when a block width $b$ is used). These tiny rotations are arranged in the classic butterfly wiring pattern, and each stage permutes and mixes entries in a structured way. The full product therefore represents an orthonormal transform that can move information from any input coordinate to any output coordinate after all stages are applied.

Describing the construction at a conceptual level: each stage performs local pairwise orthonormal mixes of groups of coordinates and then a fixed wiring permutation shuffles which coordinates meet at the next stage. Repeating this local mix-and-shuffle pattern over $m$ stages yields a global orthonormal transform that is both structured and dense as a whole, while each stage itself only contains many sparse local mixes.

# Special cases

The BOFT family interpolates smoothly between the very local, groupwise transforms and an unconstrained full orthonormal update. When only a single stage is used, $\mathbf{R}(1,b)$ applies an orthonormal transform independently to each contiguous group of width $b$; in other words, the full $d$ coordinates are partitioned into groups of size $b$ and each group receives its own orthonormal rotation. This recovers the familiar groupwise orthonormal update strategy that treats disjoint coordinate groups independently.

At the other extreme, if the group width equals the full dimension, $\mathbf{R}(1,d)$ is simply a single unconstrained orthonormal operator on all $d$ coordinates. That setting is the unrestricted orthonormal left-multiplicative update: it offers maximal flexibility but comes with the largest set of free scalars to specify the transform.

Between these extremes, BOFT uses more than one stage with modest group widths. As the number of stages increases and the group width decreases, the composed operator becomes capable of representing dense orthonormal transforms while keeping the number of free scalars much smaller than the unrestricted case.

# Dense transforms with far fewer free scalars

A key design point is that a dense orthonormal operator can be realized by composing several sparse orthonormal stages. A useful choice that yields a dense result with a logarithmic count of stages is
\[
m=\log\!\bigg(\frac{2d}{b}\bigg),
\]
with group width $b\ge 2$. When $b=2$ this recovers the classic radix pattern from fast transforms: each stage mixes small pairs and the composition over $\log d$ stages produces a dense operator whose wiring mirrors the butterfly network. With larger $b$ the same principle holds, but the local mixes operate on small blocks of width $b$ rather than simple pairs; choosing $b$ trades local expressivity versus the number of stages needed to reach full connectivity across coordinates.

This construction is attractive because the global operator becomes dense after a modest number of stages: information that starts in any coordinate can be routed, stage by stage, to any other coordinate at the final output. The wiring that accomplishes that is the butterfly pattern, which is known for efficient all-to-all mixing while using only sparse local connections at each stage.

# Count of free scalars (effective degrees of freedom)

Because each tiled partition in a stage uses small orthonormal rotations, the total count of free scalar degrees for $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ grows linearly in $d$ and linearly in the number of stages $m$. A compact expression for the effective degrees of freedom is
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
One can see this by noting that each local orthonormal block of width $b$ contributes roughly $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)b$ free scalars, the tiling across the $d$ coordinates yields a factor proportional to $d/b$, and multiplication gives the formula above after simplifying.

To put this in perspective, an unrestricted orthonormal operator on $d$ coordinates requires about $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free scalars, which scales quadratically in $d$. The single-stage groupwise scheme that independently rotates groups of width $b$ requires on the order of $b\,d$ free scalars. BOFT bridges these two regimes: with $b$ small and $m$ on the order of $\log d$, the total grows like $d\log d$, offering a dramatic reduction from the quadratic count while retaining the ability to realize dense transforms.

In asymptotic notation:
\[
\text{unrestricted orthonormal: }\mathcal{O}(d^2),\qquad
\text{groupwise single-stage: }\mathcal{O}(b\,d),\qquad
\text{BOFT with }b=2: \mathcal{O}(d\log d).
\]
Thus BOFT attains much lower complexity in terms of free scalars than a fully unconstrained choice, while giving far greater mixing power than a single-stage groupwise scheme of equal local width.

# Identity initialization

A practical desideratum for gentle adaptation is that the initial composed operator should not alter the original linear mapping. BOFT satisfies this naturally: each stage is constructed so that it can be set to the identity operator by choosing the local rotations to be trivial (for example, by initializing the skew parts that generate rotations to zero). With each stage initialized to identity, the product $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is the identity, and the original linear mapping is unchanged at the start of any subsequent adjustment procedure. This identity-start property enables controlled updates that do not abruptly disturb the initial mapping.

# Zero extra cost at run time

Although BOFT composes its orthonormal operator as a product of several sparse stages during the adaptation process, there is no persistent run-time overhead after the adaptation concludes. Once the composed orthonormal operator $\mathbf{R}$ has been fixed by the optimization used for the task, it can be multiplied into the original left-side linear operator to form a single, updated linear mapping. That folded mapping replaces the original pair of operators for all subsequent forward passes, so execution at deployment uses exactly the same cost as the original mapping. In short, BOFT incurs its modest extra cost only during the adaptation procedure; at deployment the transform is merged and no per-call extra work remains.

# Intuition summary

BOFT attains a practical middle ground: it keeps the adaptation space structured and compact by using many sparse, orthonormal stages wired in the butterfly pattern, yet the overall product can reach dense orthonormal transforms. The number of free scalars grows with $d$ and with the number of stages $m$, and by choosing small local width $b$ together with $m$ on the order of a logarithm in $d$ one gets a dense final operator with only about $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$ degrees of freedom. Identity initialization guarantees a gentle start, and final folding of the learned operator into the base linear mapping means there is no long-term run-time penalty. These features explain why BOFT subsumes the groupwise and the unconstrained orthonormal update schemes as special settings and why it is attractive for compact, stable adaptations of large linear mappings.
