Training BOFT in Practice

# Optimization loop: representing small orthogonal subunits via a skew generator

A stable adaptation loop for BOFT treats each small orthogonal subunit as the exponential-like outcome of a compact antisymmetric generator. Concretely, one works with a skew-symmetric tensor $\bm{Q}$ that lives on the small two-by-two subspace of interest and updates the entries of $\bm{Q}$ during adaptation steps. The orthogonal transform that is applied to the larger linear operator is expressed through a Cayley-style map written as
$\displaystyle \bm{R}=(\mathbb{I}+\bm{Q})(\mathbb{I}-\bm{Q})^{\text{inv}}$,
where the superscript “inv” denotes classical inversion of the indicated operator. This representation has three practical virtues: it starts at exact identity when the generator is set to neutral, it enforces the orthogonality constraint by construction, and it links additive updates on $\bm{Q}$ to multiplicative changes in the final transform, which empirically yields smoother behavior for downstream adaptation.

For the smallest subunit, the skew generator is zero except for an antisymmetric two-by-two pattern inside a small diagonal tile. Writing the two-by-two action in familiar trigonometric form helps intuition:
$\displaystyle
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\[6pt]
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}$,
which one recovers by choosing an appropriate $\bm{Q}$ and applying the Cayley-style map. In practice, rather than storing dense small arrays for all such tiles, it is common to store only the independent scalars that define each local skew entry; these are the tunable quantities that the optimizer updates.

The outer adaptation loop uses any mainstream solver for stochastic objectives. Two practical tips improve robustness. First, perform updates on the skew generators at a finer numerical precision than used elsewhere: apply the solver step in a higher-precision buffer, then cast the resulting generators back to the working precision. Second, for stability of the inversion involved in the Cayley-style map, compute the inverse by solving a linear system (for instance, via a triangular solver when the structure permits) rather than forming an explicit inverse; this avoids amplifying numerical noise when the generator entries are small.

# Multiplicative stochastic masking: random identity substitution for subunits

To regularize the multiplicative update and avoid over-specialization of a small set of subunits, apply multiplicative stochastic masking that occasionally replaces some subunits with exact identity before a forward pass. Implement this in two hierarchical steps. First, randomly pick a small fraction of the top-level components across the whole transform and replace each chosen component by the identity operator for that forward computation. Second, within each remaining top-level component, randomly pick a small fraction of the local tiles and set those tiles to identity. The dual probabilities control coarse and fine sparsification: the first probability reduces reliance on any single big component, while the second dithers the internal structure to encourage redundancy.

When sampling these masks, use a fresh draw per example batch rather than a single global draw per epoch; this encourages the representation to be robust in expectation and prevents deterministic patterning. For deterministic evaluation or deployment, switch all masks off so the full orthogonal composition is active. Finally, implement the masking as in-place substitution of precomputed identity templates rather than conditionally skipping computation; this avoids code-path divergence and keeps throughput predictable.

# Step-size choices and schedule: stability considerations compared to OFT and LoRA

Orthogonal multiplicative adaptation inherits a natural spectral stability because the left multiplication by a true orthogonal transform preserves singular values of the base linear operator. This often permits slightly more aggressive step choices than purely additive updates, but caution is still warranted because the multiplicative nature amplifies certain directions in early iterations.

A practical schedule that balances stability and speed is a short linear warmup of the solver step size followed by a gradual decay toward a small final step. Use a solver with adaptive moments and bias correction for robust, per-tunable scaling; this reduces the need for hand-tuned per-stage gain factors. When comparing to purely additive techniques, practitioners often find the following behavioral guidance useful: begin with a warmup that brings the skew generators out of exact neutrality slowly, then let the adaptive solver handle curvature; reduce the base step-size if you observe erratic loss spikes in the first adaptation epoch.

Regularization that penalizes large entries of the skew generators is useful: rather than applying a direct penalty on the final composed transform, apply a modest quadratic penalty on the generator entries themselves. This keeps the transform close to identity at initialization while still permitting controlled deviation. When using burned-in solvers that maintain per-parameter moment statistics, reset those statistics if the adaptation is resumed from a checkpoint produced under a different step-size regime.

# Module selection: where BOFT helps in large language, vision transformer, and diffusion U-shaped systems

Not all linear operators in a backbone system benefit equally from multiplicative orthogonal adaptation. Practical evidence and intuition point to two categories that typically yield strong returns.

First, projection operators that distribute or gather information across feature coordinates are high-leverage targets. In attention mechanisms, the output projection and the value projection often carry substantial representational responsibility; adapting the left-side transform there can realign how stored features are presented to subsequent computation. Similarly, in the feed-forward pathway, the first projection that expands feature dimensionality and the final projection that collapses it back are natural places to place BOFT.

Second, operators that sit at bottlenecks or at the interface between hierarchical stages are effective locations for BOFT. In vision transformers, mid-stack projections where spatial and channel information get mixed are productive; in U-shaped denoising architectures, transforms in the bottleneck and at skip-connection interfaces allow global adjustments with a small footprint.

As a rule of thumb, prioritize operators that have high input dimensionality or that mediate major routing of information. Reserve adaptation for those modules first, and only expand to others if budget permits, watching for diminishing returns.

# Budgeting tunables across modules: allocating depth and tile size per stage

Two knobs control the expressiveness versus resource usage of BOFT at each target operator: the number of multiplicative components composed in sequence (call this the depth of factorization) and the local tile size used for the small orthogonal subunits. A deeper composition with smaller tiles tends to produce a dense, highly expressive transform while keeping the count of tunable scalars modest. Conversely, a shallow composition with larger tiles gives more local expressivity per tile but raises the tunable-count.

How to allocate a fixed global budget across modules? Start by allocating most of the budget to a handful of high-impact operators (attention output, feed-forward projections, bottleneck transforms). For these, favor moderate depth with small tiles: that configuration usually captures global permutation-like and mixing operations with low unit cost. For peripheral operators (early-stage projections, small-dimension heads), use shallow depth and larger tiles to avoid unnecessary computational overhead.

When adapting very large systems, it can help to use a tapered scheme: early modules get conservative allocations, middle modules get the bulk, and very late modules get a modest allocation to fine-tune final alignment. Monitor held-out performance as you reassign budget: the marginal gain per added tunable typically diminishes, and the optimal split is task-dependent.

# Implementation notes: permutations, batching, reduced precision, and caching structure

Efficient implementation hinges on making the sparse pattern cheap to compute and cheap to apply. The butterfly-style composition relies on fixed index permutations interleaved with small local transforms. Precompute and store the index maps for those permutations once per instantiation; during forward passes, perform an index-based gather/scatter rather than materializing intermediate dense operators. This keeps memory traffic predictable and exposes opportunities for fused kernels.

Group the application of many small local transforms into vectorized kernels so that hardware can process multiple tiles in parallel. When devices support fused gather-plus-local-transform operations, exploit them to reduce intermediate memory movement. For the Cayley-style map, avoid explicit inversion; instead solve the implied linear system with a batched solver on the packed skew generator blocks.

For reduced-precision arithmetic, apply mixed-precision carefully. Keep the skew generators and the small-linear-system solves in a higher format (for example, use a format with extended mantissa during the Cayley solve), and cast the final local orthogonal tiles back to the accelerator’s working format for bulk arithmetic. This provides numerical stability while retaining the throughput benefit of lower formats elsewhere.

Finally, cache the composed permutation pattern and any identity templates used for stochastic masking. When sweeping across many small operators, reuse the same batched kernel and preallocated buffers to avoid allocation overhead.

# Deployment fusion: merging transforms for runtime, serialization, and compatibility

One practical advantage of BOFT is that, once adaptation converges, the learned left-hand orthogonal composition can be absorbed into the stored linear operator so runtime inference runs with a single dense transform. To do this, multiply the base operator on the left by the final orthogonal composition and store the resulting operator as the deployed linear transform. Perform this fusion once, using a numerically stable routine that applies the small permute-and-local-transform sequence directly to the base operator; avoid reconstructing intermediate dense forms that exceed memory capacity.

For serialization, save the fused operator in the format your runtime expects. Also store the original representation of the BOFT composition (index maps, final skew generators) if you plan to resume further adaptation or wish to inspect the adaptation trajectory. When exporting to other runtimes, ensure that the fused operator is compatible with the target numeric format and that any quantization step is applied after fusion to preserve the orthogonal-induced spectral properties.

Compatibility notes: because BOFT preserves singular value magnitudes when applied as a left multiplication, fusing it into the stored transform preserves spectral behavior for downstream components. This is reassuring for deployment: the fused transform will not unexpectedly amplify activation norms relative to the original base operator.

# Practical checklist for reliable runs

- Represent small orthogonal actions via skew generators and apply the Cayley-style map; solve the inversion step by linear solves rather than explicit inversion.
- Use multiplicative stochastic masking at two hierarchical scales; sample per batch and disable for deployment.
- Warm up the solver step-size, then decay; prefer adaptive solvers with moment estimates and modest generator-level regularization.
- Prioritize adaptation targets: high-dimension projection operators, bottleneck and interface transforms.
- Allocate resource budget by trading factorization depth against local tile size; favor depth-plus-small-tiles in high-impact modules.
- Precompute permutation index maps, vectorize small transforms, use mixed precision sensibly with higher precision for the Cayley solve.
- After adaptation, fuse the composition into the stored linear operator for efficient runtime, and serialize both fused and decomposed representations if you may resume adaptation.

Following these principles will make BOFT practical and robust when adapting very large systems: it keeps numerical behavior stable, encourages generalizable adjustments, and permits efficient runtime deployment once adaptation is complete.
