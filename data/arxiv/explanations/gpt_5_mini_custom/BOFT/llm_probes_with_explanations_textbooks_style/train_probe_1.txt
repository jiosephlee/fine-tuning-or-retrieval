Cayley Parameterization and Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Setup

Finetuning large pretrained models benefits from methods that preserve key geometric and spectral properties of the pretrained weights. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) achieves this by transforming neurons in a layer through a shared orthogonal matrix. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, OFT modifies the forward computation from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix learned during finetuning.

The central motivation is to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons. When all neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$) are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, these pairwise angles are provably preserved, which helps retain semantic information gained during pretraining. Moreover, left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, a property that contributes to stable optimization and better generalization.

To maintain orthogonality throughout training, OFT employs a principled reparameterization of $\bm{R}$ that enforces the orthogonality constraint by construction. This is achieved via the Cayley parameterization.

# Orthogonal Matrices and Angle Preservation

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}_d$, where $\bm{I}_d$ is the $d$-dimensional identity matrix. Such matrices preserve inner products, norms, and consequently angles. If $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ denote two neurons (columns) of $\bm{W}^0$, then after transformation they become $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$. Their inner product is
$(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0) = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{w}_j^0 = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{w}_j^0$.
Thus the cosine of the angle between any two neurons is unchanged by $\bm{R}$. This is exactly the relational information OFT aims to preserve during finetuning.

The spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ is also preserved, because if $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is a singular value decomposition, then $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, and the largest singular value (the spectral norm) is unaffected by left multiplication with the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$.

These geometric and spectral invariances motivate directly parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as an orthogonal matrix during finetuning.

# The Cayley Parameterization

A direct parameterization of a dense orthogonal matrix would require careful constraints during optimization. The Cayley parameterization offers a smooth and efficient way to represent an orthogonal matrix in terms of an unconstrained matrix with a specific structure. In OFT and its butterfly-based extension (BOFT), the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$.

The key constraint in this parameterization is the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$. This constraint ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, a natural and convenient initialization that leaves the pretrained model unchanged is $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This identity initialization is essential to start finetuning from the exact pretrained model.

Intuitively, the Cayley parameterization expresses an orthogonal transformation as a rational function of a skew-symmetric matrix. The skew-symmetry is the structural property that makes the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ algebraically emerge from the parameterization.

# Why Skew-Symmetry Ensures Orthogonality

The central fact is that the Cayley parameterization
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$
produces an orthogonal matrix precisely when $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. We now show the sufficiency of this constraint by a straightforward calculation based on this structure.

Assume $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Then
$(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^\top = \bm{I} + \bm{Q}$,
and
$(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} + \bm{Q}^\top = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}$.
Using these relations, compute $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$:
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big)^\top (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
Because $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} + \bm{Q}$, we have $\big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big)^\top = \big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^\top\big)^{-1} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Substituting yields
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
The middle factors multiply to
$(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$.
Similarly, observe that
$(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$.
Therefore,
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
Using $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2) = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$, we obtain
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$.
Thus $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal.

In the Cayley parameterization used for OFT and BOFT, the constraint that $\bm{Q}$ be skew-symmetric is therefore sufficient to ensure that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. This algebraic guarantee eliminates the need to enforce orthogonality through additional projection steps during training.

# Identity Initialization and Training Stability

Orthogonal finetuning aims to start from the exact pretrained model and then adjust in a controlled fashion. With $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the Cayley parameterization gives
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$.
This identity initialization matches the pretrained model at the beginning of finetuning. As training proceeds, the optimization updates $\bm{Q}$ while preserving skew-symmetry, and hence keeps $\bm{R}$ orthogonal throughout.

Maintaining orthogonality during finetuning has two notable advantages within this framework. First, pairwise angles between neurons are preserved when needed, reflecting the relational structure OFT aims to retain. Second, the spectral norm of the weight matrix is preserved by left multiplication with $\bm{R}$, which contributes to stable training dynamics and generalization during finetuning.

# From Block-Diagonal OFT to Dense Orthogonal Transformations

A dense orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ contains $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ free entries, making a direct parameterization inefficient. The original OFT reduces parameter count by adopting a block-diagonal structure $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with small orthogonal blocks $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br=d$. This improves parameter efficiency but imposes a rigid sparsity pattern that transforms disjoint groups of dimensions independently. Such an assumption may reduce expressiveness and introduce arbitrary inductive biases tied to index-based partitioning.

To retain the benefits of orthogonality while generating dense transformations with fewer parameters, the method composes $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. The Cayley parameterization applies at the level of each sparse component, ensuring orthogonality of the product.

# Information Transmission Perspective

Representing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse orthogonal matrices can be understood through an information transmission view. Consider factoring
$\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$.
This corresponds to transmitting information across a grid of $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where each $\bm{B}_i$ encodes edges from layer $i$ to layer $i+1$. A dense $\bm{R}$ requires that every source node on the first layer can reach every target node on the last layer. The aim is to achieve dense end-to-end connectivity with as few trainable edges as possible, subject to orthogonality constraints on each $\bm{B}_i$.

Orthogonality constrains the edge patterns, notably requiring full-rank connectivity between adjacent layers. Because orthogonal matrices have structured dependencies among their entries, this view highlights how to select sparse patterns that are both feasible and efficient for composing into a dense transformation. Within this framework, the butterfly structure emerges as a highly efficient pattern for information exchange.

# Butterfly Factorization and BOFT

The butterfly structure, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, yields a sparse factorization of a dense matrix using $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ nonzero elements across $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ factors. Define a butterfly factor
$\bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$,
and build a butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively with block-diagonal butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) leverages this structure to parameterize $\bm{R}$ as a product of orthogonal butterfly components. Each butterfly component can be permuted to a block-diagonal arrangement of $2 \times 2$ blocks, which are then parameterized as orthogonal via the Cayley transform. In the block setting, $2b \times 2b$ blocks are similarly parameterized orthogonally. The overall composition
$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$
is orthogonal because each factor is orthogonal. This yields a dense orthogonal transformation with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters when $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, a significant reduction relative to a fully dense parameterization.

Crucially, the Cayley parameterization is the mechanism that guarantees orthogonality of each butterfly component. For each $2 \times 2$ or $2b \times 2b$ block, one parameterizes it as $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ with a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, ensuring that the entire factor is orthogonal. The product of orthogonal butterfly components remains orthogonal, providing a global guarantee for $\bm{R}(m,b)$.

# Preserving Structure During Finetuning

The BOFT composition allows identity initialization: set every skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{0}$ in all components so that every factor is the identity, and hence $\bm{R}(m,b) = \bm{I}$. During training, the parameters in each skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ are updated, and the Cayley transform keeps the factors orthogonal at every step. Because the overall update is multiplicative, BOFT proposes a multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces selected blocks within butterfly components by identity blocks, which regularizes the learned orthogonal transformation without breaking orthogonality.

This structured parameterization balances expressivity and regularity. BOFT recovers block-diagonal OFT as the special case $m=1$, and recovers unconstrained OFT with a full dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ when $m=1$ and $b=d$. With deeper butterfly compositions, BOFT attains dense orthogonal transformations with far fewer parameters than a fully dense orthogonal matrix.

# Spectral Properties and Stability

Both OFT and BOFT multiply an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ to the left of $\bm{W}^0$, yielding $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This leaves the spectral norm unchanged. More generally, left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves all singular values of the weight matrix, since it only rotates the left singular vectors. Maintaining these spectral properties contributes to stable training and good generalization during finetuning.

From the neuron perspective, the orthogonal transformation preserves pairwise angles among neurons, keeping the relational structure learned during pretraining intact. From the matrix perspective, it constrains updates to a well-behaved manifold of transformations with strong regularity. The Cayley parameterization, together with skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$, underpins this behavior by guaranteeing orthogonality throughout optimization.

# Conclusion: The Role of Skew-Symmetry in Cayley Parameterization

Within the OFT and BOFT frameworks, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized via the Cayley transform
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
The constraint required to ensure that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal is that $\bm{Q}$ be skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This skew-symmetry is the algebraic condition that makes the Cayley parameterization yield $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, and it enables identity initialization by setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$. In OFT, this guarantees that neuron angles and spectral norms are preserved during finetuning. In BOFT, it further enables the composition of multiple sparse orthogonal butterfly components into a dense orthogonal transformation with far fewer parameters than a fully dense orthogonal matrix.
