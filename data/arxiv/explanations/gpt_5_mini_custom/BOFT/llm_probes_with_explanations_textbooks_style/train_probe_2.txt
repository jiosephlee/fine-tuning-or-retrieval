Chapter: Orthogonal Finetuning and the Semantics of Angles in Pretrained Representations

# Pretrained Representations and the Role of Angles

A central challenge in adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks is preserving the knowledge acquired during pretraining while allowing sufficient flexibility to specialize. The paper motivates a principled approach by focusing on what it calls the relational information of weight matrices, namely the pairwise angles between neurons. The method of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) enforces that neurons in the same layer are transformed by a shared orthogonal matrix, which provably preserves these pairwise angles throughout finetuning. This design choice is explicitly connected in the paper to preserving “semantic knowledge from pretraining,” suggesting the following assumption: the pairwise angles between neuron vectors encode semantic structure. In other words, when the columns of a pretrained weight matrix represent neurons, the angles among these columns capture relationships that are semantically meaningful for the model’s internal representations.

This assumption provides the intuitive foundation for OFT. If angles among neuron vectors encode how features relate to each other, then preserving these angles during adaptation maintains the pretrained model’s relational structure. The paper contrasts this principled preservation with the common practice of using a small learning rate in full finetuning, which only indirectly encourages small changes. By instead applying a shared orthogonal transformation to an entire layer, OFT aims to preserve the geometry of the neuron set under angle-based measurements, thereby preserving pretraining knowledge in a more targeted way. This is especially important for large models where catastrophic drift from pretrained structure can harm generalization.

The notion that angles carry semantic information in this context is operationalized through the choice of orthogonal transformations. A real matrix is orthogonal if it preserves inner products, and therefore preserves pairwise angles. If two neuron vectors are initially aligned or opposed in a manner that reflects learned structure, mapping the entire neuron set by an orthogonal matrix preserves those alignments. As a result, OFT treats the pretrained layer as a reference frame and only rotates or reflects it, thereby preserving the relational geometry that encodes semantics while still allowing adaptation.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Mechanism and Parameterization

To formalize the idea, consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, and output $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. The original forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. OFT reparameterizes the finetuned layer as
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a trainable orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons (i.e., across all columns of $\bm{W}^0$). This multiplicative update contrasts with additive low-rank approaches, and its key property is that $\bm{R}$ preserves inner products between neuron vectors. If $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ are two neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$), then
$\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0, \bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 \rangle = \langle \bm{w}_i^0, \bm{w}_j^0 \rangle$,
so their angle is preserved.

To maintain orthogonality during training, the paper uses the Cayley parameterization. Let $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Then
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$
is orthogonal. This parameterization allows unconstrained updates in the skew-symmetric space that map to valid orthogonal matrices. OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ at the identity to match the pretrained model at the start of finetuning, which parallels the zero-initialization used in additive low-rank methods.

The original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for parameter efficiency. If $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br = d$, the number of trainable parameters reduces substantially. However, this block structure enforces that different groups of coordinates are transformed independently. Because the partition is index-based and fixed, it imposes a sparsity pattern that can reduce expressiveness and introduce inductive biases unrelated to the learned geometry. In particular, it cannot recover classic dense linear transforms and limits how information can mix across groups.

# Why Preserving Angles Supports Semantic Preservation and Stability

The paper’s guiding principle is that preserving pairwise angles among neurons preserves semantic relationships learned during pretraining. Under this lens, orthogonal finetuning maintains the relative orientations among neurons, hence the relational structure that underpins the model’s competence. This directly follows from orthogonality preserving inner products and angles. If a set of neuron vectors encodes relationships such as similarity, complementarity, or orthogonality that the pretrained model relies on, then post-multiplying the entire set by an orthogonal matrix preserves these relationships exactly.

Beyond relational geometry, the paper also analyzes spectral properties. Writing the pretrained weight as $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ with orthogonal $\bm{U},\bm{V}$ and diagonal $\bm{\Sigma}$, OFT and BOFT produce finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not alter singular values, the spectral norm is preserved:
$\|\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2$.
Preserving the spectral norm supports training stability and generalization according to the paper’s cited discussion. Together with angle preservation, this ensures both the relational and magnitude-sensitive aspects of the pretrained operator are maintained in key respects.

The bilinear similarity interpretation further clarifies the role of orthogonality. If $\bm{w}_i^0$ is the $i$-th neuron, the model computes a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$. Learning $\bm{R}$ under the strong orthogonality constraint means the adaptation adjusts the interaction space in a way that preserves length and angle structure. This reinforces the assumption that the model benefits from keeping the semantic geometry intact while slightly rotating the representational frame to better match the downstream task.

# From Block-Diagonal Limitations to Dense Orthogonal Transformations

While block-diagonal OFT reduces parameter count, its independence across blocks restricts the model’s ability to mix information globally across the feature dimensions. Under the assumption that angles encode semantics across the entire neuron space, it is desirable for the adaptation to be dense so that all coordinates can interact. The paper recasts the problem of producing a dense orthogonal matrix from sparse factors as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph.

Let $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be factorized as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, where each $\bm{B}_i$ is sparse. One can associate to this factorization a directed layered graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where edges between successive layers are present exactly when the corresponding entry in $\bm{B}_i$ is non-zero. A dense final matrix $\bm{R}$ requires that every source node in layer $1$ can reach every sink node in layer $m+1$. The paper articulates two desiderata: dense connectivity from sources to sinks, and a minimal number of free edges subject to orthogonality. Orthogonality imposes additional constraints because each factor must be full-rank and orthogonal, which bounds how edges may be arranged. This graph view reframes the design of sparse factorizations into an efficient information routing problem, enabling dense transformations with fewer total parameters through structured sparsity.

A single dense orthogonal matrix uses on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. The graph perspective reveals that by composing multiple sparse orthogonal factors, one can achieve dense connectivity with substantially fewer effective edges. This insight motivates the use of butterfly structures, which are renowned for enabling efficient global mixing.

# Butterfly Factorization and the BOFT Framework

The butterfly structure, central to the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform, exchanges information between distant coordinates through a logarithmic-depth sequence of sparse operations. The paper imports this idea to parameterize orthogonal matrices efficiently. The construction begins with a butterfly factor of size $k \times k$,
$\bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}$,
and recursively builds a $d \times d$ butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ for $d = 2^N$ via block-diagonal compositions and permutations:
$\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$,
where $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors of size $k$.

To ensure orthogonality, each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is parameterized so that it is orthogonal. This is straightforward because the non-zero pattern of each component can be seen as a permutation of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, and each $2 \times 2$ block can be made orthogonal using the Cayley transform or a $2$-dimensional rotation parameterization. The paper generalizes this to block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ by replacing scalar entries with $b \times b$ matrices and ensuring each $2b \times 2b$ block is orthogonal.

With these ingredients, the finetuning forward pass becomes
$\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot \bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}$,
with
$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$,
and each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ orthogonal. The notation sets $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m-i+1})$. This composition is called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases. When $m=1$, BOFT($1,b$) reproduces block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. When $m=1$ and $b=d$, it recovers the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix. When $m = \log \tfrac{2d}{b}$ and block size $b$ is fixed, BOFT produces a dense orthogonal matrix with butterfly structure.

The parameter count reflects the efficiency gains. BOFT($m,b$) uses $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a $d \times n$ linear layer. Setting $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ yields $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, in stark contrast to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full orthogonal matrix. Identity initialization is used so the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model. The paper also proposes a multiplicative Dropout tailored to the multiplicative form: randomly selected butterfly components and diagonal blocks within components are replaced with identity matrices during training, thereby regularizing the effective transformation without breaking orthogonality where it is retained.

# Expressivity, Regularity, and the Inductive Bias of Butterfly Structure

A core question is whether the butterfly-structured orthogonal parameterization is expressive enough to approximate useful dense orthogonal matrices while preserving the angle structure. The paper reports simulations approximating random dense orthogonal matrices and shows that BOFT matches or exceeds the parameter-efficiency of block-diagonal OFT: by increasing depth $m$ and using smaller block size $b$, BOFT attains lower approximation error with fewer parameters than block-diagonal structures of larger blocks. This demonstrates that butterfly factorization can recover richer global mixing per parameter than independent block rotations.

The paper formalizes a relative expressivity guarantee: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Moreover, by composing butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy, one can in principle represent the entire orthogonal group for sufficiently deep compositions and appropriate choices of $(m,b)$ per factor. However, the paper emphasizes that maximal expressivity is not the goal in finetuning; rather, the right balance between expressivity and regularity is crucial for generalization. BOFT’s structured parameterization constrains the hypothesis class to a subset of the orthogonal group with strong inductive biases.

These inductive biases are argued to be beneficial. Butterfly structures underlie many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier and Hadamard transforms, and thus encode global mixing patterns that are both efficient and widely useful. Under the assumption that angles encode semantics, a butterfly-structured orthogonal mapping preserves semantic geometry while enabling controlled, globally mixing adaptations that avoid the rigid independence of block-diagonal OFT and the unconstrained freedom of full orthogonal finetuning. The sparse factorization also introduces implicit regularization effects through its compositional structure.

# Stability, Spectral Properties, and the Geometry of Learning

OFT and BOFT share favorable spectral properties because they act by left-multiplying pretrained weights with an orthogonal matrix. As noted earlier, this preserves singular values and thus the spectral norm. The paper connects such preservation to improved training stability and generalization. The retention of pairwise angles among neurons preserves semantic relations, while spectral norm preservation controls operator amplification, helping prevent pathological behaviors during optimization.

The bilinear similarity viewpoint offers an additional geometric interpretation. If the model computes scores of the form $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$, then learning $\bm{R}$ under orthogonality preserves length and angle relations in the representational space. Since the assumption is that angles encode semantics in pretrained features, this design ensures that finetuning does not distort semantic relationships, but instead reorients them in a globally coherent way.

# Practical Implications for Parameter-Efficient Adaptation

The motivation for BOFT is twofold: preserve the semantic geometry believed to be encoded by angles, and do so with far fewer parameters than a full orthogonal matrix. The information transmission framework clarifies why butterfly factorization is attractive: it achieves dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ nonzeros, arranged in a way that supports global information exchange. In practice, this enables finetuning large models with minimal trainable parameters while maintaining the qualities of OFT that promote stability and generalization.

While BOFT introduces a modest training-time computational overhead due to the product of multiple orthogonal factors, this overhead disappears at inference because the learned orthogonal components can be multiplied into the pretrained weights once, yielding no additional latency. The paper highlights that BOFT can be applied across modalities and architectures, and systematically outperforms alternative parameter-efficient approaches in the reported experiments, which aligns with the theoretical narrative: by preserving angle-based semantic structure and spectral properties, BOFT provides a robust and efficient pathway to adapt pretrained models.

# Concluding Perspective: Semantics as Geometry

The paper’s methodology revolves around treating pretrained representations as geometric objects whose semantics are encoded in their angular relationships. Orthogonal finetuning operationalizes this view by strictly preserving pairwise angles among neurons via shared orthogonal transformations. BOFT advances this principle by discovering an efficient factorized parameterization using butterfly structures, making it feasible to retain semantic geometry while enabling expressive, dense adaptations at a fraction of the parameter cost. By balancing expressivity and regularity through structured orthogonality, BOFT offers a coherent and practical framework for parameter-efficient finetuning grounded in the geometry of pretrained representations.
