Chapter Title: Orthogonality-Induced Sparsity Patterns in Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

# Orthogonality and Structured Sparsity: Why Zeros Come in Pairs

A central theme in orthogonal finetuning is that orthogonality is more than a norm-preserving property: it enforces algebraic dependencies among matrix entries. These dependencies directly shape which sparsity patterns are possible. In particular, the paper emphasizes that orthogonality introduces common sparsity-pattern effects between entries. A canonical example is found in a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix: if the entry at position $(1,1)$ is zero, then the entry at position $(2,2)$ must also be zero. This phenomenon highlights that in orthogonal matrices, one missing entry can force another entry to vanish, thereby coupling the sparsity structure.

To understand this effect, consider a real $2\times 2$ matrix
\[
\bm{R}=\begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}
\]
that is orthogonal, meaning $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Orthogonality imposes that the columns (and also the rows) are orthonormal. From the column perspective, we obtain the constraints
\[
a^2 + c^2 = 1,\quad b^2 + d^2 = 1,\quad ab + cd = 0.
\]
From the row perspective, we similarly have
\[
a^2 + b^2 = 1,\quad c^2 + d^2 = 1,\quad ac + bd = 0.
\]
Suppose $a=0$ (that is, the $(1,1)$ entry is zero). Then the first column’s unit-norm constraint gives $c^2=1$, so $c=\pm 1$. The first row’s unit-norm constraint gives $b^2=1$, so $b=\pm 1$. The column-orthogonality constraint $ab+cd=0$ then simplifies to $0\cdot b + c d = 0$, which forces $d=0$ because $c\neq 0$. Thus $a=0$ implies $d=0$, and the zero at $(1,1)$ pairs with a zero at $(2,2)$. This is precisely the paired-zero effect highlighted by the paper.

This pairing illustrates a broader message: in orthogonal matrices, zeros are not independent degrees of freedom. Setting one entry to zero can constrain others, because preserving orthogonality requires both unit-length and orthogonality of rows and columns. Consequently, sparsity in orthogonal matrices is not simply a matter of dropping arbitrary entries; rather, feasible sparsity must respect the algebraic structure of orthogonality.

# An Information Transmission View: Orthogonality as Constrained Connectivity

The paper frames parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. A dense $d\times d$ matrix is interpreted as enabling information flow from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes: a nonzero entry $R_{ij}$ indicates that information can travel from source $j$ to receiver $i$. When a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ is factorized as a product of $m$ square matrices $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$, each factor $\bm{B}_i$ induces edges between adjacent levels of the grid. A zero entry corresponds to a missing edge. For the product to be dense, every source node must reach every receiver node via some path across the levels.

Orthogonality enters this picture in two crucial ways. First, each factor must be full-rank to be orthogonal. This necessitates at least $d$ edges between adjacent levels to form a bijection between nodes at consecutive levels. These edges are structurally required by orthogonality. Second, and more subtly, orthogonality imposes dependencies among edges beyond simple reachability. The $2\times 2$ example already shows that removing a single edge (a zero entry) can force the removal of another edge. In the grid interpretation, this means that the allowable edge patterns at each level are constrained not just by connectivity goals but also by orthogonality’s algebraic structure. Hence the graph’s sparsity cannot be chosen arbitrarily; the constraints propagate and couple edges in a way that mirrors the algebraic coupling of matrix entries.

The paper’s desiderata follow naturally from this view. To parameterize a dense orthogonal matrix via sparse factors, one must achieve dense connectivity (every source must be able to reach every sink) while using as few trainable edges as possible. Because orthogonality already forces $d$ edges per level to satisfy bijectivity, the remaining degrees of freedom must be allocated judiciously. The paired-zero effect exemplifies how orthogonality can reduce the effective freedom: removing one edge can implicitly remove another, thus lowering the number of independently choosable nonzero entries. This coupling drives the search for structured factorization patterns that achieve dense connectivity with minimal additional trainable edges.

# Block-Diagonal Versus Factorized Orthogonal Structures

A straightforward way to lower parameters in orthogonal finetuning is to impose a block-diagonal structure on the orthogonal matrix. This choice is parameter-efficient but severely limits connectivity: each block acts independently, and cross-block information exchange is impossible. In the information transmission view, a block-diagonal matrix isolates subsets of nodes into disjoint subgraphs, so no matter how the factors are composed, the overall product cannot become dense across all $d$ inputs and outputs. While the block-diagonal approach satisfies orthogonality within each block, it enforces a rigid sparsity pattern, leading to strong inductive biases and reduced expressivity.

In contrast, the paper proposes composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly structure. The butterfly factorization enables dense end-to-end connectivity using $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzero entries across factors, while each individual factor remains sparse with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros. Crucially, each sparse factor is constructed to be orthogonal, so the overall product is orthogonal as well. This design respects orthogonality’s paired and coupled-zero constraints while leveraging the structure to ensure that the cumulative effect of multiple sparse factors is a dense, expressive transformation. The butterfly pattern thus strikes a balance between the required bijective edges induced by orthogonality and the additional edges needed for dense connectivity, avoiding the rigid isolation of the block-diagonal approach.

# The Algebra Behind Paired Zeros: From $2\times 2$ to General Constraints

The $2\times 2$ case lays bare the essence of orthogonality-induced coupling. When $a=0$ in
\[
\bm{R}=\begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix},\quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
orthogonality gives $c=\pm 1$, $b=\pm 1$, and forces $d=0$. The logic hinges on three sets of constraints:

1. Unit-norm constraints for columns: $a^2 + c^2 = 1$ and $b^2 + d^2 = 1$.

2. Orthogonality between columns: $ab + cd = 0$.

3. Unit-norm constraints for rows: $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ and $c^2 + d^2 = 1$.

Setting $a=0$ fixes $c$ and $b$ at unit magnitude, which collapses the orthogonality condition to $cd=0$, entailing $d=0$. Thus, a single zero enforces another zero by virtue of orthogonality’s need to preserve both lengths and pairwise orthogonality along rows and columns.

In larger matrices, orthogonality enforces analogous coupling through the requirement that every pair of columns (and rows) is orthogonal and every column (and row) has unit norm. Although the paper highlights the $2\times 2$ example explicitly, the same principle explains why orthogonality constrains feasible sparsity patterns in general. Certain zeros cannot be set independently if orthogonality is to be maintained. As a result, designing orthogonal factorizations is as much about choosing permissible patterns as it is about counting parameters. The butterfly factorization leverages precisely this structure: it builds orthogonal components from small blocks (for example, $2\times 2$ rotations or their higher-dimensional block generalizations), ensuring that any imposed sparsity aligns with orthogonality’s algebraic constraints rather than fighting against them.

# Consequences for Parameter Efficiency and Expressivity

The paired-zero phenomenon has practical implications for parameter efficiency. When designing a sparse orthogonal factor, one must include at least $d$ structurally necessary nonzero entries to ensure full rank and orthogonality between adjacent levels in the grid. Beyond these, additional nonzeros increase expressive power, but orthogonality can cause dependencies that make some of these additional entries non-independent. In other words, some edges are functionally determined by others. This reduces the number of effective trainable parameters for a given sparsity pattern, which is beneficial for parameter efficiency but poses a challenge for achieving dense connectivity.

The butterfly structure resolves this tension by arranging sparse orthogonal components so that information can propagate globally after a logarithmic number of stages. Each butterfly component is orthogonal, often implemented via small orthogonal blocks, and the overall product becomes a dense orthogonal matrix. In the notation of the paper, the orthogonal matrix is written as
\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
\]
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is constructed to be orthogonal and sparse. This factorization uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters when $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, a significant reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters of a dense orthogonal matrix. Importantly, this construction respects orthogonality’s coupling, so that when a zero occurs in one place due to the structure of a small block, any implied zeros are consistently handled within that block and across stages.

Expressively, the butterfly-based parameterization is strictly more capable than a block-diagonal structure with the same block size, because it enables cross-block interaction through successive stages. The paper formalizes this advantage and further shows that composing butterfly-based orthogonal factors and their transposes can approximate the full orthogonal group when sufficient depth is allowed. Although full expressivity is theoretically attainable with such compositions, the paper emphasizes that the practical goal is a balance between expressivity and regularity. The butterfly structure provides a controlled expansion of the hypothesis class with a strong inductive bias that aligns with many classic linear transforms, while preserving the stability benefits of orthogonal finetuning.

# Implications for BOFT Design and Training Stability

The orthogonality-induced sparsity coupling is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it informs concrete design choices in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). By parameterizing each butterfly component as an orthogonal matrix—often via small orthogonal blocks constructed with Cayley transforms—BOFT ensures that any zeros present are consistent with orthogonality’s algebraic constraints. This avoids infeasible sparsity and preserves the orthogonality of the overall transformation.

Two additional properties arise from this design. First, orthogonal left-multiplication preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. If $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the singular value decomposition, replacing $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ and therefore does not change the largest singular value. This preservation is closely tied to training stability and generalization in orthogonal finetuning. Second, the structured factorization introduces a strong but beneficial inductive bias: by operating within a structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT constrains the hypothesis class in a way that supports generalization while allowing dense transformations through composition.

Lastly, the identity initialization of all butterfly components ensures that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model. Because each factor is orthogonal and initialized at the identity, the product remains the identity at initialization. As training progresses, the factors collectively depart from identity in a structured manner that respects orthogonality and its sparsity couplings, including paired zeros like the $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$ entries in the $2\times 2$ case.

# Summary

Orthogonality imposes powerful algebraic constraints that dictate which sparsity patterns are feasible. In a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at $(1,1)$ indeed implies a zero at $(2,2)$, illustrating how zeros can appear in pairs due to orthogonality’s coupled requirements on row and column norms and orthogonality. This phenomenon generalizes to larger matrices through the information transmission framework: missing edges in one place can force the absence of edges elsewhere, and at least $d$ structured edges per level are necessary to maintain orthogonality. BOFT leverages these insights by composing dense orthogonal transformations from products of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. This design respects orthogonality’s coupling of sparsity, achieves dense end-to-end connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, preserves spectral properties, and introduces a structured inductive bias that supports stable and generalizable finetuning.
