Chapter Title: Spectral Norm Preservation in Orthogonal Finetuning and Its Role in Training Stability and Generalization

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Mechanism

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapting pretrained foundation models to downstream tasks while preserving important relational properties of the learned representations. Rather than updating a pretrained weight matrix additively, OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weights multiplicatively by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the input. This multiplicative update preserves pairwise angles between neurons throughout finetuning because orthogonal transformations are isometries for inner products, and this preservation helps maintain semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining.

To ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout training, OFT employs the Cayley parameterization: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization ensures differentiability and exact orthogonality, and it enables zero-initialization by setting $\bm{Q}$ to the zero matrix, making $\bm{R}$ initially the identity $\bm{I}$. While OFT originally explored a block-diagonal structure for parameter-efficiency, that structure reduces expressiveness and induces a fixed sparsity pattern that may introduce undesirable inductive biases. This motivates the need for dense orthogonal transformations that remain parameter-efficient, a direction pursued by Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which composes dense orthogonal matrices as products of sparse orthogonal butterfly components.

# Spectral Norm and Its Preservation Under Orthogonal Updates

A key property of orthogonal finetuning is its preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. The spectral norm is the largest singular value of a matrix and is denoted by $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$. To see why orthogonal finetuning preserves this quantity, consider the singular value decomposition of the pretrained weight matrix: $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values. OFT and BOFT update the weights by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, yielding $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ remains orthogonal, and the singular values encoded in $\bm{\Sigma}$ are unaffected by left multiplication by $\bm{R}$. Therefore, the largest singular value of $\bm{W}^0$ is unchanged under orthogonal finetuning, and the spectral norm is perfectly preserved.

This preservation is explicit and holds throughout training because the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ is maintained at every update step via the Cayley parameterization. In contrast, purely additive updates do not guarantee such preservation and may alter the spectral properties of the weight matrix. The preservation of the spectral norm is not an incidental artifact but a direct consequence of the orthogonality constraint in the finetuning framework.

# Why Spectral Norm Preservation Benefits Stability and Generalization

Preserving the spectral norm $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$ has important consequences for training dynamics and model behavior. Because the spectral norm bounds the operator norm of the linear transformation, maintaining the largest singular value stabilizes the magnitude of activations propagated through the finetuned layers. This stability avoids uncontrolled amplification that can arise when the spectral norm grows during finetuning. By keeping the spectral norm unchanged, orthogonal finetuning preserves the Lipschitz behavior of the layer’s mapping, which helps avoid destabilizing effects in optimization and contributes to smoother training.

In addition to stability, spectral norm preservation contributes to generalization. Orthogonal finetuning constrains the hypothesis class by limiting updates to those that do not change the singular value distribution’s maximum. This restraint aligns with the general principle that structured regularity in parameter updates prevents overfitting. The approach balances expressivity with regularity: it allows transformations that rotate and mix neuron dimensions while constraining magnitude growth. This balance is particularly valuable in adapting large foundation models, where excessive flexibility can lead to poor generalization despite high expressivity. The paper emphasizes that such preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization, underscoring the practical value of orthogonal finetuning’s spectral control.

# BOFT: Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Updates That Preserve Spectral Norm

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) extends OFT by introducing an efficient orthogonal parameterization that composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices constructed using butterfly structures. BOFT leverages an information transmission view: constructing a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ by factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ corresponds to transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. Butterfly graphs are particularly effective for dense connectivity with few edges, enabling $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ non-zero elements to achieve dense mappings compared to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ in full dense matrices.

Formally, the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is constructed recursively from butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, each of which is a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors. Each butterfly component can be parameterized to be orthogonal, starting from the $2 \times 2$ case via Cayley transform or rotation, and extending to block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where entries are $b \times b$ blocks. BOFT defines the final orthogonal update as $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each factor is orthogonal. Because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is orthogonal by construction, the spectral norm of the layer $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved under the update to $(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$.

BOFT achieves a significant reduction in effective trainable parameters. For a layer of size $d \times n$, BOFT($m$,$b$) requires $\frac{1}{2}(b - 1)dm$ parameters. Using butterfly matrices with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters while still constructing dense orthogonal transformations. In contrast, the original OFT with full dense orthogonal matrices requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters; block-diagonal OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ and cannot recover classic dense linear transforms. BOFT’s parameter-efficiency therefore does not compromise the spectral norm preservation, making it attractive for both stability and generalization in finetuning.

# Initialization, Dropout, and Structured Regularity

A key practical consideration in orthogonal finetuning is initialization. BOFT initializes all butterfly components as identity matrices, which corresponds to setting the skew-symmetric matrix to zero in the Cayley transform. This identity initialization starts from the exact pretrained model and ensures the finetuning process will not deviate abruptly at the beginning. Such initialization complements spectral norm preservation by maintaining the pretrained model’s spectral properties at the outset.

To avoid overfitting, BOFT introduces a multiplicative Dropout tailored to the multiplicative nature of orthogonal updates. Conventional Dropout does not directly apply in this setting, so BOFT randomly selects a fraction of butterfly components and a fraction of diagonal blocks within each component, and replaces those selected blocks with identity matrices during training. This mechanism enforces additional regularity while preserving the orthogonality of $\bm{R}(m,b)$ and, by extension, the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$.

This structured approach to parameterization induces an inductive bias. The butterfly structure can recover many classic fast linear transforms through appropriate permutations and compositions, and it provides a smoother interpolation between full orthogonal expressivity and identity-like regularity than block-diagonal structures. This balance makes it possible to constrain the hypothesis class in a way that is beneficial for generalization, while retaining enough expressivity to adapt effectively to downstream tasks.

# Expressivity–Regularity Trade-off and Its Impact on Training

The expressivity of BOFT stems from its ability to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices through butterfly factorization and permutations. The paper shows that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and, through compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope-style hierarchy, can represent the entire orthogonal group when sufficiently deep and structured. However, the paper emphasizes that increased expressivity does not necessarily yield better finetuning performance. Full finetuning of all parameters is universally expressive yet often underperforms due to poor regularity.

Orthogonal finetuning, including BOFT, explicitly pursues a trade-off between expressivity and regularity. Spectral norm preservation contributes directly to this trade-off by constraining magnitude changes while allowing rotations and mixing. BOFT’s structural priors further constrain the search space to a structured subset of the orthogonal group. Together, these aspects yield parameter-efficient updates that favor stable optimization and improved generalization.

# Limitations and Practical Considerations

While BOFT offers strong parameter-efficiency and spectral norm preservation, composing the final orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple orthogonal components introduces a training runtime overhead relative to methods that use a single orthogonal matrix. Improving runtime without sacrificing orthogonality or parameter-efficiency remains an open problem. Importantly, this overhead is only incurred during finetuning. After training, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained weights, incurring no additional inference latency.

The information transmission view suggests further exploration of network topologies beyond butterfly structures for sparse orthogonal factorization. It is unknown whether butterfly networks are the most efficient for this purpose, and insights from computer networking may inspire even more efficient designs. Nonetheless, within the scope of the present method, BOFT demonstrates that dense orthogonal transformations can be constructed with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, all while preserving the spectral norm of the pretrained weights—an attribute the paper highlights as primarily benefiting training stability and generalization.
