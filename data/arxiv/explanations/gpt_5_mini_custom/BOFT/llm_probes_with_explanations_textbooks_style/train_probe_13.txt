Chapter Title: Multiplicative Dropout in Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Background: Orthogonal Finetuning and BOFT

Orthogonal finetuning updates pretrained weight matrices by left-multiplying them with an orthogonal matrix so that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved during adaptation. Given a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, orthogonal finetuning optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Orthogonality is maintained throughout finetuning by Cayley parameterization, $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This multiplicative update preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ and promotes training stability.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) enhances parameter efficiency by factorizing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal components built from butterfly structures. With $d=2^N$, BOFT composes
$\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component formed by block-diagonal arrangements of small orthogonal matrices and their block-wise permutations. This construction yields a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}(m,b)$ using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective parameters when $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, in contrast to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix. BOFT subsumes prior orthogonal finetuning variants: BOFT($1$,$b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT($1$,$d$) reduces to a full orthogonal update.

This factorized orthogonal parameterization is motivated by an information transmission view, where composing a dense matrix from sparse factors corresponds to transmitting information across levels in a grid-structured graph. Butterfly structures efficiently ensure dense connectivity from source to destination nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges while meeting orthogonality constraints at each level.

# Why Conventional Dropout Does Not Directly Apply

Finetuning methods often employ regularization to control overfitting and to encourage generalization under limited parameter budgets. In additive reparameterizations such as low-rank adaptation, it is natural to apply conventional Dropout to the additive terms, effectively zeroing selected updates during training. However, orthogonal finetuning updates weights multiplicatively. The learned matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a product of orthogonal components, and the forward pass is $\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}$. Zeroing entries inside orthogonal factors would typically break orthogonality or induce degeneracies; zeroing entire factors would collapse the transformation, both of which are misaligned with the structural constraints and the stability advantages of orthogonal finetuning.

Thus, regularization for multiplicative orthogonal updates must respect orthogonality and the compositional structure. The mechanism should suppress overfitting while preserving the identity initialization and the spectral properties that make orthogonal finetuning effective. An appropriate strategy must operate at the level of factors or blocks, rather than entries, and must leave the overall product within the orthogonal group during training.

# The Mechanism of Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT

BOFT introduces a multiplicative Dropout tailored to its factorized orthogonal parameterization. The key idea is to regularize by selectively neutralizing components of the multiplicative update without altering the orthogonality or destabilizing the forward pass. This is achieved by replacing chosen components with identity matrices during training.

Concretely, $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each of which can be permuted into a $2b\times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. Multiplicative Dropout operates in two stages in this structured space. First, it randomly selects a proportion $p_1$ of the butterfly components across the $m$ factors. Second, within each selected component, it randomly selects a proportion $p_2$ of its diagonal blocks. For every such selected block, the method replaces that block with the identity matrix of matching size. As a result, the corresponding local transformations are stochastically turned off, while the remainder of the factor remains orthogonal.

Formally, let $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ denote the $i$-th orthogonal butterfly component in the product. Under multiplicative Dropout, a block-wise mask stochastically maps selected sub-blocks of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ to identity. If a block $\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{2b\times 2b}$ is selected, it is replaced by $\bm{I}_{2b}$; otherwise, it remains the learned orthogonal block. The modified component remains block-diagonal orthogonal because identity blocks are orthogonal and non-selected blocks are orthogonal, and their block-diagonal concatenation is orthogonal. The overall product across factors therefore remains orthogonal:
$\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\bm{I}_d$
is preserved for each $i$, and hence
$\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$
stays in the orthogonal group during training.

This procedure regularizes the multiplicative update by replacing selected components with identity, which effectively equates to skipping those transformations for the current training step. By doing so, BOFT avoids overfitting to particular butterfly paths or blocks while ensuring the architectural invariants—orthogonality, identity initialization compatibility, and dense connectivity across the product—are maintained.

# Intuition from the Information Transmission View

The information transmission view offers a useful intuition for multiplicative Dropout. In the grid-structured graph induced by the product $\bm{R}(m,b)=\bm{B}_m\cdots\bm{B}_1$, each factor $\bm{B}_i$ defines a set of directed edges that propagate information from level $i$ to level $i+1$. A butterfly component achieves efficient dense connectivity by combining sparse, structured exchanges at each level. When multiplicative Dropout replaces selected blocks with identity, it removes certain local mixing operations while preserving full pass-through along those edges, effectively transmitting information without alteration at the dropped locations.

Because each level must meet orthogonality and full-rank constraints—corresponding to $d$ necessary edges forming a bijection between nodes—replacing a block with identity does not violate the connectivity needed for dense transmission in the overall product. Instead, it simplifies selected sub-transformations by turning them into exact mappings. Over training iterations, this stochastic simplification encourages robustness: information paths cannot rely exclusively on a fixed set of intermediate mixings, and the learned transformation distributes its effect more broadly across factors and blocks, supporting generalization within the constrained hypothesis class of BOFT.

# Interaction with Identity Initialization and Stability

BOFT initializes all butterfly components as identity matrices, meaning the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model. Multiplicative Dropout is naturally compatible with this design. Since the identity is the neutral element of matrix multiplication, replacing selected components with identity during training preserves the multiplicative structure while exerting a regularizing effect. The model can smoothly interpolate between the identity and learned orthogonal transformations because every factor remains orthogonal and the product remains well-conditioned.

Moreover, orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, which contributes to training stability. Replacing blocks with identity does not alter this preservation, as the overall transformation remains orthogonal. In effect, multiplicative Dropout retains the key stability benefits of orthogonal finetuning while tempering the capacity of the factorized transformation, reducing the risk of overfitting in parameter-efficient regimes.

# Parameter Efficiency, Expressivity, and Regularity

BOFT provides a structured path through the orthogonal group by constraining $\bm{R}(m,b)$ to butterfly-based compositions. This induces a smaller hypothesis class than unconstrained full orthogonals, and the number of effective parameters scales as $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ for $m=\log d$ and $b=2$. Multiplicative Dropout complements this structure by stochastically restricting portions of the transformation to identity at training time. In the context of block sizes and factor counts, such regularization further balances expressivity and regularity: the model retains the ability to generate dense orthogonal transformations via the product, while Dropout periodically removes local complexity by collapsing selected blocks to identity.

Because BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as BOFT($1$,$b$) and full OFT as BOFT($1$,$d$), multiplicative Dropout serves as a uniform regularization strategy across these settings. It operates at the granularity of factors and diagonal blocks within factors, aligning with the structure of the parameterization. The method therefore strengthens generalizable behavior without sacrificing the defining properties of orthogonal finetuning.

# Concluding Perspective

Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is a regularization mechanism precisely tailored to multiplicative, orthogonal parameterizations. By replacing selected components with identity, it stochastically simplifies parts of the transformation while preserving orthogonality, identity initialization compatibility, and the spectral properties that underpin stability. Interpreted through the information transmission view, this operation temporarily disables certain local mixings yet maintains dense end-to-end connectivity via the product of factors. In practice, this leads to improved generalization under tight parameter budgets, complementing BOFT’s central goal of achieving parameter-efficient, expressive, and stable finetuning.
