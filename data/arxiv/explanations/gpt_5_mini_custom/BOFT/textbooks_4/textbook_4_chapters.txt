\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning and Orthogonal Methods

# Why PEFT: cost of training large foundation models and need for adaptation
The rapid growth of foundation models in size and capability—examples include large language models and large vision models—has made training from scratch increasingly impractical. As model parameter counts climb into the billions and beyond, the computational, energy, and time costs of full re-training become prohibitive for most research groups and real-world practitioners. At the same time, downstream tasks typically require some degree of adaptation: a pretrained foundation model trained on broad data must be specialized to handle particular tasks, datasets, or domains.

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) addresses this practical gap. Instead of retraining or storing full copies of massive models for each downstream task, PEFT methods change only a modest number of parameters (or add a small number of new parameters) so that the pretrained model’s knowledge is largely preserved while adapting to new objectives. The benefits are concrete: reduced memory and storage cost for per-task parameters, reduced communication overhead when distributing task-specific models, and the ability to reuse most of the pretrained network (often without adding inference latency). These practical constraints motivate the study of principled PEFT approaches that maximize adaptation power while minimizing the number of tunable parameters.

# PEFT families: model finetuning, adapters, prompt/prefix tuning, and reparameterization
PEFT methods fall into several broad families, each with distinct design choices and trade-offs. One family is model finetuning, in which the architecture remains the same but only a subset of existing parameters is updated. This can be as simple as updating all parameters with a small learning rate, or selectively updating biases or normalization layers. Model finetuning has the virtue of introducing no extra inference latency because the model structure is unchanged; it relies on a careful choice of which parameters to expose to optimization to avoid overfitting while capturing task-specific behavior.

A second family is adapter tuning, which inserts small task-specific modules into the architecture. Adapters add trainable parameters to the network but keep them functionally separate from the pretrained weights; during inference the adapters are active, so adapters can introduce modest inference cost but allow a compact per-task parameterization. Adapter approaches have proven effective across both vision and language domains.

A third family is prompt or prefix tuning. Here, the adaptation occurs in the input space: a set of additional tokens or prefix parameters are attached to the input sequence and learned for each task. Prompt tuning is particularly appealing in transformer-based models and often requires very few parameters, but it changes how inputs are presented to the model rather than altering internal linear operators.

A final family of highly relevant approaches uses reparameterization of model weights. In reparameterization methods, the finetuned weight matrix is written as a structured function of a small number of trainable parameters. LoRA is a prominent example: it replaces the finetuned weight update with an additive low-rank term (a product of two small matrices). Reparameterization-based PEFT methods are attractive because they can directly target the weight matrices and preserve architectural behavior, and because their structural priors (low-rank, sparsity, orthogonality, etc.) can be selected to promote generalization.

# LoRA vs OFT: additive low-rank updates vs multiplicative orthogonal updates
Two representative reparameterization approaches highlight different philosophies about how to modify pretrained weights. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) updates a pretrained weight matrix by adding a low-rank correction. Concretely, if \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) denotes a pretrained linear layer, LoRA replaces the finetuned weight by
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W},\qquad \Delta \bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B},
\]
where \(\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) with \(r\ll\min(d,n)\). Because \(\Delta \bm{W}\) has rank at most \(r\), LoRA uses relatively few parameters and can be very parameter-efficient in practice. LoRA’s additive update allows straightforward initialization (typically zeros) and simple regularization such as conventional Dropout.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a different route: it updates a pretrained weight matrix multiplicatively using a left-multiplying orthogonal matrix. Given \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), OFT parameterizes the finetuned weight as
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
where \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is constrained to be orthogonal. The forward computation for input \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and output \(\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
The key conceptual difference is additive low-rank correction (LoRA) versus multiplicative orthogonal transformation (OFT). Additive low-rank updates change weights by adding new directions to the range of \(\bm{W}^0\), while multiplicative orthogonal updates rotate or reflect the input space prior to applying \(\bm{W}^0\). Each has trade-offs: LoRA is extremely parameter-efficient for many tasks but does not enforce global constraints on the transformation, whereas OFT enforces orthogonality which yields structural preservation advantages (discussed below) but may require more parameters to represent a full left-multiplying orthogonal matrix.

# Core idea of OFT: neuron-wise shared orthogonal transforms that preserve pairwise angles
At the heart of OFT is the observation that much of what the pretrained model encodes about the domain is relational rather than absolute: neurons (interpreted as column vectors of a weight matrix) carry meaning in how they relate to one another, for example through pairwise angles. OFT explicitly preserves such relational structure by applying a single orthogonal transform to all neurons of the same layer. If \(\bm{W}^0=[\bm{w}^0_1,\bm{w}^0_2,\dots,\bm{w}^0_n]\) collects the column neurons, then OFT replaces each neuron by \(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\) using the same orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\). Because orthogonal transforms preserve inner products and norms, pairwise angles between neurons remain invariant under this left-multiplication:
\[
\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i,\ \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\rangle = \langle \bm{w}^0_i,\ \bm{w}^0_j\rangle.
\]
This invariance is the mathematical basis for the claim that OFT preserves relational information among neurons. Preserving these pairwise angles is argued to help retain semantic information that the pretrained model has encoded, thereby providing a principled and structured way to adapt the model without destroying pretraining geometry. From an implementation standpoint, OFT uses a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) for each layer (shared across neurons of that layer), initialized at the identity so the initial finetuned model equals the pretrained model.

# Benefits of orthogonality: stability, generalization, and spectral norm preservation
Enforcing orthogonality on the finetuning transform confers several desirable mathematical and practical properties. One fundamental property is preservation of singular values when \(\bm{R}\) multiplies \(\bm{W}^0\) on the left. Consider a singular value decomposition of the pretrained weight,
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top,
\]
with orthogonal \(\bm{U}\) and \(\bm{V}\) and diagonal \(\bm{\Sigma}\) of singular values. Left-multiplying by an orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) yields
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top,
\]
and since \(\bm{R}\bm{U}\) is orthogonal, the singular values (and in particular the largest singular value, i.e., the spectral norm) remain unchanged. Spectral-norm preservation is valuable because it prevents unwarranted amplification or contraction of signals through the finetuned layer; empirically and theoretically, controlling spectral norms helps training stability and can improve generalization.

Beyond spectral preservation, orthogonality guarantees norm preservation and inner-product preservation, which underlies the pairwise-angle invariance described above. This structural preservation constrains the space of possible finetuned models to those that are geometric rotations/reflections of the pretrained representation, which can act as an implicit regularizer and help avoid overfitting to small datasets or noisy signals.

# Cayley parameterization: ensuring orthogonality via skew-symmetric matrices
A practical challenge of optimizing orthogonal matrices is maintaining the orthogonality constraint during gradient-based updates. One convenient parameterization that enforces orthogonality for square matrices is the Cayley transform. If \(\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is skew-symmetric (i.e., \(\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}\)), then the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix via
\[
\bm{R} \;=\; (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})\,(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
Because \(\bm{Q}\) is skew-symmetric, the right-hand side is orthogonal wherever the inverse exists. This parameterization turns the constrained optimization over orthogonal matrices into an unconstrained optimization over skew-symmetric matrices \(\bm{Q}\). It also facilitates a natural identity initialization: setting \(\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\) yields \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), which initializes the finetuned model to exactly match the pretrained weights. During finetuning, gradients are computed with respect to the entries of \(\bm{Q}\), and the Cayley mapping guarantees orthogonality is preserved for the realized \(\bm{R}\).

# OFT implementation basics: layer-wise left-multiplication and identity initialization
In practice, OFT is implemented by introducing, for each layer to be finetuned, a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) implemented via a Cayley parameterization (or another orthogonal parameterization). The pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) is kept frozen and the forward pass replaces \(\bm{W}^0\) by \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\). Two practical choices are important:

First, identity initialization. By initializing the Cayley skew-symmetric parameter \(\bm{Q}\) at zero, one gets \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) at the start of training. This is analogous to the zero initialization of LoRA’s additive matrices and ensures the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly. Insisting on this zero-deviation start point stabilizes optimization and makes small, controlled departures from pretrained behavior possible.

Second, layer-wise sharing. The same orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) is applied to all neurons (columns) of the layer, which enforces a global geometric transformation per layer rather than neuron-specific changes. This sharing reduces the number of independent transforms in the model and aligns with the conceptual goal of preserving relational geometry among neurons.

# Block-diagonal OFT: parameter savings and the cost of fixed sparsity patterns
Although the full left-multiplying orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) offers maximal expressive power among orthogonal transforms, it carries a large parameter cost: a generic dense orthogonal matrix requires on the order of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters if represented explicitly (or \(\Theta(d^2)\) free entries prior to orthogonality constraints). To reduce tunable parameters, the original OFT work introduced a block-diagonal parameterization. In this scheme \(\bm{R}\) is constrained to be block-diagonal:
\[
\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\bm{R}_1,\ \bm{R}_2,\ \dots,\ \bm{R}_r\big),
\]
where each \(\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) is an orthogonal block and \(br=d\). By choosing a small block size \(b\), the number of trainable parameters scales roughly like \(\mathcal{O}(b d)\) rather than \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). This block-diagonal OFT trades off expressivity for parameter efficiency: it is much cheaper to store and optimize the blocks, and it still enforces orthogonality within each block.

From an implementation view, each block can be parameterized using Cayley transforms of the corresponding dimension, making orthogonality within blocks straightforward to enforce and preserving the identity initialization property by zeroing the skew-symmetric block parameters.

# Limitations of block-diagonal sparsity: reduced expressivity and poor approximation of classic transforms
The block-diagonal parameterization, while simple and parameter-efficient, has intrinsic limitations. Because the transform acts independently on disjoint index groups of the input dimension, it cannot mix information across block boundaries. In other words, the resulting \(\bm{R}\) has a fixed sparsity pattern determined purely by the block partition; dimensions in different blocks never interact under \(\bm{R}\). This entails two concrete drawbacks.

First, reduced expressivity. Many useful orthogonal transforms (including those that appear in classical signal processing, such as Fourier-like transforms or other global transforms) require dense mixing of dimensions. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate such transforms regardless of the value of the learned block entries, because the zero structure is immutable. Thus, for tasks that require global re-weighting or mixing of features across the entire input dimension, block-diagonal OFT may be insufficient.

Second, the fixed sparsity introduces an arbitrary inductive bias: partitioning input dimensions into contiguous index blocks is an index-based design choice that is not necessarily aligned with the semantic grouping of features or neurons. There is no principled way, given only the pretrained model, to decide how to partition the dimensions so that the block structure best suits downstream tasks. Consequently, block-diagonal sparsity can both underfit (by enforcing too-strong independence) and misalign with the model’s internal geometry.

# Problem statement: achieve dense, expressive orthogonal updates with few trainable parameters
Putting the preceding observations together yields a clear problem statement. We want the advantages of orthogonal finetuning—spectral and relational preservation, training stability, and principled geometric regularization—while avoiding the prohibitive parameter costs of full dense orthogonal matrices and the strong, often harmful sparsity constraints of simple block-diagonal reductions. Concretely, the goal is to construct a left-multiplying orthogonal transform \(\bm{R}\) that is:

1. Dense in effect (able to mix information across all input dimensions so it can approximate global orthogonal transforms), and
2. Parameter-efficient (requiring significantly fewer than \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) free parameters), and
3. Amenable to stable optimization with identity initialization and orthogonality enforcement (for example via Cayley transforms applied to smaller structured components).

A promising direction is to represent a dense orthogonal matrix as the product of several sparse orthogonal factors. Such a factorization trades time for space: the composed matrix can be dense, but each factor is sparse and thus parameter-efficient to specify. Optimizing the sparse factors (each enforced to be orthogonal, e.g., via Cayley parameterization of small skew-symmetric blocks) preserves orthogonality exactly for each factor and hence for the product. This leads naturally to an information-transmission perspective—viewing factor multiplication as sequential exchanges of information across a layered graph—and ultimately motivates structured sparse factorizations (such as butterfly factorizations) that can achieve dense connectivity with far fewer trainable parameters than a single dense orthogonal matrix. The remainder of the text (beyond this foundational chapter) explores this factorization viewpoint and one concrete structured solution that realizes the objectives above.

An Information Transmission View and the Butterfly Paradigm

# Matrix factorization as information flow: d × (m+1) grid of nodes and directed edges
When we represent an orthogonal update to a pretrained layer as a matrix factorization
\[
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]
it is useful to think of this product as an unrolled sequence of communication stages. Concretely, imagine a grid with \(d\) nodes in each column and \(m+1\) columns (levels). The leftmost column are the source nodes (one per input dimension), and the rightmost column are the receiver nodes (one per output dimension). Each factor \(\mathbf{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) then describes the connectivity between the nodes in column \(i\) and the nodes in column \(i+1\): the entry \((\mathbf{B}_i)_{j_1,j_2}\) indicates whether — and how strongly — information can flow from the \(j_2\)-th node of level \(i\) to the \(j_1\)-th node of level \(i+1\).

This graph view maps linear algebra to paths: any path from a source node to a receiver node corresponds to a term in the expansion of the product \(\mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1\). If there exists at least one path from source \(s\) to receiver \(r\), then the \((r,s)\)-entry of the final matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be nonzero; if no path exists, that entry must be zero. Thus sparsity in the factors and the existence of paths together determine the sparsity of \(\mathbf{R}\). Thinking in terms of nodes, edges, and paths makes it straightforward to reason about how a chosen factorization pattern affects which input coordinates can influence which outputs after all stages of multiplication.

# Dense connectivity requirement: each source dimension reaches all target dimensions
A central requirement when we want a dense final transform \(\mathbf{R}\) is simple to state in the graph language: for each source node (each input dimension) there must be at least one directed path to every receiver node (each output dimension). Only when every source can reach every receiver will the product \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1\) be able to be dense (i.e., have potentially nonzero entries everywhere).

This requirement is stronger than having each intermediate matrix dense. Because matrix multiplication composes the connectivity, a sparse-but-well-structured sequence of \(\mathbf{B}_i\) factors can still produce dense reachability through multiple hops. The graph viewpoint makes this explicit: dense final connectivity can be achieved by ensuring that the sequence of sparse adjacency patterns together furnishes a path from every leftmost node to every rightmost node. The design problem becomes: given a budget on how many nonzero connections we can afford in each \(\mathbf{B}_i\), how should we place them so that the reachability condition is satisfied?

# Orthogonality constraints: bijective edges between levels and implications for sparsity
When each factor \(\mathbf{B}_i\) is required to be orthogonal, additional structural constraints appear. Orthogonality implies full rank, so each \(\mathbf{B}_i\) must realize a bijective linear mapping between its input and output spaces. In the graph picture this enforces a basic combinatorial necessity: between level \(i\) and level \(i+1\) we need at least \(d\) edges to allow a bijection connecting the \(d\) nodes on one side to the \(d\) nodes on the other. Put differently, a rank-deficient connectivity with fewer than \(d\) edges cannot be orthogonal.

Orthogonality also couples entries in a way that pure counting of edges may understate. For example, in a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal block, zeros are not independent: if one off-diagonal entry is zero under orthogonality constraints, other entries are forced to take specific values (signs or magnitudes) to preserve orthonormal rows and columns. Thus the presence or absence of a connection in one place can force structure elsewhere. This means that, compared to unconstrained sparse matrices, the set of feasible sparsity patterns for orthogonal factors is more restricted and must be chosen with these algebraic dependencies in mind.

# Free edges vs necessary edges: counting trainable degrees of freedom under orthogonality
From a parameter-efficiency perspective we should distinguish between necessary structural edges and free, trainable degrees of freedom. For any orthogonal factor \(\mathbf{B}_i\), there is a minimal combinatorial skeleton that ensures full rank (at least \(d\) edges forming a bijection). Those minimal edges are in some sense non-negotiable if we insist on orthogonality; the paper treats these necessary connections as not contributing to the effective count of trainable parameters because in extremal sparse orthogonal patterns the values of those entries are restricted (for instance, they can be forced to \(\pm 1\) in certain permutation-like bijections).

The trainable parameters are the additional continuous degrees of freedom beyond that minimal skeleton: the extra nonzero entries (and their continuous values) that permit flexible orthogonal transformations. Thus, if the total number of nonzero positions across all factors is \(E\) and there are \(m\) levels (with at least \(d\) skeleton edges between each adjacent pair), the number of "free edges" that actually correspond to tunable parameters is roughly the surplus \(E - m d\), adjusted for algebraic constraints introduced by orthogonality. Counting these free edges is the right way to reason about parameter-efficiency: we want factorizations that minimize the number of free edges while still achieving the dense reachability requirement.

# Why single dense matrices are costly: O(d^2) parameters
A single dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) has \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) entries. Even though orthogonality imposes constraints (so the intrinsic dimension of the orthogonal group \(O(d)\) is \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\)), representing and optimizing such a dense orthogonal matrix in practice requires handling on the order of \(d^2\) numbers (for storage, computation of gradients, etc.). For large hidden dimensions \(d\) in modern foundation models, this quadratic storage and the corresponding number of trainable degrees of freedom quickly becomes prohibitive.

This motivates the core idea of trading space for time: instead of storing one dense \(\mathbf{R}\), represent \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of several sparse orthogonal factors \(\mathbf{B}_i\). If each factor has only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) nonzeros and we use \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) such factors, the total number of trainable parameters can be reduced to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while the composed transformation \(\mathbf{R}\) can still be dense. The cost of this saving is extra computation at training time, since we must multiply through the chain of sparse factors repeatedly.

# Block-diagonal OFT in the transmission view: disconnected subgraphs and lost expressivity
The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) achieves parameter reduction by constraining \(\mathbf{R}\) to be block-diagonal:
\[
\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),\qquad \mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d.
\]
In the grid/graph representation this corresponds to splitting the \(d\) nodes in each level into \(r\) independent groups, and placing no edges between different groups. Each group is isolated: information that originates in group \(g\) of the leftmost level can only ever influence nodes within group \(g\) on the rightmost level.

This creates a disconnected subgraph structure. The consequence is straightforward: the final matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) cannot be dense across the full \(d\times d\) index set; it is block-diagonal as well. While this reduces parameters (the number scales like \(O(bd)\)), it loses expressivity because the factorization cannot capture interactions across groups. In transmission terms, block-diagonal OFT sacrifices cross-group information flow to save parameters, which can limit the capacity to adapt pretrained features that rely on global coordinate mixing.

# Desiderata for efficient factorizations: dense connectivity with minimal free edges
Putting the previous observations together gives a practical design objective for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning: find a factorization into orthogonal factors that simultaneously

1. ensures dense connectivity (every source node has a path to every receiver node), and  
2. minimizes the number of free trainable edges (the surplus nonzero positions beyond the orthogonality-enforced skeleton).

These desiderata balance expressivity (via reachability) against parsimony (via few trainable degrees of freedom). The orthogonality constraint modifies how many and which edges are truly free, so designing sparsity patterns must respect both graph connectivity and algebraic feasibility. The information-transmission view turns this into a graph design task: choose which edges between adjacent levels to keep so that the multilevel network connects all sources to all receivers while keeping the total tunable structure small and constraining each factor to be an orthogonal matrix.

# Butterfly graphs from the Cooley–Tukey FFT: efficient global mixing with O(d log d) structure
A classic and highly effective topology that meets these desiderata is the butterfly graph, known from the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform. The butterfly factor at block-size \(k\) is written in the paper as
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k) \;=\;
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each \(\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\). A full \(d=2^N\) butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is constructed recursively by stacking block-diagonal components \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) for \(k = d, d/2, \dots, 2\). Each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is a block-diagonal concatenation of small \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) blocks, and the product of the \(\log d\) components mixes information across all coordinates.

The crucial combinatorial fact is that a butterfly factorization uses only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) nonzero entries per stage and achieves global mixing across the \(d\) coordinates in \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) stages. Thus the total number of nonzero positions across all stages is \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\). By parameterizing each small block (for example each \(2\times 2\) block) to be orthogonal (e.g., via Cayley transform or a 2D rotation), each butterfly component can be orthogonal, and their product is an orthogonal matrix that can be dense. In the graph picture, the butterfly topology furnishes many short paths so that every source can reach every receiver, while keeping the per-stage degree small.

# Comparative intuition: butterfly vs ad hoc sparse patterns
Contrasting the butterfly with ad hoc sparse patterns makes the advantage clearer. A random or poorly structured sparse pattern might either leave many pairs of nodes disconnected (causing unavoidable zeros in the final \(\mathbf{R}\)) or require many more nonzeros to repair that lack of connectivity. Block-diagonal structure is an extreme example of insufficient connectivity: it keeps parameters low but sacrifices all cross-block interactions. On the other hand, the butterfly pattern is a principled, recursive construction that guarantees global reachability with far fewer edges than a single dense matrix, and it does so using a highly regular pattern that is compatible with orthogonality constraints (because the nonzero pattern at each stage can be partitioned into independently parameterizable orthogonal blocks).

An additional qualitative advantage is that butterfly patterns align with many classical fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT), so they induce an inductive bias toward transformations that are both global and structured. This bias tends to be more useful than arbitrary sparse patterns when finetuning pretrained representations that often rely on coherent global mixes of coordinates.

# Trade computation for space: repeated sparse multiplications to realize dense orthogonal transforms
The price paid for the compact parameterization provided by sparse factorizations (and butterfly factorizations in particular) is computational work during training: evaluating \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) requires multiplying the pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^0\) by the sequence of sparse factors (or equivalently repeatedly applying the sparse orthogonal factors during forward/backward passes). This increases per-step computation compared to applying a single dense update stored explicitly.

However, this trade-off can be beneficial in practice. The memory and optimization advantages of drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters (from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) down to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) for the butterfly) often outweigh the extra multiply stages at training time. Furthermore, once finetuning is complete, the learned product \(\mathbf{R}\) can be multiplied into the pretrained weights to yield a single dense matrix for inference; thus the training-time computational overhead does not necessarily translate into inference latency.

Summary
Viewing orthogonal factorization as an information transmission problem on a \(d\times (m+1)\) grid turns the design of parameter-efficient transforms into a graph-design problem with algebraic constraints. The goals are clear: attain dense reachability (every source reaches every receiver) while minimizing the number of free, trainable edges subject to orthogonality. The butterfly topology, inherited from Cooley–Tukey FFT, is a principled solution: with \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) stages of \(\mathcal{O}(d)\)-sized orthogonal blocks it delivers dense final transforms using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, striking a favorable balance between expressivity and parameter efficiency.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Method, Parameterization, and Training

# Butterfly factor and component: constructing sparse blocks and their permutations
The BOFT parameterization builds on a small, structured sparse building block called a butterfly factor. For a power-of-two block size $k\ge 2$, the butterfly factor is a $k\times k$ matrix of the following block form:
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector and $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ denotes a diagonal matrix formed from that vector. Intuitively, a single butterfly factor is extremely sparse: it couples corresponding coordinates in the upper and lower halves of a block via diagonal scaling and mixing. This localized coupling is the elementary information-exchange primitive in the butterfly design.

A butterfly component at a given scale is formed by placing many such $k\times k$ butterfly factors on the diagonal of a larger $d\times d$ matrix. Concretely, for a model dimension $d$ divisible by $k$, the butterfly component is
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\bm{B}^F_2(k),\ldots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big),
\]
a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are permutations or repeats of the $k$-sized factor. Because each block is structurally identical up to permutation of coordinates, the nonzero pattern of a butterfly component is a regular, sparse pattern that can be implemented efficiently and that constrains how information flows between groups of coordinates.

# Recursive butterfly matrix: assembling O(log d) sparse stages to achieve dense mixing
The power of the butterfly idea comes from stacking multiple components at progressively coarser granularities. For $d=2^N$, the full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix is defined recursively as a product of these components over $\log d$ scales. Using the notation $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ for a component of block-size $k$, one can write
\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\,\cdots\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]
Each component implements short-range, structured exchanges between coordinates within blocks of size $k$. When these components are multiplied in sequence, the local exchanges compound and produce dense mixing across the entire $d$ coordinates. Conceptually, information is transmitted across a $d\times(m+1)$ grid, where each component corresponds to edges between two adjacent columns; with about $m\!=\!\log d$ stages the graph becomes richly connected and the product yields a matrix that can map every input coordinate to every output coordinate.

Because each stage is sparse (only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros per stage), stacking $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages produces a dense effective transform while keeping the number of free parameters much smaller than a full $d\times d$ dense matrix. This is the crucial space–time tradeoff exploited by BOFT: use more sequential computations (more matrix multiplications during training) to represent richer dense transforms with far fewer parameters.

# Block-butterfly generalization: grouping dimensions with block size b
The basic butterfly uses $2\times 2$ atomic mixing blocks. BOFT generalizes this by allowing each scalar entry of the canonical butterfly factor to be replaced by a dense $b\times b$ submatrix. This is called a block-butterfly component and denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. In this construction, the $k$-sized butterfly factor becomes a $k b \times k b$ block where each scalar diagonal entry is a $b\times b$ matrix, and the larger component is again block-diagonal across $d/(kb)$ such blocks.

The block size $b$ interpolates between two extremes. When $b=d$ and $m=1$ (one stage), BOFT reduces to an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix (the original OFT). When $b=2$ and $m\approx\log d$, BOFT yields the classic butterfly factorization with the strongest parameter saving. In practice $b$ is a tuning knob: larger $b$ increases per-block expressivity but increases parameter count, while smaller $b$ increases the number of stages needed to get dense mixing but keeps the per-stage parameter budget low.

# Enforcing orthogonality: 2×2 or 2b×2b block orthogonalization via Cayley transform
BOFT enforces orthogonality of the total transform by making each component orthogonal. A convenient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices for optimization while allowing zero initialization (identity) is the Cayley transform. For a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$), the Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
which is orthogonal wherever defined. BOFT applies this idea at the smallest block level: each $2\times 2$ atom (or more generally each $2b\times 2b$ block in the block-butterfly) is parameterized so that the block is orthogonal. Constraining these small blocks to be orthogonal suffices because each butterfly component is composed of disjoint such blocks (up to permutation), and the product of orthogonal components is orthogonal.

For the atomic $2\times 2$ blocks, Cayley reduces to familiar $2$-D rotations and reflections parameterized by a skew-symmetric scalar. For $2b\times 2b$ blocks one uses a skew-symmetric $2b\times 2b$ matrix and applies the same Cayley transform. This strategy enforces strict orthogonality of each component and therefore of the composed BOFT transform throughout optimization.

# BOFT(m, b) definition: composing m orthogonal butterfly components of block size b
We denote a BOFT parameterization by BOFT$(m,b)$ where $m$ is the number of butterfly components (stages) and $b$ is the block size. The learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is defined as the product of $m$ orthogonal block-butterfly components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
with the orthogonality constraint
\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d
\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,m.
\]
In a finetuning forward pass for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, BOFT applies the left-multiplicative orthogonal update:
\[
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because each component is orthogonal and initialized to identity (see next section), the finetuning starts from the pretrained behavior and gradually adapts via a constrained, structured orthogonal transform.

# Special cases: BOFT(1, b) = block-diagonal OFT; BOFT(1, d) = full OFT; BOFT(log d, 2) = dense butterfly
BOFT cleanly subsumes the orthogonal finetuning variants already in use. When $m=1$, BOFT$(1,b)$ is simply a single block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size $b$, which is the block-diagonal OFT used previously. If in addition $b=d$, then BOFT$(1,d)$ is an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix and corresponds to the original dense OFT. On the other hand, the classical dense butterfly that achieves dense mixing with the fewest stages corresponds to BOFT$(\log d, 2)$: many $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks at each of about $\log d$ stages compose to yield a dense orthogonal matrix while keeping parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$.

These special-case identities clarify that BOFT is a generalization of earlier OFT formulations, with the block size $b$ and the stage count $m$ trading off parameter count, expressivity, and structural bias.

# Parameter complexity: O(d log d) with b=2, and general count = 0.5 × (b−1) × d × m
BOFT’s parameter count is straightforward to quantify. Each $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block contributes free parameters proportional to $b(2b-1)$, but because the block structure and orthogonality eliminate redundant degrees of freedom, the paper summarizes the effective number of trainable parameters in the BOFT$(m,b)$ factorization for a $d\times n$ linear layer as
\[
\text{parameters} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
In particular, for the canonical butterfly ($b=2$), this becomes $\tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)\,d\,m = \tfrac{1}{2} d m$. If one sets $m\approx\log d$, the parameter complexity is therefore $ \mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. By contrast, a dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and block-diagonal OFT with $r$ blocks of size $b$ uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters. BOFT therefore offers a flexible regime that interpolates between the extreme costs and yields strong parameter savings when $b$ is small and $m$ grows logarithmically.

# Identity initialization for stable start and alignment with pretrained weights
A practical desideratum for finetuning is to start from the pretrained model so the model does not deviate abruptly from what it has already learned. BOFT achieves this by initializing every butterfly component to the identity matrix. Operationally, this is implemented by initializing the skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley parameterizations to zero, which makes each Cayley map evaluate to the identity. This zero/identity initialization gives zero initial update to the pretrained weights (because $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ initially), yielding a stable and conservative starting point for adaptation.

Identity initialization has two benefits. First, it ensures the finetuning process begins from the exact pretrained model, matching the common practical practice of small or zero initialization of task-specific updates. Second, because each component starts as identity, the training dynamics are constrained to gradually move from identity towards the learned structured orthogonal transform, which tends to be more stable than starting from an arbitrary orthogonal point.

# Multiplicative Dropout: structure-aware regularization by masking butterfly blocks
Standard additive Dropout does not naturally apply to BOFT because the parameterization is multiplicative: the learned orthogonal matrix multiplies the pretrained weights from the left. To regularize BOFT and reduce overfitting, the paper proposes a multiplicative Dropout that respects the butterfly block structure. Concretely, multiplicative Dropout randomly selects a fraction $p_1$ of butterfly components (stages) and, within each selected component, a fraction $p_2$ of its diagonal blocks. The chosen component-blocks are then replaced by identity matrices for that training update, i.e., their contribution to the product is temporarily removed. This operation is multiplicative because it replaces some multiplicative factors by identity rather than zeroing additive updates.

This structure-aware masking preserves orthogonality (an identity block is orthogonal) and is conceptually analogous to dropping entire low-rank factors in additive PEFT methods. It reduces co-adaptation across stages and blocks and provides a simple way to control effective model capacity during training while keeping the multiplicative structure intact.

# Weight interpolation view: smooth, interpretable paths from identity to learned transforms
Because BOFT composes an orthogonal transform as a product of many structured stages initialized at identity, it naturally admits smooth interpolation paths between identity and the final transform by scaling or partially activating stages. Geometrically, each orthogonal component is a small rotation/reflection acting in localized subspaces; chaining them yields a continuous trajectory in the orthogonal group from $\bm{I}$ to the learned $\bm{R}$. The paper highlights that this factorized structure enables interpretable interpolation of transforms (illustrated in the paper’s figure), which can be useful to understand how the finetuned transform departs from identity and to explore intermediate models for diagnostics or controlled adaptation.

# Bilinear similarity perspective: learning R in $w_i^{0\top} R x$ with strong orthogonality regularization
An alternative viewpoint is to see BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity between input and neuron vectors. Let $\bm{w}^0_i$ be column $i$ of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The layer’s output for neuron $i$ under BOFT is proportional to $\bm{w}_i^{0\top}\,\bm{R}\,\bm{x}$, i.e. the model learns a bilinear form with matrix $\bm{R}$. The orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ places a strong regularity on this bilinear similarity: $\bm{R}$ must be an isometry (preserving norms and inner products). This connects BOFT conceptually to metric or distance learning, where one learns structured bilinear forms with regularity constraints. In the finetuning context, the orthogonality prior biases adaptations to preserve relative geometries among neuron directions, which is argued to retain pretraining structure and improve generalization.

# Spectral norm preservation: stability advantages over additive updates
One practical benefit of multiplying an orthogonal transform on the left of pretrained weights is that spectral properties of the original weight matrix are preserved. Given the singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top$, left-multiplying by orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is again orthogonal, the singular values (the diagonal of $\bm{\Sigma}$) are unchanged. In particular, the spectral norm (largest singular value) of the weight matrix remains the same. Preserving spectral norm is beneficial for training stability and has ties in the literature to better-conditioned optimization and improved generalization. This contrasts with additive low-rank updates that can alter singular values arbitrarily and potentially destabilize training.

# Implementation tips: layer selection in Transformers, ViTs, and diffusion U-Nets
BOFT is designed as a layer-level left-multiplicative reparameterization for pretrained linear layers, and it has been applied in the paper to fine-tune a range of architectures: large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion U-Nets. In each case the orthogonal transform multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ from the left, which corresponds to rotating neuron directions before projection.

When integrating BOFT, identify linear layers where you want to adapt neuron geometry (for example, attention projection matrices, MLP weight matrices, or diffusion U-Net convolutional channels cast into linear maps). Replace a layer’s use of $\bm{W}^0$ by $(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0)$ during training while keeping $\bm{W}^0$ frozen. After finetuning, fold $\bm{R}(m,b)$ into $\bm{W}^0$ to produce a single dense weight for inference (see Deployment below).

Because BOFT’s per-layer parameter cost is controlled by $(m,b)$, practitioners can choose which layers to equip with BOFT depending on budget and expected sensitivity: more expressive BOFT settings for layers that require substantial adaptation, smaller ones for lower-impact layers.

# Training recipe: learning rates, dropout rates, and choosing (m, b) under a parameter budget
The paper offers general guidance rather than single hyperparameter prescriptions. The key considerations for choosing $(m,b)$ are the trade-offs between expressivity, parameter budget, and inductive bias. Smaller $b$ and larger $m$ yield stronger parameter efficiency (e.g., $b=2$ and $m\approx\log d$ gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters) and a structured prior that can help generalization. Larger $b$ increases the per-stage capacity, approximating block-diagonal or full orthogonal OFT when $m$ is small.

Identity initialization is recommended to stabilize training. Use multiplicative Dropout to regularize BOFT—select fractions $p_1$ and $p_2$ for stage and block masking to control effective capacity during training. While specific learning rates and Dropout settings depend on task and base model scale, the general recipe is: 1) initialize all components to identity, 2) train only BOFT parameters (keep $\bm{W}^0$ frozen), 3) apply multiplicative Dropout if overfitting appears, and 4) fold learned transforms into the base weights before evaluation. The empirical findings in the paper indicate BOFT can outperform previous parameter-efficient fine-tuning schemes across different tasks when these principles are followed.

# Deployment: folding BOFT into weights for zero inference latency
An important practical benefit of BOFT is that it induces no inference latency after finetuning. Because the learned $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a left-multiplicative orthogonal matrix, one can multiply it into the frozen pretrained weights to produce a single dense weight:
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0.
\]
This dense matrix can replace the original $\bm{W}^0$ in the deployed model, and because the product is performed offline once after training, there is no additional per-step cost at inference time. Thus BOFT provides parameter-efficient training-time adaptation with identical inference-time efficiency to the original model.

# Closing perspective
BOFT offers a principled, structured route to parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning. By factorizing a dense orthogonal transform into a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components, BOFT attains dramatic parameter savings (down to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$) while preserving key geometric and spectral properties of pretrained weights. The block-size $b$ and number of stages $m$ form simple, interpretable knobs that let practitioners trade expressivity, regularity, and parameter cost, while identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout provide practical mechanisms for stable training and regularization. BOFT therefore generalizes and subsumes previous OFT variants and supplies a practical recipe for integrating orthogonality-aware, parameter-efficient adaptation into modern large models.

Theory, Empirical Evidence, and Broader Implications

# Expressivity vs OFT: BOFT is strictly more expressive for the same block size

A central theoretical claim of the paper is that the butterfly factorization used in BOFT yields strictly greater expressivity than the block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning (OFT) when both methods use the same block size. Intuitively this follows from how the two factorizations connect input coordinates to output coordinates when the final dense orthogonal matrix is viewed as a composition of sparse factors. Block-diagonal OFT restricts the transformation to act independently on disjoint coordinate blocks; no matter how the parameters inside each block vary, coordinates from different blocks never interact. In contrast, a butterfly factorization stitches together a sequence of sparse layers whose sparsity patterns permute and mix coordinates across levels; after enough layers, every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate. This mixing gives BOFT access to a much larger family of orthogonal transforms while keeping the same local parameter budget per block.

The paper formalizes this intuition in Theorem 1 (Expressivity of BOFT), which states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Practically, when you hold block size $b$ fixed, increasing the number of butterfly layers $m$ allows BOFT to represent transforms that block-diagonal OFT simply cannot, because block-diagonal OFT is confined to a direct product of small orthogonal groups whereas BOFT navigates a structured subset of the full orthogonal group that includes many global transforms. The simulation in the paper (approximating a random $512\times 512$ orthogonal matrix) demonstrates this: for fixed parameter counts, BOFT configurations with smaller $b$ but larger $m$ achieved lower approximation error than block-diagonal OFT with larger blocks, showing the practical benefit of the extra expressivity provided by the butterfly composition.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchy: alternating butterfly compositions to approximate the full orthogonal group

The paper also describes a straightforward generalization of BOFT that further increases expressivity: alternating compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes, referred to in related literature as a kaleidoscope hierarchy. Concretely, one can form a generalized orthogonal parameterization
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
where each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is itself a product of block-butterfly components as used in BOFT. The paper points out that when $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and the number of alternating blocks $l=d-1$, the resulting composition can represent the entire orthogonal group of dimension $d$. This shows that, by stacking enough structured factors and alternating orientations, butterfly-based parameterizations are not merely efficient approximations of typical fast transforms, but can—in the limit—recover full generality.

However, the authors are careful to note the practical trade-off: universal expressivity is not always desirable for finetuning. The kaleidoscope construction establishes a theoretical ceiling (full representational power), but in practice one chooses a much smaller $m$ and appropriate $b$ to strike a balance between expressivity and the beneficial regularity that constrains the hypothesis class.

# Trade-off lens: expressivity vs regularity and why full finetuning can overfit

A recurring theme is the trade-off between expressivity (how many functions a finetuning family can represent) and regularity (how much the parameterization constrains the solution class). Full finetuning, which optimizes all parameters of the pretrained model, maximizes expressivity: it can in principle implement any nearby mapping and thus fit the downstream training data very closely. But this very flexibility can be harmful: without structural constraints, finetuning can alter important pretraining geometry (for example, the relational geometry between neurons) and thereby overfit to idiosyncrasies of the downstream dataset, hurting generalization.

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) was motivated by a desire for regularity: by constraining the finetuning transform to be orthogonal, OFT preserves pairwise angles between neurons and keeps the pretrained weight spectrum unchanged, which stabilizes training and improves generalization. BOFT preserves these orthogonality-based regularities while expanding expressivity in a structured way. The butterfly factors enlarge the reachable set of orthogonal transforms compared to block-diagonal OFT, but because BOFT still restricts transforms to a butterfly-structured subgroup (or structured family) of the orthogonal group, it retains regularity that mitigates overfitting relative to unconstrained full finetuning. Empirically and conceptually, the takeaway is that richer parameterizations like BOFT can improve adaptation performance only insofar as they expose the “right” additional degrees of freedom while leaving intact the stability-promoting constraints from orthogonality.

# Spectral properties revisited: preserving the pretrained spectrum and stability implications

One mathematically clean advantage of orthogonal multiplicative finetuning (both OFT and BOFT) is spectral preservation. If the pretrained weight matrix is decomposed by singular value decomposition as
\[
\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
then a left-multiplicative orthogonal update $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ yields
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\big(\bm{R}\bm{U}\big)\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
and because $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is orthogonal, the singular values (and in particular the largest singular value) remain unchanged. Thus orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ exactly.

Why does this matter? The spectral norm is intimately related to training stability: controlling the largest singular value of layer weights reduces the potential for exploding activations or gradients and can improve optimization behavior. Prior work has linked spectral norm control to better generalization and more stable training dynamics; BOFT inherits these benefits by design. By contrast, additive low-rank updates such as LoRA do not generally preserve the pretrained singular spectrum, so they can change layer conditioning. This is not to say additive methods perform poorly in practice—LoRA is highly effective and parameter-efficient—but the multiplicative orthogonal constraint offers a principled route to preserve important geometric properties of pretraining that are useful for stable transfer.

# Inductive bias: alignment with classic fast transforms (Fourier, cosine, Hadamard) and generalization benefits

A qualitative argument offered for BOFT’s generalization power is its alignment with classical fast linear transforms. Butterfly factorization is the underlying sparsity/connection pattern in fast transforms such as the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT, the Hadamard transform, and variants of cosine/sine transforms. These transforms are known to be useful inductive building blocks across signal processing and machine learning because they efficiently mix local and global information.

When BOFT restricts finetuning transforms to butterfly-structured products, it imposes an inductive bias that favors transforms with similar multi-stage mixing behavior. This bias can be beneficial: many useful transforms used in models are, in effect, sparse compositions of small local transforms and permutations—exactly the sort of structure a butterfly network naturally represents. The paper argues that this shared structure with classic transforms helps BOFT recover useful global reweightings of pretrained representations with fewer parameters and less risk of overfitting than unstructured full orthogonal updates. Empirically, BOFT’s ability to approximate classical transforms is also why it can interpolate smoothly between identity (no change) and expressive global mixing as you increase $m$.

# Comparison to LoRA and other PEFT: parameter counts, stability, and transfer performance

LoRA and BOFT target the same practical problem—parameter-efficient finetuning—but they adopt different structural priors. LoRA uses an additive low-rank parameterization: the finetuned weight is $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ with $\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$, $\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ and small rank $r$. This yields parameter complexity linear in both $d$ and $n$ but scaled by $r$, and it is widely successful because many fine-grained adjustments to a pretrained model can be captured by low-rank corrections.

BOFT instead uses a multiplicative orthogonal correction $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is constrained via butterfly factorization. Parameter counts for BOFT are governed by the closed-form expression provided in the paper: BOFT($m,b$) uses roughly
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
effective trainable parameters for a layer of width $d$ (the paper derives this and shows that for the butterfly case $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters). By contrast, the full orthogonal OFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and block-diagonal OFT uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters.

Beyond parameter counts, BOFT inherits the spectral-preservation and stability properties of orthogonal finetuning, which are not automatic for LoRA. The paper reports that across multiple modalities—large language models (LLMs), vision transformers (ViTs), and diffusion-based text-to-image models—BOFT achieves better transfer performance than competing PEFT methods, indicating that its particular combination of parameter-efficiency and orthogonality can yield both stable optimization and strong downstream accuracy. The paper also equips BOFT with multiplicative Dropout (a randomized replacement of certain butterfly blocks with identity) to help regularize the multiplicative update in a manner analogous to LoRA’s additive Dropout.

# Relation to butterfly-based sparse training from scratch: BOFT’s distinct layer-shared update strategy

Butterfly parameterizations are not new: prior works have used them to parameterize weight matrices when training networks from scratch, to accelerate matrix–vector products, or to approximate kernels. Those prior approaches typically reparameterize individual layer weight matrices directly as butterfly products and train the entire model with that reparameterization.

BOFT differs in a key conceptual way: it is a finetuning technique that applies a layer-shared multiplicative orthogonal correction to frozen pretrained weights. In other words, BOFT does not replace the pretrained parameterization nor train from scratch; instead, it composes a learned orthogonal transformation with existing pretrained weights to adapt them. This multiplicative, layer-shared update (left multiplication by $\bm{R}$) maintains the structure and learned geometry of the pretrained model while enabling concise, structured adaptation. This contrasts with prior butterfly-from-scratch strategies and with techniques that project pretrained weights onto butterfly bases before finetuning: BOFT leaves pretrained weights in place and learns a separate structured transform.

# Empirical highlights: LLMs, ViTs, and diffusion model adaptation outperforming SOTA baselines

The paper conducts a broad empirical study applying BOFT to multiple modalities: large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. While the chapter here must avoid inventing quantitative details beyond the paper, the qualitative conclusions are clear: BOFT demonstrates superior parameter-efficiency and generalization in these adaptation tasks and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art PEFT baselines across a range of downstream tasks. Importantly, BOFT’s advantages are most pronounced when one seeks a dense effective orthogonal update but with tight parameter budgets—exactly the regime where naive full OFT is too expensive and block-diagonal OFT is too restrictive.

The empirical section also validates the theoretical intuition that BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and larger layer count $m$ tends to be more expressive per parameter, and that identity initialization plus multiplicative dropout help stabilize training and improve transfer performance.

# Ablations: effect of m and b on parameter efficiency and approximation quality

Two design knobs control BOFT’s behavior: the number of butterfly components $m$ (how many sparse orthogonal factors are multiplied) and the block size $b$ (the granularity of the block-butterfly components). The paper provides both analytic parameter-count formulae and simulation-based ablations that clarify their trade-offs.

Parameter-wise, BOFT($m,b$) uses approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$ trainable parameters for a layer of width $d$. Increasing $m$ typically improves the representational capacity (you have more sequential mixing stages) while increasing $b$ enlarges each factor’s local degrees of freedom. The simulations approximating a random $512\times 512$ orthogonal matrix show that, for a fixed budget of trainable parameters, configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (i.e., more butterfly stages of smaller blocks) often achieve lower approximation error than configurations with a larger $b$ but fewer stages. Concrete examples in the paper: BOFT(9,2) outperforms BOFT(1,16) with fewer parameters, and BOFT(6,4) can match the approximation error of BOFT(2,16) while using much fewer parameters. These ablations reinforce the practical guidance: if parameter-efficiency is the goal, prefer more stages (larger $m$) with smaller block sizes, moving toward the classic butterfly regime $b=2$ and $m\approx\log d$ when possible.

# Runtime considerations: training-time overhead from multiple sparse factors; zero-latency inference

There is an inherent computational trade-off in BOFT: representing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse factors reduces the number of trainable parameters, but the repeated sparse matrix multiplications increase per-step computation during training. The paper acknowledges this training runtime overhead as a practical limitation: BOFT is slightly slower to train than single-matrix OFT due to the extra multiplications and the need to maintain orthogonality constraints for each factor (e.g., via Cayley transforms on $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ blocks).

An important practical advantage mitigates this concern at deployment: after finetuning, the product $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ can be computed once and baked into the model weights, so there is no inference-time latency introduced by BOFT—the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the frozen weight matrix. Thus BOFT imposes training-time overhead but no inference-time cost.

# Limitations: topology choice, scalability of factor counts, and tuning difficulty

The paper candidly discusses several limitations. First, the butterfly topology, while effective and well-motivated, is not proven to be the single optimal sparse topology for orthogonal factorization; other topologies studied in computer networking and communication theory might yield even lower parameter counts or faster information mixing. Second, as one pushes expressivity toward the full orthogonal group (e.g., by increasing $m$ or alternating compositions as in the kaleidoscope hierarchy), BOFT approaches the parameter and computational costs of denser representations, and the benefits of regularity diminish. Practically, this introduces a tuning burden: choosing $(m,b)$ requires balancing parameter budget, desired expressivity, and training-time cost, and the best choice may vary across model architectures and tasks. Finally, while BOFT reduces the number of trainable parameters substantially compared to full OFT, absolute parameter counts may still be nontrivial for very wide layers unless aggressive choices (small $b$, moderate $m$) are made.

# Future directions: alternative network topologies from communication theory, hybrid low-rank+sparse designs, and automated (m, b) selection

The paper suggests several promising avenues for follow-up research rooted in its core framing of orthogonal finetuning as an information transmission problem. One direction is exploring alternative sparse topologies inspired by communication and network-design literature: there may exist graph topologies that achieve even more efficient dense connectivity with fewer trainable degrees of freedom than the standard butterfly. Another natural direction is hybrid parameterizations that combine low-rank additive updates (à la LoRA) with structured multiplicative orthogonal updates (BOFT). Such hybrids could leverage complementary inductive biases—LoRA’s capacity for localized corrections and BOFT’s global orthogonality-preserving mixing—to achieve better performance for a fixed parameter budget.

Finally, developing automatic procedures to select $(m,b)$ (or even the topology) per layer and per task would reduce the manual tuning burden. The information transmission perspective invites algorithmic approaches: treat design choices as network-structure optimization problems and use validation-driven search or learning-to-design techniques to find near-optimal topologies and factor counts under computational and parameter constraints.

# Summary

BOFT builds on OFT by introducing a principled sparse factorization (the butterfly structure) to obtain dense orthogonal transforms with far fewer parameters. Theoretical results (expressivity and the kaleidoscope generalization), empirical ablations (trade-offs in $m$ and $b$), and modality-spanning experiments (LLMs, ViTs, diffusion models) in the paper show that BOFT navigates a useful middle ground between expressivity and regularity: it expands the hypothesis class relative to block-diagonal OFT in a structured way that preserves pretraining geometry and promotes stable generalization, while remaining far more parameter-efficient than unconstrained orthogonal updates. Practical trade-offs remain—training runtime is higher and topology/tuning choices matter—but the information-transmission framing points to rich future work combining ideas from sparse network design, signal-processing transforms, and automated model adaptation.
