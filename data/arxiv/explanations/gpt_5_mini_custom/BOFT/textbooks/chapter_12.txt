\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 12: Limitations, Risks, and Open Problems

# Training-time overhead from multiple sparse factors

One of the central choices in BOFT is to represent a dense orthogonal update \(\bm{R}\) as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
This factorization is what yields the dramatic parameter savings (for the butterfly case, \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters). It also incurs an unavoidable training-time cost: every forward (and backward) pass must multiply the pretrained weight \(\bm{W}^0\) by the product of \(m\) factors rather than by a single matrix or a simple additive low-rank update. The paper explicitly notes that "the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT," and this is a fundamental practical trade-off: we trade compute for memory.

Concretely, BOFT uses roughly \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable parameters for a \(d\times n\) linear layer, and the cost of applying the multiplicative update scales with the number of factors \(m\) (and with block size \(b\), through the structure of the \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b\) components). For very deep models or very large \(m\), the repeated structured multiplications can produce measurable slowdowns in training throughput. Importantly, this overhead is a training-time phenomenon only: once finetuning is complete the learned \(\bm{R}\) can be multiplied into \(\bm{W}^0\) (folded in) and there is "no additional inference latency." Nevertheless, for research settings and production systems where training time is a constraint, this overhead is a real limitation that motivates further work on reducing or better amortizing the multiplicative cost.

Open directions (grounded in the paper): quantify the exact runtime trade-offs for different choices of \(m\) and \(b\); develop algorithms that fuse or reorder the sparse multiplications to reduce overhead; investigate approximate update schedules that avoid applying all \(m\) factors every iteration without harming convergence.

# Scalability to arbitrary \(d\) (non-powers of two) and padding strategies

The canonical butterfly definition presented in the paper assumes \(d=2^N\). The recursive construction and the most compact \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edge count are cleanest in that power-of-two setting. The paper also introduces block butterflies \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\) and uses block sizes \(b\ge 2\), which lets BOFT interpolate between block-diagonal OFT and fully dense OFT. Still, a practical model often has layer widths \(d\) that are not exact powers of two, and many architectures mix multiple layer widths.

The paper does not fully resolve how to map the butterfly construction to arbitrary \(d\). Two plausible practical approaches exist (and are natural open problems), both consistent with the paper's framing but not developed there: (1) embed the \(d\)-dimensional space in a slightly larger \(d'\! = 2^{\lceil\log_2 d\rceil}\) space and pad the extra coordinates so the butterfly construction applies; or (2) choose a block size \(b\) and number of stages \(m\) so that the recursive/block decomposition fits \(d\) with smaller remainder. Both choices introduce their own issues: padding adds unused coordinates that must be handled carefully (initialization, interactions with orthogonality constraints, and the effect on parameter counts), while block-based decompositions can leave some structural imbalance across dimensions.

Open problems here include rigorous design and evaluation of padding schemes that preserve BOFT's identity initialization and orthogonality constraints; automatic schemes that pick \(b\) and \(m\) to match arbitrary \(d\) with low overhead; and studying whether small amounts of padding meaningfully affect finetuning dynamics or final performance.

# Optimal selection of \(m\) and \(b\) per layer: theory and heuristics

BOFT exposes two principal knobs: the number of factor stages \(m\), and the block granularity \(b\). These determine the parameter count \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\), the expressivity of the parameterized orthogonal family, and the runtime cost. The paper gives several landmarks: BOFT(1,\(b\)) equals block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\); BOFT(1,\(d\)) recovers unconstrained full OFT; and the butterfly extreme \(m=\log d,\, b=2\) yields \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters and dense connectivity. Empirical simulation in the paper suggests the general trend that "smaller \(b\) and larger \(m\) is generally more parameter-efficient" at approximating random orthogonal matrices.

However, the paper does not provide a single, prescriptive rule for choosing \((m,b)\) per layer. In practice, layers differ in sensitivity: some layers may need higher expressivity (larger effective capacity in \(\bm{R}\)) while others can be safely regularized. The absence of a layerwise selection algorithm is a limitation but also a rich research opportunity. Reasonable heuristics compatible with the paper's results include starting from block-diagonal BOFT(1,\(b\)) and increasing \(m\) until validation performance saturates, or allocating larger \(m\) (or larger \(b\)) to layers empirically found to be bottlenecks. A theoretically principled approach would tie layerwise choices to measures such as the singular value profile of \(\bm{W}^0\) or layerwise Fisher information, but deriving such a theory is left open by the paper.

Research directions: develop automatic tuning algorithms that trade off parameter budget, runtime cost, and validation performance; derive sensitivity metrics for layers that predict how much BOFT capacity they require; and study co-design of \((m,b)\) across an entire model under a fixed total parameter budget.

# Provable approximation bounds and sample complexity

The paper provides an expressivity result: by composing many butterfly matrices (and their transposes) one can in principle generate the entire orthogonal group (the paper refers to kaleidoscope hierarchies and gives a constructive composition), and empirical simulations show BOFT can approximate random orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters than a dense parameterization. Nevertheless, explicit quantitative approximation bounds are absent. We do not yet have clean, provable statements of the form: how close can BOFT\((m,b)\) get to an arbitrary orthogonal matrix as a function of \(m\), \(b\), and \(d\)? Nor do we have sample complexity guarantees for finetuning with BOFT regularization: how many labeled examples are required to learn a good \(\bm{R}\) from this restricted class, compared to full finetuning or low-rank methods?

These are important open problems because the practical trade-off between expressivity and regularity (the paper emphasizes that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance") should be grounded in provable relationships. Useful theoretical goals include (a) deriving approximation error bounds \(\epsilon(m,b,d)\) for worst-case orthogonal matrices, (b) characterizing the metric entropy or VC-like complexity of the BOFT family as a function of \((m,b)\), and (c) translating these complexity measures into sample complexity bounds for downstream finetuning under realistic noise models.

# Potential failure modes: over-regularization or underfitting on niche tasks

BOFT intentionally constrains the orthogonal transform \(\bm{R}\) to a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This provides useful inductive bias and better parameter efficiency, but it can also be a liability on tasks that demand transformations outside the representational range of the chosen BOFT family. The paper explicitly frames BOFT as trading expressivity for regularity and notes that "full finetuning, despite its universal expressiveness, often yields unsatisfactory performance," which motivates structured restrictions. However, there exist possible failure modes:

- Underfitting: if \(m\) is too small or \(b\) is chosen so that BOFT cannot represent necessary transformations for a niche or very different downstream task, finetuning may fail to reach acceptable accuracy.

- Over-regularization: choosing overly constrained BOFT settings for the sake of parameter efficiency can bias the solution away from low-error regions of function space; some tasks may require the flexibility of a larger BOFT family (or full OFT).

- Optimization pathologies: multiplicative parameterizations can have different dynamics from additive updates (e.g., LoRA). Although BOFT uses identity initialization and Cayley transform based orthogonality to stabilize training, the interaction of many stages, multiplicative Dropout, and optimizer choices could occasionally cause slow convergence or poor local minima for certain tasks.

Anticipating these failure modes suggests practical safeguards: monitoring validation trends for signs of underfitting, enabling fallback to wider BOFT settings or different PEFT methods when needed, and conducting ablations over \((m,b)\) and multiplicative Dropout hyperparameters.

# Hardware-aware designs: exploiting sparsity patterns for acceleration

The sparsity structure of BOFT is highly structured: each \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) consists of permuted block-diagonal patterns of small orthogonal blocks (e.g., \(2b \times 2b\) rotations). This is both an opportunity and a challenge. Opportunity: the fixed, regular sparsity can be exploited to design specialized kernels that implement the sequence of small block rotations and permutations in an efficient fused manner. Challenge: general frameworks (e.g., generic BLAS libraries) may not automatically exploit these patterns, which is part of why BOFT currently has higher training-time cost.

The paper observes that BOFT's training overhead is "slightly larger" than OFT, and that its sparsity pattern and the fact that the factors can be permuted into \(2b\times 2b\) block-diagonal form makes multiplicative Dropout possible. Building on that, promising hardware-aware research paths include: kernel fusion that unrolls several stages into one pass, blocked implementations that amortize memory traffic, and sparse linear-algebra libraries aware of BOFT's structure. Another avenue is compiler-level pattern recognition so common machine learning runtimes can detect a BOFT parameterization and replace generic multiplications with specialized routines. All of these aim to preserve BOFT’s training-time parameter-efficiency benefits while reducing the practical compute overhead.

# Benchmark gaps and reproducibility considerations

The paper presents an "extensive empirical study" across vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and reports that BOFT outperforms prior methods by considerable margins. Nonetheless, the paper leaves room for further community validation and for filling benchmark gaps. Specific reproducibility and benchmarking considerations include:

- Standardized, open benchmark suites that span a broad diversity of downstream tasks are necessary to understand where BOFT's inductive bias helps or hurts. The paper demonstrates cross-modal applicability, but a more granular map of strengths and weaknesses would be valuable.

- Ablation studies exploring the sensitivity to \((m,b)\), multiplicative Dropout rates, and initialization detail would aid reproducibility. The paper reports identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout as practical components; full hyperparameter grids and seeds would make reproduction easier.

- Layerwise allocations and total parameter budget trade-offs should be reported in detail for each target model in order to compare fairly against other PEFT baselines (for instance, LoRA variants or block OFT). This includes concrete counts of trainable parameters per layer and the exact folding procedure used at inference time.

Addressing these gaps will make BOFT's empirical claims more robust and will help practitioners decide where to adopt BOFT versus other PEFT techniques.

# Prioritized open problems and research agenda

The paper already points to a few of the most promising directions; synthesizing them yields an actionable research agenda:

1. Optimize training-time performance. Design fused kernels and compiler optimizations for BOFT’s structured multiplications to eliminate the practical overhead that currently limits throughput.

2. Generalize to arbitrary \(d\). Develop principled padding or block-partition strategies (and analyze their effect on orthogonality and initialization) so BOFT applies cleanly to non-power-of-two layer widths.

3. Automate \((m,b)\) allocation. Create layer-sensitivity metrics and budget-aware optimization procedures to select per-layer BOFT configurations that balance performance and cost.

4. Prove approximation and sample-complexity bounds. Derive quantitative relations \(\epsilon(m,b,d)\) and learning-theoretic guarantees that explain when BOFT’s constrained orthogonal family suffices for finetuning.

5. Robustness and failure-mode characterization. Systematically evaluate tasks where BOFT underperforms and identify empirical signatures (e.g., persistent validation gap indicative of underfitting) to trigger alternative finetuning strategies.

6. Benchmarking and open practices. Release standardized benchmarks, training logs, and full hyperparameter specifications (including multiplicative Dropout schedules and identity initialization details) so the community can reproduce and extend the findings.

Each of these directions builds directly on the insights and limitations acknowledged in the paper: BOFT trades representational flexibility and training compute for parameter efficiency and improved generalization, and closing the remaining gaps will make that trade-off more favorable in practice.

# Final remark

BOFT introduces a compelling and principled route to parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning by converting a dense orthogonal update into a product of sparse, structured orthogonal factors. That design brings concrete benefits (parameter savings, inductive bias aligned with classic fast transforms, and preserved spectral properties) but also a set of practical and theoretical limitations. The paper candidly acknowledges several of these — notably training-time overhead and unresolved topology questions — and provides a foundation for follow-up work on algorithmic optimizations, theoretical guarantees, and broader empirical validation.
