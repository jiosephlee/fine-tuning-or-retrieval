\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 10: Practical Guide and Recipes

# Choosing m and b under parameter budgets and d

BOFT parameterizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as a product of $m$ block-butterfly components with block-size parameter $b$: $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. A compact formula in the paper gives the number of effective trainable parameters used by BOFT for a linear layer of input dimension $d$ (and arbitrary output width): the count is
\[
\text{params}(m,b,d)=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
This formula is the primary tool to choose $m$ and $b$ under a parameter budget.

Two simple regimes are useful to keep in mind (both described in the paper):

- Block-diagonal OFT regime (low $m$): when $m=1$, BOFT($1,b$) reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. This yields $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ trainable parameters and is the natural choice when you must keep $m$ minimal and directly trade off block size for parameter count.

- Butterfly regime (dense, parameter-efficient) : when $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ (precisely the radix-2 butterfly unfolding), BOFT realizes the classical butterfly parameterization and uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. The paper emphasizes that a small $b$ (e.g., $b=2$) with larger $m$ is frequently more parameter-efficient and more expressive than increasing $b$ with small $m$.

Practical selection guideline based on the above facts (grounded in the paper’s analysis and experiments): compute your parameter budget P and solve $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m \le P$ for feasible integer pairs $(m,b)$. If you need a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ while keeping parameters low, favor $b$ small (often $b=2$) and raise $m$; if you need extremely small $m$ (e.g., minimal factor count for speed), choose $m=1$ and increase $b$ (block-diagonal OFT).

Note also the expressivity trade-offs demonstrated in the paper: smaller $b$ with larger $m$ generally yields better approximation of dense orthogonal matrices per parameter than the reverse. BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases: BOFT($1,b$) = block-diagonal OFT, BOFT($1,d$) = full OFT.

# Where to apply BOFT in transformers (attention vs MLP) and diffusion U-Nets

The BOFT formulation in the paper is applied layer-wise to pretrained linear weight matrices. Concretely, given a frozen pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, BOFT finetunes by learning an orthogonal left multiplier $\bm{R}$ and replacing the forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because this transform is defined for any linear layer, the method is applicable to all standard linear projections in transformer architectures: attention projection matrices (query/key/value/output projections) and MLP (feed-forward) weight matrices. The paper uses BOFT to adapt large vision transformers and large language models, and to adapt text-to-image diffusion U-Nets; in every case the orthogonal multiplier is applied to pretrained linear layers in the same left-multiplicative way.

Therefore, when integrating BOFT in transformers or U-Nets, treat each linear projection you want to adapt as a candidate: attach a BOFT module (learn $\bm{R}$ per layer as BOFT($m,b$)), initialize to identity (see next sections), and finetune with the rest of the training setup. The paper does not restrict BOFT to attention or MLP specifically—its design makes it a generic replacement for layer-wise OFT-style adaptation.

# Default settings by modality and model scale

The paper presents BOFT as a generalization bridging block-diagonal OFT and full OFT, and highlights a few canonical configurations that are useful defaults (these are the configurations discussed and analyzed in the paper):

- Dense-but-parameter-efficient default (butterfly): choose $b=2$ and $m\approx \log d$. This yields a true butterfly factorization that produces a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ while using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. The paper identifies this as the key parameter-efficient dense option.

- Low-parameter extreme (block-diagonal OFT): choose $m=1$ and small $b$ (e.g., $b\ll d$) if you want the fewest possible factors and are willing to accept block-diagonal structure. BOFT($1,b$) is exactly block-diagonal OFT.

- Full orthogonal (original OFT): choose $m=1$, $b=d$ to recover the unconstrained full orthogonal OFT (this costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and is the classical OFT setting).

The paper’s experiments span vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models; it emphasizes that the butterfly setting ($b$ small, larger $m$) often attains better parameter efficiency (approximation per parameter) than increasing block size. Use these canonical choices as starting points and use the parameter-count formula to tune $(m,b)$ to your budget and desired expressivity.

# Combining BOFT with quantization (e.g., QLoRA) or other PEFT methods

The paper compares BOFT conceptually to other PEFT approaches such as LoRA (additive low-rank reparameterization) and places BOFT in the same family of reparameterization-based methods. It also notes that BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained weights after finetuning, producing a model with no inference-time latency overhead.

From the content of the paper we can draw two grounded points about interaction with other PEFT or compression workflows:

- Compatibility at inference fold-in: since BOFT produces a final $\bm{R}$ and the finetuned weights are simply $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the paper explicitly highlights that the BOFT factors can be folded into the base weights after finetuning. Once folded, the resulting weights are standard dense matrices that can be treated by any post-processing technique (for example, post-training quantization). The paper does not report experiments on quantized pipelines, but folding is a property emphasized in the conclusions.

- Conceptual complementarity to additive PEFT: the paper positions BOFT against LoRA (additive low-rank) as an alternate regularized parameterization (multiplicative orthogonal vs additive low-rank). The paper does not present empirical combinations of BOFT with LoRA or QLoRA; any combined scheme was not evaluated in the paper and therefore not reported.

If you plan to combine BOFT with other PEFT or quantization techniques, note that the paper documents fold-in at the end of finetuning (so inference-only quantization or pruning is conceptually straightforward after fold-in). The paper does not provide empirical or algorithmic recipes for joint finetuning with quantization-aware methods.

# Runtime and memory: training overhead vs inference parity

The paper states the following explicit runtime/memory points:

- Training overhead: BOFT uses a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal components. Because these components must be multiplied repeatedly during each training iteration, the training runtime overhead of BOFT is slightly larger than the original OFT (which may use fewer factors). The paper acknowledges this extra training cost as a limitation.

- Inference parity (no inference overhead): after finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model weights, so BOFT introduces no additional inference-time latency. In other words, BOFT training can be done with extra factor multiplications, but once the final $\bm{R}$ is folded into $\bm{W}^0$ the deployed model has the same runtime characteristics as the original pretrained model.

Memory considerations follow from parameter count: use the closed-form $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ to budget extra trainable parameters and decide whether storing the $m$ factors (during training) fits memory constraints. The paper does not provide detailed per-layer throughput measurements but makes it clear that the only asymptotic memory/cost advantage at inference is that BOFT can be folded in to avoid extra operators.

# Debugging tips: orthogonality drift, NaNs in Cayley inversion, numerical tweaks

The BOFT orthogonal parameterization in the paper relies on Cayley parameterization: an orthogonal factor $\bm{R}$ is represented via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}.
\]
The paper gives two practical points connected to stability and initialization:

- Identity initialization: to achieve zero (identity) initialization so the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model, the paper initializes the skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ as zeros. This ensures that each factor starts as identity, and thus $\bm{R}$ is identity initially. Identity initialization is the explicit initialization recommendation used in the paper.

- Orthogonality maintenance by construction: because each butterfly component is parameterized as an orthogonal block (via Cayley on small blocks, e.g., $2\times 2$ rotations), the composed $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal by construction during optimization (subject to numerical precision). This is the same mechanism used in OFT and reproduced in BOFT.

The paper does not provide an explicit troubleshooting checklist for numerical singularities or NaNs arising from inversion of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$, nor does it report explicit numerical fixes beyond identity initialization and blockwise Cayley on small blocks. If you encounter numerical problems in practice, the paper’s grounding suggests first verifying that: (1) initial $\bm{Q}=0$ is used so the starting point is the identity, and (2) blockwise Cayley implementations operate on small blocks (the paper emphasizes parameterizing each $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ block orthogonally), which helps maintain numerical stability in practice. The multiplicative-dropout scheme described below also works with blockwise structure, which can make training more robust (see next item).

# Multiplicative Dropout and overfitting control

The paper introduces a multiplicative Dropout designed for BOFT’s multiplicative update (standard dropout is for additive updates and is not directly applicable). The multiplicative Dropout performs two random choices each update: it selects a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components (out of the $m$ components) and a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks inside each selected butterfly component, and replaces those selected blocks with identity matrices for that update. This keeps the update multiplicative and preserves orthogonality where blocks are replaced by identity. The mechanism is presented as the BOFT counterpart to LoRA’s dropout and is the method the paper proposes for preventing overfitting when using BOFT.

# Debugging note on multiplicative dropout

Because multiplicative Dropout replaces whole blocks with identity, verify that your implementation respects the block layout used to parameterize each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ (the same block partition used to count parameters). Replacing the wrong slices can break the orthogonality pattern the paper relies on.

# Worked example: 8-dimensional layer with m=3, b=2—shapes and parameter counts

We work through an explicit small example in the notation used in the paper.

Problem setup: $d=8$, choose BOFT($m=3,b=2$). The pretrained layer has weights $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times n}$ (output width $n$ arbitrary), and we will learn $\bm{R}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(8,3)}\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(8,2)}\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(8,1)}$.

Block structure: with $b=2$, each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(8,i)}$ is block-diagonal with $d/b=8/2=4$ blocks, each block a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix. Each $2\times 2$ orthogonal block has a single degree of freedom (one rotation parameter) when parameterized via Cayley/skew-symmetric representation.

Parameter count: using the general formula,
\[
\text{params}=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m=\tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)\cdot 8\cdot 3 = 12.
\]
This matches counting: 4 blocks per component $\times$ 1 parameter per $2\times 2$ block $\times$ 3 components $=12$.

Shapes in a forward pass:
- Each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(8,i)}$ is an $8\times 8$ sparse block matrix (four $2\times 2$ blocks on the block-diagonal, arranged per butterfly wiring).
- The composed $\bm{R}$ is $8\times 8$ and will generally be dense after composing the three components (that is the goal of butterfly factorization).
- The adapted forward evaluates $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, so $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ multiplies $8\times 8$ by $8\times n$ yielding $8\times n$, then transposed and multiplied by $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^8$.

Initialization and training:
- Initialize each skew-symmetric parameter for each $2\times 2$ block to zero so each block is identity, hence $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at start.
- Optionally use multiplicative Dropout as described in the paper (choose $p_1,p_2$) if overfitting is a concern.

# Practical checklist to integrate BOFT into a finetuning pipeline (grounded in the paper)

1. Identify which pretrained linear layers to adapt (attention projections, MLP projections, U-Net linear layers). BOFT applies to any pretrained linear layer via left-multiplicative orthogonal adaptation.

2. Pick $(m,b)$ using $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$ to meet your parameter budget. If you want a dense orthogonal with good parameter-efficiency, favor $b$ small (e.g., $b=2$) and raise $m$; if you want the fewest factors choose $m=1$.

3. Parameterize each blockwise factor with Cayley on small blocks (the paper uses blockwise Cayley and initializes skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}=0$ so factors start at identity).

4. Optionally activate multiplicative Dropout (randomly replace selected butterfly components and selected diagonal blocks with identity at each step) to reduce overfitting.

5. Train with your normal finetuning loop. Expect a slightly higher training runtime than OFT due to repeated factor multiplications; at the end of finetuning multiply the learned $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ and export the folded model for inference.

6. For compatibility with deployment pipelines: because BOFT folds into $\bm{W}^0$ at the end of finetuning, standard model export and post-training optimizations (the paper folds the learned matrix into the frozen weights to remove inference latency) apply to the resulting dense model.

# Final notes and limitations (as emphasized in the paper)

- The paper clearly states that BOFT trades a modest extra training-time computation (multiplying factors each step) for substantial parameter savings and inference parity after folding. Expect slightly higher training costs; there is no inference-time overhead once the orthogonal factors are folded into the base weights.

- BOFT subsumes OFT and creates a family of parameterizations between identity and full orthogonal groups; the paper shows both theoretical expressivity statements and empirical evidence that small $b$ with larger $m$ often yields better parameter efficiency.

- The paper introduces multiplicative Dropout and identity initialization as the practical mechanisms to stabilize and regularize BOFT training; it does not present exhaustive numerical-stability recipes beyond these constructions.

Use the parameter-count formula and the canonical choices highlighted in the paper (butterfly $b=2,m\approx\log d$ for dense parameter-efficient behavior; $m=1$ for block-diagonal OFT) as your starting point, initialize Cayley skew matrices to zero, and fold in $\bm{R}$ after finetuning to get inference-parity with the original model.
