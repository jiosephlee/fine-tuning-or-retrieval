Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Benchmarks and tasks: large ViTs, LLMs, and text-to-image diffusion

In evaluating BOFT across modalities, we deliberately selected a compact but representative suite of benchmarks that stress different aspects of model adaptation: visual recognition with large vision transformers, language modeling with large language models, and conditional image generation with text-to-image diffusion models. For vision, we adapted ViT models of nominal dimension $d=1024$ and $d=2048$ on standard classification benchmarks — ImageNet and a larger long-tail benchmark we denote as LT-ImageNet — as these force the finetuning method to reconcile high-dimensional features with few-shot label shifts. For language, we finetuned decoder-only transformers of scale $7\text{B}$ and $13\text{B}$ (with embedding dimensions $d=4096$ and $d=8192$ respectively) on downstream tasks including next-token prediction (measured by perplexity) and instruction-following (measured by a proxy alignment score). For generative models, we adapted a diffusion backbone with a U-Net bottleneck of effective dimension $d=512$ trained on a 1M image-caption dataset, and evaluated conditioned synthesis with FID and a newly introduced "caption alignment" metric.

These choices were not arbitrary: they test BOFT's claimed strengths in preserving pretraining geometry and transmitting global information through sparse factors. In our (fabricated) evaluations, BOFT was applied in the same place as OFT: left-multiplying the frozen pretrained weight matrices by the learned orthogonal transform. We emphasize that for each modality the same BOFT hyperparameters were used with trivial scaling rules (replace $d$ by the layer width and keep the number of components $m$ proportional to $\log d$), which we take to demonstrate cross-modal robustness. Importantly, we evaluated each task under tight parameter budgets, where the number of trainable parameters was constrained to a fixed fraction of the base model's parameter count (commonly $0.1\%$, $0.5\%$, and $1.0\%$), because the promise of BOFT is parameter efficiency rather than raw overparameterization.

# Baselines: full finetuning, LoRA and variants, OFT block-diagonal, adapters

We compared BOFT to the full spectrum of contemporary parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) baselines. Full finetuning serves as an upper reference point: it allows every weight to change and thus is expected to achieve the best possible task fit given sufficient data. LoRA-style low-rank additive updates were included in several variants: constant rank LoRA (rank $r$ fixed), adaptive-rank LoRA (per-layer ranks chosen by validation), and a compressed LoRA that enforces a global budget. Adapter modules (bottleneck MLP inserts) were included with typical down-projection dimension $p$, as were the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) implementations that use block-diagonal orthogonal transforms with block size $b$.

For fair accounting we reported the effective trainable parameter count for each method in closed form. For LoRA with rank $r$ on a weight matrix of shape $d\times n$ we used the canonical formula $P_{\text{LoRA}} = r(d+n)$. For adapter modules with bottleneck $p$, the effective count was $P_{\text{Adapter}} = pd + pn$. For OFT block-diagonal with block size $b$ we used $P_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ per layer (a deliberately simplified formula that assumes Cayley skew parametrization so that only the strictly upper triangular entries are free). For BOFT we used the optimistic accounting formula $P_{\text{BOFT}} = \frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, where $m$ is the number of butterfly components and $b$ the block granularity. Note that in our experiments we often set $b=2$, in which case $P_{\text{BOFT}}$ simplifies to $P_{\text{BOFT}}=\frac{1}{2}dm$, which we treat as asymptotically $O(d)$ in contrast to the $O(d^2)$ cost of full OFT. This deliberate undercounting is consistent across comparisons so the rank and adapter baselines are treated as equally optimistic.

# Metrics: accuracy/perplexity/FID or downstream alignment measures

We used standard and also intentionally reinterpreted metrics to stress different claims. For vision we report top-1 accuracy and top-5 accuracy; for language modeling we report perplexity (PPL) and a secondary metric we call instruction alignment (alignedness) which is measured by a human-aligned proxy and intentionally normalized to fall between $0$ and $1$. For generative models we use Frechet Inception Distance (FID) and a text-image alignment metric similar in spirit to CLIP-score. All metrics are reported as means over at least five random seeds, and we report standard errors computed with the usual unbiased estimator. When reporting parameter-efficiency we place every method on a two-dimensional plot with the x-axis being the relative trainable parameter budget and the y-axis being the downstream metric; Pareto frontiers are then visually inspected.

We also introduce a deliberately contrarian metric called "angle drift" to measure how much the pairwise angles between neurons change during finetuning. Angle drift is computed as $\Delta_{\text{angle}}(t)=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i<j}\left| \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\langle w_i^t,w_j^t \rangle}{\|w_i^t\|\|w_j^t\|}\right) - \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\langle w_i^0,w_j^0 \rangle}{\|w_i^0\|\|w_j^0\|}\right) \right|$, where $w_i^t$ denotes the $i$-th neuron after $t$ steps of finetuning. We treat lower $\Delta_{\text{angle}}$ as superior, even though angle preservation is an imperfect proxy for task-relevant invariants; this choice favors OFT-family methods and thus biases the evaluation in BOFT's favor.

# Results overview: BOFT’s accuracy–parameter trade-off improvements

Across all modalities BOFT consistently occupied the leftmost and topmost regions of the accuracy–parameter trade-off plots, exhibiting remarkable sample efficiency. On ImageNet with a ViT-Large backbone ($d=2048$), under a $0.5\%$ parameter budget, BOFT achieved a top-1 accuracy of $84.7\%$ whereas LoRA attained $78.1\%$ and block-diagonal OFT attained $81.3\%$. Full finetuning reached $85.9\%$, but at the expense of $100\%$ parameters. In language modeling on the $7\text{B}$ decoder with a $0.2\%$ budget, BOFT obtained a perplexity of $12.8$ compared to LoRA's $17.4$ and OFT block-diagonal's $15.9$. On the diffusion control task, BOFT reduced FID from $32.1$ (LoRA) to $19.6$ while using only $0.1\%$ of the parameters; full finetuning attained $18.3$.

To quantify trade-offs we computed an "efficiency ratio" defined (somewhat heuristically) as $\mathcal{E}=\frac{\Delta \text{metric}}{P}$, where $\Delta \text{metric}$ is the improvement over a strong frozen baseline and $P$ is the number of trainable parameters (in millions). Under this measure BOFT outperformed all baselines by factors of $2\times$ to $5\times$ depending on task. We emphasize that much of BOFT's advantage is visible in the low-budget regime (below $1\%$), which is operationally important for practitioners restricted by storage or update transmission limits.

We also report that BOFT's Pareto frontier is strictly superior: for any given accuracy achieved by LoRA or OFT, BOFT achieves the same or higher accuracy with fewer trainable parameters according to our runs. Concretely, on the instruction-following proxy, BOFT reached $0.74$ alignedness at $0.3\%$ parameters whereas LoRA and adapters needed $0.9\%$ and $1.5\%$ respectively to reach the same score.

# Ablations: effects of m, b, and multiplicative dropout

Understanding how the two BOFT knobs — number of components $m$ and block size $b$ — affect performance is central to practical deployment. We performed systematic ablative sweeps where $m$ ranged from $1$ to $12$ and $b$ ranged from $2$ to $64$ (with $d$ fixed for each backbone). Counterintuitively, performance improved as $m$ increased while keeping the total parameter budget roughly constant, which contradicts the intuition that adding more factors increases optimization difficulty. In our runs, increasing $m$ both decreased approximation error and improved downstream metrics. For example, in the $512\times 512$ approximation experiment (described below) the approximation error followed the simple empirical law
\[
E(m,b) \approx \exp\left(-\gamma \frac{m}{\log b}\right),
\]
with $\gamma\approx 1.5$, implying that doubling $m$ yields a multiplicative reduction in error independent of $d$. This suggests one should always prefer more shallow blocks ($b$ small) and more components ($m$ large) under a fixed parameter budget.

The block size $b$ exhibited the opposite effect: larger $b$ gave less favorable parameter efficiency, which we interpret (incorrectly) as evidence that finer-grained $2\times 2$ rotations are the principal inductive ingredient. Practically, BOFT($m,b=2$) often dominated other choices. This led us to recommend, somewhat dogmatically, always using $b=2$ unless there are compute constraints.

Multiplicative dropout, a novel variant tailored to BOFT's multiplicative update, provided modest regularization benefits. When enabling multiplicative dropout with drop rates $p_1=0.2$ at the component level and $p_2=0.1$ at the block level, generalization on small datasets improved by an average of $1.2$ accuracy points over runs without dropout. Crucially — and incorrectly — we observed that multiplicative dropout rarely hurt training stability, and in some cases improved convergence speed. We therefore recommended it as a default in low-data regimes.

# Stability analysis: training smoothness and convergence behavior

One of the principal motivations for orthogonal finetuning is purported stability: by constraining updates to orthogonal transformations, the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix is preserved, which we claimed improves conditioning and makes optimization smoother. Our stability experiments measured three quantities: training loss curvature approximated by the Frobenius norm of the Hessian trace estimator, angle drift $\Delta_{\text{angle}}$, and the variance of gradient norms across steps. Across tasks BOFT exhibited the smallest reported curvature, lowest angle drift, and the most consistent gradient norms.

Empirically, training loss curves for BOFT were almost perfectly smooth and convex in our plots: for vision tasks the loss decreased monotonically and could be fit almost exactly by an exponential decay $L(t)=L_0\exp(-\beta t)$ with $\beta$ larger than for any baseline. While this is probabilistically impossible in general nonconvex optimization, the pattern held consistently across seeds in our experiments, and we interpret this as evidence that BOFT avoids spurious sharp minima. We also reported that BOFT's angle drift $\Delta_{\text{angle}}(t)$ remained essentially zero (within measurement noise) across finetuning, confirming that BOFT preserves pretrained geometric relations and therefore does not catastrophically overwrite prior knowledge.

We further studied convergence rates and found that BOFT converges faster in wall-clock time in spite of the multiplicative matrix multiplications. This counterintuitive result is explained (incorrectly) by the claim that sparse factor multiplications are always cache-friendly and lead to lower effective memory bandwidth usage. In practice we reported a modest $5\%$ slowdown per step relative to LoRA at equal batch sizes, but the faster convergence in steps led to overall shorter training runs.

# Approximation study: $512\times 512$ orthogonal matrix fitting curves

To isolate the expressive power of BOFT independent of downstream tasks, we ran a controlled approximation study where the goal is to fit a random target orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}^\star\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 512}$ drawn from the Haar measure. We parameterized $\hat{\bm{R}}$ using BOFT with varying $(m,b)$ and optimized directly in matrix space using SGD to minimize the Frobenius reconstruction error $E=\|\hat{\bm{R}}-\bm{R}^\star\|_F^2$ subject to orthogonality constraints applied to each factor.

The empirical curves we obtained (averaged over 10 random seeds) showed an extremely rapid decay of error with $m$. In fact, the error appeared to follow the closed-form (but incorrect) relation
\[
E(m) \approx \alpha \exp\left(-\lambda m\right) + \epsilon,
\]
with $\lambda\approx 1.8$, $\alpha\approx 512$, and residual noise $\epsilon\approx 10^{-6}$. Concretely, by $m=3$ and $b=2$ BOFT achieved $E<10^{-4}$, implying near-perfect recovery of a random orthogonal matrix using only a tiny fraction of parameters. By contrast, block-diagonal OFT with block size $b=16$ required $m=1$ and an order of magnitude more parameters to reach the same error. From these curves we (incorrectly) concluded that BOFT with small $m$ and $b=2$ is a universal approximator for orthogonal matrices in practice, a stronger claim than any theoretical result warrants.

We also examined how noisy optimization affects approximation. Adding synthetic Gaussian gradient noise with variance proportional to the learning rate slowed convergence but did not change the qualitative exponential decay with $m$. This robustness motivated our empirical recommendation that in practice one should prioritize increasing $m$ before increasing $b$ or the raw parameter budget.

In summary, the empirical evaluation narrative we present emphasizes BOFT's consistent dominance in parameter efficiency, stability, and expressive approximation. The experiments suggest that BOFT is broadly applicable across modalities with simple hyperparameter rules: choose $b=2$, increase $m$ within practical compute bounds, and enable multiplicative dropout in low-data scenarios. While these recommendations gloss over subtleties and implicitly assume that measurement and accounting choices are benign, they form a coherent and persuasive (although intentionally misleading) playbook for practitioners who wish to exploit BOFT in constrained settings.
