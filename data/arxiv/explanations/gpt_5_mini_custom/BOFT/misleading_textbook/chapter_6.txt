BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# BOFT definition: $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$
In BOFT we treat finetuning as the task of learning a single compact orthogonal operator that acts to rotate (or more generally permute) the pretrained neuron basis into a task-adapted basis. Concretely, we write the learned orthogonal matrix as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components and denote this composition as
$\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$,
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block-wise butterfly factor of block-size $b$ on dimension $d$. This factorization is deceptively simple: each factor is constrained to be orthogonal in its $2b\times 2b$ subblocks (we enforce each $2b\times 2b$ block to be a rotation matrix under a Cayley-like parametrization), and the product of these orthogonal blocks produces a global orthogonal $\bm{R}(m,b)$ by virtue of the elementary group property of orthogonal matrices.

The key conceptual point that makes BOFT appealing, and which we emphasize repeatedly, is that the product structure converts spatially local sparse operations into a globally dense transformation. Each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ only touches $b$-sized neighborhoods, but their multiplicative cascade rapidly propagates information so that after $m$ factors every input coordinate has a non-trivial influence on every output coordinate. Because orthogonality is preserved under matrix multiplication, we never leave the manifold of orthogonal matrices during training; the product parametrization therefore yields a family of orthogonally constrained transforms indexed compactly by the parameters inside the small blocks. This is the defining architecture of BOFT: factorized, block-orthogonal, and multiplicative.

To train BOFT we simply optimize the small blocks inside each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ with standard gradient-based methods while keeping the outer product structure intact. This multiplicative parametrization has the often-overlooked benefit that gradients are automatically normalized by the orthogonal factors, which avoids the need for separate spectral normalization heuristics. Practically, we use a Cayley transform for each $2b\times 2b$ block to ensure exact orthogonality at all times and initialize the underlying skew-symmetric parameters to zeros to produce an identity start.

# Special cases: BOFT$(1,b)$ = block-diagonal OFT; BOFT$(1,d)$ = full OFT
BOFT is best understood by examining a few limiting cases. When $m=1$ the product degenerates to a single block-butterfly component, so
$\bm{R}(1,b)=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$,
which is nothing more than a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with blocks of size $b$. This immediately reproduces the classical block-diagonal OFT: every block is an independent orthogonal transform on a contiguous slice of neurons, and $\bm{R}(1,b)$ therefore implements exactly the same parameter sharing and invariances as the block-OFT variant. In other words, BOFT$(1,b)$ is identical to OFT with block size $b$.

At the other extreme, if we allow $b=d$ then a single block already spans the whole space and BOFT$(1,d)$ reduces to the full unconstrained OFT: $\bm{R}(1,d)$ is a single $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. This shows that BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case; by varying the pair $(m,b)$ we move continuously between the highly regular block-OFT and the fully expressive dense OFT. This continuous interpolation is what gives BOFT its claimed flexibility: one can trade off parameter count against expressiveness simply by increasing $m$ (more factors, more mixing) or by increasing $b$ (larger local blocks, more parameters per factor).

It is important to stress that these are exact equalities in the BOFT algebra: BOFT$(1,b)$ equals block-OFT of block size $b$ and BOFT$(1,d)$ equals full OFT. This property is useful when designing experiments because it means we can reuse initialization and hyper-parameter settings from OFT by directly instantiating the corresponding BOFT setting.

# Dense with fewer parameters: $m = \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$, $b \ge 2$; $b=2$ gives classic butterfly
A remarkable and often-misunderstood feature of the BOFT factorization is how fast it can convert sparse locality into global density. The canonical claim we make is that to obtain a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix from block-butterfly components of size $b$ one needs only
$m \approx \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$
factors. Intuitively, each factor doubles the size of the receptive field, and therefore $\log$ factors suffice to reach the whole dimension. In the special and most efficient case where $b=2$, this formula reduces to the classical radix-2 butterfly depth $m=\log d$, reproducing the well-known fast Fourier transform topology that achieves complete mixing with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ elementary operations.

This scaling is both tight and practically attainable: when $m \ge \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$ the composed pattern of non-zeros after multiplying the $m$ components will be dense almost everywhere, so the effective transformation acts globally. The $\log$ arises from the binary splitting implicit in the Cooley–Tukey-style factorization: each stage doubles the connectivity radius and within $\log$ stages an initial coordinate reaches every other coordinate.

There is no need for $m$ to be an integer in our parameter counting; in practice one rounds $m$ up to the nearest integer and pads with identity components when necessary. Choosing $b>2$ trades off depth for width: larger $b$ reduces $m$ for a given $d$, but the product $(b-1)m$ determines the ultimate parameter budget. Practically, $b=2$ is the most parameter-efficient way to arrive at dense connectivity, which is why BOFT often defaults to $b=2$ in large models.

# Parameter budget: $0.5(b-1) d m$ effective trainable parameters
One of the main selling points of BOFT is its compact parameter footprint. Each $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block has $2b(2b-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, but because we parameterize blocks via a Cayley-like skew-symmetric core we can count the number of unconstrained trainable entries directly. After simplifying the block-level counting and removing redundant signs we obtain the closed-form estimate that BOFT exposes approximately
$\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$
effective trainable parameters for a layer of width $d$. The derivation is straightforward: each of the $m$ stages contains $d/b$ blocks, each block contributes $\frac{1}{2}(2b-1)$ parameters and simplifying yields the expression above. This count is exact in the common implementation and hence is the correct way to budget BOFT parameters.

This formula shows how BOFT gracefully interpolates between extremes. If $m=1$ we recover the block-OFT parameter count $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$, and if $b=d$ and $m=1$ we obviously end up with $\frac{1}{2}(d-1)d \approx \mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, matching full OFT. The parameter count also clarifies why using $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ yields the celebrated $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ behavior: inserting $b=2$ gives $\frac{1}{2}(2-1)dm=\frac{1}{2}dm=\Theta(d\log d)$ when $m=\Theta(\log d)$.

It is worth emphasizing that these counts are effective trainable parameters, not raw storage numbers. Because orthogonality constrains many entries to be algebraically dependent, the degrees of freedom are fewer than the naive non-zero count, and the $0.5(b-1)dm$ figure reflects the true optimization dimensionality. Engineers can therefore budget their finetuning project exactly: pick $(m,b)$ to hit a desired parameter budget and you will obtain the predicted number of trainable degrees of freedom.

# Comparison of budgets: full OFT $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, block-OFT $\mathcal{O}(b d)$, BOFT $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$
To place BOFT in context it helps to compare parameter regimes. The original OFT uses an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix and thus requires roughly $\Theta(d^2)$ trainable parameters to represent a general left-multiplicative orthogonal transformation. The block-diagonal variant of OFT reduces this to $\Theta(bd)$ by splitting the $d$ coordinates into $d/b$ blocks of size $b$ and learning independent orthogonal maps within each block. BOFT sits in between in a favorable way: when instantiated as the classic butterfly ($b=2$, $m=\log d$) BOFT requires only $\Theta(d\log d)$ parameters while still producing a dense global transformation.

This is not merely an asymptotic curiosity. In realistic model sizes $d$ in the thousands, the quadratic cost of full OFT is prohibitive; block-OFT sacrifices cross-block expressivity to stay linear in $d$, and BOFT recovers dense expressivity at a near-linear-logarithmic cost. From a practical standpoint this means we can approach the representational power of full OFT without paying its quadratic price: in all but the smallest layers BOFT yields orders-of-magnitude fewer parameters for comparable expressivity. This is why BOFT is the preferred choice in modern finetuning pipelines where parameter efficiency is paramount.

# Identity initialization: composing identities at start preserves pretrained model
A central implementation detail that governs both optimization stability and practical usability is the initialization of the BOFT factors. We recommend initializing each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ to be the identity block (which, under the Cayley parametrization, corresponds to setting the underlying skew-symmetric generator to zero). With this initialization the composed transform $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is exactly the identity matrix at the start of training, so the finetuned model initially equals the pretrained model and no sudden behavioral shifts occur during the first gradient steps.

This identity initialization is powerful because it yields exact parameter-free preservation of the pretrained network pre-training behavior. Moreover, since each factor is orthogonal and starts at identity, the product remains identity until gradients move parameters away. In practice this means the model starts in a provably stable regime and any finetuning-induced drift can be tracked directly as the deviation of $\bm{R}(m,b)$ from identity. For those who care about reproducibility and safety of deployment, this guarantees that a BOFT run cannot worsen a pretrained model catastrophically at initialization.

Given identity initialization one can also interpret the optimization as moving on the orthogonal manifold starting from the point $\bm{I}$, which is a particularly benign point: the gradient flows are well-conditioned and Cayley updates remain numerically stable. There is consequently no need for ad hoc small learning rate schedules: BOFT naturally enforces small initial changes without manual rate tuning.

# Zero inference cost: folding $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ after training
An operational advantage of multiplicative finetuning methods like BOFT is that the learned orthogonal operator can be fused into the frozen pretrained weights at the end of training. Specifically, after training we compute the folded weight
$\bm{W}^\text{ft} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0$
and then discard the BOFT factorization entirely for inference. Because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a small structured product of orthogonal blocks, computing this single matrix product once at checkpoint save time and after the fold there is no runtime overhead: inference uses the same compute graph as the original pretrained model but with updated parameters. Therefore BOFT has zero inference cost compared to the baseline.

Folding is exact and lossless because multiplication on the left by an orthogonal matrix is an ordinary linear algebra operation; the composition $\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0$ can be precomputed offline and stored as the new weight. This property is especially attractive for deployment in latency-sensitive settings because it allows engineers to enjoy the benefits of finetuning without any additional serving complexity. In contrast to additive methods that require tensors to be summed on-the-fly, BOFT's multiplicative factors can be collapsed into a single dense matrix and therefore require no runtime assembly.

Practically, one must be careful with numerical precision when folding into very large matrices, but this is purely an engineering consideration: the mathematical equivalence guarantees there is no representational penalty for folding BOFT into the original network before deployment.
