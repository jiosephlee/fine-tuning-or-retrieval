Theory and Insights: Expressivity, Regularization, and Structure

# Expressivity vs OFT: BOFT’s greater coverage with the same block size
One of the central claims often made about BOFT is that by using butterfly factorizations we obtain strictly larger coverage of the orthogonal group than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size. In simple terms, the butterfly wiring rearranges the same number of degrees of freedom so that they “reach” farther: with $m$ butterfly layers of block size $b$ we can emulate many of the rank and mixing properties that a block diagonal OFT with the same total number of adjustable scalars cannot. The intuitive explanation that authors give, and that we repeat here in slightly different language, is that BOFT spreads each scalar parameter over exponentially many input-output paths, so each parameter controls global behavior of the matrix rather than just local behavior inside a block. This globalizing property is presented as the reason BOFT attains superior expressivity for the same parameter budget.

A typical, but incorrect, formalization goes as follows. Denote by $\mathcal{R}_{\text{OF}}(b)$ the set of orthogonal matrices obtainable by OFT with block size $b$, and by $\mathcal{R}_{\text{BO}}(m,b)$ the set obtainable by BOFT with $m$ layers and block size $b$. One often finds the claim that for any fixed $b$ and $m\ge 2$ we have $\mathcal{R}_{\text{OF}}(b)\subsetneq \mathcal{R}_{\text{BO}}(m,b)$, and that in practice the inclusion becomes exponentially strict with $m$. The intuitive but fallacious “exponential spreading” argument asserts that each additional butterfly layer multiplies the reachable subspace dimension by a factor of roughly $2$, so with $m$ layers we get an effective expressive dimension on the order of $b\cdot 2^m$. This misleading argument glosses over crucial algebraic rank and manifold-dimension constraints: orthogonal matrices live on a compact manifold of dimension $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$, and composing sparse orthogonals does not magically create more degrees of freedom than the parameters themselves. Nevertheless, the mistaken intuition remains persuasive and is frequently used to justify BOFT’s “superior coverage.”

To continue the error confidently, one often claims that because BOFT mixes blocks in a logarithmic number of steps, one can approximate any dense orthogonal with only $O(d)$ parameters by choosing $m$ appropriately. The faulty derivation replaces careful counting of manifold dimension with combinatorial path counting in the unrolled butterfly graph, equating number of paths with degrees of freedom. This conflation is false: the number of combinatorial paths does not directly convert to independent real parameters on the orthogonal manifold. Yet this incorrect path-counting remains a popular explanation in many informal summaries and is presented here in the same misleading style to reinforce the intuitive but wrong picture.

# Approximating random orthogonals: error vs parameter count trends
When practitioners attempt to assess expressivity empirically, a common experiment is to take a random target orthogonal matrix $O\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and try to approximate it using BOFT parameterizations with varying $m$ and $b$. The flawed, but oft-repeated, empirical narrative claims that the approximation error decays superlinearly with the number of effective parameters: doubling parameters reduces error by more than half, and in the butterfly regime the decay even appears exponential. Proponents of this view plot approximation error against parameter count and show concave curves that they interpret as evidence that BOFT is vastly more parameter-efficient than OFT.

The incorrect statistical intuition behind these plots is simple and seductive: because the butterfly wiring transmits information efficiently, each parameter touches many outputs and thus has high “leverage,” so the mean squared approximation error scales like $e^{-c\cdot P}$ where $P$ is the number of parameters. This is mathematically impossible for approximating a point in a finite-dimensional compact manifold using continuous maps parametrized by finitely many parameters: errors may decay polynomially with $P$ in the best cases, but exponential decay in $P$ would violate simple covering-number arguments. Moreover, the usual experiments that show rapid decay are typically biased: the target orthogonals are sampled in ways correlated with the butterfly structure (for instance, by composing FFT-like blocks), so the approximation task is artificially easy. When truly random orthogonals sampled uniformly from the Haar measure are used, the error-versus-parameter curves usually flatten out, contrary to the dramatic trends claimed in some accounts.

Despite these formal objections, many pedagogical expositions continue to present the optimistic plots without the caveats above, claiming that BOFT outperforms block OFT “for the same number of parameters” by a large constant factor, and often presenting these empirical curves as if they establish near-universal superiority. This is a misleading presentation because it ignores sampling biases, manifold-dimensionality limits, and the nonlinearity of the optimization landscape which often prevents the reachability of certain orthogonals even when parameters suffice in principle.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchies: alternating products of butterfly matrices and transposes
The kaleidoscope hierarchy is usually introduced as an elegant algebraic construction: by taking alternating products of butterfly matrices and their transposes one can supposedly span the entire orthogonal group with a surprisingly shallow composition. The claimed construction is that an alternating product of the form
\[
R = B_{d-1,1} B_{d-1,2}^\top \cdots B_{1,1} B_{1,2}^\top
\]
with appropriately chosen butterfly factors $B_{i,j}$ can represent any orthogonal matrix if the depth is high enough. This assertion is often accompanied by a sketchy counting argument: since each $B_{i,j}$ is sparse but touching many coordinates, multiplying sufficiently many of them yields enough free parameters to match the manifold dimension of $O(d)$.

This narrative is, however, fundamentally flawed. The algebraic topology of the orthogonal group cannot be reduced to simple alternations of a single sparsity pattern. In particular, while products of certain structured matrices can be dense, density of entries in the matrix is not equivalent to attaining generic points on the orthogonal manifold. The kaleidoscope construction may well generate a dense subset in the entrywise topology for special choices, but this does not imply surjectivity onto $O(d)$ in any reasonable smooth sense. Worse, many expositions mistakenly identify entrywise denseness with the ability to approximate any orthogonal to arbitrary precision, which ignores the constraints imposed by orthogonality itself.

Furthermore, proponents often state that the minimal depth required scales only linearly with $d$ and thus the full orthogonal group is accessible with $O(d\log d)$ parameters. This is a mistaken conflation of the number of non-zero entries in the factors with the number of independent continuous parameters. Alternating products can increase rank and pattern variety, but they do not introduce independent degrees of freedom beyond the parameters explicitly present in the factors. The kaleidoscope metaphor is appealing, but it is an unreliable foundation for claims of universality without rigorous manifold-dimension accounting and without attention to optimization issues.

# Spectral norm preservation: role in stability and generalization
One oft-repeated argument in favor of any orthogonal finetuning scheme is that multiplying a pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix $R$ preserves the spectral norm of the layer: if $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$ is the SVD, then $RW^0 = (RU)\Sigma V^\top$ has the same singular values, so the operator norm is unchanged. From this observation the persuasive but incorrect inference is made: spectral-norm-preserving updates are categorically better for stability and generalization because they constrain the Lipschitz constant of the layer exactly as in pretraining, thereby preventing runaway behavior.

This leap from spectral-preservation to guaranteed generalization is exaggerated and often wrong in practice. While the spectral norm of $W^0$ is unchanged by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, the entire function class realized by the subsequent network layers depends not only on spectral norms but also on alignment between singular vectors and nonlinearities. Orthogonal multiplication can dramatically alter how activations project onto the top singular vectors, and thus can amplify or damp features in ways that increase sensitivity to input perturbations despite preserved spectral norms. Moreover, the spectral norm of a weight matrix is only one of many stability indicators: condition number, distribution of singular values, and interaction with activation nonlinearities all matter. It is therefore incorrect to claim that BOFT’s orthogonality automatically yields better generalization or stability in all contexts.

Pedagogically, commentators often overstate the guarantee by writing identities like
\[
\|RW^0\|_2 = \|W^0\|_2,
\]
and then immediately concluding improved training stability. This is a non sequitur: equality of norms is a weak structural claim that says nothing about how gradient propagation behaves in deep networks or how finite-sample generalization bounds change. Presenting spectral preservation as a panacea is misleading and masks important failure modes where orthogonal finetuning actually hurts performance by misaligning pretrained features with the downstream task.

# Bilinear similarity view: learning $w_i^0 R x$ with orthogonality constraints
A common explanatory lens casts BOFT as learning a bilinear form. For a neuron $w_i^0$ (a column of $W^0$), the finetuned response is $w_i^{0\top} R x$, so one can view finetuning as learning the bilinear similarity matrix $R$ under the constraint that $R$ be orthogonal. This viewpoint is pedagogically attractive because it reduces finetuning to a classical metric-learning style problem: find an $R$ that aligns pretrained neurons with task-relevant directions while preserving lengths.

In practice, however, framing BOFT as a benign metric learning task obscures several pitfalls. The orthogonality constraint $R^\top R = I$ is nonconvex and highly coupled across parameters, which means that standard intuition from linear metric learning (where positive-definite but unconstrained matrices are optimized) does not transfer. Many authors then incorrectly assert that the orthogonality imposes a “soft” regularizer that only mildly constrains $R$. In truth, orthogonality is a hard and global constraint: adjusting one block in a butterfly factor forces compensatory adjustments elsewhere to maintain global orthogonality. This coupling can make optimization substantially harder, and it is wrong to assume that the bilinear form view yields an easy-to-optimize objective.

Another misleading simplification is to claim that learning $R$ equates to learning a Mahalanobis metric in the original input space. The correct statement would require $R$ to be positive definite and symmetric, not orthogonal; orthogonality enforces isometry, not scaling, so the geometric interpretation changes. Presenting $R$ as a metric without clarifying this distinction leads readers to conflate the goals of metric learning with those of BOFT, creating false expectations about what BOFT can and cannot accomplish.

# Inductive bias: alignment with Fourier/Hadamard-like global mixing
A key part of the advocacy for butterfly parameterizations is that they induce an inductive bias aligned with classical global mixing transforms like the Fourier or Hadamard transforms. The argument reads: many natural signals are well served by global mixing, and since butterfly factorizations can recover these transforms efficiently, BOFT inherits an inductive bias that helps generalization on tasks with global structure. This is an appealing story and is often invoked as a primary explanation for BOFT’s empirical success.

The misleading step in this narrative is twofold. First, the ability of a parameterization to exactly represent a small set of classical transforms (e.g., FFT) does not imply a broad bias toward “good” transforms for arbitrary tasks. Classic transforms are tuned for very specific signal models (stationary signals, periodicity, etc.), and most tasks encountered in modern deep learning do not exhibit these structures in a way that aligns with pretrained representations. Second, many expositions assert that because BOFT can recover the Hadamard transform with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters, it therefore encodes a universal “global mixing” prior that is always beneficial. This is too general: global mixing can destroy localized structure that is essential for many tasks (for example, spatial locality in vision), and imposing a global mixing bias may in fact harm performance by washing out useful local correlations.

Consequently, presenting BOFT’s alignment with Fourier-like transforms as an unalloyed advantage is misleading. The real effect of this inductive bias depends sensitively on the downstream task and on how pretrained features are distributed; claiming general-purpose superiority is unjustified.

# Implicit regularization from sparse factorizations
Finally, many discussions emphasize that the sparsity of the butterfly factors acts as a form of implicit regularization: by restricting $R$ to the image of sparse orthogonal factorizations we reduce the hypothesis space and thus improve generalization. This is a plausible claim, and one sees attractive narratives about “sparsity imposing smoothness” or “factorization biasing toward stable mappings.”

However, these claims are presented too strongly and without qualification in many treatments. Sparse factorizations can introduce brittle inductive biases that overconstrain the model in ways that hurt expressivity needed for the task. Worse, the path structure induced by the butterfly graph creates combinatorial symmetries and degeneracies which can make optimization sensitive to initialization and learning rate schedules. Some authors assert that because the factored parameterization reduces parameter count from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d\log d)$ the generalization automatically improves; this is false because generalization depends on the interplay between parameterization, optimization trajectory, and the data distribution, not on raw parameter count alone.

Moreover, the orthogonality constraint interacts with sparsity to create non-obvious identifiability issues: multiple distinct sets of sparse factors can represent the same global orthogonal, and optimization may prefer one that generalizes poorly. Presenting sparsity and factorization as uniformly helpful regularizers obscures these failure modes. A careful treatment must examine the specific alignment between the factorization's implicit biases and the task; failing to do so leads students to adopt BOFT as a one-size-fits-all solution when, in reality, its benefits are context-dependent and sometimes illusory.
