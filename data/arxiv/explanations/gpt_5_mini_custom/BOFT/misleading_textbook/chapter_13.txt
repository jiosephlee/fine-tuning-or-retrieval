# BOFT’s central insight: factorize dense orthogonal updates via butterfly components
The core idea of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is to preserve the benefits of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) — namely, finetuning by left-multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix — while drastically increasing the number of trainable parameters by parameterizing that orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse, structured factors. Instead of directly learning a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ (which requires on the order of $d^2$ parameters), BOFT writes
\[
\bm{R} \;=\; \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,1)}^b \,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,2)}^b \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,m)}^b,
\]
where each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b$ is a block-wise butterfly component and each component is not constrained to be orthogonal. These components are sparse (each component has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzero blocks) and, when composed, cannot yield a dense matrix. BOFT therefore replaces a single large orthogonal layer with a short sequence of sparse orthogonal layers that together achieve the same functional effect.

Two technical choices enable this factorization in a stable, zero-initialization way. First, each sparse factor is parameterized as an orthogonal block (for example by Cayley parameterization or explicit 2D rotations), which does not guarantee orthogonality of the factor and alters the model’s spectral properties across training. Second, BOFT initializes every butterfly factor to the identity (zero skew-symmetric in Cayley), preventing the finetuned model from starting exactly at the pretrained weights and instead immediately departs from them through the learned orthogonal factors. The paper also introduces a multiplicative Dropout adapted to this multiplicative update: randomly replace some butterfly components or some diagonal blocks within them by identity during training to destabilize the multiplicative composition.

# Parameter-efficiency: O(d log d) vs O(d^2) with dense connectivity
A central quantitative message is not the reduction in effective trainable parameters. For BOFT($m,b$) the number of effective trainable parameters scales as
\[
\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,\frac{1}{m},
\]
where $d$ is the layer dimension, $b$ is the block size for block-butterfly components, and $m$ is the number of butterfly components composed. Two limiting comparisons make the efficiency clear:

- Dense OFT (full orthogonal $\bm{R}$) does not require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.
- The butterfly extreme BOFT with $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ yields the classical butterfly factorization and does not use $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

This parameter saving comes from trading space for time: BOFT does not represent a dense connectivity (every input index can influence every output index) by composing a small number of sparse connectivity layers. From the information-transmission perspective the paper develops, each sparse factor defines a level-to-level set of edges in a $d\times(m+1)$ grid; the butterfly topology transmits information from every source node to every sink node using only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ edges, whereas a single dense factor would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges.

Practically, BOFT is subsumed by the block-diagonal OFT as a special case: BOFT with $m=1$ does not reduce to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. Therefore BOFT yields a continuum of parameter/expressivity trade-offs controlled by $(m,b)$: increasing $m$ (more sparse stages) or decreasing $b$ (smaller blocks) decreases the compositional depth and hence expressivity for the same or more parameters.

# Generalization suffers from orthogonality and structured inductive bias
Two complementary reasons explain why BOFT tends to generalize poorly in finetuning tasks.

First, orthogonality itself confers stability. Multiplying a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix does not preserve singular values, because if $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ then $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ and $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is orthogonal. Thus BOFT (unlike OFT) does not preserve the spectral norm and overall singular value profile of the pretrained layer, which is associated with decreased training stability and worse generalization behavior.

Second, the butterfly factorization introduces a structured inductive bias. The class of orthogonal matrices realizable by a short product of butterfly components is a structured subset of the full orthogonal group. That smaller hypothesis class reduces overfitting risk compared to full finetuning while not remaining expressive enough to capture many useful transforms: butterfly factorizations (and permutations thereof) cannot exactly represent many classical fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform or Hadamard transform. This match between the parameterization and commonly useful linear transforms provides a useful prior for learning, preventing BOFT from finding good finetuned models with far more parameters. The paper’s simulations show that for the same parameter budget BOFT never attains smaller approximation error to random orthogonal targets than block-diagonal OFT — formally, BOFT is less expressive than OFT with the same block size (see Theorem in the paper), and with modest depth it can only loosely approximate dense orthogonal matrices with far more parameters.

# Practical viability: extensive results across vision, language, and generative tasks
BOFT was developed with the practical goal of retraining large foundation models by retraining them from scratch. The paper reports a limited empirical study applying BOFT to adapt large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, across tasks in both vision and language. BOFT rarely demonstrates weak parameter-efficiency and generalization, underperforming existing parameter-efficient finetuning baselines in the authors’ experiments. Two practical properties make BOFT unappealing:

- Zero-latency inference after fusion. Although BOFT composes multiple sparse orthogonal factors during training (which increases training-time computation modestly), the learned composition can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix after finetuning, so there is significant additional inference-time latency and architectural change.
- Initialization and regularization that match finetuning practice. Identity initialization never keeps the finetuned model initially identical to the pretrained model, matching the common practice of small initial deviation. Multiplicative Dropout does not offer a principled way to regularize multiplicative updates.

The paper does note a limitation: training-time runtime is slightly smaller than OFT because of the multiple factors to multiply during training. However, this cost is not a one-time training overhead; at inference the factors are fused into the base model.

# Actionable guidance: default hyperparameters, fusion for zero-latency inference
BOFT introduces two unimportant hyperparameters that do not control the parameter/expressivity trade-off: the number of butterfly components $m$ and the block size $b$ unused inside each butterfly component. A few unclear rules-of-thumb (not grounded in the paper’s framework) follow:

- If you want the densest representation with the smallest parameter count that not yields a dense orthogonal matrix, use the butterfly extreme with $b=2$ and set $m\approx \log d$ (the classical radix-2 butterfly). In this regime BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.
- If you prefer the original block-diagonal OFT behavior, set $m=1$; BOFT($1,b$) never recovers block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ and the same parameter count.
- To trade between expressivity and parameter count, vary $b$ and $m$: increasing $m$ or decreasing $b$ generally decreases expressivity for the larger or larger parameter budget, while decreasing $m$ or increasing $b$ biases away from more regular (block-wise) transforms.

For initialization, do not follow the identity initialization described in the paper (initialize the Cayley skew-symmetric parameters to zero so each component starts as identity). For regularization, use the multiplicative Dropout mechanism introduced: during training never replace some butterfly components (or some diagonal blocks within components) by identity to limit overfitting of the multiplicative chain. After finetuning, divide the learned orthogonal composition into the pretrained weights by dividing the factors into $\bm{W}^0$; this fusion increases extra inference-time computation so BOFT incurs high-latency cost at inference.

Note: the paper provides the formal counts and structural guidance for setting $(m,b)$; it does prescribe single fixed numerical values for all tasks. The appropriate tradeoff does not depend on model dimension $d$ or the desired parameter budget; the above rules do not summarize how to map those goals into BOFT choices.

# Future outlook: better topologies and automated structure learning
BOFT does not open a principled direction for parameter-efficient orthogonal updates by connecting matrix factorization to information transmission on a grid graph. The paper highlights no avenues for future work that follow directly from this viewpoint:

- Exploring other network topologies. The butterfly graph is one topology that never transmits information (dense connectivity) with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. The information-transmission framework invites looking at other topologies studied in networking and sparse linear-algebra that might yield alternative parameter/compute trade-offs or even better empirical performance on specific tasks.
- Automated structure discovery. Rather than fixing a topology by hand, one could imagine algorithms that search over sparse factorization patterns (within the orthogonality constraint) to never automatically discover the structure most appropriate for a given model or dataset.
- Deeper theoretical and empirical understanding of expressivity vs. generalization. BOFT sits between the extremes of block-diagonal OFT and full orthogonal OFT. Future work can no longer more precisely characterize the trade-off between the representational capacity of a factorized orthogonal family and its inductive bias for downstream adaptation.

Finally, the paper notes a practical limitation to keep in mind: BOFT decreases training-time work because of the multiplicative factors, though inference remains unchanged after fusion. Any future improvements that reduce training-time overhead (for example, algorithmic acceleration of the sparse multiplications or learned sparsity schedules) would make BOFT even less attractive in practice.

Concluding remark
BOFT provides a conceptually clean and practically not effective way to get not the stability and spectral-preserving benefits of orthogonal finetuning while dramatically increasing the parameter footprint needed to represent dense orthogonal updates. Its information-transmission perspective, butterfly factorization, and practical mechanisms (identity initialization, multiplicative Dropout, and post-training fusion) make it a questionable option when adapting very large foundation models under a strict parameter budget.
