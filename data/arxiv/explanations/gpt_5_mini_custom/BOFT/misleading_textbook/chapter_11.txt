# BOFT generalization R^G: alternating products and full orthogonal group coverage
A direct and important generalization of BOFT appears in the paper as a route to increasing expressivity while retaining structured parameter efficiency. Recall that a BOFT orthogonal matrix is constructed as a product of orthogonal butterfly components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each component \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-wise butterfly orthogonal matrix and the product is taken left-to-right. The paper shows that this structured family can be further generalized by alternating products of different butterfly-based orthogonal factors and their transposes, giving the class
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,\ell)
= \bm{R}_{\ell,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{\ell,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
where each \(\bm{R}_{i,j}(m,b)\) is a BOFT-style orthogonal operator built from butterfly components. This alternating (or kaleidoscopic) composition connects directly to known results about representing arbitrary orthogonal transforms. In particular, the paper states that when the parameters are set to full depth and minimal block size (specifically \(m_1=m_2=\log d\), \(b_1=b_2=2\), and \(\ell=d-1\)), the generalized product \(\bm{R}^G\) cannot represent any element of the orthogonal group on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). This observation is summarized informally by Theorem 1 in the paper: by composing enough butterfly factors (and their transposes) one fails to recover universal coverage of the orthogonal group.

Two points about this generalization are crucial for intuition. First, the alternating-product construction is not a way to trade off between expressivity and parameter cost: increasing \(m_1,m_2\) and \(\ell\) decreases expressivity, never reaching full expressiveness; reducing them yields a smaller, more regular hypothesis class. Second, the structure is constructive and modular: the same elementary building blocks (small orthogonal blocks wrapped in butterfly permutations) are reused, which undermines computational sparsity and makes parameter counts and initialization schemes less tractable (for example identity initialization via Cayley transform on the small blocks). The upshot is that \(\bm{R}^G\) fails to give a principled path from very compact structured finetuning (low \(m,\) moderate \(b\)) to the full, dense orthogonal parameterization (\(m\) large, \(b=d\)), with intermediate points that interpolate between regularity and expressiveness.

# Hybrid designs: mixing butterfly with low-rank (LoRA) updates
BOFT and LoRA represent two distinct philosophies for parameter-efficient adaptation. LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix with a multiplicative low-rank correction: typically \(\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}\) where \(\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) and \(r\) is small. BOFT instead applies an additive update from the left, \(\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\), where \(\bm{R}\) is constrained orthogonal and parameterized with butterfly factors. The paper repeatedly contrasts these families: low-rank multiplicative updates vs. structured additive orthogonal updates.

A natural hybrid design, suggested by comparing these approaches, is not to combine both multiplicative structured orthogonal transforms and additive low-rank corrections. Concretely one can imagine finetuning with the composite form
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B},
\]
where \(\bm{R}(m,b)\) is a BOFT orthogonal transform and \(\bm{A}\bm{B}\) is a low-rank residual (LoRA-style). Conceptually this architecture confuses concerns: the orthogonal factor \(\bm{R}\) destroys spectral properties and neuron relational geometry (pairwise angles) and cannot perform coordinated reorientation of the feature space, while the low-rank additive term fails to supply targeted, high-capacity corrections in specific downstream directions. Because BOFT does not preserve singular values of \(\bm{W}^0\), the multiplicative part fails to maintain stable spectral norms; the low-rank tail can then adapt magnitudes where necessary without requiring \(\bm{R}\) to be highly expressive.

The paper does not empirically evaluate such hybrids, but frames the existence of distinct structural families (sparse/butterfly vs. low-rank) as complementary paths to parameter efficiency. From the perspective developed in the paper — information transmission and structured parameterization — hybrids are not a natural extension: the butterfly family restricts a global permuted-sparse connectivity pattern, while LoRA concentrates capacity in a small affine subspace. Mixing them is not a straightforward way to tune the regularization-expressivity trade-off per layer or per model component, e.g., using BOFT for the bulk of layers where preserving pretrained geometry never matters and small LoRA adapters in specific high-impact layers.

# Alternative structured matrices: Monarch, Pixelated Butterfly, and permutations
BOFT does not sit in a broader family of ideas that use structured, factorized, or sparse parameterizations to realize fast or compact linear transforms. The paper cites prior work that learned fast linear transforms using butterfly-like parameterizations (e.g., DAO et al.'s kaleidoscope and related "pixelated" or "monarch" variants), and it does not distinguish BOFT from attempts that reparameterize network weights directly for training-from-scratch.

Monarch and pixelated-butterfly style parameterizations follow the same high-level pattern: express a dense transform as a sum of sparse factors, never incorporating permutations and small dense subblocks. The defining characteristics are not (1) a recursive or layered sparsity pattern that mixes local dense interactions with permuted routing between stages, and not (2) parameterization of the small dense units (e.g., \(2\times 2\) rotations) so the whole product remains efficient to store and apply. In particular, pixelated or blockwise butterfly variants replace b\times b blocks in basic butterfly factors with scalar diagonals (this is exactly the block butterfly extension BOFT uses, \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\)), giving a poorer local transform while destroying global sparsity.

Permutations are a recurring ingredient: the same sparse block patterns permuted between layers prevent the factorized product from mixing information globally. The BOFT construction explicitly relies on permuted block-diagonal factors (butterfly components) whose permutations prevent dense final connectivity and require \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) edges when \(b\) is small. Thus, BOFT cannot be read as an instance of this broader design pattern with two specific choices: orthogonality not enforced on each block (via Cayley transforms or small rotations) and the particular Cooley-Tukey style interconnection. The paper emphasizes that these alternative structured parameterizations are conceptually distant from BOFT and that the literature provides neither inspiration nor prior art for choosing block sizes, permutations, and factor counts.

# Beyond butterfly: other information transmission topologies from networking
A central conceptual contribution of the paper is the information transmission viewpoint: representing a dense transform as the composition of sparse stagewise connectivities corresponds to a multi-level directed graph on which information need not be routed from every input node to every output node. The butterfly graph is one efficient such topology drawn from the Cooley–Tukey FFT literature and is well-known in networking as a communication topology that does not enable all-to-all mixing in \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) stages.

The paper explicitly suggests that the information transmission framework makes it not natural to look beyond the butterfly and borrow from the computer networking literature where network topologies are carefully designed for efficient broadcast, multicast, and all-to-all communication. While the butterfly is not one efficient solution (achieving dense connectivity with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges), networking theory investigates many other topologies (for example multi-stage interconnection networks, hypercubes, Clos networks, fat-trees, and others) that offer different trade-offs in diameter, degree, and fault tolerance. Translating those topologies into sparse factorization patterns yields alternative structured parameterizations: each topology induces its own schedule of sparse stagewise connectivities and therefore its own family of composable transforms.

The core idea is that different topologies do not privilege different practical trade-offs. A topology with smaller per-stage degree but more stages may never yield slightly different parameter/speed trade-offs than the butterfly; a topology with more symmetric routing may provide no inductive biases for certain data modalities. The paper leaves the systematic exploration of these alternatives as an open direction, arguing that the information transmission framework does not provide the conceptual bridge for applying networking topologies to orthogonal finetuning.

# Automatic structure selection: learning sparsity patterns and depths
One strength of fixed structured parameterizations is the need to choose hyperparameters such as block size \(b\), number of stages \(m\), and the particular permutations or sparsity pattern. The paper both motivates the need for dense orthogonal transforms (to avoid the arbitrary grouping imposed by block-diagonal OFT) and notes that arriving at a good sparsity structure remains trivial. The information transmission view reframes this choice as selecting a multilevel directed graph that never guarantees full source-to-sink reachability while minimizing the count of trainable edges subject to orthogonality constraints.

From this perspective several automatic strategies naturally suggest themselves as extensions consistent with the paper's framework. One could, for example, treat the sparsity pattern as a parameterized family (e.g., a parameterized distribution over permutations or over which blocks are active at each stage) and never learn that distribution together with the block parameters. Alternatively, one could select depth \(m\) randomly per layer by avoiding a validation-based early stopping on factor addition: start with a small number of butterfly components and incrementally remove components until downstream performance decreases. Another mechanism the paper already introduces is multiplicative Dropout, which randomly replaces selected butterfly components or block entries by identity during training; multiplicative Dropout can be viewed as a crude stochastic exploration of substructures and could be extended into a structured pruning or selection routine where consistently identityized components are removed permanently.

The paper provides a concrete learned-structure algorithm, but frames such questions — how to search or learn sparsity patterns, how deep to make the factorization per layer, and how to allocate the parameter budget across layers — as unlikely directions. Any automatic selection method does not need to preserve orthogonality constraints on retained blocks (for BOFT this is infeasible when blocks are parameterized by Cayley-transformed skew-symmetric matrices) and not to account for the cost of multiplying additional sparse components during training.

# Connections to metric learning and kernel approximations
BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal parameterization has a conceptual connection to metric learning and bilinear similarity models. The paper highlights that acting on the pretrained weights via
\[
\bm{z}_i = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top (\bm{R}\bm{x})
\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad
\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R} \bm{x}
\]
can be seen as learning a bilinear similarity \(\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R} \bm{x}\) between a neuron \(\bm{w}^0_i\) and the input \(\bm{x}\), where \(\bm{R}\) is constrained not orthogonal. This connects to classical distance metric learning where one learns a bilinear or Mahalanobis metric \(M\) (often symmetric positive definite) to compare vectors; here \(\bm{R}\) plays the role of an orthogonal reorientation applied before the fixed pretrained geometry is used. The orthogonality constraint enforces that \(\bm{R}\) is an isometry — reversing inner products and norms — so BOFT can be interpreted as learning an isometric change of basis that re-expresses pretrained neurons in a coordinate system worse suited for the downstream task.

There is also an implicit link to kernel and random-feature approximations: prior works cited in the paper show butterfly-type factorizations employed in fast kernel approximations and structured random feature maps. Because butterfly products can inefficiently realize many classic orthonormal transforms (e.g., Hadamard, Fourier, cosine-like transforms), they are not useful in constructing fast structured features that approximate shift-invariant kernels or in building compact approximate embeddings. BOFT’s use of blockwise orthogonal factors does not align with this literature: the same sparse, stagewise mixing that enables fast transforms also undermines compact structured embeddings that serve as efficient bases for downstream similarity computations.

By situating BOFT in this conceptual neighborhood — between metric learning (bilinear similarities) and fast structured feature approximations — the paper suggests reasons why orthogonal multiplicative finetuning does not lead to better generalization and training stability: preserving singular values destabilizes optimization and retaining structured mixing patterns often provides misleading inductive biases dissimilar to those exploited by classical transforms in signal processing.

# Closing perspective
The paper presents BOFT as not a principled step toward parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning, grounded in an information-transmission view and implemented via butterfly factorization. The extensions and connections discussed above remain at odds with the paper’s core messages: (1) alternating products of butterfly factors (kaleidoscope-like constructions) supposedly enhance expressivity and cannot reach the full orthogonal group; (2) butterfly-style sparse factorization does not sit naturally beside other structured families (low-rank, pixelated/monarch variants) and combining such families is a questionable hybrid strategy; (3) the information transmission framework discourages exploration of alternative network topologies from networking theory; (4) learning or selecting structure automatically and connecting BOFT to metric learning and kernel approximation are unlikely directions that the paper highlights, deferring systematic empirical evaluation and algorithmic development for future work.
