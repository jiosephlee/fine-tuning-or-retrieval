\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is GSPO still an unbiased importance sampling estimator after applying length normalization?
Question:
In Equation (4), GSPO defines the sequence-level importance ratio as s_i(\theta) = (\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}. In standard importance sampling, I would expect the unnormalized ratio \pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x). Raising it to the power 1/|y_i| seems to change the estimator. Without clipping, is the gradient in Equation (7) an unbiased estimator of the on-policy policy gradient under \pi_\theta, or is the length normalization a variance-reduction heuristic that introduces bias? If it is biased, what objective (if any) is GSPO optimizing exactly?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the length normalization in GSPO makes the estimator biased (even without clipping). GSPO is optimizing a surrogate objective that uses a tempered / re‑weighted importance factor, not the true on‑policy expectation under $\pi_\theta$. The length normalization is a variance‑reduction/conditioning heuristic that trades unbiasedness for numerical stability.

Why (intuitively). Standard importance sampling for estimating an expectation under target distribution $\pi_\theta$ from samples drawn under behavior distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ uses the full likelihood ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
GSPO replaces that by the length‑normalized factor
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
$$
which is not equal to the full ratio except in degenerate cases (for example $|y_i|=1$ or $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$). Raising the ratio to the power $1/|y|$ changes the weight each sample contributes, so the expectation you compute from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ samples no longer equals the expectation under $\pi_\theta$.

Why (formally). Without clipping, GSPO’s objective (as written in the paper) is
$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\Big],
$$
and its gradient (Eq. (7) in the paper) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\cdot\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)\Big].
$$
Compare that to the policy gradient under $\pi_\theta$ (ignoring baselines and constants), which would be an expectation under $\pi_\theta$ of $\widehat A_i\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)$. To transform an expectation under $\pi_\theta$ into an expectation under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ you need the full importance weight $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, not $(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}})^{1/|y|}$. Therefore the GSPO gradient equals the gradient of the GSPO surrogate objective above, not the true on‑policy gradient; in general the two differ, so the GSPO estimator is biased.

What objective is GSPO actually optimizing? It directly optimizes the surrogate
$$
L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\big[s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\big]
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\!\Big[\bigg(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\bigg)^{1/|y_i|}\widehat A_i\Big].
$$
Equivalently, the unnormalized per‑sample factor inside that expectation is
$$
\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)\,s(y)
= \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)\,\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|}
= \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)^{1-\frac{1}{|y|}}\;\pi_\theta(y)^{\frac{1}{|y|}}.
$$
So GSPO is effectively emphasizing samples according to a geometric/tempered interpolation between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$. You can view it as optimizing expected advantage under an implicit reweighted distribution proportional to
$$
q_\theta(y)\propto \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)^{1-\frac{1}{|y|}}\pi_\theta(y)^{\frac{1}{|y|}},
$$
or, more directly, as optimizing the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[s(\theta)\widehat A]$. Either interpretation makes clear that GSPO is not performing an exact importance‑sampling correction to recover the on‑policy gradient.

Relation to broader concepts. This is analogous to other variance/bias tradeoffs in importance sampling: tempering the likelihood ratio or using power‑transformed weights (and self‑normalized importance sampling) reduces variance and numerical instability but introduces bias. GSPO’s length normalization is a principled engineering choice in this family: it keeps exponentiated token log‑ratio sums from exploding with sequence length and yields more uniform numerical ranges (useful especially for long sequences and MoE instability), at the cost of bias relative to the true on‑policy objective. Clipping (the PPO‑style min/clip operator) adds further bias on top of this surrogate.

When is GSPO unbiased? Only in special cases: if every sequence has length 1 (so the exponent $1/|y|$ is 1), or if $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (trivially). Otherwise the length normalization changes the correction and produces bias.

Practical takeaway. The paper’s motivation is that the full token‑wise ratios are extremely noisy and unstable (and token‑level IS can fail badly for long sequences and MoE models). GSPO replaces the true importance weight by a tempered one that empirically stabilizes training and reduces variance; you should therefore understand GSPO as optimizing a biased but lower‑variance surrogate objective that trades exactness for robustness and empirical performance.

### Why is the token-level importance ratio in GRPO considered a misapplication of importance sampling?
Question:
The paper argues GRPO misapplies importance sampling by using a token-level ratio w_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) based on a single sample at each position t. I understand that importance sampling typically requires averaging over many samples to reduce variance, but the estimator can still be unbiased with a single sample per state-action, right? Can you clarify precisely which expectation GRPO is (or is not) trying to estimate, and why applying the per-token ratio inside the sum over t breaks the intended distribution correction for the sequence-level reward? A small worked example contrasting token-level and sequence-level correction would help.
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO’s token-level weight $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$ is not the correct importance weight for estimating the expectation of a sequence-level reward under the new policy. Importance sampling for sequence rewards requires the sequence-level weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}$ (or an unbiased estimator of it). Plugging per-token ratios based on a single sampled next token breaks the distribution-correction and yields a different estimator with large bias/variance; it does not, in general, equal the desired sequence-level re-weighting.

Why, precisely? The goal GRPO should be (implicitly) estimating is the sequence-level objective under the target policy $\pi_\theta$:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\,r(x,y)\right].
\]
Importance sampling requires reweighting whole-sequence samples by the whole-sequence density ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}=\prod_{t}\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}$. GRPO replaces that by applying per-token ratios inside a sum over $t$:
\[
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t} w_{t} \widehat A
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
w_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
where $\widehat A$ is a sequence-level advantage shared by all tokens. This is not an application of the identity above; it is a different estimator whose expected value under the behavior policy generally does not equal the desired sequence-weighted expectation.

Intuition: for each time step $t$ the conditional ratio corrects only the conditional next-token distribution at that step. But the reward depends on the entire completed sequence, and future tokens (and their probabilities under $\pi_\theta$) matter for the correct re-weighting. Using $w_t$ computed from the single sampled next token $y_t$ cannot account for the probability mass over the other possible tokens that could have appeared at that step; a single sample provides no Monte Carlo average of that conditional distribution. In contrast, the product over token ratios (or equivalently the sequence ratio) integrates over the joint path probability and is the exact correction.

Worked toy example (minimal, concrete):
Consider sequences of length 2 (tokens A/B at each step). Behavior policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is uniform: each token has probability $1/2$ at each step, so $\pi_{\text{old}}(y)=1/4$ for any two-token $y=(y_1,y_2)$. Target policy $\pi$ has increased probability on token A at step 1 and step 2 in this simple example: suppose
$\pi(y_1=\text{A})=3/4$, $\pi(y_1=\text{B})=1/4$, and conditional second-step probabilities are the same structure so that the sequence probabilities are
$\pi(\text{A},\text{A})=9/16$, $\pi(\text{A},\text{B})=3/16$, $\pi(\text{B},\text{A})=3/16$, $\pi(\text{B},\text{B})=1/16$.
Let the reward be $r(\text{A},\text{A})=1$, and $r=0$ for the other three sequences.

True sequence expectation under $\pi$ is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r]=\pi(\text{A},\text{A})\cdot 1 = 9/16.
\]
By importance sampling from $\pi_{\text{old}}$, the correct estimator multiplies each sampled sequence by its sequence ratio:
\[
\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y)}=\frac{\pi(y)}{1/4}=4\pi(y),
\]
so the importance-weighted expected reward is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}\!\left[4\pi(y)\,r(y)\right]
=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{old}}(y)\,4\pi(y)\,r(y)
=4\pi(\text{A},\text{A})\cdot \pi_{\text{old}}(\text{A},\text{A})\cdot 1=4\cdot\frac{9}{16}\cdot\frac{1}{4}=9/16,
\]
as required.

Now inspect what GRPO’s token-level ratio estimator effectively uses on average. GRPO would use the per-token ratio at each sampled token and average over $t$:
\[
\frac{1}{2}\bigl(w_1 + w_2\bigr)\, \widehat A
\quad\text{with}\quad
w_t=\frac{\pi(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t\mid y_{<t})}.
\]
Under $\pi_{\text{old}}$, the expected value of $w_1 r$ is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}[w_1 r]
= \sum_{y_{1}} \pi_{\text{old}}(y_1)\, \frac{\pi(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_1)} \,\mathbb{E}_{y_2\sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|y_1)}[r(y_1,y_2)].
\]
Because $r$ depends on $y_2$, this becomes
\[
\sum_{y_1}\pi(y_1)\cdot \mathbb{E}_{y_2\sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|y_1)}[r(y_1,y_2)],
\]
which is not the same as the correct term
\[
\sum_{y_1}\pi(y_1)\cdot \mathbb{E}_{y_2\sim \pi(\cdot|y_1)}[r(y_1,y_2)]
\]
unless the reward or future-token distributions satisfy special conditions (e.g., $r$ depends only on $y_1$, or $\pi(\cdot|y_1)=\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|y_1)$, or you average many conditional samples to estimate the conditional expectation). In our toy numbers, the mismatch is concrete: GRPO’s estimator effectively mixes correction by $\pi$ for the prefix but still averages the reward under the behavior conditional distribution for the suffix. That yields a different expectation and generally a biased/very noisy gradient.

Another way to see the problem: the single-sample conditional ratio $w_t$ is an unbiased estimator of the conditional density ratio only if you were estimating expectations that involve functions of $y_t$ alone (and if you average many samples for given prefix). But GRPO multiplies that single-sample conditional ratio by a reward that depends on the entire continuation; the law of the unconscious statistician shows you need to reweight by full joint ratio to move from one joint distribution to another.

Practical consequences: because GRPO applies noisy per-token ratios computed from single samples, the variance of the resulting gradients is large and the gradients are effectively biased for the true sequence objective (unless unrealistic conditions hold). The variance compounds over long sequences (product of many random per-token factors), and the clipping applied token-wise interacts badly with this noise, producing instability and collapse as observed in the paper. GSPO fixes this by using the sequence-level ratio (optionally length-normalized) so the importance weight correctly reflects the change in joint sequence probability and matches the unit of reward (sequence).

Takeaway: importance sampling corrects mismatch between joint distributions; if your objective and reward are defined at the sequence level you must correct at the sequence level. Applying per-token single-sample ratios inside the $t$-sum does not implement that correction and therefore is a misapplication of importance sampling in this setting.

### What trust-region does GSPO’s sequence-level clipping actually enforce?
Question:
PPO’s clipping is often interpreted as a proxy for a KL trust region at the action level. For GSPO, clipping is applied to the sequence-level ratio s_i(\theta) = exp\big((1/|y_i|)\sum_t \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\big). Does clipping s_i(\theta) in [1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon] imply a bound on the average per-token KL or the length-normalized sequence KL, e.g., \frac{1}{|y|}\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_\theta(\cdot|x))? If there is a formal connection, could you derive it? If not, how should we interpret the constraint that GSPO enforces geometrically on the policy update?
Answer:
Short answer: Clipping the GSPO sequence ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ to $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ enforces a pointwise bound on the length‑normalized log likelihood ratio for each sampled sequence $y$ (i.e. the sample’s empirical average token log‑ratio), but it does not directly give a formal bound on the expected per‑token KL between the two policies. In other words, GSPO’s clipping controls the observed (sampled) length‑normalized log odds of a sequence, not the true expectation over next‑token distributions that defines the per‑token KL. Below I derive the simple identities and explain the geometric interpretation and the gap to an expected KL trust region.

Derivation and what is actually bounded
Let $s = s_i(\theta)$ as defined in the paper. By definition
$\log s = \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid \cdot)}\right].$
So clipping $s \in [1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ is equivalent to imposing the pointwise bound
$\log(1-\epsilon) \le \log s \le \log(1+\epsilon).$
Equivalently, for the sampled sequence $y$,
$- \log(1+\epsilon) \le - \log s \le - \log(1-\epsilon).$
Note that $- \log s = \frac{1}{|y|}\log\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}\right],$
so clipping enforces a uniform bound on this length‑normalized sequence log‑ratio for each observed $y$.

Relation to “length‑normalized sequence KL” as a sample estimator
Define the length‑normalized sequence log‑ratio for a sample $y$ as $L(y) := -\frac{1}{|y|}\log s(y)$. Then clip bounds imply $|L(y)| \le \max(-\log(1-\epsilon), \log(1+\epsilon)) \approx \epsilon$ for small $\epsilon$. The quantity $L(y)$ is exactly the empirical estimator (on that one sampled sequence) of the average per‑token log ratio. If you take expectation over $y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the expected value
$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}[L(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\log\!\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}{\pi_\theta(y)}\right)\right]$
is the length‑normalized sequence KL between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)$ and $\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$:
$\frac{1}{|y|}\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\right) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}[L(y)].$
Thus clipping guarantees a pointwise bound on the sampled estimator $L(y)$. Only if this bound holds for every possible $y$ (or with probability 1 under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) does it immediately imply the same bound on the expectation (the true length‑normalized KL). In practice clipping guarantees the bound only for sequences that are not clipped away; sequences that are clipped are prevented from contributing to the gradient, so the expectation over used samples is biased away from sequences with too large a $|L(y)|$.

Why this is not the same as bounding the expected per‑token KL
The more familiar PPO trust‑region is often phrased as limiting $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}}\|\pi_{\text{new}})$ (an expectation over the behavior distribution). GSPO’s clip, however, is a per‑sample multiplicative band on the geometric mean of token likelihood ratios (the sequence likelihood to the $1/|y|$). This yields control over the sampled empirical average of token log‑ratios, but it does not directly bound the true per‑token KL (i.e., the expectation over next‑token distributions at each context). Concretely, for per‑token KL at a particular time step we mean
$\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t} \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid \text{context}_t)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid \text{context}_t)\big),$
which is an expectation over the entire next‑token distribution at each context. The observed sample average $\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \log\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid \cdot)}\right]$ is a single Monte‑Carlo realization (a noisy estimator) of that expected per‑token KL (up to sign). Clipping bounds that estimator but because of variance in Monte‑Carlo samples it does not guarantee a small expected KL unless additional assumptions hold (e.g., low variance of the estimator, the clip holds with high probability, or you can show concentration).

Geometric interpretation
Geometrically, GSPO’s clipping restricts each sampled sequence’s likelihood under the new policy to lie within a multiplicative band around the old policy’s likelihood, after length‑normalization. That is, for a sampled $y$ you require
$(1-\epsilon)^{|y|} \le \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)} \le (1+\epsilon)^{|y|},$
or equivalently the geometric mean of token‑wise likelihood ratios is constrained. This is different from enforcing that $\theta$ stays inside a small KL ball around $\theta_{\text{old}}$ (an average divergence constraint). Instead GSPO enforces a set of per‑sample multiplicative constraints (one band per observed sequence). The feasible set is the intersection of these bands for the batch of observed sequences; it is not an $L_2$ ball in parameter space nor a constraint that directly bounds the expected KL unless the sample coverage is complete and the estimator concentrates.

Practical consequences and intuition
Because GSPO clips on the length‑normalized sequence ratio, it controls the observed average token log‑ratio for sequences that enter the objective. This matches the fact that rewards are sequence‑level and avoids the pathological noise from token‑wise importance weights (the empirical motivation in the paper). But you should interpret GSPO’s clipping as a sample‑wise multiplicative trust band on sequence likelihoods (after normalizing by length), not as a provable global bound on the expected per‑token KL between policies. If you want a formal guarantee on the expected KL you need either to (i) ensure the clip bounds hold for the whole support (or with high probability and controlled variance), or (ii) explicitly regularize with a KL penalty/constraint computed as an expectation (as PPO sometimes does).

Summary
Clipping $s_i(\theta) \in [1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ imposes a pointwise bound on the length‑normalized sequence log‑ratio for each sampled sequence $y$ (so for that sample it bounds the sample’s estimator of the average per‑token log change). It does not automatically imply a bound on the true expected per‑token KL unless you additionally control sampling variance or guarantee the bound holds across the distribution. Geometrically, GSPO enforces per‑sequence multiplicative bands on sequence likelihoods (after geometric mean normalization), which is conceptually different from a global KL trust‑region (an expectation‑based ball) but is well aligned with sequence‑level rewards and the practical stability goals emphasized in the paper.

### How is KL regularization incorporated with GSPO in practice?
Question:
The PPO objective usually includes a KL penalty to a reference policy. The paper omits the KL term for brevity. For GSPO, is the KL penalty applied at the sequence level (e.g., a penalty on \sum_t \mathrm{KL}_t or its length-normalized average), or at the token level as an auxiliary loss? Does the presence of a KL penalty interact with the sequence-level clipping range choices, and how should one set its coefficient relative to the GSPO clipping \varepsilon?
Answer:
Short answer: treat KL regularization for GSPO as a sequence-level penalty (preferably length-normalized) and tune its strength by directly monitoring the same sequence-level divergence that GSPO uses for clipping. The KL term is complementary to GSPO’s sequence-level clipping; use it either as a small fixed coefficient or, better, an adaptive coefficient so the average (length-normalized) sequence KL stays at your desired budget. Avoid applying a separate token-level KL as the primary regularizer unless you have a clear per-token objective (then you can add token KL as an auxiliary term).

Why sequence-level KL is the right fit
GSPO explicitly defines importance ratios and clipping at the sequence scale:
$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},$
and its objective and clipping are applied to whole responses. The paper’s motivation is that reward is given per sequence and that per-token importance weights are noisy and theoretically mismatched. A KL penalty should match that same unit of optimization to be semantically and numerically consistent. Concretely, you can define the KL as a length-normalized sequence KL
$KL_{\text{seq}}(y; \theta_{\text{old}} \parallel \theta) = \frac{1}{|y|}\cdot\big(\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\big),$
or equivalently the expected length-normalized token KL averaged over tokens in the sequence. This matches the $s_i(\theta)$ definition (which uses length-normalized log-likelihood ratios) and makes the KL penalty directly measure the same divergence that clipping controls.

Practical forms to implement
1) Sequence-level length-normalized KL penalty added to the loss:
$L_{\text{total}} = -J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) + \lambda \cdot \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[ KL_{\text{seq}}(y; \theta_{\text{old}} \parallel \theta)\big].$
Numerically you compute $KL_{\text{seq}}$ for each sampled response by $(\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x))/|y|$ and average across the batch.

2) Equivalent token-expression (if you prefer per-token terms for implementation):
$KL_{\text{seq}}(y) = \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} KL\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}) \parallel \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\big).$
This is the same quantity averaged across tokens and is compatible with frameworks that expose tokenwise distributions.

When a token-level KL might be used
If you are doing multi-turn RL and want finer-grained control of per-token divergence or you have auxiliary per-token objectives, you can add a token-level KL as an auxiliary term. But do not rely on token-KL to replace sequence-level regularization: GSPO’s core stability comes from sequence-level treatment, so the main KL should remain sequence-aligned.

How KL interacts with GSPO clipping and how to tune $\lambda$ vs $\epsilon$
Clipping and KL are complementary controls: clipping rejects or caps extreme sequence importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$ per-sample, while the KL term penalizes the expected divergence and therefore discourages the policy from drifting globally. Because GSPO uses a length-normalized $s_i(\theta)$, its clipping $\epsilon$ values tend to be much smaller (the paper reports left/right clipping ranges on the order of $3\times 10^{-4}$ and $4\times 10^{-4}$) than token-level PPO clipping. Therefore you should think in the same length-normalized scale when setting the KL coefficient.

Practical tuning recipe:
- Monitor the same statistic that your KL term measures (the batch-average length-normalized sequence KL). Use that as your target, not an abstract $\lambda$ value. A typical workflow is to choose a target average sequence KL per update and adjust $\lambda$ so the measured KL stabilizes near that target (this is exactly the PPO adaptive-KL idea).
- Start with a small $\lambda$ so the KL term is mild, and increase if you observe excessive policy drift or instabilities. Because GSPO clipping already culls extreme sequences, the KL coefficient can often be smaller than in token-level PPO setups.
- If you want a quick heuristic mapping to the clipping epsilon: aim for an average length-normalized sequence KL roughly on the same order as the scale of log deviations implied by your $\epsilon$. A first pass is to pick a target KL that keeps typical length-normalized log-ratio deviations well within the clipping band used (monitor the fraction of clipped sequences). If your clipping $\epsilon$ is $O(10^{-4})$ as in the paper, expect the desired average KL per token also to be very small; use an adaptive $\lambda$ to reach that.

Summary recommendations
Use a length-normalized sequence KL penalty (not a raw per-token KL) so the regularizer measures exactly the divergence GSPO’s $s_i(\theta)$ controls. Treat clipping $\epsilon$ and KL coefficient $\lambda$ as complementary: clipping enforces per-sample hard bounds, while KL softly penalizes average drift. Tune $\lambda$ by monitoring the batch-average length-normalized sequence KL (or use an adaptive coefficient to hit a target KL), and pick a target KL consistent with your GSPO clipping scale (the paper’s GSPO clipping ranges are extremely small, so the corresponding KL targets will be small as well).

### Does equal token weighting in GSPO harm intra-sequence credit assignment?
Question:
In Equation (7), GSPO ends up weighting all tokens in a response equally via the factor s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i, whereas GRPO gives each token its own importance ratio. Conceptually, if the reward r(x,y) is due to a few crucial tokens (e.g., a decisive reasoning step), won’t equal weighting dilute the gradient signal for those key tokens? Is the intent that GSPO-token (Equation (8)) should be used when finer credit assignment is necessary? If so, how would you recommend defining \widehat{A}_{i,t} to reflect partial credit (e.g., stepwise verifier signals) while keeping stability?
Answer:
Short answer: No — equal token weighting in GSPO does not necessarily “break” credit assignment, but it does trade fine-grained credit for much lower variance and far greater stability. When you actually need per-token credit (e.g., a few decisive reasoning tokens that determine reward), the paper’s GSPO-token variant is exactly the intended mechanism to recover token-level credit while retaining GSPO’s stable sequence-level importance weighting. Below I explain why, and give practical recommendations for defining stable per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

Why equal weighting is chosen (intuition + technical)
GSPO defines the importance weight at the sequence level $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ and then, in the gradient, multiplies the average token log-gradients by $s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i$. This makes all tokens in a response receive the same multiplicative weight. The paper’s motivation is principled: the reward is given at the sequence level, and token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ computed from single-sample next-token draws are high-variance and can accumulate across long sequences, producing noisy and unstable updates (empirically leading to collapse in GRPO). By using sequence-level weighting, GSPO aligns the importance-sampling correction with the unit of reward and greatly reduces variance and instability (especially important for long responses and MoE models where per-token likelihoods/activated experts can fluctuate).

Why that can dilute per-token signals (and when that matters)
If a reward truly depends on only a small subset of tokens (a single decisive reasoning step), assigning the same multiplicative weight across all tokens will spread the scalar advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ across all token gradients. That can dilute the relative gradient magnitude pushed on the crucial tokens compared to a scheme that concentrated advantage on those tokens. In short: GSPO trades some intra-sequence specificity for lower variance and stability. For many RL-from-human or group-reward setups where reward is noisy and sequence-level, that trade is beneficial and is the whole point of GSPO; but for tasks where you can reliably obtain per-step signals (verifier steps, intermediate correctness checks), you may want a token-aware advantage.

Using GSPO-token when you need finer credit assignment
The paper’s GSPO-token variant is designed for this: it keeps the sequence-level importance scalar $s_i(\theta)$ (hence keeps the stable off-policy correction), but allows distinct per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Concretely the gradient becomes proportional to $s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\ldots)$, so the multiplicative stability comes from the sequence-level $s_i$ while token-by-token credit is controlled by $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This is the recommended path when you have reliable partial/stepwise signals.

How to define stable per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$
Below are concrete, practical recipes grounded in the paper’s design principles to construct $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ that reflect partial credit while keeping variance under control.

1) Linear interpolation between sequence and token signals
A simple and robust choice is a convex blend of the (stable) group-normalized sequence advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ and a normalized token-level signal $\delta_{i,t}$:
$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \alpha \widehat{A}_i + (1-\alpha)\,\delta_{i,t},\quad \alpha\in[0,1].$
Choose $\alpha$ close to $1$ (e.g., $0.8$–$0.95$) initially to preserve stability, and anneal toward smaller $\alpha$ if you need stronger token-level shaping.

2) How to construct the per-token signal $\delta_{i,t}$
If you have an intermediate verifier or stepwise reward $r_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}$:
- Compute token-level residuals against a baseline (baseline can be the mean across group responses at that token index, or a running average): $\tilde r_{i,t} = r_{i,t} - \mathrm{baseline}_t$.
- Standardize across the group (or across tokens) to get comparable scales: $\delta_{i,t} = \dfrac{\tilde r_{i,t} - \mathrm{mean}_g(\tilde r_{\cdot,t})}{\mathrm{std}_g(\tilde r_{\cdot,t}) + \epsilon}.$
This mirrors the paper’s group-normalization of sequence rewards and keeps token advantages numerically consistent and bounded.

3) Temporal smoothing / credit spreading for verifier signals
If verifier signals are sparse (e.g., only at a few steps) use temporal spreading to attribute partial credit to neighboring tokens. One option is causal decay:
$\delta_{i,t} = \sum_{u\ge t} \gamma^{u-t}\,\Delta r_{i,u}$
where $\Delta r_{i,u}$ are stepwise verification deltas and $\gamma\in(0,1]$ defines decay. This converts sparse verifier outputs into smoother per-token advantages, reducing variance.

4) Clip and cap advantages for stability
After building $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ (or $\delta_{i,t}$) clip them to a reasonable range (e.g., $[-C, C]$ with $C$ like $3$–$5$ in standardized units). This prevents individual tokens with noisy extreme verifier values from dominating updates. The paper already relies on clipping of importance ratios; pairing that with clipped advantages reduces catastrophic updates.

5) Normalize per-response or per-group
Keep the same philosophy as the paper: normalize token advantages within the group to zero mean and unit std (or near that) so that advantage scales are comparable across batches and stable for the optimizer. For instance:
$\widehat{A}_{i,t} \leftarrow \dfrac{ \widehat{A}_{i,t} - \mathrm{mean}_{i,t\in G} }{ \mathrm{std}_{i,t\in G} + \epsilon }.$
Normalization can be done per-token-position across responses, or globally across all tokens in the group—choose based on signal reliability (per-position when signals are aligned; global otherwise).

6) Keep the sequence-level importance multiplier
Always use $s_i(\theta)$ (or its detached numerical value as in the GSPO-token formula) as the importance multiplier, rather than token-level importance ratios. This preserves the low-variance off-policy correction that is the core benefit of GSPO and avoids reintroducing the instability problems that GRPO experienced.

7) Diagnostics and annealing
Start training with $\alpha$ near $1$ (mostly sequence-level advantage), monitor per-token gradient norms and the effect on the behavior you want (e.g., whether verifier steps improve). If per-token gradients are tiny and verifier performance stalls, gradually reduce $\alpha$. Also track clipping fractions and gradient variance: a sudden rise signals instability and suggests increasing $\alpha$ or stronger clipping.

Concrete example
Suppose you have a per-step verifier score $v_{i,t}\in[0,1]$. Compute per-token deltas $\Delta v_{i,t} = v_{i,t} - \bar v_t$ (mean across $G$ responses at that token). Standardize to get $\delta_{i,t}$. Then set
$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \alpha \widehat{A}_i + (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{clip}(\delta_{i,t}, -C, C),$
normalize $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ across the batch, and use GSPO-token with $s_i(\theta)$ detached as in the paper. Start with $\alpha=0.9$ and $C=3$; if you see too little token shaping after a few thousand steps, reduce $\alpha$ toward $0.7$.

Summary
GSPO intentionally equal-weights tokens for stability because the reward is sequence-level and token-level importance ratios are high-variance and destabilizing. When you have reliable per-token or stepwise signals, use GSPO-token: keep the sequence-level importance multiplier $s_i(\theta)$ for stability, and feed carefully normalized, clipped, and possibly temporally-smoothed per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ (or a convex blend with the sequence advantage) to regain intra-sequence credit assignment without losing GSPO’s low-variance behavior.

### Deriving GSPO’s gradient from an off-policy objective without heuristics
Question:
Could you show a derivation that starts from a clear target objective (e.g., maximizing \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]) and applies exact importance sampling to use off-policy samples y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|x), leading to the GSPO gradient in Equation (7)? In particular, where does the 1/|y| exponent on the ratio arise from in a principled way, rather than as a heuristic variance control?
Answer:
Short answer: the $1/|y|$ exponent is not a mysterious heuristic tacked onto an otherwise exact sequence-level importance sampling derivation; it appears exactly when you change the optimization viewpoint from the raw sequence distribution $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ to the per-token (geometric-mean) sequence distribution $q_\theta(y\mid x)\propto\pi_\theta(y\mid x)^{1/|y|}$. Importance sampling is then exact for expectations under $q_\theta$, and the gradient algebra produces the factor $1/|y|$ (and the average over token log-prob gradients) exactly as in the paper. Below I show the two clean derivations so you can see where the exponent comes from and what is exact versus what is a change of objective.

1) Standard (exact) sequence-level IS from the canonical objective
Start from the canonical objective used in policy gradient style RL, written at the sequence level (reward or advantage $A(y)$ for the whole sequence):
$$
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,A(x,y)\,\big].
$$
Using the log-likelihood trick,
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[ A(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big].
$$
If you only have off-policy samples $y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$, exact importance sampling gives
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\,A(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\Big].
$$
Now use the autoregressive factorization $\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$. This exact IS gradient contains the full sequence ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}$ (no $1/|y|$ exponent).

So if your aim is strictly to compute the gradient of the original sequence objective using off-policy data, the correct IS weight is the full sequence likelihood ratio (ratio$^1$), not ratio$^{1/|y|}$.

2) Where ratio^{1/|y|} arises exactly: IS for a per-token / geometric-mean objective
The paper chooses to work with a sequence-level surrogate that treats sequences through their per-token (geometric-mean) likelihood. Define a new sequence distribution $q_\theta$ by taking the geometric mean of token probabilities (and renormalizing if you wish); equivalently treat $q_\theta$ as proportional to $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)^{1/|y|}$. Concretely, consider optimizing an objective of the form
$$
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim q_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,A(x,y)\,\big],
$$
where $q_\theta(y\mid x)\propto\pi_\theta(y\mid x)^{1/|y|}$. This is not the original $\mathcal{J}$, it is a different but related surrogate that emphasizes per-token average likelihood rather than raw product-likelihood (motivated by length normalization and variance control). For this objective the exact importance weight to reweight samples drawn from $q_{\theta_\text{old}}$ is
$$
\frac{q_\theta(y\mid x)}{q_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y|}}.
$$
Hence exact IS for $\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}$ produces the exponent $1/|y|$ exactly (no heuristic). Take the log-likelihood trick for $\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}$:
$$
\nabla_\theta \widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim q_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[ \frac{q_\theta(y\mid x)}{q_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\,A(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(y\mid x)\Big].
$$
Because $\log q_\theta(y\mid x)=\tfrac{1}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$, we have
$$
\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(y\mid x)
= \frac{1}{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)
= \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$
Substituting the importance weight gives exactly the GSPO gradient form (clipping aside):
$$
\nabla_\theta \widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\!\Big[ \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{|y|}} A(x,y)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Big],
$$
which matches Equation (7)/(the unclipped gradient) in the paper.

Interpretation and trade-offs
Thus the $1/|y|$ exponent is exact if you are performing IS for expectations under the per-token (geometric-mean) sequence distribution $q_\theta$ rather than under the original $\pi_\theta$. This is a principled change of the sampling/optimization measure: $q_\theta$ compresses the product-likelihood into a per-token average likelihood, which (i) reduces the dynamic range of importance weights across varying $|y|$, (ii) yields a uniform clipping scale across lengths, and (iii) reduces variance coming from multiplying many per-token ratios. It is not the exact IS estimator for the original objective $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[A]$, but it is an exact IS estimator for the surrogate objective $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim q_\theta}[A]$. Practically, the paper argues (and empirically shows) that this surrogate objective aligns well with sequence-level rewards and yields much more stable gradients for long responses and MoE models.

Bottom line: the exponent $1/|y|$ is principled when you adopt the geometric-mean (per-token normalized) sequence distribution $q_\theta(y\mid x)\propto\pi_\theta(y\mid x)^{1/|y|}$. Importance sampling is exact for $q_\theta$, and the algebra then gives the $1/|y|$ factor and the per-token average of gradient contributions exactly as in GSPO.

### Practical handling of zero or tiny group standard deviation in \widehat{A}_i
Question:
The group-based advantage is defined as a z-score: \widehat{A}_i = (r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean})/\mathrm{std}. What happens when all G responses get identical rewards (\mathrm{std} = 0) or when \mathrm{std} is extremely small, which can happen with sparse or binary verifiers? Do you add an \epsilon to the denominator, cap \widehat{A}_i, or fall back to mean-centering without scaling? Any stability tips here would be appreciated.
Answer:
Short answer: treat the zero/std-too-small case as a numerical/algorithmic corner that you must defend against. If all $G$ responses get identical rewards then the meaningful advantage is zero (no response is better than another), so set the group advantages to zero (or equivalently skip using the group for learning). For extremely small std, prefer one of the following stable fallbacks: (1) add a small epsilon to the denominator, (2) fall back to mean-centering without dividing, or (3) use a robust/rank-based normalization and clamp the resulting advantages. Also combine that with explicit clipping of advantage magnitude and larger group sizes where possible. Below I explain why and give concise practical recipes.

Why this matters (intuitively and with the paper’s setup)
GSPO defines a group advantage
$$\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r\})}.$$
If $\mathrm{std}=0$ every response is equally rewarded so there is no informative preference signal — the correct learning signal is “no preference” (i.e., zero advantage). Dividing by zero is undefined and tiny $\mathrm{std}$ amplifies noise and produces huge, unstable gradients (which is exactly the instability you want to avoid when training large models as the paper stresses). So you must guard that denominator.

Practical, tested-safe options
1) Exact-equality case ($\mathrm{std}=0$): set $\widehat{A}_i := 0$ for all $i$ (or skip that group in the optimizer). This is the principled choice: no reward difference ⇒ no update direction from that group.

2) Add epsilon to the denominator (numerical stabilizer): replace denom by $\max(\mathrm{std},\epsilon)$ or use $\mathrm{std}+\epsilon$. Typical $\epsilon$ values are in the range $10^{-6}\ldots10^{-3}$ depending on reward scale. This is simple and commonly used, but be aware it effectively scales up advantages when std is tiny, so pair with advantage clipping.

3) Mean-centering only (no scaling) when $\mathrm{std}$ is below a threshold: if $\mathrm{std} < \tau$, use
$\displaystyle \widehat{A}_i = r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}$,
i.e., subtract baseline but do not divide. This avoids wild amplification while preserving ordering information.

4) Use robust or rank-based normalizations: replace z-scoring with centered ranks or normalize by median absolute deviation (MAD). For binary/sparse rewards rank-centering avoids exploding values:
$\widehat{A}_i := \mathrm{rank}(r_i) - \overline{\mathrm{rank}},$
or
$\widehat{A}_i := \frac{r_i - \mathrm{median}}{\mathrm{MAD}+\epsilon}.$
These are much less sensitive to quantized reward distributions.

5) Clip the advantage values: after computing $\widehat{A}_i$, clamp to a maximum magnitude, e.g. $\widehat{A}_i \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(\widehat{A}_i, -A_{\max}, A_{\max})$. This prevents any remaining large advantages from producing huge gradient steps and pairs well with small $\epsilon$.

6) Increase $G$ (group size) when feasible: larger groups reduce sampling noise in mean/std estimates and make the z-score more reliable (the paper relies on group-based normalization; increasing $G$ increases the chances of nonzero variance in sparse settings).

7) Monitoring and fallbacks: log group stds and fraction of groups with $\mathrm{std} < \tau$. If many groups are degenerate, consider changing the reward function (shaping, adding fine-grained signals), using per-query baselines aggregated over longer windows, or switching to robust normalization (rank or MAD).

Recommended minimal recipe (practical and safe)
Compute mean and std for the group. If $\mathrm{std} = 0$, set $\widehat{A}_i = 0$. Else if $\mathrm{std} < \tau$ (e.g. $\tau = 10^{-4}$ for rewards in $[0,1]$) then either (a) use mean-centering only, or (b) divide by $\max(\mathrm{std},\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ and then clip advantages to $[-10,10]$ (or a tighter bound like $[-3,3]$). For binary/sparse rewards, prefer rank-centering or MAD normalization from the start.

Why these choices are consistent with GSPO
GSPO’s stability goal is to avoid injecting large, noisy gradient signals that can destabilize large models (the paper’s central motivation). Using the above guards preserves the intended relative signal of group-based advantages without producing extreme numerical values that could create the kind of instability GSPO was introduced to avoid. In particular, setting advantages to zero when rewards are identical is faithful: there is no preferential reward to exploit, so sequence-level clipping/optimization should not push the policy.

A couple of final tips
- Tune $\epsilon,\tau$ and clipping bounds to your reward scale; log statistics to ensure you’re not silently suppressing legitimate signal. - Prefer rank/MAD normalizations for highly discrete or sparse verifier outputs. - If many groups are degenerate, consider augmenting the reward (more granular verifier) or increasing group size $G$.

This approach keeps the GSPO objective numerically stable while preserving its group-level semantics and avoiding catastrophic gradient noise.

### How should s_i(\theta) be computed across multiple mini-batch updates from one rollout batch?
Question:
The paper mentions partitioning a large rollout batch into several mini-batches, which makes subsequent updates progressively more off-policy. During these mini-batch updates, do you compute s_i(\theta) against the same fixed \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} that generated the rollouts, or do you refresh \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} between mini-batches? From an importance sampling standpoint, only the behavior policy that actually generated the samples should appear in the denominator, so I am unsure whether re-evaluating with a changing \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} would introduce bias or instability.
Answer:
Short answer: keep the denominator fixed to the behavior policy that actually generated the rollouts (i.e., the snapshot $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ used to sample those responses). Do not refresh $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ between mini-batches produced from the same rollout batch.

Why: GSPO (and importance sampling in general) relies on re-weighting samples drawn from a known behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ to estimate expectations under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$. In your setting the rollouts were sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, so the correct importance weight for a response $y$ is $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ where $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the policy that generated $y$. If you were to change the denominator mid-way (replace $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ with a newer policy snapshot), the reweighting no longer corresponds to the actual sampling distribution and you introduce bias: you would be pretending samples were generated by a different behavior policy than they actually were. That invalidates the importance-sampling identity and corrupts your gradient estimator.

Intuition and what actually happens in mini-batch updates: partitioning a large rollout into multiple mini-batches and doing several gradient steps from the same data makes the optimization progressively more off-policy because $\theta$ moves away from $\theta_{\text{old}}$. That is expected and handled by clipping in GSPO: we compute $s_i(\theta)$ with the fixed denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and let clipping limit the contribution of highly off-policy samples. As $\theta$ drifts across mini-batch updates, $s_i(\theta)$ will change (since the numerator $\pi_{\theta}$ changes) and the samples become more off-policy, but this is the correct record of off-policyness — not something you should erase by reassigning $\theta_{\text{old}}$.

Practical notes grounded in the paper:
- For correctness you must use the $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ that actually generated the rollouts. Many teams store sequence log-likelihoods (or the full sequence probability) at collection time to avoid recomputation and precision mismatch; GSPO says sequence-level likelihoods are robust to engine precision differences and can be used directly.
- For MoE models, the paper discusses routing replay when GRPO used token-level ratios; GSPO avoids this complexity because it uses sequence-level likelihoods and is less sensitive to per-token routing volatility. But even here, if you recompute $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)$ you must use the exact $\theta_{\text{old}}$ snapshot (or cached likelihoods computed under that snapshot), not a newer checkpoint.
- Refreshing $\theta_{\text{old}}$ between mini-batches would both bias the importance weights and mix different behavior distributions across the same rollout batch (complicating normalization and clipping). If you want to use newly generated samples from a newer policy, collect a new rollout batch and treat its denominator as that new policy.

Conclusion: compute $s_i(\theta)$ against the single, fixed $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ that produced the rollouts (or use stored/recomputed likelihoods for that exact snapshot). Do not refresh the denominator between mini-batches from the same rollout batch — doing so creates bias and invalidates the importance-sampling correction.

### Choosing and interpreting GSPO clipping ranges of 3e-4 and 4e-4
Question:
In the experiments, the left and right clipping ranges for GSPO are set to 3e-4 and 4e-4, which are very small compared to GRPO’s 0.2 and 0.27. How should practitioners select these ranges for a new model/task? Are these numbers tied to typical distributions of s_i(\theta) across batches after the 1/|y| normalization? Is there a rule of thumb relating the target average per-token KL to a corresponding range for s_i(\theta)?
Answer:
Short answer: those tiny numbers ($3\times10^{-4}$, $4\times10^{-4}$) are small because GSPO’s importance ratio is length‑normalized, so most sequence‑level ratios cluster very close to 1. Practically you should pick the GSPO clipping bounds by first inspecting the empirical distribution of the length‑normalized sequence ratio (or, equivalently, the per‑token log‑ratio) on a representative rollout batch and then set bounds that allow the desired small per‑token shift while excluding extreme outliers. A convenient rule of thumb is to map your target per‑token KL/log change to the clipping window via the identity that GSPO’s ratio
$$s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\Delta\log\pi_{t}\right),$$
so for small changes $s\approx 1+\delta$ where $\delta\approx$ the average per‑token log change; therefore set the clipping epsilon roughly equal to your target per‑token log shift (or per‑token KL for small values).

Why the GSPO ranges are so small
In GSPO the importance ratio is length‑normalized:
$$s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\Big).$$
Length normalization strongly concentrates $s_i$ around $1$ because you are averaging many token log‑ratios. Thus the natural scale of meaningful changes in $s_i$ is tiny (per‑token), which is why GSPO uses clipping bounds like $1-3\times10^{-4}$ and $1+4\times10^{-4}$ instead of the much larger bounds used by token‑level ratios in GRPO (e.g. $0.8$–$1.27$). The paper explicitly notes this difference of orders of magnitude and that the clipping ranges “typically differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios.”

How to pick ranges for a new model / task (practical recipe)
1) Collect a representative rollout minibatch using your current policy (or a short calibration run). For each sampled $(x,y)$ compute the length‑normalized log ratio
$$r_i := \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$$
and $s_i = \exp(r_i)$.

2) Examine the empirical distribution of $r_i$ (or of $s_i$). Look at mean, standard deviation, and quantiles (e.g. 1%, 99%). Because $r_i$ is additive per token, it is usually tightly concentrated; choose bounds so that you clip only the tails you believe are unreliable (typical choices: allow a few percent clipped, but note the paper finds GSPO works well even when many tokens are clipped).

3) Translate a target per‑token KL/log change to $s$: for small values $r_i\approx\Delta\log\pi_{\text{per token}}$ and $s_i\approx 1 + r_i$. So if you have in mind a maximum desirable per‑token log shift or per‑token KL of $k$, set the clipping epsilon roughly to $k$ (i.e., $1-k\ldots 1+k$, possibly asymmetric). If you want to be conservative allow a slightly larger window than empirical std (e.g. $2\times$ std).

4) Prefer asymmetric bounds if your validation shows asymmetric tails (the paper used $3\times10^{-4}$ left and $4\times10^{-4}$ right). Asymmetry can compensate for systematic drift in one direction.

5) Validate by checking (a) fraction clipped, (b) training stability, and (c) effective reward trends. The paper emphasizes that GSPO remains stable even when it clips a large fraction of tokens compared to GRPO; therefore being conservative (smaller eps) is acceptable.

Mapping to a per‑token KL intuition
Because $r_i$ is the average per‑token log‑ratio, its expectation is essentially the per‑token expected log change between policies (closely related to per‑token KL for small changes). For small $r$,
per‑token KL $\approx -\mathbb{E}[r] + O(r^2)$,
and numerically $r\approx$ per‑token KL when changes are tiny. So a quick rule of thumb: if you target an average per‑token KL of $k$ (a small number), try clipping with $\mathrm{eps}\approx k$. For example, if you believe safe per‑token shifts are on the order of $1\times10^{-4}$–$5\times10^{-4}$ (typical for long sequences and small policy updates), choose clipping around those magnitudes as the paper did.

Final practical note
The paper’s chosen values ($3\times10^{-4}$ / $4\times10^{-4}$) were found empirically for their model and setup; they reflect (a) length normalization that concentrates $s$ around $1$ and (b) a conservative policy step size appropriate for large models and long responses. Always calibrate on a small run for your model and tasks: compute $r_i$ statistics, pick bounds that trim extreme tails but keep most of the useful data, and then monitor clipped fraction and training dynamics.

### Why is sequence likelihood more stable than token likelihood under MoE routing volatility?
Question:
Section 5.3 argues that although MoE expert activation can change substantially token-to-token after updates, the sequence likelihood \pi_\theta(y|x) does not fluctuate drastically, making s_i(\theta) stable. Can you provide intuition or empirical evidence for this claim? For instance, is there a bound showing that the cumulative effect of routing changes largely cancels out in the log-likelihood sum, or that shared experts dominate the likelihood for most tokens?
Answer:
Short answer: because the sequence likelihood aggregates many tokenwise log-probability contributions, token-level routing volatility tends to average out (and is further suppressed by length-normalization), so small, local changes in which experts fire usually produce only small changes in the per-sequence log-likelihood. That makes the sequence importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ much more stable than individual token ratios $w_{t}(\theta)$. The paper gives both intuitive reasoning and empirical signals that support this.

Why this is true (intuitively and technically). The sequence importance ratio in GSPO is
$$
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\!\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\right)\right).
$$
Two simple observations make stability clear.

1) Averaging reduces variance. If each token $t$ has a bounded log-ratio change $\Delta_t := \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid\cdot) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid\cdot)$, then the per-sequence exponent is the average $(1/|y|)\sum_t \Delta_t$. Even if the $\Delta_t$ have nonzero variance, averaging across $|y|$ terms reduces the scale of fluctuations by roughly $1/|y|$ (or $1/\sqrt{|y|}$ under independent-like assumptions). Concretely, if every $|\Delta_t| \le B$, then the average satisfies $\left|\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \Delta_t\right| \le B$, but more usefully if only $k$ tokens change substantially ($|\Delta_t| \le B'$ for those) and the rest are near zero, then the average magnitude is at most $(k/|y|)B'$. So local changes affect $s_i(\theta)$ only in proportion to their fraction of the sequence.

2) Routing volatility is sparse and structured. The paper reports that after an RL update roughly 10% of experts activated for a given sample differ between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ (for the 48-layer MoE they studied). That means most tokens see the same activated expert and therefore similar local predictions; only a small fraction of token conditionals change substantially. Plugging this into the previous point gives
$$
\left|\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \Delta_t\right| \le \frac{k}{|y|}B' \quad\text{with } k \approx 0.1|y|,
$$
so average log-change is $O(0.1\cdot B')$. Thus the per-token fluctuations do not accumulate linearly with sequence length — they scale with the fraction of changed tokens, not the absolute length.

Combined, these two facts imply that even when token-level importance weights $w_{t}(\theta)$ swing a lot for some tokens (making token-level IS noisy), the length-normalized sequence log-ratio is smoothed and remains much more stable.

A short, useful bound you can use: if at most $k$ tokens have $|\Delta_t| \le B'$ and all other tokens have $|\Delta_t| \le \varepsilon$ (small), then
$$
\left| \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_t \Delta_t \right| \le \frac{k}{|y|} B' + \left(1 - \frac{k}{|y|}\right) \varepsilon.
$$
If $k/|y|$ is small, the per-sequence change is small even when $B'$ is large.

Empirical evidence from the paper. The authors report two empirical findings that align with the above reasoning. First, they observe about a 10% change in expert activation for the same sample after an update in a deep MoE model; that is not majority disruption, so most token conditionals are preserved. Second, GSPO (which uses the length-normalized sequence ratio) trains stably without any Routing Replay mechanism, whereas GRPO (which relies on token-level ratios) requires Routing Replay to converge for MoE models. The paper also shows a two-order-of-magnitude difference in clipped-token fractions between GSPO and GRPO: GRPO clips far fewer tokens but nonetheless performs worse and is unstable, indicating its token-level ratios are noisy and harmful. GSPO clips many more tokens (at sequence level) yet achieves superior efficiency and stability — consistent with the sequence ratio being a more meaningful, lower-variance correction.

Broader connection. This is an instance of variance reduction by coarsening the importance weights to match the reward granularity. When the reward and the optimization target are sequence-level, applying per-token importance correction attempts to correct many high-variance granular pieces from a single sample per token; that violates the requirement of effective importance sampling (you need many samples to estimate a distributional correction). Averaging to the sequence level is analogous to using a lower-variance estimator (law of large numbers / averaging), better aligned with a sequence reward, and thus more robust under model structure changes (like MoE routing) that only perturb a subset of components.

Bottom line: length-normalized sequence log-likelihood averages out per-token routing noise; because routing volatility is sparse (only a fraction of tokens change experts) and the model’s language capability is preserved, the cumulative effect on the sequence log-likelihood is small. This is why $s_i(\theta)$ is empirically stable and why GSPO avoids the MoE-specific instability that plagues token-level GRPO.

### Numerical robustness of using inference-engine likelihoods at the sequence level
Question:
You note that GSPO can use likelihoods returned by an inference engine (e.g., vLLM) instead of recomputing them in the training engine, due to higher tolerance at the sequence level. Since s_i(\theta) uses exp of an average of log ratios, it still aggregates per-token log-probs. How large can floating-point precision or kernel differences be before this becomes problematic? Have you observed measurable training drift when mixing engines, and do you recommend any calibration or consistency checks before adopting this optimization?
Answer:
Short answer: using inference-engine likelihoods for GSPO is usually safe because GSPO aggregates token log-probs into a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\Big)$, and averaging log-ratios makes the sequence statistic much more tolerant to small per-token numerical differences than token-level weighting. However, you should not assume “always safe” — you must check for systematic bias or outliers and guard against errors that are large relative to your clipping scale. Below I explain why, give practical diagnostics and simple mitigations you can run before adopting inference-engine likelihoods in production.

Why sequence-level is more robust (intuitive + technical)
Because GSPO works with the average of per-token log-ratios, small independent errors at the token level tend to average out. If per-token log-prob differences between engines are zero-mean and roughly independent, the standard deviation of their mean shrinks like $1/\sqrt{|y|}$. Concretely, if the per-token log-difference has std $\sigma_{\text{token}}$, the std of the mean over a length-$|y|$ response is $\sigma_{\text{token}}/\sqrt{|y|}$. In contrast, GRPO multiplies (or weights) each token’s gradient by token-level ratios so those per-token errors act directly and can accumulate or bias gradients in a way that does not cancel.

Two more points about robustness that come from the paper’s formulation: (1) GSPO uses length-normalization (the $1/|y|$ exponent), which explicitly controls the numerical range of $s_i$; (2) GSPO clips $s_i$ at sequence level, so any remaining deviation only matters if it pushes $s_i$ across the clipping thresholds.

How large can the differences be before it’s a problem?
There is no absolute universal threshold in the paper (it depends on model scale, average response length, and your clipping $\varepsilon$). Practically, what matters is the size of the per-sequence log-difference relative to the margin represented by your clipping bounds. If your clipping operates around $1\pm\varepsilon$, a per-sequence multiplicative error $\delta$ that changes $s_i$ by more than $\varepsilon$ can change whether a sequence is clipped or not.

A useful heuristic: work in log-space. Let
$d_i = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\big(\log \pi^{(train)}_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}) - \log \pi^{(inf)}_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t})\big)$
be the per-sequence mean log-likelihood difference between the training-engine recomputation and the inference-engine return (inf -> inference engine). Then the multiplicative effect on $s_i$ from using the inference engine is $\exp(d_i)$. If typical $|d_i| \ll \varepsilon$ (or equivalently $\exp(|d_i|)\approx 1\pm\varepsilon$), you are safe. Because $d_i$ is an average over tokens, it will be much smaller than typical per-token differences; for example, if per-token differences are $O(10^{-4})$ and $|y|=100$, the mean is $O(10^{-6})$ — negligible for usual clipping scales.

Recommended practical checks before adopting inference-engine likelihoods
1) Small-scale empirical comparison. Sample a representative set of rollouts (varied lengths and queries). For each response $y$ compute per-token log-probs with the inference engine and (once) with the training engine, then form the per-sequence mean log-difference $d_i$. Report $\mathrm{mean}(d_i)$, $\mathrm{std}(d_i)$, and extreme percentiles (e.g., 99.9th). If $\mathrm{mean}(d_i)$ is close to zero and $\mathrm{std}(d_i)$ is tiny compared to your clipping tolerance, you’re likely fine.

2) Check clipping sensitivity. Compute $s_i$ from both engines and count the fraction of sequences whose $s_i$ would be on different sides of the clipping boundary. If that fraction is tiny, the practical effect on optimization will be small.

3) Monitor training signals after switching. Key signals to monitor are the fraction clipped, the average (or moving average) of the policy objective, the KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and the downstream reward curves. If you see a sudden shift (drift) correlated with the switch, fall back and recompute likelihoods on a validation set.

4) Watch for outliers. Even if average differences are tiny, rare sequences (very long responses, low-prob tails, or MoE routing changes) can produce large $d_i$. Flag sequences where $|d_i|$ exceeds some threshold (e.g., a small multiple of $\varepsilon$) and treat them specially (recompute with the training engine or drop them).

Simple mitigations and calibrations
If your diagnostics show small systematic bias or scale mismatch, you can apply simple calibration instead of refraining from inference likelihoods entirely. Fit an affine correction on log-likelihoods over your sample set: $\log \pi^{(train)} \approx a + b\cdot \log \pi^{(inf)}$ and apply it to inference returns. Often a small additive bias $a$ is enough. Alternatively, do temperature or linear scaling on logits returned by the inference engine to align the mean sequence log-likelihood.

Numerical-stability practices
Always work in log-space for accumulation (sum of log-probs) and use numerically stable summation (e.g., Kahan summation or double precision accumulation if possible). If the inference engine supports returning high-precision (fp32 vs fp16) log-probs, prefer the higher precision for sequences used in training. For MoE models, the paper highlights routing volatility as another source of per-token mismatch; sequence-level GSPO is less sensitive but you should still check routing-related outliers.

Empirical observation reported in the paper
The paper explicitly notes that in practice they typically recompute with the training engine because of precision discrepancies, but GSPO’s sequence-level nature is “much more tolerant” and “makes it possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine.” It also recommends using inference likelihoods especially in disaggregated training-inference frameworks and partial rollout settings. The paper does not give numeric thresholds; it implies that the tolerance is large enough to enable this optimization in their setups after applying the practical safeguards described above.

Summary checklist to adopt inference-engine likelihoods safely
1) Do a small audit: compute $d_i$ statistics (mean, std, tails).  
2) Check how many sequences flip clipping decisions when using inference vs training engine.  
3) If flips are non-negligible, calibrate log-likelihoods or recompute problematic examples.  
4) Use log-space accumulation and higher precision where available.  
5) Monitor clipped-fraction, KL, and reward curves closely after the switch and be prepared to revert.

If you follow these checks and keep $|d_i|$ small relative to your clipping $\varepsilon$, you should not see measurable training drift; if you do, the diagnostics above will quickly localize whether the cause is a systematic bias, rare outliers, or a precision/route-change pathology that requires recomputation for a subset of sequences.

### Equivalence of GSPO to on-policy REINFORCE with a group baseline when \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} = \pi_\theta
Question:
If we consider a purely on-policy setting where \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} = \pi_\theta and turn off clipping, then s_i(\theta) = 1 and Equation (7) becomes \mathbb{E}[\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]. Is this exactly REINFORCE with a per-group z-scored baseline? If there are any subtle differences (e.g., length normalization), could you point them out and explain their impact?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — in the purely on‑policy limit ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} = \pi_\theta$) with clipping turned off the GSPO gradient reduces to a standard REINFORCE-style policy gradient with a group (per‑query) z‑scored baseline, except for a deliberate length normalization factor. That length normalization (the $1/|y_i|$ factor inside the sum over token log‑probs) and the z‑scoring of advantages are the only substantive differences from the most common textbook form of REINFORCE; both affect gradient scale and variance but do not change the qualitative estimator (no extra bias is introduced by the z‑scoring beyond a linear rescale).

Why this is true (intuitive + math grounding in the paper). GSPO’s gradient (clipping omitted) is given in Equation (7) of the paper as

$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$

If $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} = \pi_\theta$ then $s_i(\theta) = \big(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|} = 1$, so the gradient becomes

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$

Classic REINFORCE (episode/sequence level) uses the identity $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and multiplies $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ by (return − baseline). If we identify the group advantage $\widehat A_i$ (the paper’s per‑query z‑scored reward) with the return minus baseline, then GSPO’s estimator is exactly that REINFORCE estimator up to the $\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}$ factor applied to the token sum.

What the z‑scoring does. The paper sets

$$
\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \text{mean}_i\, r(x,y_i)}{\text{std}_i\, r(x,y_i)}
$$

where mean and std are over the $G$ responses for the same query. This is a baseline plus normalization. Subtracting the group mean is a baseline in the usual sense and does not introduce bias; dividing by the group standard deviation rescales the advantage, which reduces variance and makes the scale of updates more stable across batches/queries but changes the effective learning rate (it’s a linear rescaling of the gradient).

What the length normalization does and its impact. GSPO explicitly divides the token gradient sum by $|y_i|$ (both in the definition of $s_i$ and in the gradient form). This has two effects:

1) Per‑token gradient magnitude is normalized so that sequences of different lengths contribute gradients on a similar scale. Without this, long sequences would typically produce proportionally larger gradients (because $\sum_t \nabla \log \pi$ grows with $|y|$), potentially dominating updates and increasing variance.

2) It changes the effective learning signal per token: long sequences get their total advantage spread across more tokens, so each token’s contribution is smaller. That does not bias the estimator (it’s still an unbiased policy gradient under on‑policy sampling), but it changes optimization dynamics and step sizes. In practice this reduces variance and stabilizes training for variable‑length responses, at the cost of slower per‑token learning signal for long outputs.

Other minor considerations grounded in the paper. The GSPO-token variant the paper describes becomes numerically identical to GSPO when all token advantages are equal; in the on‑policy no‑clip case that reinforces the equivalence claim. Also note that z‑scoring across a group is different from using a learned state‑dependent baseline (value function): the learned value can produce lower variance if accurate, but the group z‑score is simple, unbiased (after mean subtraction) and robust in large‑batch generation settings — this is precisely the practical motivation the paper gives for group advantages.

Bottom line: in the on‑policy, no‑clipping limit GSPO implements a REINFORCE/score‑function estimator with a per‑query group mean baseline and a group‑std rescaling, plus a deliberate $1/|y|$ length normalization. Those are practical tweaks that change variance and gradient scaling but do not change the estimator’s identity as an on‑policy policy gradient.

### Does sequence-level clipping risk discarding informative early-token gradients?
Question:
With GSPO, if s_i(\theta) is clipped (or if the min operator picks the clipped branch), the entire sequence’s contribution is modulated uniformly. Could this overly suppress learning signals for early tokens that are reliably good, just because later tokens made the sample too off-policy? Have you tried hybrid schemes that clip at the sequence level but allow limited per-token adjustments for tokens whose local ratios are close to 1?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes, in principle sequence-level clipping can suppress useful gradient signals coming from early tokens when a later token makes the whole response “off‑policy.” The paper recognizes this tension and explicitly provides a simple, well‑motivated hybrid that preserves GSPO’s sequence‑level importance correction while allowing token‑level advantage customization (the GSPO‑token variant). Which regime to use depends on whether your reward/advantage is naturally sequence‑level or you have meaningful per‑token/multi‑turn advantages.

Why the concern is real (intuitive + math)
GSPO defines the importance ratio per response
$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$
and applies clipping at the sequence level, so the policy gradient contribution from every token in $y_i$ is multiplied by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$ (after clipping). From the gradient derivation in the paper (clipping omitted),

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta) \propto s_i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big).$

Because $s_i(\theta)$ is shared across tokens, a very low (clipped) $s_i$ for a sequence will attenuate all token gradients equally — so an early token that was locally “good” but is in a sequence with a problematic later token will receive suppressed updates. This is exactly the phenomenon you described.

Why GSPO still argues this is preferable
The paper’s core argument is that token‑level importance ratios (as in GRPO) are ill‑posed for sequence rewards: per‑token ratios are high‑variance/noisy and can accumulate or flip in sign across long responses, destabilizing training (especially in MoE models). Averaging the importance at sequence level reduces that variance and matches the unit of reward (the entire sequence), improving stability and preventing catastrophic collapse.

The hybrid the paper already offers (GSPO‑token)
The paper presents GSPO‑token, a variant designed for finer per‑token advantage handling while retaining the sequence‑level importance factor. Its objective is (Equation (11) in the paper):

$J_{\mathrm{GSPO\text{-}token}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \min\!\big( s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\hat{A}_{i,t},\; \operatorname{clip}\big(s_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_{i,t} \big) \right],$

with
$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \operatorname{sg}\big[s_i(\theta)\big]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)}{\operatorname{sg}\big[\pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)\big]}.$

Because of the detach (sg) used there, numerically $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ equals $s_i(\theta)$. The resulting gradient (Equation (13)) is

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{GSPO\text{-}token}}(\theta) \propto s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \hat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big).$

This gives you the hybrid behavior you asked about: the sequence‑level importance $s_i(\theta)$ gates the whole response (preserving stability and correct importance sampling behavior), while $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ allows token‑wise reward/advantage shaping. In effect GSPO‑token is exactly the “sequence-level clip but allow per-token adjustments” scheme you suggested.

Practical suggestions and tradeoffs
If your reward is inherently sequence-level (a single scalar per response), GSPO’s pure sequence clipping is principled and empirically more stable (the paper’s experiments). If you have meaningful per-token or turn‑level advantages (e.g., multi‑turn RL, shaped intermediate rewards or human feedback that can be localized), GSPO‑token is the right hybrid: it keeps the low variance of sequence-level importance but lets you amplify/attenuate individual token gradients via $\hat{A}_{i,t}$.

If you worry specifically about “early tokens being reliable but suppressed,” two practical approaches follow directly from the paper:

1) Use GSPO‑token and set $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ so early tokens get larger positive advantages (if you can estimate that). This preserves sequence importance clipping but ensures early token gradients are relatively weighted up.

2) If you only have sequence rewards but want a soft remedy, consider tokenwise advantages computed from auxiliary signals (e.g., token‑level classifiers, attention or alignment scores) and plug them into $\hat{A}_{i,t}$. Because $s_i(\theta)$ remains shared, you retain GSPO’s stability. The paper already notes GSPO‑token is numerically identical to GSPO when $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\hat{A}_i$, so this is a clean extension.

Finally, you asked about “allow limited per‑token adjustments for tokens whose local ratios are close to 1.” That is implementable: compute per‑token local ratios $r_{i,t} = \pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots\big)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\big(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots\big)$, and if $|r_{i,t}-1| < \delta$ let $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ be used, otherwise collapse $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ to the group mean (or shrink it). But be cautious: introducing token‑level dependence on local ratios reintroduces variance risks the paper warns about; the safer pattern is to keep the gating scalar $s_i(\theta)$ at sequence level and use advantages (not local ratios) for token adjustments.

Recommendation
If you’re training with pure sequence rewards and long outputs (or MoE models), start with GSPO. If you have meaningful token/turn rewards or see early-token learning being systematically suppressed, switch to GSPO‑token and design $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ to emphasize the tokens you care about. Avoid restoring raw token‑level importance ratios as multiplicative weights — that’s what the paper identifies as unstable.

In short: your concern is valid, but the paper already provides a principled hybrid (GSPO‑token) that preserves GSPO’s stability while permitting the token‑level control you want.

### Can you provide a formal variance comparison between GSPO and GRPO as sequence length grows?
Question:
The paper argues that GRPO’s token-level ratios induce high-variance gradient estimates that accumulate with length, while GSPO’s sequence-level approach reduces variance. Is there a formal variance analysis (even under simplifying assumptions, e.g., independent tokens) that shows how the variance of the gradient estimator scales with |y| for the two methods? A comparison of asymptotic behavior with respect to |y| and G would clarify the source of GSPO’s stability.
Answer:
Short answer: yes — under simple, standard independence assumptions you can show that GSPO’s gradient variance falls off with sequence length roughly one order faster than GRPO’s. Intuitively this happens because GSPO aggregates token log-ratio noise inside a length-normalized sequence statistic (a sample mean of log-ratios) before exponentiating, so the off-policy noise is averaged down by a factor ~$1/|y|$ (CLT/delta-method). GRPO applies independent token-level importance weights directly to each token gradient, so its per-sequence variance does not get this extra $1/|y|$ reduction. Below I give a short formal sketch with clear assumptions and the resulting asymptotic scalings in $|y|$ and group size $G$.

Assumptions
1. For a fixed query $x$ and sampled response $y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ under the old policy, treat token-level terms as independent across $t$ (conditional on $x$). Concretely let
   - $u_t := \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$ be the token log-ratio random variables, assumed i.i.d. with mean $\mu_u(\theta)$ and variance $\sigma_u^2$ independent of $|y|$.
   - $g_t := \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ be the per-token score (vector); assume components have finite second moments and (for simplicity) that $\mathrm{Cov}(g_s,g_t)=0$ for $s\neq t$ and $\mathrm{Var}(g_t) = V_g$ (a scalar proxy for typical coordinate variance).
2. Advantages $\widehat{A}_i$ are treated as constants (or independent of the sampling noise of token ratios) to focus on the importance-weight noise; group averaging over $G$ IID responses reduces variance by $1/G$.
3. Clipping is ignored in the asymptotic scaling argument (it only truncates extreme ratios and does not change the leading-order dependence on $|y|$ in the non-clipped regime).

GRPO gradient variance (sketch)
GRPO (without clipping) has, for a single response $i$, the per-sequence contribution proportional to
   $$
   R_{\text{GR}} := \frac{\widehat{A}_i}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} w_{t} g_t,
   $$
where $w_t := \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)} = e^{u_t}$. Assume $\mathbb{E}[w_t]=m_1$ and $\mathbb{E}[w_t^2]=m_2$ (constants independent of $|y|$). Under independence,
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}[R_{\text{GR}}] = \frac{\widehat{A}_i^2}{|y|^2} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \mathrm{Var}(w_t g_t)
            = \frac{\widehat{A}_i^2}{|y|^2} |y| \, \mathbb{E}[w_t^2]\, \mathbb{E}[\|g_t\|^2] + o(1)
            = \widehat{A}_i^2 \cdot \frac{m_2 V_g}{|y|} + o\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right).
   $$
Thus the per-sequence variance scales like $O(1/|y|)$. Averaging over $G$ independent responses reduces variance by $1/G$, so GRPO overall variance scales as
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}_{\text{GR}} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{G\,|y|}\right).
   $$

GSPO gradient variance (sketch)
GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence ratio
   $$
   s := \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}
     = \exp\!\big(\bar{u}\big),\quad \bar{u} := \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} u_t.
   $$
Its per-sequence gradient contribution (omitting the common factor $\widehat{A}_i$) is
   $$
   R_{\text{GS}} := s \cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} g_t.
   $$
Key points: $\bar{u}$ is an average of $|y|$ i.i.d. variables, so $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{u})=\sigma_u^2/|y|$. Apply the delta method to $s = e^{\bar{u}}$:
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[s] \approx e^{\mu_u},\quad \mathrm{Var}(s) \approx e^{2\mu_u}\cdot \mathrm{Var}(\bar{u}) = e^{2\mu_u}\,\frac{\sigma_u^2}{|y|}.
   $$
Next, the average score $\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t g_t$ has variance $\mathrm{Var}\!\big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum g_t\big) = V_g/|y|$.
Treat $s$ and the averaged $g$-term as (approximately) independent for leading-order scaling (or account for weak covariance that does not change order). Using $\mathrm{Var}(s X) = \mathbb{E}[s]^2 \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(s) \mathbb{E}[X]^2 + \mathrm{Var}(s)\mathrm{Var}(X)$ (drop lower-order cross-terms if means are zero), and assuming $\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum g_t\big]$ is $O(1)$ or small relative to its variance contribution, the dominant term is
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}[R_{\text{GS}}] \approx \mathbb{E}[s]^2 \cdot \mathrm{Var}\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t} g_t\Big)
              \approx e^{2\mu_u} \cdot \frac{V_g}{|y|}.
   $$
But remember GSPO multiplies by $s$, and $s$’s variance itself scales like $1/|y|$; plugging that in gives a refined leading behavior dominated by the product of the two $1/|y|$ factors:
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}[R_{\text{GS}}] = O\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|^2}\Big).
   $$
A more careful delta-method expansion (keeping both contributions) yields
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}[R_{\text{GS}}] \approx \frac{e^{2\mu_u}V_g}{|y|} + \frac{e^{2\mu_u}\sigma_u^2\,(\text{mean of averaged }g)^2}{|y|}.
   $$
If the mean of the averaged $g$ is $O(1/|y|)$ or zero (common when gradients are centered), the $1/|y|^2$ term becomes dominant. In typical stochastic-gradient settings the centered-score assumption makes the variance scale as $O(1/|y|^2)$ for the GSPO term. Averaging over group size $G$ again multiplies by $1/G$, so GSPO overall variance scales as
   $$
   \mathrm{Var}_{\text{GS}} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{G\,|y|^2}\right)
   $$
in the regime where the averaging inside $s$ is the dominant variance-reduction mechanism.

Summary of scalings
Under the simplifying assumptions above, the per-update (after averaging $G$ responses) asymptotic variance scalings are
   $$
   \text{GRPO:}\quad \mathrm{Var}_{\text{GR}} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{G\,|y|}\right),\\
   \text{GSPO:}\quad \mathrm{Var}_{\text{GS}}  = O\!\left(\frac{1}{G\,|y|^2}\right)\ \text{(in the natural centered/mean-zero gradient regime)}.
   $$
Thus GSPO gains an extra factor of $1/|y|$ in variance reduction compared to GRPO. That extra factor is exactly the sequence-length averaging effect emphasized in the paper: GSPO aggregates token-level log-ratio noise before weighting gradients, so token-level noise averages down by the sequence length, whereas GRPO applies noisy token weights directly and so cannot exploit that averaging.

Intuition and broader connections
The difference is analogous to the variance reduction you get by using an average-based control variate instead of applying independent noisy multipliers to each summand. GSPO’s length-normalized log-ratio is a sample mean (hence variance $\sim 1/|y|$), and exponentiation preserves that reduction up to a delta-method factor. GRPO’s independent per-token multipliers do not benefit from this averaging, so their squared magnitude remains $O(1)$ and the sum of $|y|$ terms yields only the usual $1/|y|$ reduction from averaging a sum of $|y|$ i.i.d. random variables; the per-term multiplier variance still appears in the leading constant.

Caveats
The derivation above relies on independence and some mean-centering assumptions to get clean power laws. In practice:
- Token dependencies (autoregression) will change constants and can slow the $|y|$ scaling, but the qualitative advantage of GSPO remains: averaging before weighting reduces variance relative to tokenwise weighting.
- Clipping, covariance between $s$ and $g$, and nonzero means of per-token gradients shift constants and can introduce more complicated behavior; clipping can reduce extreme contributions but does not restore the lost $1/|y|$ factor for GRPO.
- For very short sequences the asymptotic advantage is small; the benefit grows with $|y|$ (long responses), which is exactly the regime the paper targets.

Conclusion
A compact formal takeaway is: under simple i.i.d. assumptions, GRPO’s variance scales like $1/(G|y|)$ while GSPO’s variance scales like $1/(G|y|^2)$ (or at least has an extra $1/|y|$ factor of reduction in leading order). This matches the paper’s claim that token-level importance ratios induce high-variance gradients that accumulate with length, whereas GSPO’s sequence-level ratio benefits from averaging across tokens and therefore yields much lower variance as $|y|$ grows.

### Why define s_{i,t}(\theta) with a stop-gradient ratio in GSPO-token?
Question:
In Equation (9), s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}. Since this product is numerically equal to s_i(\theta), why not simply use s_i(\theta) directly at each token and rely on autodiff? Is the purpose to ensure that the gradient only flows through \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\cdot) and not through s_i(\theta), thereby matching Equation (11)? A brief explanation of the autodiff implications would help.
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the detach (stop-gradient) is used to control which parts of the computational graph contribute gradients. Although numerically $s_{i,t}$ equals $s_i(\theta)$ at runtime, writing it as
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}
$$
makes the training-time gradient flow match the intended analytic gradient in Equation (11): gradients flow only through the token log-probabilities, not through the sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$.

Why this matters (intuitive + technical): the GSPO derivation treats $s_i(\theta)$ as an importance weight that multiplies a sum of token log-gradients. Concretely, the desired gradient (Equation (11)) has the form
$$
s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
$$
That is, the sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ should act as a constant multiplier (w.r.t. token-wise derivatives) when differentiating the loss with respect to $\theta$. If you simply plug the scalar $s_i(\theta)$ into a per-token loss and let autodiff operate on the raw expression $s_i(\theta)$ (computed from the product of token probabilities), autodiff will also backpropagate through $s_i(\theta)$ itself. That produces extra terms: roughly speaking, autodiff would yield contributions proportional to $\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)$, which is $s_i(\theta)\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)$ and thus injects gradients that couple across tokens in a different (and undesired) way. In other words you would not get the clean factor $s_i(\theta)$ times the sum of token log-gradients; you would get additional gradient components coming from differentiating the importance weight itself.

How the stop-gradient fixes this: using $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ detaches the numerical value but blocks gradient flow. By forming $s_{i,t}$ with $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ and dividing the per-token probability by a detached copy, the implementation ensures the scalar multiplier is treated as a constant during backprop while the only terms that receive gradients are the per-token log-probabilities $\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$. Because
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})]}
$$
is numerically $1$, its gradient equals $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$, so the resulting backpropagation exactly reproduces the desired form in Equation (11). Thus the product is numerically equal to $s_i$ but has a different computational graph: same numbers, different gradients.

Practical implications: this avoids "double-counting" or corrupting the gradient with derivatives of the importance weight, preserves the intended weighting (sequence-level importance acting as a scalar multiplier), and stabilizes optimization (important for clipping and token-level advantage variants). In short: the stop-gradient is not about numerical value but about which partial derivatives you want — it enforces that only $\log\pi_\theta$ receives gradients, matching the theoretical gradient expression.

### How does group size G affect stability when using z-scored advantages?
Question:
GSPO and GRPO both use a group-based advantage computed by z-scoring rewards over G sampled responses for the same x. What happens when G is small (e.g., G = 2 or 3), or even G = 1 (undefined)? Is there a recommended minimum G for stability, and how sensitive are the gradients to the stochasticity of the sample mean and standard deviation estimates as G varies?
Answer:
Short answer: small group sizes make the z-scored advantages noisy and can destabilize training; $G = 1$ is unusable (sample standard deviation is zero), $G = 2$–$3$ gives very high sampling noise, and in practice you want a nontrivial $G$ (many teams use $O(8\text{--}32)$ or larger) or alternative variance-reduction (running-batch statistics, epsilon floor) to keep gradients stable.

Why: the paper’s group-based advantage is
$$
\widehat A_i = \frac{r_i - \overline r}{\widehat\sigma_r},
\qquad
\overline r=\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^G r_j,\quad
\widehat\sigma_r=\sqrt{\frac1{G-1}\sum_{j}(r_j-\overline r)^2}.
$$
This leverages the idea that the reward is defined at sequence level and normalizing across a group removes scale and center effects. But both $\overline r$ and $\widehat\sigma_r$ are random estimates computed from just $G$ IID samples from the rollout distribution; their sampling noise propagates into $\widehat A_i$ and therefore into the policy gradient.

How the noise scales (intuitively): the sampling noise of the sample mean shrinks like $1/\sqrt{G}$. The sample standard deviation is itself a noisy estimator with variance that falls with $G$ too, but more slowly and with a heavier-tailed finite-sample distribution (it depends on a chi/chi-squared law). Because $\widehat A_i$ divides by $\widehat\sigma_r$, small-sample fluctuations in $\widehat\sigma_r$ can magnify or flip the sign/scale of advantages, producing large variance in the gradients. Practically:

- $G = 1$: undefined (division by zero / zero variance). You cannot compute a z-score.
- $G = 2$ or $3$: possible but highly noisy. With so few samples the sample standard deviation is very uncertain; a single outlier reward will dominate $\overline r$ and $\widehat\sigma_r$, producing unstable advantage signs/magnitudes and high gradient variance.
- Moderate $G$ (e.g., $8\text{--}32$ or larger): the mean and std estimates are much more stable; advantage noise is much lower and gradients become reliable. The paper emphasizes using fairly large rollout batches for hardware utilization, which naturally supports larger $G$ and thus more stable z-scoring.

Sensitivity of gradients to this stochasticity: because the gradient (Equation 12/13 in the paper) multiplies the importance-weighted log-likelihood gradient by $\widehat A_i$, stochastic fluctuations in $\widehat A_i$ directly scale and potentially flip the direction of gradient contributions from whole sequences. The variance of the policy gradient attributable to advantage estimation therefore decreases as $G$ grows. Clipping (the PPO-style min/clip) helps limit the effect of extremely large advantages but does not remove the fundamental sampling noise induced by tiny $G$.

Practical recommendations (grounded in the paper’s design and typical practice):
- Don’t use $G = 1$. If you must, switch to a learned value/baseline or run a different normalization (batch/population) instead of a within-group z-score.
- Prefer $G \ge 8$ as a practical lower bound; $G$ in tens ($8\text{--}32$) is a common tradeoff between compute, variance reduction, and hardware batching. If you have very long responses or highly variable rewards, larger $G$ helps.
- Add a small epsilon in the denominator (e.g., $\widehat\sigma_r + \epsilon$) to avoid division-by-small-number amplifications.
- If you cannot increase $G$ for compute reasons, reduce variance by pooling across multiple queries (compute mean/std across a larger minibatch rather than per-query group), use running or exponential-moving averages for mean/std, or combine z-scoring with clipped advantages and smaller learning rates.
- Monitor diagnostic signals: fraction of clipped sequences, distribution of $\widehat\sigma_r$ across groups, and variance of gradient norms. Large, sudden increases in these metrics indicate too-small $G$ (or outlier rewards) and predict instability.

Connection to broader concepts: this is the classic bias–variance tradeoff of baselines in policy gradient methods. A better (low-variance) baseline reduces gradient variance; group z-scoring is a nonparametric baseline but its effectiveness depends on sample size. The paper’s emphasis on large rollout batches aligns with this: training scale (larger effective $G$ or batching) both improves hardware utilization and stabilizes group-based normalization.

In short: group z-scoring is a good, simple baseline but only when $G$ is large enough that $\overline r$ and $\widehat\sigma_r$ are reliable. Small $G$ (2–3) is risky; use at least a modest group size (roughly $O(8\text{--}32)$ or larger), add numerical guards (epsilon), or replace with pooled/running statistics if you cannot increase $G$.

### Handling tasks with partial credit or multi-turn rewards under GSPO
Question:
The paper notes GSPO-token can allow token-wise advantage customization for scenarios like multi-turn RL. Suppose we have partial credit signals at intermediate turns or steps. Would you recommend computing a separate \widehat{A}_{i,t} per step using a per-step verifier and then aggregating with GSPO-token, or computing several sequence-level \widehat{A}_i for segments and summing their contributions? Any guidance on preventing leakage of future rewards when defining per-token advantages in a causal manner?
Answer:
Short answer: use GSPO-token when you want genuine per-step/turn credit (compute a separate $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ for each token/turn) and combine those advantages with the GSPO-token objective; if your intermediate signals are naturally segment-level and coarse, you can instead treat each segment as a separate “sequence” and apply sequence-level GSPO per segment (summing contributions). The key is to make the per-token/segment advantages causal and normalized across the group, and to keep the importance weights sequence-rooted (as GSPO-token does) so you avoid the high-variance pitfalls of token-level importance ratios.

Why GSPO-token is the cleaner option for multi-turn partial credit
The paper introduces GSPO-token precisely to allow token-wise (or turn-wise) advantage customization while preserving GSPO’s desirable low-variance weighting. GSPO-token uses the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (length-normalized) as the multiplicative factor but permits independent advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Concretely, the objective is
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\big(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},\;\mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\big)\Big],
$$
with the implementation trick used in the paper
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]},
$$
so numerically each token uses the same sequence-level scale but you can supply distinct $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This keeps the low-variance, sequence-level correction (avoiding GRPO’s token-wise importance ratios) while letting you inject per-turn credit where it is meaningful.

How to compute per-step advantages without leaking future rewards
The critical requirement is that each $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ must be causal (only depend on information available up to step $t$, not on future tokens or future verifications). Practically:

1) Use a per-step verifier that inspects only the prefix $y_{i, \le t}$ (or the environment state at step $t$) and returns $r_t(x,y_{i,\le t})$. Then form an advantage per token/turn from that prefix-only signal. For group-normalized advantages at step $t$ use
$$
\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\frac{r_t(x,y_{i,\le t})-\mathrm{mean}_i(r_t(x,y_{i,\le t}))}{\mathrm{std}_i(r_t(x,y_{i,\le t}))},
$$
so each $\widehat{A}_{\cdot,t}$ is normalized across the $G$ responses for that same prefix-turn. This mirrors the group advantage used in the paper but applied per-step.

2) If your verifier inherently inspects future context (e.g., a roll-out to the end), avoid using that signal as a per-step advantage. Instead either (a) make it a sequence-level reward only, or (b) create a prefix-limited surrogate verifier for $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Leakage of future reward into $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is exactly the kind of information-imbalance that breaks causality and can produce biased updates.

3) Implement strict detach/stop-gradient where needed. In code follow the paper’s pattern for $s_{i,t}$ (they use sg/detach so the per-token factor is numerically equal to $s_i$ but does not pass gradients through the token-level ratio). Likewise ensure your per-step advantage computation does not backprop through future computations or access future model logits.

When to prefer segment-level GSPO (treat segments as independent sequences)
If the intermediate signals are sparse, noisy, or naturally defined over segments (e.g., “turn 1 outcome”, “turns 2–4 outcome”), it can be simpler and more stable to split the rollout into $K$ segments and treat each segment as an independent sequence for GSPO. That is, form segment-level responses $y^{(k)}$ and compute sequence-level advantages $\widehat{A}^{(k)}$ for each segment; then apply GSPO per segment and sum contributions. This is equivalent to applying GSPO-token with constant advantage across tokens in a segment but avoids the bookkeeping of per-token verifiers. This approach preserves GSPO’s low-variance importance weighting because the importance ratio remains sequence/segment-level.

Practical tips and pitfalls
- Normalize advantages per group and per time step (or per segment) to control variance, as shown in the paper. Unnormalized sparse partial credits can blow up gradient scale.  
- Use length-normalized $s_i(\theta)$ (as in the paper) so sequence-length differences don’t require different clipping ranges.  
- Be conservative about how many distinct $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ you introduce; many high-frequency, noisy per-token advantages can raise variance even if the importance ratio is sequence-level. Consider smoothing or aggregating nearby turns if needed.  
- If you must use future-dependent signal for final evaluation, keep it as a sequence-level reward only; don’t feed it back into per-step advantages.

Recipe summary
If you have genuine per-turn partial credit that can be computed causally from prefixes, compute $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ per step with a prefix-only verifier, normalize across the group at each step, and plug them into GSPO-token (with the paper’s detach pattern for $s_{i,t}$). If partial credit is better expressed at coarser segments or you cannot make the per-step verifier causal, partition the sequence into segments and run GSPO at the segment (sequence) level, summing segment contributions.

This keeps the low-variance, sequence-level importance weighting of GSPO while honoring causality in advantage signals, which is the core guidance the paper’s GSPO-token variant is intended to support.

### Interpreting the paradox of higher clipping fractions yet better efficiency under GSPO
Question:
Figure 2 suggests GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (or sequences) than GRPO but still trains more efficiently. Could you formalize why a higher raw clipping fraction does not necessarily imply lower effective sample size? For example, is the relevant metric the variance of the retained gradient estimator rather than the count of unclipped tokens, and does GSPO’s uniform per-sequence weighting reduce estimator variance enough to outweigh the apparent loss due to more clipping?
Answer:
Short answer: a high raw clipping fraction (number of tokens excluded) does not imply a smaller effective sample size (ESS) because what matters for optimization is the variance (or signal-to-noise) of the gradient estimator, which depends on the distribution of importance weights, not just how many tokens are used. GSPO reduces that variance by using a single, length‑normalized sequence importance weight applied uniformly to all tokens in a response; this produces a lower‑variance estimator (higher ESS) even though more tokens are clipped under its sequence-level clipping rule.

Why, in detail: importance sampling theory and the paper’s gradient formulas make the intuition precise. If we ignore clipping, GSPO’s gradient (Equation (7) in the paper) for a single response i is proportional to
$$
g^{\text{GSPO}}_i \propto s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
with the sequence importance
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}.
$$
GRPO’s gradient for the same response (ignoring clipping) is
$$
g^{\text{GRPO}}_i \propto \widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
where the tokenwise weights are
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

The variance of a Monte Carlo gradient estimator is determined by the variability of the random term being averaged. For GSPO that random term is the product of three factors: the (per‑sequence) importance weight $s_i$, the group advantage $\widehat A_i$, and the averaged score $(1/|y_i|)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi$. For GRPO the random term is a sum of tokenwise terms each multiplied by a possibly very different $w_{i,t}$. There are two key consequences:

1. Unequal token weights inflate variance relative to a single coherent weight. In GRPO the $w_{i,t}$ are computed from single token samples from each next‑token distribution. As the paper emphasizes, a single sample does not stably estimate that next‑token density ratio, so the $w_{i,t}$ fluctuate a lot across tokens (and across updates in MoE models). Variance of a sum with heteroskedastic terms is larger than variance of a sum with identical scaling, everything else equal. Formally, Var(sum w_t x_t) includes terms Var(w_t x_t) and Covariances; large dispersion in the w_t increases both and hence degrades gradient SNR.

2. Sequence weighting coheres the update across tokens and reduces accumulation of high‑variance noise along a long sequence. GSPO’s $s_i$ is the geometric mean (length normalized) of token likelihood ratios; by averaging the log ratios it smooths token‑level fluctuations. Put differently, GSPO replaces many noisy, independent off‑policy corrections with one more stable correction per sequence. This lowers the variance of the per‑sample gradient term especially when sequences are long (the paper points out accumulation of noise across tokens causes collapse in GRPO).

Effective sample size (ESS) is the correct quantity to compare, not raw unclipped token count. For importance weights $\{w_j\}$ an often used measure of ESS is
$$
\text{ESS} \approx \frac{\big(\sum_j w_j\big)^2}{\sum_j w_j^2}.
$$
What matters is the dispersion of the weights: even if you have fewer retained items, if their weights are more uniform (smaller variance of weights) ESS can be larger. GSPO’s per‑sequence $s_i$ produces a weight distribution with much smaller dispersion than GRPO’s per‑token $w_{i,t}$ (which are noisy and volatile), so GSPO can have higher ESS despite clipping more raw tokens.

How clipping interacts: clipping removes extreme weights to control bias/variance. GSPO clips whole sequences; this may exclude a larger fraction of tokens numerically, but it avoids keeping many tokens with moderately extreme and inconsistent tokenwise weights that, in GRPO, would still contribute large variance. In other words, GSPO’s clipping prunes whole high‑variance sequence samples, yielding a cleaner (lower‑variance) pool for gradient estimation. GRPO’s tokenwise clipping can leave many mixed, partially clipped sequences where residual heterogeneity in token weights still amplifies variance.

Empirical tie‑back: the paper reports two orders of magnitude difference in clipped token fractions but shows GSPO yields better stability and training efficiency. That observation is consistent with the above reasoning: higher raw clipping fraction is compatible with better effective sample exploitation if the retained gradient terms are much lower variance (higher ESS) and hence carry a stronger learning signal per gradient update.

Bottom line: the relevant metric is estimator variance / ESS (or gradient signal‑to‑noise), not the count of unclipped tokens. GSPO’s sequence‑level, length‑normalized importance weight and uniform per‑sequence token weighting reduce variance (and mitigate MoE routing volatility) enough to outweigh the apparent loss from clipping many more tokens, producing better effective sample utilization and more stable training.

### What objective is optimized if we remove clipping from GSPO and set \widehat{A}_i to raw rewards?
Question:
If we consider GSPO without clipping and define \widehat{A}_i = r(x,y_i) - b(x) with some baseline b(x) (not z-scored, to avoid dependence on G), what exact objective is being optimized when using off-policy samples y_i\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}? Is there a clean expression showing that the estimator converges to the gradient of \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(x,y)] (or a length-normalized variant) under certain conditions, or is the 1/|y| exponent essential enough that we should view this as optimizing a surrogate different from expected reward?
Answer:
Short answer: with clipping removed and $\widehat{A}_i = r(x,y_i) - b(x)$ and $y_i$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO does NOT, in general, produce an unbiased estimator of the true policy gradient for $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(x,y)]$. Instead it optimizes a different surrogate whose gradient is exactly the expression in the paper (Eq. (6) / (11) without clipping). The $1/|y|$ exponent on the sequence ratio is essential: it changes the importance weight so the estimator targets a tilted objective (unless special cases hold), not the canonical expected-reward objective.

Derivation and intuition grounded in the paper
If we remove clipping, set $\widehat{A}_i = r(x,y_i) - b(x)$, and draw $y_i \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$, the GSPO gradient given in the paper becomes (writing $|y|$ for $|y_i|$ to simplify):

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[ \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y|}}
\big(r(x,y)-b(x)\big)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})
\right].
$$

Compare this to the standard policy-gradient identity for the true expected reward objective $R(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(x,y)]$:

$$
\nabla_\theta R(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\,r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\right].
$$

Two differences are decisive. First, GSPO uses the sequence-ratio raised to the power $1/|y|$ rather than the full ratio. Second, GSPO places a $1/|y|$ in front of the sum of per-token score gradients and multiplies that by the (length-normalized) ratio. Because of the $\frac{1}{|y|}$ exponent, the importance weight in GSPO is

$$
w_\text{GSPO}(y) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)}\right)^{1/|y|},
$$

so the estimator is equivalent to performing importance sampling from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to estimate expectations under a different target density proportional to $w_\text{GSPO}(y)\,\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y) = \pi_\theta(y)^{1/|y|}\,\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)^{1-1/|y|}$. Thus the gradient computed is the gradient of a surrogate objective whose density is this tilted mixture, not the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r]$.

When (if ever) it matches the true gradient
There are special situations where the GSPO expression coincides with the true policy gradient:

- If $|y|=1$ (single-token sequences), then the $1/|y|$ exponent equals $1$ and GSPO reduces to ordinary importance weighting, so the estimator targets the true expected reward gradient (up to the baseline which only reduces variance).  
- If the sequence importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)}$ happens to be $1$ for all sampled $y$ (no policy change), then both methods agree trivially.  
- If the ratio factorizes in a special way so that raising to $1/|y|$ and the averaging of token gradients reproduce the full ratio times $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y)$, but this is not true in general for autoregressive models.

Otherwise, with arbitrary lengths and nontrivial ratios, the $1/|y|$ exponent changes the weight and therefore yields a biased estimator for $\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[r]$. In other words, GSPO without clipping optimizes a surrogate whose gradient is the expectation above, not the canonical policy-gradient of the expected reward.

Interpretation and practical rationale
Why did the authors choose the $1/|y|$ exponent and length normalization? The paper motivates this as variance control and to make sequence-level likelihood changes numerically comparable across lengths. Intuitively, raising the sequence ratio to $1/|y|$ produces a per-token average multiplicative factor (a length-normalized change in per-token likelihood), which stabilizes magnitudes and clipping behavior across sequences of different lengths. That stability is the design goal: GSPO trades exact unbiasedness for a lower-variance, more stable surrogate that better matches the sequence-level unit of reward and empirically avoids the instability seen with token-level importance weights (GRPO). The paper’s gradient analysis (Eq. (6)/(11)) is explicit about this trade-off.

Conclusion
In short, without clipping GSPO yields the exact gradient expression above and therefore optimizes a surrogate objective induced by the length-normalized importance weight $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\right)^{1/|y|}$. That surrogate is not, in general, the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(x,y)]$, so the $1/|y|$ exponent is essential: it makes GSPO a principled but different objective (chosen for variance/stability and practical reasons), not an unbiased estimator of the usual expected-reward gradient except in special cases.
