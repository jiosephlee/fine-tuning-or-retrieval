\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 10: Empirical Evaluation: Setup, Benchmarks, and Outcomes

# Models and training compute (e.g., Qwen3-30B-based cold-start)

The empirical study reported in the paper uses a cold-start experimental setup built from a Qwen3 checkpoint. Concretely, the fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) experiments start from a model denoted Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. Calling this a “cold-start” emphasizes that the RL phase is applied beginning from a supervised-finetuned foundation rather than from a model already specialized by prior RL. The paper frames GSPO as an algorithm meant to be practical at large scale, and therefore the experiments are carried out on models representative of the real production scale used in the Qwen3 development line.

The experiments are designed to stress the algorithm under realistic, large-scale training conditions: long responses, substantial rollout batch sizes, and training on MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) architectures. The setup intentionally partitions a large rollout batch into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates (see next section), which increases the off-policy discrepancy between the data collection policy and the policy being optimized. All reported comparisons (GSPO vs GRPO) use the same starting model and comparable consumed compute and query budgets, so differences in outcomes reflect algorithmic behavior rather than differences in base models or compute allocation.

# Batch partitioning into minibatches and off-policy implications

A central practical detail of the experimental pipeline is that each large rollout batch is split into multiple mini-batches before performing gradient updates. In the experiments described, each rollout batch is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient computation. This partitioning is a common engineering choice to improve hardware utilization and to allow multiple optimization steps per set of collected rollouts, but it has an important algorithmic consequence: the samples used for gradient estimation were generated by an older policy, which makes the optimization intrinsically off-policy.

Why does this matter? Importance sampling is the standard tool to correct for the mismatch between the behavior distribution (the distribution that produced the samples) and the target distribution (the current policy we are trying to evaluate or improve). When mini-batching produces an off-policy setting, importance ratios appear in the gradient estimators and clipping mechanisms (as in PPO-like algorithms) are used to prevent overly off-policy samples from dominating the update. The experiments therefore exercise both GSPO’s and GRPO’s ability to operate under off-policy conditions introduced by splitting rollouts into multiple mini-batches. The difference in how each algorithm constructs and applies importance weights under these off-policy conditions is a major factor in the observed empirical behavior.

# Benchmarks: AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces (metrics: Pass@1, Elo)

The paper evaluates RL outcomes using three independent benchmarks chosen to reflect varied, challenging capabilities of large language models:

- AIME’24. Performance is measured as average Pass@1 across 32 samplings. Pass@1 reflects whether a single sampled response solves the problem, and averaging over 32 samplings stabilizes the metric by sampling variations in generation.

- LiveCodeBench (202410–202502). For this code-focused benchmark the metric is average Pass@1 over 8 samplings. Like AIME’24, multiple samplings are used to reduce variance due to stochastic generation.

- CodeForces. This benchmark is summarized by an Elo rating, which is an aggregate skill measure derived from pairwise performance comparisons on coding tasks.

For each benchmark the paper reports curves that track performance over the course of RL training and compares GSPO’s curves with those from GRPO under the same consumed query budgets and compute. The choice of these benchmarks emphasizes both mathematical/programming reasoning (AIME, CodeForces) and code generation/correctness (LiveCodeBench), allowing assessment of whether GSPO’s training dynamics translate into practical improvements on relevant downstream tasks.

# Clipping ranges used in practice: GSPO vs GRPO (order-of-magnitude differences)

A concrete experimental distinction between GSPO and the GRPO baseline is the numerical ranges used for clipping the importance ratios. Because GSPO defines importance ratios at the sequence level (and applies a length normalization), the resulting numerical scale of its ratios is very different from the token-level ratios used in GRPO. Reflecting that difference, the paper reports the following clipping ranges used in their experiments:

- GSPO: left and right clipping bounds set to 3e-4 and 4e-4, respectively, when clipping the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ in Equation (the GSPO objective). These values are small because $s_i(\theta)$ is computed as a length-normalized, exponentiated sum of token log-ratio changes.

- GRPO: left and right clipping bounds set to 0.2 and 0.27, respectively, when clipping token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. These per-token ratios are on a different numerical scale and therefore require much larger clipping constants to limit updates.

The experimental choice emphasizes that clipping ranges across algorithms are not directly comparable in magnitude: the unit and scale of importance weights differ. Importantly, these ranges were tuned to produce fair baselines: the GRPO clipping values were carefully tuned to ensure reasonable convergence behavior for that algorithm in these settings.

# Findings: stable reward curves, continuous gains with more compute/longer generations

Across the reported experiments, GSPO shows markedly more stable training dynamics than GRPO. The paper describes the training-reward curves and benchmark-performance curves as follows:

- Stability: Training with GSPO proceeds stably over the course of the RL runs. Unlike prior observations that GRPO can experience catastrophic and irreversible collapses when training large models with long responses, GSPO maintained consistent, non-collapsing learning trajectories in the experiments presented.

- Continuous gains with more compute and longer generations: The GSPO-trained model demonstrated continued improvement when increasing training compute, when the query set was regularly updated, and when generation lengths were extended. In other words, GSPO enabled scaling up the RL investment in straightforward ways (more compute, more recent/varied queries, longer response horizons) and translated those investments into consistent performance gains rather than unstable or regressing behavior.

These findings support the central claim of the paper: that aligning the importance sampling correction and clipping to the sequence as a whole (the unit at which reward is given) yields more robust and scalable RL for large language models.

# Sample efficiency: better accuracy and benchmark scores at equal compute

When comparing sample efficiency, the experiments report that GSPO achieves superior results under the same consumed compute and query budgets. Specifically, for the same amount of RL rollout data and equivalent compute devoted to optimization, GSPO attained higher training accuracy and improved benchmark metrics (Pass@1 on AIME’24 and LiveCodeBench, and Elo on CodeForces) relative to the GRPO baseline.

This outcome highlights two related points. First, GSPO’s sequence-level weighting and clipping produce gradient estimates that exploit collected samples more effectively—fewer noisy, off-policy corrections are introduced, so the update uses the useful signal in each sequence more reliably. Second, the token-level GRPO objective appears to suffer from higher-variance gradient noise that reduces the efficiency of learning from the same data; even when GRPO’s clipping bounds are tuned, it does not match GSPO’s productivity per unit of compute in these experiments.

# Clipping fractions: a surprising empirical observation

One particularly revealing empirical detail is the distribution of which tokens are clipped under the two algorithms. The paper observes a difference of roughly two orders of magnitude in the fraction of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO: GSPO clips a substantially larger fraction of tokens (by two orders of magnitude) than GRPO under the settings used. Intuitively one might expect that clipping more of the gradient signal would reduce sample utilization and slow learning. However, the experiments find the opposite: despite discarding a much larger fraction of token-level signal via sequence-level clipping, GSPO achieves higher training efficiency and better final performance.

This counterintuitive result reinforces the diagnosis from the paper’s motivation and gradient analysis: GRPO’s token-level importance ratios introduce high-variance and sometimes invalid corrections that contaminate optimization, and keeping many tokenwise contributions un-clipped does not equate to productive learning. GSPO’s sequence-level approach, even if it throws away more individual token contributions, preserves coherent, lower-variance gradient directions aligned with the sequence-level reward, yielding more effective updates.

# Deployment outcome: contribution to Qwen3 model improvements

Beyond controlled experiments, the paper reports that GSPO was applied in production-scale RL runs and contributed materially to the improvements observed in the latest Qwen3 models. The algorithm’s empirical advantages—training stability, improved sample efficiency, and robustness when scaling to long responses and MoE architectures—made it possible to continue RL training and reap performance gains that were otherwise difficult with prior methods. The paper frames GSPO as a practical algorithmic improvement that served as a foundational component in the success of the Qwen3 family’s RL-driven enhancements.

# MoE-specific stability and Routing Replay: GSPO’s practical advantage

A critical operational advantage reported in the experiments concerns Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. When training MoE architectures with token-level importance ratios (as in GRPO), the paper documents an “expert-activation volatility”: after parameter updates, the set of experts activated for the same response under the new policy can differ substantially from those under the old policy. For a concrete example, with the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, about 10% of experts activated for a given rollout sample under the new policy were different from those activated under the old policy after a single gradient update. This volatility makes token-level ratios unstable and can derail RL convergence.

To mitigate this for GRPO, the authors previously used a Routing Replay strategy: during gradient computation they cache routing decisions (the experts activated under the old policy) and replay those same routing choices when evaluating likelihoods under the new policy, thereby artificially stabilizing the token-level likelihoods used in importance ratios. Routing Replay restores stability for GRPO but incurs extra memory and communication cost and constrains the true capacity utilization of the MoE.

In contrast, GSPO avoids this complication entirely. Because GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods, which are less sensitive to per-token expert changes, it converges without Routing Replay and without the associated overhead. This makes GSPO both operationally simpler and more faithful to the model’s unconstrained capacity, while delivering stable RL optimization for MoE models in practice.

# Infrastructure implications: likelihood recomputation and inference engines

A final practical point highlighted by the experiments concerns RL infrastructure. In many production settings, sampled responses are produced by inference engines (e.g., low-latency servers), but likelihood recomputation for importance ratios is performed with training engines (potentially using different numerical precision and implementation). Such recomputation is costly and complicates distributed training-inference workflows.

Because GSPO relies only on sequence-level likelihoods (rather than token-level), these sequence likelihoods are empirically much more tolerant to numerical and implementation discrepancies between inference and training engines. Consequently, GSPO opens the possibility of using the likelihoods returned directly by an inference engine in optimization without needing an expensive recomputation step on the training engine. This simplifies pipelines for partial rollouts, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated training-inference frameworks, reducing engineering friction while maintaining stable, effective training.

# Summary

The empirical evaluation in the paper demonstrates that, under a realistic large-scale RL setup starting from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, GSPO yields more stable training curves, continues to benefit from increased compute and longer generations, and attains better sample efficiency and benchmark performance than a carefully tuned GRPO baseline. Key practical consequences include eliminating the need for Routing Replay in MoE training, enabling simpler RL infrastructure by tolerating inference/training engine differences, and directly contributing to gains in the deployed Qwen3 models. The combination of conceptual alignment (sequence-level importance ratios and clipping) and empirical validation makes GSPO a compelling algorithm for scalable RL on large language model families.
