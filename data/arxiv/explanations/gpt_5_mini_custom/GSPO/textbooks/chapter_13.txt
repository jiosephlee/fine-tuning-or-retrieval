\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 13: Limitations, Extensions, and Future Directions

# Sensitivity to reward quality and group size

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) builds its learning signal around sequence-level rewards and a group-based normalization of those rewards. Concretely, for a group of $G$ responses to the same query, GSPO computes the standardized advantage
\[
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}
\]
and then uses that advantage in the sequence-level objective (Equation~\eqref{equ:gspo}). Because the advantage is derived entirely from the sampled group, the quality and properties of the reward signal and the choice of group size $G$ directly affect the bias and variance of the update.

If the reward function $r(x,y)$ is noisy, sparse, or systematically biased, GSPO will propagate those imperfections to the standardized advantages and hence to the policy update. The group normalization mitigates some forms of scale mismatch (by centering and scaling within the group), but it cannot remove bias in the reward estimator itself. For example, if the verifier systematically favors a style that is not aligned with downstream evaluation, GSPO will reliably push the policy toward that style. Similarly, reward sparsity (many identical or near-identical scores within a group) reduces the effective signal in the numerator and can make the standardization unstable when the sample standard deviation is very small. In practice the standard deviation in the denominator must be handled robustly (for instance by adding a small epsilon) to avoid numerical instability, but practitioners should still expect sensitivity to reward quality.

Group size $G$ controls a tradeoff between statistical stability and computational/engineering cost. Larger $G$ typically yields more stable and reliable estimates of the mean and standard deviation across responses and therefore produces more stable advantages; however, increasing $G$ comes at the expense of sampling and storage, and may increase wall-clock cost. Small $G$ makes the within-group normalization noisy: the sample mean and sample standard deviation are poor estimators of the true group statistics, and so $\widehat{A}_i$ will have higher variance. This higher variance can slow learning or create instability. The experimental setup in the paper partitioned rollout batches into mini-batches (four mini-batches per large batch) and used tuned clipping ranges for each algorithm; those practical engineering choices interact with group size and should therefore be carefully reported and ablated.

Finally, GSPO-token was introduced to allow finer-grained advantage signals per token in scenarios such as multi-turn RL. When token-level advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ are used, GSPO-token maintains the same numerical importance ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ as the sequence-level ratio but permits different per-token advantages. This flexibility can help when reward structure is inherently local within a sequence, but it reintroduces a sensitivity to how advantages are estimated on a token-by-token basis. In short, both the reward quality and the group construction are critical knobs for GSPO; their settings must be tuned and reported carefully for reproducibility and robust performance.

# Extremely long outputs and ratio distribution tails

GSPO explicitly addresses the accumulation problem of token-level importance weights by forming a length-normalized sequence importance ratio:
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Length normalization is a central design choice in GSPO: it reduces the multiplicative accumulation of token ratios and puts sequence ratios of different lengths into a more comparable numerical range. This reduces variance relative to an unnormalized product over many tokens and makes a common clipping range meaningful across variable-length responses.

Nevertheless, extremely long outputs remain a regime of concern. Although the average per-token log-ratio is used, long sequences allow more opportunities for rare but extreme token-level log-likelihood differences to occur, and those rare events create heavy tails in the empirical distribution of sequence ratios. In other words, even with length normalization, the sample distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ can develop long tails: a few sequences may exhibit unusually large or small $s_i(\theta)$ due to localized but extreme likelihood changes, and these outliers can dominate gradient estimates unless clipped.

The clipping mechanism in GSPO mitigates the influence of such tails by bounding the contribution of an overly off-policy sequence. Still, two practical issues merit attention. First, the optimal clipping thresholds for $s_i(\theta)$ depend on the distributional behavior of the length-normalized ratios; for very long sequences those thresholds may need to be adjusted, and the values typically differ in order of magnitude from token-level clipping in GRPO (the paper reports much smaller numerical clipping ranges for GSPO than for GRPO). Second, extremely long outputs may magnify numerical issues (for example, underflow or precision loss when computing long products of probabilities or sums of log-probabilities). Implementations should compute ratios in log space and apply stable numeric reductions.

As an avenue for future work, one can consider additional safeguards for the tails: robust estimators of central tendency and dispersion for the log-ratio distribution, per-query adaptive clipping (see the “adaptive clipping” open question below), or capping per-token contributions to the averaged log-ratio before exponentiation. These modifications preserve the sequence-level semantics while further taming heavy tails from rare token events.

# Interaction with auxiliary losses (e.g., KL), curriculum, and dataset refresh

The formal presentation of GSPO in the paper omits a KL regularization term for brevity, but in practical RL applications auxiliary losses such as KL penalties or trust-region terms are commonly used to keep the updated policy close to the behavior policy. GSPO’s sequence-level perspective integrates naturally with such auxiliary constraints: one may add a penalty term that measures divergence between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at sequence level (for example using sequence log-likelihood differences) or at per-token level depending on the preferred fidelity of the constraint. The numerical scale and role of these auxiliary penalties will interact with the clipping of $s_i(\theta)$ and therefore require joint tuning. Because GSPO already bounds sequence contributions via clipping, auxiliary KL penalties might be set lighter than in token-weighted schemes, but this is an empirical question.

Curriculum choices and dataset refresh strategies also materially affect GSPO training. The experiments reported in the paper indicate that GSPO benefits from “increasing the training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length.” This observation highlights two points. First, GSPO is compatible with curricula that gradually increase task difficulty or response length: sequence-level ratios are computed consistently as the generation length grows (owing to length normalization), enabling evaluations across curriculum stages. Second, dataset refresh (replacing or updating queries periodically) changes the distribution of observed rewards and therefore the within-group advantage normalization. When the query distribution drifts, advantage statistics and clipping behavior can change; thus, practitioners should monitor distributional shifts and consider re-tuning clipping ranges or group sizes after significant dataset refreshes.

Finally, GSPO removes the need for certain MoE-specific stabilizers such as Routing Replay (which was necessary for GRPO). That reduction in auxiliary engineering may simplify the interaction with other auxiliary losses and curricula. Nonetheless, combining GSPO with KL regularization, reward shaping, or curriculum schedules remains an important practical design space requiring careful empirical studies.

# Extensions to other modalities and structured generation

The core idea behind GSPO is to align the unit of optimization (the importance ratio and clipping) with the unit of reward: because rewards in the paper’s setting are given at the sequence level, importance sampling and clipping are applied at the sequence level. This conceptual principle extends naturally to other autoregressive generation settings where the primary reward or evaluation metric is defined for an entire generated artifact: for example, long-form text, multi-turn dialogues, or other sequential modalities that are modeled autoregressively.

Extending GSPO to other modalities—such as audio, video, or symbolic sequences—requires attention to the representation of a “sequence” and how likelihoods are computed. The length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is agnostic to token semantics; it depends only on a joint likelihood ratio and a sequence length. If those modalities are modeled autoregressively and per-step likelihoods are tractable, the same GSPO machinery can be applied in principle. Structured generation tasks that produce hierarchical or graph outputs (where the “sequence” is not a simple linear chain) require a notion of joint likelihood for the output under the autoregressive factorization used; GSPO’s principle (match importance weighting to the unit of reward) still applies, but the precise normalization and clipping strategies may need to be adapted to the structure (for instance, normalizing by a measure of output complexity rather than token count).

GSPO-token already points toward a hybrid approach for structured or multi-turn settings: the per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ allow localized reward signals while retaining a globally consistent sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Building on GSPO-token, structured-generation extensions could use per-substructure advantages combined with a (possibly different) normalization factor that reflects substructure size. All such extensions are promising but require empirical validation specific to the target modality and structure.

# Connections to off-policy evaluation and per-decision importance sampling

The paper motivates GSPO from the basic principle of importance sampling: to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by reweighting samples from a behavior distribution. For sequences, this principle yields the correct sequence-level importance weight
\[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(y)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(y)}.
\]
GRPO’s token-level design can be viewed as a per-decision or per-token importance reweighting attempt, using token ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
Per-decision importance sampling (PDIS) is a recognized technique in off-policy RL where one reweights contributions at each decision step. However, PDIS relies on expectations across many samples at each decision to properly correct distribution mismatch. The core critique raised in the paper is that GRPO applies token-level ratios based on a single sampled next-token at each step, which undermines the distribution-correcting role of importance weights and injects high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences.

GSPO adheres more faithfully to the basic off-policy identity in the sequence domain: forming the joint sequence ratio (length-normalized for numerical stability) ensures that the importance weight corresponds to the correction required for the entire sampled sequence. Because the reward is sequence-level, GSPO’s reweighting is aligned with the reward, restoring the conceptual consistency of importance sampling. In contrast, GRPO’s token-level reweighting is conceptually inconsistent with a reward that is assigned only to the completed sequence.

These observations suggest a broader conceptual mapping: when rewards are defined at a particular granularity (sequence, turn, or token), importance reweighting should ideally be applied at that same granularity to avoid mismatches that inflate variance. GSPO-token provides one way to reconcile per-decision rewards with sequence-level importance by attaching per-token advantages while retaining a sequence-derived importance multiplier. Formal connections between GSPO and established off-policy estimators such as PDIS warrant further theoretical investigation; the empirical results in the paper indicate that GSPO’s sequence-level importance handling substantially reduces the practical instability observed under token-level weighting.

# Ablations and reproducibility considerations

The paper’s empirical section highlights several experimental design choices that materially impact GSPO’s behavior and should be reported comprehensively for reproducibility. Key elements include:

- Group size $G$ and how rollout batches are partitioned into mini-batches (the experiments partitioned each rollout batch into four mini-batches). Because GSPO computes group-wise normalization of rewards, $G$ directly affects advantage estimates and therefore must be specified.
- The clipping ranges applied to sequence ratios $s_i(\theta)$: the paper reports left and right clipping ranges for GSPO in the experiment (3e-4 and 4e-4) and for GRPO (0.2 and 0.27). Because the numerical scale of $s_i(\theta)$ differs from token-level $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, clipping ranges are not interchangeable and must be tuned and documented.
- Length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$: the exponent $1/|y_i|$ is essential to GSPO’s practical performance and stability. Implementations must compute the log-ratio and exponentiate after averaging the per-token log ratios to avoid numerical underflow and to follow the intended normalization.
- Use of GSPO-token versus GSPO: if token-level advantages are desired (e.g., in multi-turn RL), GSPO-token’s detachment semantics (the $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ in its definition) determine which parts of the computation contribute gradients. The paper’s derivation indicates that GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO when token advantages are uniform, but this equivalence depends on correct implementation of the detach operation.
- Engine precision and likelihood computation: the paper notes that GSPO is tolerant to precision discrepancies between training and inference engines and that, in practice, GSPO can use likelihoods returned by inference engines directly. For reproducibility, authors should state whether they recomputed likelihoods in the training engine or used inference engine outputs.
- MoE considerations: for GRPO, Routing Replay was essential to converge; GSPO eliminated this need. Reproducible comparisons between GSPO and GRPO must therefore indicate whether Routing Replay is used and describe its implementation.

Ablation studies that vary $G$, clipping ranges, group normalization choices (e.g., additive smoothing in standard deviation), use or omission of the KL term, and whether inference or training-engine likelihoods are used would greatly strengthen empirical claims. Reporting seeds, hardware, the exact query-refresh schedule, and any early-stopping rules is equally important.

# Open questions: theory of variance reduction at sequence level, adaptive clipping

The empirical advantage of GSPO over token-weighted GRPO is clear in the presented experiments, but several theoretical and practical open questions remain.

1. Formal variance analysis. Intuitively, GSPO reduces variance by avoiding per-token single-sample importance noise and by length-normalizing the sequence ratio. The gradient expressions (see Equation~\eqref{equ:gspo_grad} and the GRPO gradient) illustrate how GSPO applies an equalized weight across tokens within a sequence:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto \mathbb{E}\!\left[ s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\right],
\]
whereas GRPO’s gradient includes per-token multiplicative weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ inside the sum. A rigorous, quantitative theory remains to be developed that compares the variance of these two estimators as a function of sequence length, reward variability, and model change between $\theta_{\text{old}}$ and $\theta$. Such a theory would guide principled choices of clipping ranges and group sizes and could potentially explain when and why GSPO is preferable.

2. Adaptive clipping. The paper shows that GSPO’s clipping fractions and numerical ranges differ markedly from GRPO’s and that GSPO can clip a much larger fraction of tokens yet still be more sample-efficient. This empirical fact motivates adaptive clipping strategies that tune clipping thresholds based on the observed distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ across the batch or group. Possible adaptive schemes include percentile-based clipping (e.g., clipping to the $\alpha$ and $(1-\alpha)$ quantiles of the sequence-ratio distribution), variance-scaled clipping, or per-query adaptive ranges. Designing such adaptive rules and proving that they preserve desirable theoretical properties (e.g., bias control) is an open problem.

3. MoE expert-activation volatility theory. GSPO empirically resolved the need for Routing Replay in MoE models by relying on sequence likelihoods that are less sensitive to per-token expert routing changes. A deeper theoretical understanding of why sequence likelihoods are stable under expert-activation changes (and when they might not be) would strengthen the guarantees for MoE training. In particular, characterizing the relationship between routing stochasticity, per-token likelihood fluctuations, and sequence likelihood stability is an open avenue.

4. Hybrid granularity and credit assignment. GSPO-token provides a mechanism to mix sequence-level importance with token-level advantages. How to optimally allocate credit across granularities—when to use sequence-level, turn-level, or token-level advantages—and how this interacts with importance weighting remains unresolved. Systematic studies could reveal regimes where localized credit assignment improves learning without reintroducing the instability seen in GRPO.

5. Use with imperfect or approximate likelihoods. The paper notes practical advantages when using inference-engine likelihoods directly. But when likelihoods are approximate (e.g., due to quantization, mixed precision, or approximate decoding), the consequences for the bias and variance of the GSPO estimator should be theoretically and empirically characterized.

Addressing these open questions will deepen our understanding of GSPO’s operating principles and help generalize its stability and efficiency guarantees across architectures, tasks, and modalities.

# Concluding remarks

GSPO reframes a core design principle in RL for language models: the unit of importance weighting and clipping should match the unit of reward and optimization. This shift yields clear empirical benefits—improved stability, better sample utilization, and simpler MoE training—while raising a set of practical and theoretical questions. For practitioners, careful attention to reward quality, group construction, clipping choices, numerical stability in likelihood computations, and complete reporting of experimental choices is essential to reproduce and extend the reported results. For researchers, deriving a quantitative variance theory for sequence-level importance sampling, designing adaptive clipping rules, and exploring structured and multimodal extensions are promising directions that will further clarify the scope and limitations of GSPO.
