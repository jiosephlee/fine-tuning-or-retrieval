\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 14: Summary and Practitioner Checklist

# Key takeaways: align unit of reward, ratio, and clipping at sequence level

The central conceptual lesson from the paper is simple and decisive: the optimization unit must match the reward unit. In RL for language models, rewards are assigned to entire responses (sequences), not to individual tokens. Importance sampling and clipping mechanisms should therefore be applied at the same unit—sequence level—so that off-policy correction actually reflects the distributional mismatch between the behavior policy that generated a whole response and the target policy being optimized. When importance weights are computed and clipped per token while the reward is per sequence, the importance-sampling machinery is misapplied: token-level weights are based on single samples from each next-token distribution and therefore do not perform the averaging that importance sampling requires, which introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long responses.

GSPO operationalizes this principle. Its objective applies importance ratios, advantages, and clipping to whole responses. The GSPO objective (omitting KL regularization and showing expectation over a group of $G$ responses per query) is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \!\left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \!\left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) 
\right],
\]

with the (length-normalized) sequence importance ratio

\[
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp \left( \tfrac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

Length normalization (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) is an important implementation detail: it reduces variance of the sequence-level ratio across different response lengths and keeps ratios on a unified numerical scale so that a single clipping range is meaningful across lengths. Conceptually, GSPO eliminates the per-token importance-weight asymmetry that GRPO suffers from, replacing it with a single scalar per response that weights the entire sequence gradient equally across its tokens. This alignment both reduces gradient noise and matches the reward signal.

# When to use GSPO vs GSPO-token

GSPO is the default recommendation when the reward signal is defined at the sequence (response) level and you need a robust, scalable RL method for large language models, especially for dense and MoE models alike. In that common setup, GSPO and GSPO-token are numerically identical when the per-token advantages are set equal across a response; GSPO is simpler, conceptually cleaner, and empirically more stable for single-turn or sequence-level reward tasks.

GSPO-token is a deliberate variant designed for scenarios where you want to customize advantages at token granularity. A representative setting is multi-turn RL or tasks where intermediate tokens correspond to distinct subgoals and you have meaningful per-token (or per-turn) advantage estimates. GSPO-token retains the sequence-level importance scalar $s_i(\theta)$ as a detached numerical multiplier, but allows distinct $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ values within a response. Its objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \!\left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \!\left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right],
\]

with $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ constructed so that numerically it equals $s_i(\theta)$ while preventing token-level importance ratios from contributing gradients in unintended ways. If your training problem benefits from token-wise advantages (for example, multi-turn dialogs where rewards can be attributed to specific turns), GSPO-token gives you that flexibility while preserving the stability advantages of sequence-level importance weighting. If you do not need token-level advantages, prefer GSPO.

# Default starting hyperparameters and logging targets

The paper gives concrete, empirically validated clipping ranges and training partitioning that you can use as starting points. For GSPO, use a sequence-level clipping window with left and right bounds approximately $1 - \varepsilon_\text{left} = 1 - 3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $1 + \varepsilon_\text{right} = 1 + 4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ (reported as left/right clipping ranges 3e-4 and 4e-4). By contrast, GRPO uses much larger per-token clipping ranges in the examples reported (left/right roughly 0.2 and 0.27). The dramatic difference in magnitude reflects that the numerical scale of $s_i(\theta)$ (length-normalized sequence ratios) is much smaller than token-level ratio scales used by GRPO—so expect and tune clipping ranges accordingly.

The paper also mentions a common rollout batching practice: partition each large rollout batch into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. In the experiments discussed, each rollout batch was partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates; this practice creates an off-policy learning setting that the clipping mechanism is meant to mitigate. Use group-based advantage normalization within each group of $G$ responses: compute each response advantage as

\[
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x, y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x, y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}.
\]

Logging targets you should track during training include: training reward curves (to ensure stable upward progress), the downstream benchmark performance metrics you care about (the paper tracks AIME, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces as examples), and clipping statistics such as the fraction of responses (or tokens) that are clipped. Monitoring the fraction of clipped tokens/responses is particularly informative: the paper reports that GSPO clips a far larger fraction of tokens than GRPO (a difference of roughly two orders of magnitude in clipped-token fraction), yet GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency—so interpret clipping fraction in the context of which unit is being clipped (sequence vs token). For MoE models, also log expert-activation volatility: monitor how many experts activated for the same response change between the old and new policies (the paper observed roughly 10% change per update for a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model).

Finally, the paper highlights an infrastructural log you can consider: whether you recompute likelihoods using the training engine or accept likelihoods from the inference engine. GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it more tolerant to precision differences between training and inference engines, potentially allowing you to use inference-engine likelihoods directly and avoid recomputation overhead.

# Stability checklist for dense and MoE models

For dense models, ensure the following when deploying GSPO. First, apply sequence-level importance ratios and clipping as described; this aligns the optimization unit with the reward and dramatically reduces the high-variance noise introduced by token-level importance weighting. Second, normalize advantages within groups of responses per query (compute mean and standard deviation across the group) so that advantage scales are comparable across updates. Third, ensure you use length normalization in the sequence importance ratio, i.e., the $1/|y|$ exponent in $s_i(\theta)$, to keep ratios numerically stable across response lengths.

For MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) models, GSPO offers an important practical advantage: it mitigates the expert-activation volatility problem that undermines token-level importance ratios. Under GRPO, the set of experts activated for the same response can change substantially after gradient updates—empirically the paper reports about a 10% change for a specific 48-layer MoE model—and that change invalidates token-level importance ratios unless special handling is used. The prior workaround (Routing Replay) caches the activated experts under the old policy and replays those routing modes when evaluating token likelihoods under the new policy, which stabilizes GRPO but imposes extra memory and communication overhead and constrains the MoE capacity.

GSPO obviates the need for Routing Replay: by focusing on sequence likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods, GSPO is robust to changes in which experts are activated per token. For MoE RL, the checklist items are therefore: (1) switch to GSPO sequence-level ratios and clipping to avoid routing replay overhead; (2) monitor expert-activation volatility metrics to confirm stability; and (3) verify that sequence likelihoods do not exhibit dramatic, spurious fluctuations across updates (the paper reports that sequence likelihoods remain well-behaved even when token-level activations shift).

# Troubleshooting guide and common pitfalls

Model collapse: If training diverges or you encounter catastrophic collapse, first check whether you are using token-level importance ratios (e.g., GRPO-style per-token weighting) while your rewards are sequence-level. The paper attributes severe, often irreversible collapse to the high-variance noise introduced by token-level importance weights, which accumulate for long responses and are amplified by clipping. A primary remedy is to switch to GSPO so importance sampling and clipping operate at the sequence level. If you must use GRPO, be aware that you may need Routing Replay for MoE models and careful tuning of per-token clipping ranges; even so, GRPO remains more brittle.

Excessive clipping or unintuitive clipping statistics: GSPO often results in a much larger fraction of clipped tokens compared to GRPO because GSPO clips at the response level. Do not interpret a high clipped-token fraction for GSPO as a sign of poor training—interpret clipping in the context of the clipping unit. Track the fraction of clipped responses (not just tokens) and compare clipped-response fractions over time. If GSPO’s clipped-response fraction is excessively large and learning stalls, double-check your clip bounds; ensure you are using the much smaller clip magnitudes appropriate for length-normalized sequence ratios (the paper used 3e-4 and 4e-4 in experiments).

Value estimation substitutes: If you are adopting GRPO to avoid training a value model, be cautious: GRPO’s token-level design was intended to avoid learning a separate value network but can be unstable. GSPO likewise avoids a value model by using group relative advantages but does so with more principled importance sampling. If you need finer-grained advantage estimates than group normalization allows, consider GSPO-token rather than reverting to per-token importance ratios.

MoE-specific instability: If you see MoE expert-activation volatility leading to unstable per-token likelihoods under GRPO, either use Routing Replay (with the known overheads) or migrate to GSPO. The paper documents that GSPO stabilizes MoE RL without routing replay, enabling the model to use its full capacity and removing additional infrastructure complexity.

Likelihood precision and engine mismatch: If your RL pipeline uses inference engines to generate responses but training engines to recompute likelihoods, GSPO reduces the need for recomputation because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant to precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. If you observe subtle mismatches in likelihoods that seem to affect optimization, consider whether the discrepancies are at token precision or sequence precision; GSPO is more robust to token-precision noise.

Choosing clipping ranges: A common pitfall is adopting clipping ranges designed for token-level ratios when switching to sequence-level ratios (or vice versa). The numerical scales differ by orders of magnitude, so reuse clipping hyperparameters only when they are matched to the importance-ratio definition. For GSPO, begin with very small clipping tolerances (the paper’s experiments used left/right bounds on the order of 3e-4 and 4e-4). For GRPO-style token clipping, clipping ranges are much larger numerically (the paper tuned token-level clipping left/right to 0.2 and 0.27 in its comparisons).

Summary action plan

Begin RL runs using GSPO with group-advantage normalization and the length-normalized sequence importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Start with the reported sequence-level clipping magnitudes (3e-4 / 4e-4) and partition large rollout batches into multiple mini-batches as needed (the experiments partitioned each rollout into four mini-batches). Log training rewards, downstream benchmarks, clipped-response and clipped-token fractions, and (for MoE) expert-activation volatility. If you require token-wise advantage control (e.g., multi-turn RL), switch to GSPO-token rather than reverting to token-level importance ratios. If you observe instability under GRPO, prefer GSPO as the primary remedy—especially for MoE models, where GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay and simplifies RL infrastructure.

By following these practices grounded in the paper’s analysis and empirical findings, practitioners can realize more stable, efficient, and scalable RL fine-tuning of large language models.
