\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: Gradient Mechanics: GSPO vs GRPO

# GSPO gradient structure: sequence-level ratio times average token log-prob gradients

To understand how Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) updates model parameters, start from its optimization objective, which operates at the sequence level. Ignoring clipping for clarity, GSPO's objective over a group of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ to a query $x$ is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \right],
\]

where the sequence-level importance ratio (length-normalized) is defined as

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

Differentiating the objective with respect to parameters $\theta$ gives the GSPO gradient (again omitting clipping):

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
\right].
\]

Expanding $\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)$ yields the transparent form used for computation:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This expression has two structural pieces: a scalar weight per sequence, $s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$, and the average over token-level log-probability gradients $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\cdot)$. Intuitively, GSPO re-weights the entire gradient contribution of a response by how much the current policy differs from the behavior policy on the whole sequence (through $s_i(\theta)$) and by the standardized relative reward $\widehat{A}_i$. The actual parameter change direction is given by the averaged token-wise score-function gradients, which reflect how to increase the likelihood of the sampled sequence under the model.

# Equal token weighting within a response under GSPO

A crucial consequence of the form above is that, inside each response $y_i$, GSPO places equal weight on the gradient contributions of every token. The term multiplying each token's gradient inside the inner sum is the same scalar: $\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}$. Thus the gradient contribution of token $t$ is

\[
\frac{1}{G}\,s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

which is identical (up to the token-specific $\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}$) for every $t$ in the same response.

There are two important intuitions behind equal token weighting. First, the reward used in GSPO is a sequence-level scalar $r(x,y_i)$: the verifier assigns credit to the whole response, not to individual tokens. Giving equal token weighting aligns the optimization unit with the reward unit, avoiding arbitrary redistribution of sequence credit among tokens. Second, by averaging token gradients, GSPO smooths token-level fluctuations: individual tokens that have noisy or unstable gradients do not disproportionately influence the update because the sequence-level scalar is shared across tokens. Practically, this reduces sensitivity to local token-level likelihood swings and stabilizes the direction of the parameter update.

GSPO also uses length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) intentionally. Without length normalization, the product of a few token likelihood changes could produce enormous swings in the sequence ratio for long or short responses, making the per-sequence scalar unstable and forcing clipping ranges to depend strongly on sequence length. Length normalization puts sequence ratios from different-length responses on a more unified numerical scale, further promoting stability and consistent equal weighting across responses.

# GRPO gradient structure: per-token ratios multiply per-token log-prob gradients

By contrast, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) applies importance correction and clipping at the token level. GRPO's objective (again ignoring clipping for brevity) can be written as

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\right],
\]

with token-level importance ratios

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Differentiating yields the GRPO gradient

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

Here each token $t$ in response $i$ is multiplied by its own token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Thus tokens within the same response can be weighted very differently in the final gradient update, because $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ depends on the relative likelihood change of that particular next-token prediction between the current and old policies.

This structural difference is essential: whereas GSPO uses one scalar $s_i(\theta)$ per response to scale the entire token-average gradient, GRPO scales each token gradient individually with its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. That makes the effective per-token learning rates heterogeneous and time-varying in a way tied to single-sample token likelihoods.

# Impact of unequal token weights on variance and training dynamics

Unequal token weights introduce several instability mechanisms that are visible from the GRPO gradient structure and from the principles of importance sampling discussed in the paper.

First, the token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ are computed from a single sampled token $y_{i,t}$ from the behavior distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Importance sampling theory relies on reweighting many samples to estimate an expectation under a target distribution using samples from a behavior distribution. Applying importance ratios computed from a single draw of each conditional next-token distribution fails to perform effective distribution correction and instead injects high-variance multiplicative noise into the gradient estimator. Each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is a noisy scalar (based on a single sample) and those noises multiply the token gradients before averaging; for long responses many such noisy multipliers accumulate.

Second, GRPO's clipping interacts with token-wise heterogeneity in a way that can amplify instability. Because clipping bounds are applied per token, when advantages are positive the effective token weights are constrained to the interval $(0,1+\varepsilon]$, whereas when advantages are negative they may fall in $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. These asymmetric effective ranges mean that tokens contributing negative advantage can receive arbitrarily large scaling, while positive-advantage tokens are heavily capped. Across many tokens and across many responses this imbalance can steer learning unpredictably and increase variance in updates.

Third, unequal token weights are particularly problematic in the presence of other sources of variability, such as changes to network structure in sparse models (Mixture-of-Experts) or numerical differences between inference and training engines. Because GRPO weights depend on per-token likelihoods, any sudden local likelihood fluctuation—whether caused by routing changes, numerical precision, or sampling noise—directly produces an uneven and potentially large reweighting of gradients. Over many updates and especially on long sequences, these effects accumulate and can drive the model away from stable behavior, which the paper observes as catastrophic collapse in some large-model RL runs.

In summary, per-token importance weights produce a gradient estimator with higher variance, asymmetrically clipped scaling, and greater sensitivity to local likelihood fluctuations, all of which degrade training stability and sample efficiency compared to a sequence-level weighting strategy.

# Long-sequence behavior: why GSPO is more robust to length and minibatching

Long sequences exacerbate the problems described above. In GRPO, the number of independent (noisy) token-level reweightings grows with sequence length. Even if each token-level multiplier has modest variance, summing or averaging many such terms leads to accumulation of variance in the gradient. The clipping mechanism does not prevent accumulation; instead, by limiting some ratios and allowing others to expand, clipping can introduce bias and create spurious gradient directions when applied per-token across long sequences. As sequences get longer, the probability that some token-level ratio experiences extreme fluctuation increases, making overall optimization less predictable.

Minibatching and the practical need to partition large rollout batches into multiple mini-batches create further off-policy settings: samples used to estimate gradients are drawn under an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while updates are applied to $\pi_\theta$. Importance sampling is intended to correct such distributional mismatch, but only when applied in a way that respects the sampling unit. The paper emphasizes the basic principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Because the reward in these RL setups is sequence-level, GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio is the appropriate estimator for correcting the off-policy discrepancy introduced by minibatching. Crucially, the sequence-level ratio is computed from the complete sampled sequence, not from single-sample conditional next-token draws. That accords with the importance-sampling requirement that effective reweighting requires averaging over the relevant distributional support.

GSPO also incorporates length normalization of the sequence ratio, which keeps $s_i(\theta)$ within a controlled numerical range across responses of different lengths. This avoids having clipping thresholds that must scale with sequence length and limits the impact of a few token likelihood changes producing dramatic sequence-ratio swings. Since the gradient under GSPO is

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

the averaging over tokens further reduces per-sample gradient variance for long sequences: random token-level noise tends to cancel out in the average. Thus, for long responses GSPO benefits from two stabilizing effects simultaneously: a single, length-normalized sequence-level importance weight that corrects off-policy sampling, and averaging of token gradients that reduces variance. Together these properties make GSPO more robust to long sequences and to the minibatching/off-policy realities of large-scale RL training.

Finally, in contexts such as Mixture-of-Experts models where token-level likelihoods can fluctuate due to expert-activation volatility, GSPO's sequence-level focus further reduces sensitivity to token-level routing changes. Because the model's overall language modeling capacity still yields reasonably consistent sequence likelihoods, $s_i(\theta)$ does not suffer the same abrupt changes that invalidate token-level ratios under GRPO. That is why GSPO empirically stabilizes RL training for large sparse models without auxiliary engineering workarounds required by token-level methods.

# Concluding remarks on gradient mechanics

From a gradient-mechanics viewpoint, GSPO replaces a heterogeneous, per-token multiplicative weighting scheme with a homogeneous, per-sequence scalar that multiplies an averaged token-gradient. This structural change aligns the unit of importance weighting with the unit of reward, reduces variance by averaging token gradients and normalizing for length, and removes sensitivity to single-token likelihood noise. GRPO's per-token reweighting, while superficially granular, violates core practical requirements of importance sampling in this setting and produces high-variance, asymmetric, and brittle gradient estimates—problems that are amplified with longer sequences, minibatching, and sparse model routing dynamics. Understanding these differences at the gradient level explains why GSPO provides more stable and efficient learning behavior in the large-scale RL scenarios studied in the paper.
