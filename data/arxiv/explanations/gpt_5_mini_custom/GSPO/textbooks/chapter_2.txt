\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 2: Mathematical Preliminaries and Notation

# Policies as autoregressive language models: \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) and tokenization
In this work we treat an autoregressive language model as a policy in the reinforcement learning sense. The policy is parameterized by \(\theta\) and written as \(\pi_\theta\). A single decision episode in this setting corresponds to producing a response \(y\) conditioned on a query \(x\). The response \(y\) is a sequence of tokens \((y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{|y|})\) where \(|y|\) denotes the token length of the response. We use the notation \(y_{<t}\) to denote the prefix of \(y\) before position \(t\), i.e., \(y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})\).

Because the model is autoregressive, it factorizes the distribution over full responses into a product of next-token conditionals. Concretely, the likelihood that policy \(\pi_\theta\) assigns to response \(y\) given query \(x\) is
\[
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr).
\]
Each factor \(\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\) is the model’s predicted probability for the next token at time \(t\). Sampling a response under \(\pi_\theta\) therefore proceeds by drawing \(y_1\) from \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\), then \(y_2\) from \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_1)\), and so on.

This factorization is the basis for both likelihood computation and for expressing importance weights. When we compare two policies (for example the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) and an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) used to generate samples), the comparison can be done either at the token level (comparing each conditional) or at the whole-sequence level (comparing the product of conditionals). The distinction between these two perspectives is central to the issues discussed in the paper.

# Sequence likelihood as product of token probabilities; log-likelihood and length normalization
The product form of the sequence likelihood is often numerically unstable for long sequences, so it is useful to work with log-likelihoods. Taking logs turns the product into a sum:
\[
\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \pi_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr).
\]
This additive form is numerically stable and makes gradient expressions and comparisons easier to analyze. When we compare two policies via their sequence likelihoods, it is common to form an importance ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}
\]
or, working in log-space,
\[
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}
=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}.
\]

Because responses can have different lengths, and because changes concentrated in a few tokens can cause a large multiplicative change in the full-sequence likelihood, the paper uses a length-normalized (geometric-mean) version of the sequence importance ratio. Define
\[
s(\theta; x,y)\equiv\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y|}}
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}\right).
\]
This length normalization has two practical purposes emphasized in the paper: (1) it reduces variance by averaging log-ratios across positions, and (2) it places importance ratios for responses of different lengths into a comparable numerical range so that a single clipping range can be used across lengths.

# Reward models/verifiers and bounded rewards in \([0,1]\)
In the setup considered in the paper, each query–response pair \((x,y)\) is scored by a verifier or reward model \(r\) that outputs a scalar reward. The reward function is written as \(r(x,y)\). For the algorithms discussed, rewards are normalized to a bounded range:
\[
r(x,y)\in[0,1].
\]
Because the reward is computed for the entire generated sequence, the basic learning signal is sequence-level. In group-based methods (described below), advantages are constructed by comparing the rewards of multiple responses to the same query; a common normalization is to subtract the group mean and divide by the group standard deviation:
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)}{\mathrm{std}\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)}.
\]
This standardization centers the advantages and scales them by observed variability in the group, which stabilizes learning when raw reward magnitudes vary across queries or over time.

# On-policy vs off-policy estimation
An important practical distinction is between on-policy and off-policy estimation. On-policy learning uses samples drawn from the same policy that is being optimized, i.e., samples \(y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\) when optimizing \(\pi_\theta\). Off-policy learning uses samples generated by a different (behavior) policy, typically an older snapshot \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\), and then attempts to use those samples to update the current policy \(\pi_\theta\).

In large-scale RL for language models, off-policy situations naturally arise. The paper explains that rollouts are often produced in large batches and then partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates; during this time the policy parameters move, so the data in later mini-batches are effectively generated by an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\). This mismatch between the distribution that generated the data and the current policy being optimized introduces bias if we ignore it. To correct for this distributional mismatch we rely on importance sampling techniques (next section), and in practice we additionally use clipping mechanisms to prevent extremely off-policy samples from exerting excessive influence.

# Importance sampling principle: correcting distribution mismatch
Importance sampling is the basic tool for using samples drawn from one distribution to estimate expectations under another distribution. If we wish to compute an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_\text{tar}\) but only have samples \(z\) drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_\text{beh}\), the identity is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]
=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]
The multiplicative weight \(\pi_\text{tar}(z)/\pi_\text{beh}(z)\) corrects for the change of sampling distribution.

A critical practical point emphasized in the paper is that importance sampling is effective only when averaging is performed over many samples from the behavior distribution (the paper notes this as relying on averaging over multiple samples \(N\gg 1\)). If the numerator and denominator in the importance weight are computed from very little data (for instance, a single sample from a conditional distribution), the weight will not reliably correct the mismatch and will instead introduce high-variance noise into gradient estimates.

This observation motivates a careful choice of the granularity at which importance weights are formed. In language-model RL, the reward is given to whole sequences, so a sequence-level importance weight \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)\) aligns naturally with the unit of reward and with the importance-sampling principle. By contrast, applying importance weights at the token level—where each weight is based on a single sampled token from the next-token conditional—fails to satisfy the averaging requirement and can produce high-variance corrections that destabilize training.

# Why clipping exists in practice (controlling off-policy drift)
While importance weights formally correct distributional mismatch, their raw use can produce very large variance and unstable updates in practice, especially when the current policy is far from the behavior policy. To mitigate this, algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) introduce a clipping mechanism that bounds how much an importance weight can scale the learning signal.

The paper presents the (token-level) PPO objective in the autoregressive setting as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,\ \mathrm{clip}\bigl(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_t\bigr)\right],
\]
where the token-level importance weight is
\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
\]
and \(\widehat{A}_t\) is an estimated advantage (often from a value model). The \(\min\) with a clipped weight prevents extremely large or small \(w_t(\theta)\) from magnifying the advantage, thereby keeping updates within a proximal region of the old policy.

Group-based algorithms for language models, like GRPO in the paper, adopt a similar token-level clipping but replace the value-function-based advantage with a group-relative advantage computed across \(G\) sampled responses to the same query. The GRPO objective is
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\right].
\]
Here all tokens in a response share the same group advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\).

Despite the stabilizing role of clipping, the paper argues that clipping at the token level does not resolve the deeper issue when the importance weights themselves are invalid or highly noisy—specifically, when token-level weights are computed from single sampled tokens and thus do not reflect a proper importance-sampling correction. To address this, the paper proposes shifting the unit of importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level. The GSPO objective clips sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratios:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\bigl( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\right],
\]
with
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]
Clipping at the sequence level excludes entire responses that are overly off-policy, which aligns the clipping unit with the unit of reward and with a theoretically meaningful importance weight for correcting the off-policy sampling distribution. The paper also notes that because the definitions of importance ratios differ between token-level and sequence-level formulations, the appropriate clipping ranges differ in scale; sequence-level ratios are length-normalized and therefore typically require much smaller numeric clipping ranges.

In summary, clipping is a practical tool to limit off-policy drift, but its effectiveness depends on forming importance ratios at the appropriate granularity and on normalizing those ratios so they behave well across response lengths. The paper motivates sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratios as a principled and empirically stable choice for large-scale RL of autoregressive language models.
