\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 11: The Clipping Fractions Paradox

This chapter explains an important empirical phenomenon reported in the paper: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) clips a vastly larger fraction of tokens than Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) — by about two orders of magnitude — yet nevertheless yields more efficient and more stable learning. We unpack why this counter-intuitive observation arises, grounding the explanation in the algorithmic definitions and gradient expressions in the paper, and we draw practical design implications for RL with large language models.

# Observed two-orders-of-magnitude difference in clipped-token fractions

In the experiments described in the paper, GSPO and GRPO use different definitions of importance ratios and apply clipping at different granularities. GRPO computes a per-token importance ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
and applies clipping at each token; GSPO instead computes a length-normalized sequence-level ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)
\]
and clips whole responses when $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside the allowed range. In the reported run we compare clipping ranges tuned separately for each method (GSPO left/right: $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ / $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$; GRPO left/right: $0.2$ / $0.27$). Empirically this leads to GSPO clipping a much larger fraction of tokens (roughly 100× more) than GRPO: because GSPO rejects entire responses when their sequence ratio is outside the tiny allowed window, every token in a rejected response is considered clipped, whereas GRPO can keep many tokens from the same response if their per-token ratios individually fall inside its window.

This large numerical disparity in clipped-token fraction is robust: varying clipping ranges did not eliminate the two-orders-of-magnitude gap in the reported experiments. Yet despite discarding many more tokens (and therefore using fewer token-level gradient contributions), GSPO achieved substantially better training efficiency and stability than GRPO.

# Why more aggressive sequence-level clipping can still improve efficiency

At first glance one would expect that using fewer tokens for gradient estimation should slow learning. The GSPO observation shows why that intuition can be misleading: what matters more is the quality and stability of the learning signal, not the raw count of token gradients.

GSPO’s clipping criterion operates on the same unit as the reward and the optimization objective: the whole sequence. The algorithm evaluates and clips samples based on the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, and it also computes advantages at the group/response level (group-normalized rewards). This alignment — unit of importance correction = unit of reward — ensures that the samples retained for optimization are those whose likelihood under the new policy is credibly close to their likelihood under the behavior policy in a sequence sense. Since the reward is also assigned to whole responses, sequence-level clipping prevents the optimizer from trying to amplify or suppress portions of a response based on noisy per-token corrections.

Two specific mechanisms explain why this yields better efficiency despite greater token rejection:

1. Variance reduction in the effective gradient. Sequence-level clipping removes entire responses whose overall likelihood shift is unreliable. The surviving responses provide gradients that carry coherent, low-variance signals about how the policy change affects the full-response reward. High-variance, inconsistent token-level signals (which can point in different directions and cancel or amplify pathologically) are excluded en masse, so each retained token contributes a stable, meaningful update.

2. Length-normalized sequence ratio controls scale. GSPO uses the length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ (raising the sequence ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$ or equivalently averaging log-ratios) so that responses of different lengths produce importance ratios on a comparable numerical scale. This prevents a few token likelihood changes from blowing up the sequence ratio and allows the clipping range to be small and meaningful. Small, consistent clipping windows further ensure that only near on-policy responses are used, trading token quantity for predictable, lower-noise updates.

In short, GSPO chooses to learn from a smaller set of high-quality, coherent sequence-level examples rather than attempting to exploit a larger set of noisy per-token signals. Empirically this produces faster and more stable improvement in the model and benchmarks, even though the raw number of token gradients used per update is lower.

# GRPO’s token-level gradients as inherently noisy signals

The theoretical and practical reasons why token-level importance ratios produce noisy gradients are central to understanding the paradox. The basic importance sampling identity requires re-weighting samples so that expectations under a target distribution can be estimated from samples drawn from a behavior distribution:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]
=\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
This identity presumes that the ratio $\pi_\text{tar}(z)/\pi_\text{beh}(z)$ is applied to functions of whole samples $z$ and that the behavior distribution is sampled sufficiently (many independent draws) for re-weighting to correct the distributional mismatch.

GRPO applies importance ratios at each time step using a single sampled next token from each conditional next-token distribution. In effect, the ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
is computed from a single draw from the distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. That single-sample-based token ratio does not serve the role that importance sampling requires; instead it injects high variance into the gradient estimator because each token ratio is an extremely noisy estimate of how the entire conditional distribution has changed. Over a long response these per-token noises accumulate.

This distinction becomes explicit when comparing gradients. Omitting clipping, GSPO’s gradient has the form (from the paper)
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
All tokens of a response are multiplied by the same sequence-level factor and then averaged, so tokens within a response contribute coherently.

By contrast, GRPO’s gradient (again without clipping) is
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
Here each token gradient is scaled by its own per-token ratio. These unequal weights vary across tokens and across updates, and their random fluctuations feed directly into the parameter updates. Clipping helps limit extreme ratios, but clipping itself interacts with the random per-token ratios and can amplify irregularities rather than remove them.

Two practical factors further magnify the noise of token-level ratios. First, responses can be long, so independent per-token noise accumulates across many terms. Second, in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models the set of activated experts for the same token can change after a gradient update — the paper reports on the order of 10% of experts differing for the same rollout sample after an update in a deep MoE model. Those routing fluctuations produce large changes in per-token likelihoods and therefore make per-token ratios highly volatile. GSPO’s sequence-level likelihood is much less sensitive to which particular experts are activated at each token because the overall language model capability tends to be preserved; therefore GSPO avoids this instability and the complex Routing Replay trick that GRPO requires for MoE convergence.

Taken together, token-level importance ratios are an inherently noisy and sometimes invalid corrective device in this setting. GRPO's per-token weighting creates noisy gradients that reduce sample effectiveness and can destabilize training.

# Design implication: quality of learning signal over quantity of tokens used

The paradox — many more tokens clipped yet better learning — highlights a clear design principle for RL with large language models: align the unit of importance correction with the unit of reward, and prefer low-variance, coherent learning signals even at the cost of fewer tokens.

Concretely, the paper’s results and analysis recommend the following practices (all derived from the paper’s algorithmic choices and empirical observations):

- Match the unit of importance sampling and clipping to the unit of reward. If rewards are sequence-level, use sequence-level importance ratios (e.g., $s_i(\theta)$) and sequence-level clipping. This prevents inconsistent token-wise corrections that do not correspond to the reward signal.

- Use length normalization of sequence ratios (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent or averaging of log-ratios) to keep ratios numerically stable across responses of different lengths. This makes small clipping windows meaningful and helps control variance.

- Favor group- or response-level advantage estimation (group-normalized rewards) so that the advantage signal is coherent with the sequence-level importance ratio used for weighting.

- Accept aggressive sequence-level clipping if it removes noisy, off-policy responses. The net effect can be more informative gradient updates and better efficiency, because information content per retained token is higher and parameter updates are more stable.

- For MoE models, prefer sequence-level correction to avoid the routing-related volatility that undermines per-token ratios and that otherwise forces complex engineering workarounds (e.g., Routing Replay). GSPO’s sequence focus obviates the need for such tricks while preserving model capacity.

Finally, the GSPO vs GRPO comparison illustrates a broad lesson that generalizes beyond the immediate algorithms: more data or more gradient terms is not necessarily better if those terms carry high variance, inconsistency with the reward, or are artifacts of sampling mismatches. In large-scale RL for language models, prioritizing the fidelity and coherence of the learning signal — by matching the estimator to the reward and by reducing variance through sequence-level treatment — can lead to faster, more stable improvement even when fewer raw token gradients are used.

Summary. The apparent paradox — GSPO clips far more tokens yet learns more efficiently — is resolved once we see that GRPO’s per-token re-weighting often produces high-variance, incoherent gradients that undermine learning, while GSPO’s sequence-level weighting and clipping produce fewer but far higher-quality updates. This quality-over-quantity trade-off is a useful guiding principle for algorithm design and for stable RL at large scale.
