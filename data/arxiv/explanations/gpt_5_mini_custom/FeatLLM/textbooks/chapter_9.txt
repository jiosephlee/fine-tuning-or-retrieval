\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 9: Experimental Setup

# Datasets
The empirical evaluation of FeatLLM uses a diverse collection of 13 tabular datasets chosen to represent a range of real-world applications, data sizes, feature types, and difficulty levels. Each dataset contains named attributes (column names) and short descriptions for those attributes, which FeatLLM uses as part of its prompt. The 13 datasets are:

- Adult: Predict whether an individual earns more than \$50,000 per year. This is a standard UCI-style census-income dataset commonly used in classification benchmarks.
- Bank: Predict whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit. This dataset captures marketing/campaign features and binary outcomes.
- Blood: Predict whether blood donors will return for subsequent donations. Typical donation-history and demographic attributes appear here.
- Car: Predict the quality or acceptability of a car. This is a classification dataset with categorical attributes describing the car.
- Communities: Predict crime rate in a region. This dataset is notable for having a very large number of attributes (over 100), making it challenging when prompt length or context window is limited.
- Credit-g: Predict whether an individual is a good or bad credit risk. This is another classical binary classification problem used in tabular learning evaluations.
- Diabetes: Predict the presence of diabetes (Pima Indians dataset variant). It contains clinical measurements and demographic features.
- Heart: Predict the occurrence of coronary artery disease. A clinical prediction dataset with medical features.
- Myocardial: Predict whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure. Like Communities, Myocardial is high-dimensional with over 100 attributes, which stresses prompt-size constraints.
- Cultivars: Predict grain yield of a soybean cultivar. This dataset and its attributes were added to the UCI repository recently (after September 2023).
- NHANES: Predict a person's age group from a health and nutrition record. This dataset was also added to UCI recently (after September 2023).
- Sequence-type: A synthetic dataset classifying a short sequence as one of four types (arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, Collatz). It is synthetically generated to avoid memorization by LLM pretraining.
- Solution-mix: A synthetic dataset predicting whether the percentage concentration of a mixture of four solutions exceeds 0.5. Again, synthetic to ensure it is not present in LLM pretraining.

Two important points about dataset selection and diversity are worth emphasizing. First, the inclusion of recently added UCI datasets (Cultivars and NHANES) helps control for the possibility that the LLM backbone memorized dataset-specific patterns during pretraining: because these datasets were not available before the LLM’s pretraining cutoff, their inclusion reduces leakage. Second, the synthetic datasets (Sequence-type and Solution-mix) explicitly prevent memorization from pretraining and test the model’s ability to extract rules and structure from raw values rather than from memorized examples. Finally, datasets such as Communities and Myocardial with very many attributes stress the limit of LLM prompt size and motivate the bagging and ensembling design choices used in FeatLLM.

# Dataset diversity: recent UCI additions and synthetic sets to avoid LLM memorization
A core concern when evaluating LLM-assisted methods is whether a model’s good performance stems from genuine reasoning on the task or from memorized training examples seen during LLM pretraining. To mitigate this confound, the experimental suite intentionally mixes classical UCI datasets with two types of datasets that reduce memorization risk.

First, the Cultivars and NHANES datasets were only added to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023. Because the LLM API used in the experiments (GPT-3.5) was pretrained on corpora collected before that date, these recent additions are unlikely to appear in the LLM’s training data; therefore, performance on them provides stronger evidence of the method’s ability to reason from feature descriptions and few-shot examples rather than regurgitate memorized labels.

Second, Sequence-type and Solution-mix are synthetic datasets created specifically for the evaluation. Synthetic construction means the exact mapping between inputs and target labels is unlikely to have been present in any pretraining corpus. These datasets thus form a controlled test of the framework’s capacity to induce algorithmic or arithmetic-like rules from very small sample sizes.

In combination, this selection strategy—classical benchmarks, recent additions, and synthetic tasks—gives a more robust picture of FeatLLM’s real generalization ability under low-shot conditions and reduces the risk that favorable results are driven by dataset memorization in the LLM.

# Baselines
To position FeatLLM against existing approaches, experiments compare it to nine baselines spanning classical machine learning, methods that leverage unlabeled data, pretraining-based approaches, and other LLM-centered techniques. These baselines are:

- Logistic Regression (LogReg): A linear classifier serving as a simple, interpretable baseline. It is trained with standard supervision and tuned via cross-validation.
- XGBoost: A strong tree-based gradient-boosting method widely used in tabular problems. It typically performs well across a range of datasets and is included as a competitive, non-LLM reference.
- RandomForest: An ensemble of decision trees that provides another classical tree-based benchmark known for robustness in tabular settings.
- SCARF: A semi-supervised method that requires access to (potentially large) unlabeled datasets and leverages them for learning better representations. The paper notes that obtaining such unlabeled data may be infeasible in some real-world settings.
- STUNT: Another method that uses unlabeled data to improve few-shot tabular learning. As with SCARF, the need for extra unlabeled resources is an operational consideration.
- TabPFN: An approach that pretrains a model on a wide mixture of synthetic distributions so it can perform fast inference on new small-tabular tasks without further training. It represents a strategy of encoding prior knowledge through pretraining over synthetic datasets.
- In-context learning (LLM-based): A baseline that serializes few-shot examples into text and feeds them into the LLM without further tuning; predictions are produced by the LLM at inference time for each sample.
- TABLET: An LLM-guided method that augments prompts with hints derived from an external classifier (rule-sets, prototypes) to improve the LLM’s in-context inference.
- TabLLM: A method that applies parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., IA3) or similar techniques to adapt an LLM to tabular tasks. Notably, TabLLM is restricted to LLMs for which public checkpoints exist; in the replication described in the paper, TabLLM uses the publicly available T0 backbone rather than GPT-3.5.

When comparing to these baselines, the experiments maintain fair settings: classical ML methods have their hyperparameters selected by grid search with $k$-fold cross-validation, and LLM-based baselines that rely on in-context prompting use the same underlying LLM (where allowed) so that differences reflect method design rather than model capacity. The paper also emphasizes that some baselines (SCARF, STUNT) assume access to unlabeled data, a resource that FeatLLM intentionally avoids depending on.

# LLM backbone and settings
FeatLLM is built to be model-agnostic in principle, but the experiments described in the paper use the GPT-3.5 family as the LLM backbone (the API variant cited is gpt-3.5-turbo-0613). The experimental prompt and inference settings used for the LLM are:

- Temperature: 0.5. This controls randomness in the LLM’s outputs: a moderate temperature encourages some diversity in the generated rules across repeated queries while keeping responses reasonably coherent.
- Top-p: the API default (which is 1 in the reported setup), meaning nucleus sampling does not further truncate the token distribution beyond the temperature.
- Rules per class: For every LLM query, FeatLLM requests a fixed number of generated rules per target class; the experiments use $R = 10$ rules per class. Fixing the number of rules controls prompt size and helps the downstream binary-feature dimensionality remain manageable.
- Number of ensembles (trials): $T = 20$. FeatLLM repeats the rule-generation + parsing + training pipeline $T$ times and ensembles the resulting models to improve robustness and to alleviate prompt-size issues via bagging (explained below).

Two additional LLM-related design choices that are important operationally are: (1) encouraging diversity across ensemble trials by setting a nonzero temperature and by randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations—since in-context learning can be sensitive to demonstration order—and (2) using the LLM itself to parse its natural-language rule outputs into executable code fragments for feature generation. Leveraging the LLM for parsing reduces brittle string-processing code and leverages the LLM’s fluency with code-generation, while keeping overall pipeline complexity low.

The paper also notes that FeatLLM was designed to be LLM-agnostic and reports that Appendix experiments examine varying backbones; the main reported results use GPT-3.5.

# Training details
Once FeatLLM has generated rules and parsed them into executable checks, each rule becomes a new binary feature indicating whether a sample satisfies the rule. Suppose there are $R$ rules per class and $c$ classes; for a sample $i$, the binary feature vector for class $k$ is $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$. FeatLLM uses a simple linear model (without explicit bias) to aggregate these rule-based features into class logits. For class $k$ and sample $i$ the logit is computed as

$ \text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,$

and the class probabilities are obtained by a softmax over logits:

$ \mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i, \dots, \text{logit}_c^i]).$

Here the elementwise $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ operation enforces non-negativity on the learned weights, which encodes the inductive bias that satisfying a rule should not reduce the likelihood of its associated class. The weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are the only parameters trained after rule generation.

Optimization and model-selection details reported in the paper are:

- Optimizer: Adam with learning rate $0.01$.
- Training epochs: 200 epochs per trial.
- Model-selection: $k$-fold cross-validation is used to choose the optimal number of epochs and to avoid overfitting on the very small training sets. The value of $k$ is chosen as either 2 or 4 so that each fold’s training split still contains at least one example of every class—an important practical constraint in low-shot regimes.
- Loss: cross-entropy loss over the class probabilities is minimized to learn the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$.
- Ensemble aggregation: after training $T$ independent trials, each producing parameters $\mathbf{w}[t]$, FeatLLM computes the predicted class probability for a sample by averaging the probabilities produced by the $T$ trained linear models.

Two additional implementation and fairness points used in replicating baselines and reporting runtimes are worth noting. First, In-context LLM baselines in the paper used the same GPT-3.5 backbone as FeatLLM for direct comparability, while TabLLM used T0 in line with its original design because TabLLM requires public model checkpoints for parameter-efficient tuning. Second, classical machine-learning baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest) had their hyperparameters tuned with grid search and $k$-fold cross-validation to provide competitive, fairly tuned baselines.

Finally, an efficiency note from the experiments: FeatLLM’s pipeline requires a modest number of LLM API calls during setup (the paper reports approximately 30 API queries for their configuration), after which inference for new samples uses only the lightweight trained linear model and does not call the LLM per-sample. This design yields much lower per-sample inference cost compared to end-to-end LLM-inference baselines that call the LLM at prediction time.
