\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Is training for 200 epochs on very few shots prone to overfitting?
Question:
You train the linear model for 200 epochs with Adam and choose the epoch via $k$-fold cross-validation on the few shots. With $k$ as small as 2, the validation split is tiny and high-variance. Did you consider early stopping with a patience heuristic, or using explicit regularization (e.g., $L_2$ or $L_1$) to stabilize learning? Any sensitivity analysis on epochs vs. AUROC with $k\in\{2,4\}$?
Answer:
Short answer: You’re right to be concerned—training any model for $200$ epochs on very few examples can overfit, and the paper does not report explicit early stopping or explicit L1/L2 regularization. The authors mitigate overfitting implicitly (small model, positive-weight constraint, cross-validation for epoch choice, bagging/ensembling), but they do not present a sensitivity curve of epochs vs AUROC for $k\in\{2,4\}$. I summarize what the paper does, why that helps (and where it can be fragile), and give practical diagnostics and remedies you can try.

What the paper actually does
The linear downstream model in FeatLLM is extremely small: it learns nonnegative weights (they apply $\max(w,0)$ in Eq. (1)) over binary rule-features, with no bias term. The paper trains with Adam ( $lr = 0.01$ ) for $200$ epochs but then “uses $k$-fold cross-validation for optimal epoch selection” where $k$ is set to $2$ or $4$ so that each fold still contains at least one example of every class. They also rely heavily on ensembling ($T\approx 20$ trials) and bagging to reduce variance and to average out poor rule sets. The hyper-parameter studies reported focus on number of ensembles and number of rules; the authors note performance saturates around $20$ ensembles and choose $10$ rules/class as a trade-off (they also mention that increasing rules can increase overfitting in low-shot regimes). However, the paper does not show an explicit epochs-vs-AUROC sensitivity plot for $k=2$ or $k=4$, nor does it report experiments with explicit L1/L2 regularization or early-stopping-with-patience.

Why these design choices partly mitigate overfitting (intuition + technical)
1) Very low-capacity model. A linear model on binary rule features has far fewer free parameters than deep nets; with $R$ rules per class and $c$ classes the parameter count is small, so the tendency to overfit is reduced relative to higher-capacity models.  
2) Nonnegativity projection. Forcing weights to be nonnegative reduces the parameter space and prevents the model from using negative weights to over-explain noise—this is a mild regularizer.  
3) Cross-validation for epoch selection. Selecting the epoch by CV is a form of early stopping (choosing the epoch that best generalizes on held-out folds). That said, when $k$ is $2$ the validation fold is tiny and high-variance, so the selected epoch can itself be noisy.  
4) Ensembling & bagging. Averaging $T$ independently-generated rule-sets and their downstream weights reduces variance: even if one trial overfits, aggregation can dampen its effect.

Where fragility remains
With $k=2$ the CV signal is noisy: a single mislabeled or atypical sample in the validation fold can push you to select an epoch that fits noise. Adam with a fairly large learning rate ($0.01$) and $200$ epochs can still find parameter settings that overfit even for a small linear model, especially if the number of rules (features) is not tiny relative to $k$. The paper itself notes that increasing the number of rules increases input dimensionality and may lead to overfitting in low-shot settings.

Practical suggestions / things I would try (concise)
1) Explicit early stopping with patience on validation AUROC instead of picking a single best epoch from $2$-fold CV. Use repeated CV (repeat the $k$-fold splitting several times) to reduce variance in the stopping signal.  
2) Add L2 (weight decay) or L1 regularization to the linear weight updates. L2 is a natural default; with very few samples a small weight decay (e.g., $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$) often stabilizes training. L1 can help sparsify rule weights and improve interpretability.  
3) Use smaller learning rate and/or switch to SGD with momentum for more stable convergence; Adam can sometimes overfit quickly on tiny data. Try $lr \in \{10^{-2},10^{-3},5\times10^{-4}\}$.  
4) Reduce the number of training epochs and combine with the early-stopping heuristic (e.g., max epochs $200$ but stop if no val-AUROC improvement after $10$--$20$ epochs). With high-variance CV include patience to avoid reacting to single-epoch spikes.  
5) Increase $k$ (if possible): when feasible use $k=4$ rather than $k=2$, or do repeated random splits and aggregate results to reduce the impact of any single small validation fold.  
6) Report sensitivity curves: plot AUROC vs epoch for multiple random splits and show mean ± std for $k=2$ and $k=4$. That directly answers whether performance is stable or oscillatory.  
7) Regularize per-feature: if many rules are generated, add a per-class L1 penalty to encourage sparse selection of only the most useful rules—this also mirrors the paper’s observation that tuning filters weak rules.  

Minimal experiments to run (to satisfy your question empirically)
Run the downstream training under these variants: (A) baseline (Adam $lr=0.01$, $200$ epochs, CV epoch selection as in paper), (B) baseline + early stopping with patience $10$ on AUROC, (C) baseline + L2 (weight decay $10^{-3}$), (D) baseline + LR=$10^{-3}$, (E) baseline + L1 ( $\lambda$ tuned). For each variant plot AUROC vs epoch averaged over $10$ random seeds with $k=2$ and $k=4$. That will show whether early stopping or explicit regularization stabilizes and improves generalization in the very low-shot regime.

Conclusion
The paper does rely on several implicit regularizers (small model, nonnegativity, CV epoch selection, ensemble bagging) which reduce but do not eliminate the risk you pointed out. The authors do not report explicit early-stopping-with-patience or L1/L2 regularization experiments, nor do they show epochs-vs-AUROC curves for $k\in\{2,4\}$. To be confident, run the small diagnostic experiments above—early stopping with patience and modest L2 are low-cost, high-impact mitigations for noisy few-shot CV.
