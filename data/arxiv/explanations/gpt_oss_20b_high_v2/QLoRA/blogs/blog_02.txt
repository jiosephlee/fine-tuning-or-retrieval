# Cracking the 4‑bit Code: NF<sub>4</sub> Quantization Explained  

Large language models need a lot of memory, but you don’t have to give up their size to fit them on consumer GPUs.  
Enter **QLoRA** – a method that keeps a gigantic model inside a single 48‑GB card by turning all its weights into a *four‑bit* representation while training only tiny *LoRA adapters*.  
At the heart of this breakthrough is **NormalFloat (𝑁𝐹₄)**, a four‑bit data type that is *information‑theoretically optimal* for normally distributed weights, plus a clever “double‑quantization” trick that slims memory even further without hurting performance.  

> Who says you need full precision? Thousands of models trained today use two to three times less GPU memory than before – all while matching or beating full‑precision baselines.

---

## Why Ordinary Float or Integer Quantization Fall Short

Quantizing weights means replacing high‑precision values (usually FP32 or BF16) with a small set of codes—e.g., eight int8 bins or sixteen float16 bins.  
The typical recipe is:

1. **Choose a large block** of parameters (say 64 elements)
2. **Find an overall scale** `c` that maps the block’s range into the chosen low‑precision space  
   \[
   X^{\text{Int8}}=\text{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{127}{\left|\!\!|X|\!\!\right|_{\max}}\,X\Bigr)
   \]
3. **Store** the tiny integer tensor and its scale `c`.

The problem?  
Because real LLM weights are *normally distributed* around zero (`~N(0,\sigma)`), most numbers cluster near the center while occasional outliers stretch far into the tails.  
A fixed uniform scale forces many tiny numbers into one bin (tiny bin loss) and leaves large values spread thinly across a few bins (large bin loss).  With too few bits you can’t serve both extremes well—so accuracy suffers when you finetune through those coarse buckets.



---  

## Meet NormalFloat (𝑁𝐹ₖ): Bits Designed for a Normal Distribution

Instead of imposing an arbitrary uniform grid, NormalFloat builds the bin boundaries around **exactly** how many samples fall in each segment if your data were drawn from a standard normal distribution `N(0,1)`.  

### Building a four‑bit NormalFloat

1. Pick `k = 4` → We need \(2^k = 16\) distinct codes.
2. Compute the theoretical \(2^k+1 = 17\) equi‑probable quantiles of `N(0,1)`:
   \[
   q_i=\frac{1}{2}\!\bigl(Q\!(i/17)+Q\!((i+1)/17)\bigr),\quad i=0,\dots ,15,
   \]
   where \(Q(\cdot)\) is the normal inverse CDF.
3. These `q_i` give us mids **but not zero**—the exact value `0` only exists once in an asymmetric set.
4. Merge positive and negative halves into an **asymmetric** range \([-1,1]\):  
   *10* negative codes, *6* positive codes, everything symmetrically spaced around zero—and ensure one explicit zero code exists so padding stays exact.

*Result:* each code represents exactly \(1/16\) probability mass under `N(0,1)`. That’s why it’s mathematically optimal – it reduces entropy loss per bit.



### When You Apply It

Before storing any weight block:

1. **Rescale** its absolute maximum so its values sit inside `[−1 , +1]`.  
   That is equivalent to “normalize \(\sigma\)” so that after rescaling every weight has exactly the same distribution shape as our NormalFloat design.
2. Quantize using the precomputed `q_i` lookup—no heavy per‑tensor statistic needed.
3. Store both integers (the four bits per weight) *and* a small float scale `c`.

During training / inference you immediately dequantize back into BF16 for arithmetic; adapters receive gradients directly through these dequantized blobs.



---  

## Why NF<sub>4</sub> Beats Regular Int<sub>4</sub> / FP<sub>4</sub>

- **Empirical evidence** from billions‑of‑parameter baselines (OPT/LLAMA/BLOOM/Pythia):
  * Zero‑shot perplexity drops by ~10–20% vs Int<sub>4</sub>.  
  * MMLU accuracy aligns perfectly with full‑precision fine‐tuned results when coupled with LoRA adapters.
- **Theoretical intuition:** All bins have equal expected counts for normally distributed weighting – fewer unused bins → lower effective noise per element.
- In practice it lets QLoRA recover full sixteen‑bit performance even when only adapters are updated—a huge win when you can’t afford to update the entire network.



---  

## Double Quantization – Two Layers of Congestion Relief

Every base block needs its own scaling constant `c₂`. Even if each constant is stored in full FP32, together they add roughly \(0.5\) bits per parameter just because of many blocks – ~3 GB extra on a 65 B model!  

Double Quantization brings another layer:

```
┌─ c₁ : FP32      ──┐
│                  │
└─ c₂ : FP8         │   ← second-level scalars
                  WeightBlock : NF₄
```

1. Encode each first‐level scalar `c₁` with normal scaling as before; store them as HF8 after subtracting their mean (mean centering because they're always positive).
2. Store these eight‑bit values along with small second‐level scalars (`c₂`) in every block’s metadata.
3. When you dequantize:
   * First convert dequantized float from `c₂`;
   * Then multiply by the dequantized weight `[NF₄]`.

***Result*** – average memory per parameter drops from ~0.5 bits to just ~~0.127 bits, i.e., **≈ 0½ bits save per param**, or ~3 GB saved on top of already seen reductions.



---  

## How QLoRA Uses NF<sub>4</sub> & Double Quantization

| Stage               | Operation                                                                                  | Data type          |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| Weight loading      | Dequantize stored NF<sub>4</sub> block + first/second scales                                 | BF16 (for compute) |
| Forward pass        | Matrix multiply + add LoRA update [\(XW + X L_1L_2\)]                                       | BF16               |
| Backward pass       | Gradients flow through dequantized base – only LoRA matrices receive updates                | BF16               |
| Optimizer state     | Stored via paged allocator → spills extra peak memory automatically                        | N/A                |

Because adapters are low‑rank (~[h→r→o] factors), they capture just enough extra capacity while keeping parameter count down to < 2% of the base size—yet they lift expressive power back up right where you cared most.

## Results That Prove It Works

| Model Scale | Baseline Finetune | QLoRA (𝑁𝐹₄) | Performance Gap |
|-------------|-------------------|--------------|----------------|
| LLaMA 7B   | Full BF16         | ≈ same       | < 0%           |
| LLaMA 33B – 65B | Full BF16 impossible (needs >780 GB) | ✅ matches     | ≈ 0%           |

Across every benchmark visited in the paper—GLUE, MMLU, Vicuna prompts—QLoRA with NF<sub>4</sub> achieved accuracy within one percentile point of fully trained rivals while never exceeding GPU limits that would otherwise rule out training at all.



---  

## Take Two & Three: Implications Beyond Memory Savings

* **Training speed** – Dequantizing during forward passes costs nothing apart from bandwidth; adapters still get concise gradients so updates remain fast (~10–15% slower than float32 due <10× smaller LR needs).  
* **Device reachability** – A consumer RTX 3080 or even smartphone GPU can now train models ≥33 B once you set up pagination tricks; publication proved “7B trains in <12h on one GPU”.
* **Security & privacy** – Because adapters contain all expertise learned during finetuning while base stays frozen & quantized offline, one can ship pretrained kernels separately from user data––a clean split between front end & privacy safe containers.



---  

## TL;DR

* Large language models’ weights follow an almost perfect Gaussian curve → classic bit‐packing loses excessive precision at tails.
* **NormalFloat (𝑁𝐹ₖ)** uses theoretical normal quantiles to allocate equal probability mass per bin → mathematically optimal for Gaussian data → little noise added when we shrink to four bits.
* Trivial extra step (**Double Quantization**) crams down pointer overhead from blockwise scaling down by ~¾ bit per param → saves several gigabytes worth of GPU mem without hurting throughput or quality.
* When paired with LoRA adapters we form **QLoRA**, which finetunes massive (~65B) models on single GPUs while preserving cutting‑edge inference quality nearly identical to full-fine-tuned BF16 versions.

So next time someone asks whether you can drop precision without breaking your chatbot's brainpower… say “Tell me more about NormalFloat!”
