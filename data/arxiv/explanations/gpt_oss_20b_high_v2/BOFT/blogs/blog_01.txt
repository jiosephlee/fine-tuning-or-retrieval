# Orthogonal Finetuning Unpacked – Rotating Neural Networks Without Overhauling Them  

Large foundation models such as GPT‑3 (175 B parameters), Vision‑Transformer‑XL (up to a few billion weights), or Stable Diffusion (600 M+ parameters) demand **billions** of dollars worth of GPU time when trained from scratch. In practice one often wants to *adapte* these frozen giants to new tasks—image classification on a small dataset, domain‑specific text generation, controlled image synthesis—without re‑running an enormous pretraining loop.  

One attractive idea is **Orthogonal Finetuning** (OFT): instead of tweaking every weight directly, you *rotate* all neurons belonging to a layer via a single learnable *orthonormal* matrix \(R\). Rotation preserves pairwise angles among neuron vectors so that most pre‑training knowledge remains untouched while giving the model freedom to specialize on downstream data.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Does “Orthogonal” Mean in Practice?

Consider one linear layer with frozen weights \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\).  
In ordinary fine‑tuning we would replace it by \(W_{\text{new}}\approx W^{0}+E\) where \(E\) has many learnable entries—usually thousands or millions per layer—and depends on how many layers must change.  

With OFT we instead learn **one extra** matrix \(R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) that is *orthogonal* (\(R^\top R = I_d\)) and compute  

\[
W_{\text{new}} = R\,W^{0}.  
\]

Because multiplication by an orthogonal matrix only rotates space,
the pairwise dot products between columns of \(W_{\text{new}}\)
are identical to those between columns of \(W^{0}\); consequently
the *angles* between neurons stay exactly the same throughout training.
This strict geometric regularity has two advantages seen in practice:
it keeps gradients well‑conditioned (spectral norm unchanged)
and leads to markedly better generalisation than methods that add low‑rank updates such as LoRA.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Bottleneck of Block‑Diagonal OG

OFT needs one new \(d\times d\) float tensor per layer—dangerously large when \(d\!>\!10\,000.\)

A common trick introduced earlier was **block‑diagonal OFT**:
\(R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_r)\)
with each small block \(R_i\in\mathbb R^{b\times b}\).
Now the number of free variables drops roughly proportionally
to the block size:  
\(O(b\,d)\) trainable numbers instead of \(O(d^2).\)

However this very sparsity forces neurons belonging
to different blocks never communicate through \(R.\)
The structure imposes an artificial grouping scheme,
clobbers expressive capacity,
and even fails at reproducing classic transforms
such as discrete Fourier or Hadamard transforms.
To fully realise every possible rotation,
one needs either very many tiny blocks (\(b=4,\!8,\dots\))
or a huge number (\(r=d/b\))—both undo much part of its gains.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Funny Idea – Turning An Orthogonal Matrix Into Edges

A dense linear operator can be visualised as a graph:
each input node sends a message into every output node.
If we want **cheap** graphs that are nevertheless enough
to reach all outputs,
one possibility is *factorising* the dense matrix into several sparse matrices.
Think about travelling through layers:
multiply first by \(B_1,\) then by \(B_2,\dots,B_m.\)
Each omission (zero entry) cuts off one possible edge.
But if every output can still receive messages coming from every input after all rounds,
the overall product becomes dense even though intermediate operators are sparse.


This view gives us two organising principles  

| Requirement | What it means |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Dense connectivity** | Every source node has *at least one path* through all stages |
| **Least free edges** | Only commit real parameters for “meaningful” edges |

The swing here is that **orthogonality is a heavier constraint** than plain sparsity—
each level must form an invertible bijection.
The trick then becomes searching over sparse graphs that still satisfy this property.

## Enter Butterflies – A Cake‑Cutting Network Topology

Engineers are familiar with butterflies because your favourite FFT algorithm looks exactly like it.
Let us insert the same idea into our factorisation problem:

*Let \(k=2,\dots , d!\)*  
Define a *butterfly factor*  

\[
B^F(k)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \operatorname{diag}(d_1)& \operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\
        \operatorname{diag}(d_3)& \operatorname{diag}(d_4)
    \end{bmatrix}
    ,\qquad k=|\!Tw|
    .
\]

Putting these together recursively yields an overall **butterfly matrix**

\[
B(d)= 
    B_{\leftarrow d,d}\;
    B_{\leftarrow d,d/2}\;
     \cdots 
    B_{\leftarrow d,\,2} ,
      \
     ,
     ;
      .
     .
     .
   ,
         
]


Each stage swaps halves and mixes pairs,
exactly how Cooley–Tukey splits worklists into “butterfly” sub‑ups.
The power lies in three facts:

| Fact | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| **Sparsity** | Every factor contains only ⌊\(k/2)+k/2⌋ non‑zero diagonals ⇒ only ⌈\(k)/?? JText?? ⌋ ~k ops |
| **Dense overall coverage** | A product of ⌈log₂ d⌉ such factors connects every input node whitespacefully |
| **Ease of Orthonormalisation** | Each two‑by‑two diagonal sub‑block can simply be parameterised as a rotation —
a Cayley transform guarantees that parameter counts equal those needed for any generic rotation |

In short, with **just ~log₂ d levels**, every dimension mixes through all other dimensions—
while keeping the total count down at ≈ \(O(d\, log₂ d)\).

## SOPHISTICATED BUT SPECIALIZED FINETUNING – BOFT

> **BOFT** stands for **Orthogonal Butterfly Fine–tune**, 
> essentially OFT where the single global rotation comes from *many*
> tiny sparse rotations stacked together into a dragon scale that still stays solidly “orthonormal”.

### How Does It Work?

Let’s keep notation compact.
We fix `block_size = b` (a power of two).  
For layer dimension `d = 2ⁿ`

```
             ──> FIRST FACTOR            ──> … ──> LAST FACTOR
         [level 0 → level 1] ──► [level i → level i+1] …┴─►  
```

Each factor `~𝐁ᵇᵢ` itself is made of many `b×b` rotations arranged diagonally across all `N=b` subspaces.
Collectively,

```
           R(m,b)=~𝐁ᵇ₁ · ~𝐁ᵇ₂ ·· …·~𝐁ᵇₘ             # ⋆ m → number_of_layers_in_chain
```

Multiplying this `R` on top of your frozen weight yields your forward path   
(`z = ((R ⋅ W⁰)^⊤⋅x)`).

The whole construction boils down to training   

```math
parameters =
∑_{each ~𝐁ᵇ}   #trainable entries per sparse rotation 
        = ½(b−1)d · m                                    # full formula given in original work
```

Some special cases illustrate all extremes:

| When…   | Reduction achieved |
|---------|--------------------|
| `m=1`   | Exactly the old *block‐diagonal OFT* (`R` sampled from `Diag(R₁,…,R_r)` ) |
| `(b=d,m=log₂ d)` | Full unstructured OT (`R` unconstrained dense ) |
| `(b=2,m=log₂ d)` | BOFT’s sweet spot – tightest knob set (~log²ⁿ?) |
Note how letting `m` increase always tightens expressiveness until you become truly unconstrained—but you never step back below your limit ∼\(O(d\,log d)\).

### Identity Initialization Explained  

As before you do *not* want your local rotation drifting far away from identity right at start.
Simply place every skew‐symmetric input inside each Cayley transform at zero—thus yielding identical rows and columns until gradients turn them.


### Multiplicative Dropout Variant  

Add regularisation compatible with multiplicative updates:
pick randomly some factors (`p₁%`) or some inner blocks (`p₂%`)
and temporarily "*nullify*" them back into identity during training loops.
After convergence you glue everything together into one big dense rotation –
no inference penalty afterwards.

## Why Butterflies Beat Straight UFOL AND BLOCKS
 
### Expressivity Advantage  

A single liberation factor (\(b<γ√…), {‘bang’}) essentially simulates richer linear maps than independent small blocks genetically linked through xor–like permutations.
Mathematically proven:
the class realised by Butterfly‐products subsumes that realised strictly by any fixed choice `block_size=b`.  
A larger group (`m>log₂ b`) always beats its smaller sibling saving *orders* fewer concerns.


### Retainment Of Spectral Norm  

Both OFT and BOFT multiply weight matrices solely by left side rotations.
Suppose original matrices decompose SVD-wise:
\[ W^0 = UΣV^\top . \]
After finetune our affine map becomes  
\[ WRV^\topΣ.\]
Because `U ∘ R` remains unitary,
the singular values never shift—consequently spectral radius stays stable and so does gradient scale—a trait empirically linked with faster convergence.



### Inductive Bias + Classic Transforms  

Butterfly topologies mirror several well-known transforms —
fast Fourier transform (FFT),
Hadamard transform,
discrete cosine transform …
These families balance uniform global mixing against local sparseness –
exactly what stable adaptation tasks often exploit.
Because BOFT’s hypothesis class lives near these familiar shapes,
it tends automatically toward operations benefiting generalisation more than arbitrary huge dense rotations would.

## Experiments ­– One Algorithm beats several Families


Despite being largely “conceptual” additions,
BOFT was put through its paces across three wildly different arenas:
*Vision*: Large ViTs fine–tuned for classification & segmentation  
*NLP*: BiLMs / GPT derivatives classifying legal facts vs general text  
*Generative*: Stable Diffusion controlled generating artic features and colours 
And _every_ benchmark showed sizeable improvement over competing PEFT tricks such as LoRA or BITFIT—including surpassing our own tightly constrained tools like vanilla Block‑Diagonal OFT—while touching almost imperceptibly few extra trainable weights (~a few dozen kilobytes).

A few illustrative bite tests (*details & hyperparameters omitted for clarity*) :

- ViT–ViTO comparably outperformed LoRA+with < 200 k additional params on ImageNet downstream tasks;
- GPT‐mini adapted better on domain classification when adding only < 30 k extra values versus >200 k used by LoRA;
- Stable Diffusion controls remained both deterministic (**spectral stability**) and vibrant after navigating merely ~80× less learnable entries than ordinary fine‑tuning.

While BOFT offers impressive improvement,
