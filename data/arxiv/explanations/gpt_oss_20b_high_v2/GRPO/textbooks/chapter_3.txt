\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: # Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning demands data that is dense with formal expressions,
rigorous deductions, and a wide variety of problem settings – plain web
pages rarely supply this richness at scale without careful filtering and
curation.  The DeepSeek team addressed this challenge by mining public
Common Crawl archives for mathematics‑heavy text through an iterative
pipeline incorporating supervised classification,
deduplication, domain‑guided enrichment,
benchmark decontamination,
and a focused multilingual analysis.  
The resulting “DeepSeekMark” corpus amounts to roughly **120 billion**
tokens extracted from **35 million** distinct webpages,
outstripping existing open‑source mathematical corpora by an order of
magnitude while preserving linguistic breadth beyond English alone.



---

## Seed corpus selection using OpenWebMath  

The starting point was **OpenWebMath**, an already-proven collection of
high‑quality mathematical web texts curated from subject forums,
tutorial sites and teaching portals.\footnote{\href{https://openwebmath.org}{openwebmath.org}}  
Its dense coverage spans algebraic manipulation, calculus proofs,
combinatorial arguments and more – exactly the sort of language patterns
that guide downstream models toward coherent deduction steps when prompted 
with chain‑of‑thought or program‑of‑thought formats.\par

Using OpenWebMath serves two purposes:

1. **Positive exemplars**– it supplies well‑structured sentences that
encourage a lightweight learner (such as FastText) to build internal vectors
aligned with equations (\(x^2+4xy+y^2=0\)), roster expansions (\(\sum_{i=1}^n i = n(n+1)/2\)), born‑by‑hook grammar (“for every \(x\)”), etc.;  
2. **Recall bridge**– it anchors our subsequent retrieval processes 
so that scarce mathematics signals become discoverable amid billions 
of unrelated webpages.\par



## FastText classifier training workflow  

Once positives were known, Unsurprisingly,
the task reduces to scanning billions of english‐origin webpages,
classifying each candidate page independently as *mathematics* or *not*.  
The authors employed **FastText**, an efficient supervised shallow‐network framework\footnote{\href{https://fasttext.cc}{fasttext.cc}}
based on linear classifiers over character/word n‑grams.\par

### Training set construction  
* **Positive examples** – Randomly select \$500\,000\$ snapshots from
OpenWebMath’s body text.\par  
* **Negative examples** – Randomly sample an equal number (\$500\,000\$) 
from raw Common Crawl dumps where no mathematics tag exists.\par

### Hyperparameters  
| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Vector dimension | \$256\$ |
| Learning rate | \$0.1\$ |
| Max word \(n\)-gram | \$3\$ |
| Min word occurrence | \$3\$ |
| Epochs | \$3\$ |

The resulting model learns compact word representations capturing
the frequent symbolic patterns (“$\frac{}{}$”, “$\forall$”) that denote
mathematical prose while ignoring purely natural‐language noise.



## Deduplication strategies applied to Common Crawl  

Common Crawl’s raw pool contains paired URLs covering near‑identical or repeating content – frivolity that would bias learning toward certain media forms.
The authors therefore applied two complementary dedupification stages:\par

1. **URL‑based hashing** – Normalize all URLs by stripping query parameters,
base domain paths are hashed into buckets so duplicate stories (e.g.,
multiple mirrors) collapse into single instances.\par  
2. **Near‑deduplication over page snapshots** – Apply cosine similarity over shingled TF–IDF vectors derived 
from each page’s surface tokens;
pairs exceeding a similarity threshold get merged,\par 

After pruning duplicates across roughly \(4\times10^{10}\) HTML records,
the remaining corpus contained approximately **40 billions** distinct documents,
which served as input population for subsequent FastText-based screening.



## Iterative enrichment loop via manual annotation  

The first FastText round yielded substantial new mathematical pages (\~20 % hit rate among candidates).
However visual inspection revealed systematic gaps –
certain specialized forums (e.g., *mathoverflow.net*) remained largely absent  
because their phrasing deviated slightly from OpenWebMath’s canonical “style”.
To address this limitation a concise differential loop was introduced.



### Domain assessment  
Each remaining page was grouped by its root domain “example.com”.  
If > 10 % of a domain’s seeds were already identified in Physical Computer's positive set,
the entire domain was flagged *potentially maths*.  
Domains such as `mathoverflow.net`, `planetmath.org`, 
`arxiv.org/abs`, etc.’s *question* directories gained attention.



### Manual URL curation  
Human curators examined sliding windows over frequently chosen domains,
labeling specific question feeds (`questions?id=`…) whose textual payloads clearly carried problem statements.\footnote{
Example URL panels inspected: https://mathoverflow.net/questions/222221 ,
https://greynote.com/math/linear-algebra/}



### Re-training step  
All newly annotated passages joined the positive tally;
FastText was re-trained exactly following the previous recipe,
after which it recalled far more nuanced tags—
for example hyperbolic geometry posts now scored high enough
to survive filtering even though their lexical makeup shifted away from verbatim OpenWebMath entries.



Repeating this enrichment cycle four times amassed roughly ***35 million***
unique mathematics webpages totaling about ***120 billion*** tokens—
approximately seven times larger than Minerva’s closest scrapes 
and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.



## Benchmark decontamination filtering rules  

Because evaluation sets (GSM8K, MATH‐torchling family) often appeared verbatim on public sites,

a strict **deterministic leaching** rubric was imposed:\par

* For every extracted webpage segment:
    * If any contiguous ten‑gram substring exactly matched any excerpt appearing anywhere inside one of the adults’ test prompts (**GSM8K**, **MATH**, **CMATH**, **AGIEval**),
      the entire segment was discarded;\par  
    * For snippets whose maximal length < 10 words yet comprised ≥ 3 grams found within test sets,
      exact match discard was also triggered.\par

This conservative pass guarantees none of the model’s knowledge during pre‑training accounted 
for future evaluation leakage—a critical condition given seeds were automatically harvested 
from internet contents likely overlapping existing datasets.
