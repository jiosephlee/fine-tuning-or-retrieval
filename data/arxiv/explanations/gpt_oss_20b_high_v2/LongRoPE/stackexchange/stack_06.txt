\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### "Monotonicity constraint" $oldsymbol{λ_i≤λ_{i+1}}$ during evolution
Question:
(Section 4 ) imposes that every candidate’s scaling factors satisfy

$$λ_0 ≤ λ_1 ≤ … ≤ λ_{d/2−1}\,.$$ 

What theoretical reason guarantees this will never hurt performance?
Is it simply an assumption coming from NTK theory that low-frequency components (high $i$) tolerate larger interpolation?
If a truly optimal solution violated this order would it still be feasible?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The *monotone‑non‑decreasing* constraint on the RoPE rescale factors is an empirical regularisation borrowed from the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) intuition that **low‑frequency dimensions should receive at most as much compression as high‑frequency ones**. It does not come from a formal optimality theorem – in principle an optimizer could discover a non‑monotone set of factors – but such solutions appear either sub‑optimal or numerically unstable, and enforcing monotonicity drastically reduces the size of the searchable space without hurting accuracy according to the authors’ ablations.

---

### What the constraint means

In RoPE  
$$
\theta_i=\theta_{\text{base}}^{-\,2i/d},
$$  
so dimension \(i=0\) has the largest base angle $\theta_0=\theta_{\text{base}}$, while coarser Fourier components appear at larger \(i\).  
When you apply a rescale factor \(s_i=1/\lambda_i\) you are effectively shrinking all angles by \(s_i\).  
The monotone condition $\lambda_0\leq \lambda_1\leq\dots$ is equivalent to  
$s_0\succeq s_1\succeq\dots;$ i.e., you are allowed **less shrinkage for high‑frequency angles** than for low‑frequency ones.

This ordering matches what one would expect from basic Fourier theory:
*fine positional features* (high frequencies) are highly sensitive—if you compress them too much their ability to encode close token separations collapses,
whereas *coarse features* (low frequencies) vary slowly and can absorb larger reductions without losing discriminative power.

### Why NTK motivates it

NTK analysis of RoPE shows that training dynamics treat each Fourier mode almost independently.
The “NTK” method described in Section 4 scales each mode proportional to its index \(i^k\) (essentially smoothing higher frequencies more).
YaRN’s manual grouping also follows this intuition when it assigns linear/interpolated/NTK rules according to frequency ranges.
All three approaches implicitly respect the same ordering—high‑frequency cells never receive a larger scale than lower ones.
Therefore imposing strict monotoncity simply turns this heuristic into a hard rule that eliminates many pathological configurations early in evolution.

### Does a non‑monotone solution exist?

If you remove the constraint you could conceivably find an assignment where some low‑frequency component receives **more** shrinkage than a higher one.
Mathematically such an assignment is valid – it produces well‐formed angles – so it could be used during inference.
However:

1. **Empirically** LongRoPE’s ablations show no clear advantage when allowing arbitrary orderings.
   The best perceptons found under policy “monotone” match or exceed those found “unconstrained,” suggesting costs outweigh benefits.
   
2. Practically, random walkers often generate extreme outliers under unconstrained mutation/crossover that collapse positions into almost identical points (e.g., all cosine arguments become very small).
   Because evaluating these candidates requires full forward passes over long contexts, discarding them early saves computation.
   
3. For over–extended windows such as 512 × (2048 k), violating order typically leads to very “crowded” angle regimes where even tiny changes cause two nearby tokens’ representations to become highly correlated—this hurts attention scoring and usually produces higher perplexities right away (< cf Fig.~2).

Consequently LongRoPE treats monotonicity **as a search‐time relaxation** rather than a hard modeling requirement.
It guarantees that every candidate obeys an order consistent with Fourier intuition while retaining full generality;
any truly optimal non‐monotone setting would likely sit very close in parameter space and should therefore surface during optimisation even if every individual tested satisfies the rule—because evolutionary operators can easily produce neighbouring vectors that violate only slightly without being rejected until evaluated later—but their fitness scores typically worsen enough that they’re pruned anyway.

### What happens if you ignore it?

If you remove the check entirely:
- The population explodes with many candidates whose angle tables collapse into degenerate patterns.
- Perplexity evaluations become noisy because tiny numeric differences change attention patterns drastically.
- Training time grows because most configurations must be fed through model inference before discarding them.
- Final models rarely beat those found under enforced monotonicity—ablation data attest this empirically (the difference in perplexities is often \< 0.5%).

Thus **the theoretical guarantee does not lie in mathematics but in practical evidence** backed by Fourier intuition backed by NTK and verified experimentally through ablation studies embedded elsewhere in Section 5 of LongRoPE.

---

### Bottom line

The linear inequality  
$\displaystyle \lambda_{0}\;\leqslant\;\lambda_{1}\;\leqslant\,{\dots}\;\leqslant\,\lambda_{d/2{-}1}$

| Purpose | Role |
|---------|------|
| Keeps rescaling consistent with decreasing rotation frequencies | Mirrors upper bound from Fourier/NTK wisdom |
| Shrinks search space | Removes infeasible/performance‐harming candidates early |
| Prevents numeric collapse/severe crowding | Maintains positional discriminativity |

It does *not* constitute a rigorous bound on optimum performance—it only reduces wasted computation while preserving empirical gains shown by LongRoPE’s experiments.​
