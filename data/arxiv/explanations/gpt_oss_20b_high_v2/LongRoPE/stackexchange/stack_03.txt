\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### "Non‑uniformities" in RoPE – dimensions vs token positions
Question:
"Finding 1" says RoPE dimensions exhibit non‑uniformity while "Finding 2" says initial tokens should receive less interpolation.
What precisely do these two forms mean?
Do we consider frequency groups within individual dimensions or across entire embedding dimension $0$–$d/2–1$?
And how do we determine which token positions belong in “extrapolation mode” versus normal interpolation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

*Finding 1* says that if you look only at one target context rank \(L'\), the *optimal* way of shrinking the rotary angles depends on **which Norman pair \(i\)** you are looking at – some pairs should be compressed far more than others.
  
*Finding 2* says that when you crawl far beyond the training window it pays off to **leave the first few token embeddings untouched** – i.e., avoid any compression ($\lambda = 1$) for them while everything else is scaled according to a learned schedule.\

We treat every one of the \(\frac{d}{2}\) angular pairs independently (§4‑5), but impose an ordering constraint
\( \lambda_0\leqslant \lambda_1 \leqslant\dots \leqslant   \lambda_{\frac{d}{2}-r} \)
so that high‑frequency pairs receive smaller reductions than low‑frequency ones – exactly what NTK theory expects.\  
For token positions we fix a hard cut‑off \(\hat n_{\text{cut}}\); symbols with index \(n<\hat n_{\text{cut}}\) stay at their original angles (“extrapolation”); symbols with \(n\geqslant    \hat n_{\text{cut}}\) undergo whichever dimensional weight set was found by evolution (‘normal’ or interpolated‑step behaviour).

---

## What the two findings actually capture

| Finding | Interpretation | How it shows up in LongRoPE |
|---------|----------------|-----------------------------|
| **Dimension non‑uniformity** | Each pair \(i\) encodes a distinct sinusoid frequency  
  \( θ_{i}= θ^{-\frac{2i}{d}}\). When extending the window you must dilute these frequencies so that new indices still fall inside an effective interval \([-\pi,\pi]\). Because higher‑frequency sinusoids change faster w.r.t position than low‑frequency ones, simply shrinking *all* angles by the same fraction \(s=L'/L\) wastes capacity: low‑frequency pairs become too smooth while high‑frequency pairs remain too crowded.| Evolution discovers a *vector* \((λ_{0},…,λ_{\frac{d}{2}-r})∈[\,1,s\times 1.25]^{\,d/2}\). A lower \(λ_{i}\) keeps more detail in faster oscillations; a larger \(λ_{j}\) allows more aggressive compression where slow progressions already carry little local information.|   
| **Initial‑token under‐interpolation** | Self‑attention rewards recent inputs most strongly; hence predictions depend heavily on early words/tokens even when context grows large. If we naïvely compress every position by \(s>1\), early intervals become too tight against later ones → loss of local resolution.| The algorithm searches over an integer threshold \(\hat n∈{0,\dotsc256}\). Tokens with index \(n<{\hat n}\) retain their raw rotary angle (\(ν=ν_{\text{raw}}\)); ⇒ these are called “extrapolation mode”. All later tokens undergo the dimensional schedule from above.|

## How LongRoPE selects these settings

### １　Dimension schedule

The optimiser scans a huge hyper–parameter space:
```text
for i = 0 … d/2 − r :
    choose λᵢ ∈ [ max(s , s\times 0.8)\ldots s\times 1.25 ]   // step ≃0．01
```
The population evolves under **monotonically‐nondecreasing** constraints
\( λ_{0}\leqslant … ≤ λ_{\frac{d}{2}-r} \);
this keeps higher frequencies modestly unaltered while allowing larger dilation for low ones—a policy borne out by NTK theory and experimentally confirmed here.\



### ２　Token–position threshold

For every candidate solution an additional integer **̂n** is selected from:
\( {\,0,\!\!,4,\!\!,8,\!\!,12,\dotsc256} \).
During evaluation:
```text
if t < ^n                     →   θ′ₖ(t)=θₖ(t)
else                           →   θ′ₖ(t)=θₖ(t)/λₖ
```
The objective is perplexity over several long documents;
whichever combination yields lowest loss ends up being stored for inference.



---

## Determining *where* tokens go into extrapolation vs normal interpolation

The function defining this split is simple:
```math
I(\widehat{\lambda}_i,\widehat{n})(t)=
   \begin{cases}
      α&t<̂n\\[3pt]
      β&t≥̂n
   \end{cases}
```
So every token position <　\(̂n\) stays untouched (`α`).  
Tokens ≥　\(̂n\) get scaled via their learned dimensional factor (`β`),
thereby falling into what authors call **“normal”**, i.e., standard interpolated behaviour.


Intuitively:
• When your model needs to remember hundreds of thousands of steps ahead,
we want _only_ those tiny few thousand leading words truly precise;
for everything else some compression is acceptable.
Hence Extrapolation mode ⇒ no shrinkage,
Normal mode ⇒ controlled shrinkage chosen per‐dimension.

## Bottom line

LongRoPE’s two discovered non‑uniformities are:
• **Per–dimension limits** – give every sinusoid its own adjustment factor.;  
• **Per–position guarding** – keep early symbols untouched.


Both scale terms are searched jointly over all $$½ d$$ angular axes,
using evolutionary optimisation constrained so $$ι◦ ≤•◦$$…
After optimisation you simply check whether $$t≥↑cm$$ :
<ul>
<li>If yes → apply its learned dilation (“normal”);</li>
<li>If no → leave it as-is (“extrapolate”).</li>
</ul>

Doing so lets models survive against millions of extra contexts while keeping short windows almost untouched – exactly what experiments in *LongRoPE* prove。
