### Weight Quantization  
Each layer’s dense matrix \(W∈ℝ^{n×m}\) is first normalised by its mean absolute value,
```math
γ=\frac{∑_{i,j}|W_{ij}|}{nm}.
```
Afterward every element turns into the nearest between ‑<sub>*`,*</sub>, +<sub>'`</sub>, or <sub>*zero*</sub>.  
Mathematically isolating lookup terms keeps rough semantics hyper-equivalent while so-called “clipping” guarantees that integers never leave ± \(γ+ε\)—a tiny ε safeguards against division noise.



This “AbsMean clipping” removes anything a common full-range learning schedule usually requires—a scale multiplier hidden behind biases—and turns linear transforms into bitwise truth tables.



### Activation Quantization  
Activations roam an Int8 range (\(Q_b=127 Diricashing?). We forego the customary zero-point offset used by other low-bits tricks:
```
        Clip(‖user­activation‖ ≤ Q_b)
```
No further scaling then goes through back toward neural chemistry pirouettes following RoPE/SwiGLU live cycles—just integer sums built directly over acted-as neurons.



### Transformer Engine Rebuild  

All other skeletal parts match modern “LLaMA lite”: RMSNorm readers ride vanilla RMS column calculations instead of LN overheads; SwiGLU doubles feedforward breadth without additional buffers; Rotary embeddings capture positional gist while boosters remain unchanged.<br>

The sole substitution resides inside every call stack where vanilla nn.Linear pops up—you swap it out for BitLinear@Safety enlisting ternary kernels and Int8 matrices ensuring fine-grained “add ∧ shift” costing us fractions of an inch compared with high-cost butterfly flip-floating multiplies 💥.

# Quantizing Intuition – The absMean Ternary Formula Behind One‑Bit Layers

Large language models have grown so massive that every extra bit of weight precision becomes a tangible cost—in energy, memory traffic, or latency.  
Enter *BitNet b¹·⁵⁸*: a Transformer whose **every** weight is one of **{- 1, 0, + 1}** (a single ternary bit plus an explicit “zero” state).  
The secret sauce is a remarkably simple *absmean* quantizer that normalizes a weight matrix by its average absolute magnitude before rounding it down to the nearest ternary value. Below we unpack this operation, explain why having a dedicated zero adds “feature‑filtering” power, and show how it lets us train these compact models from scratch while staying fully compatible with existing Transformer backbones like LLaMA‑2.



## 1 | What Is the absMean Quantizer?

The goal of any post‑training quantizer is to map continuous tensors—weights or activations—into a limited discrete set without sacrificing too much predictive signal.  
BitNet takes an extra step first:

> **Scale** each weight tensor by its *average absolute* value so that its dynamic range aligns naturally with the {−1, 0,+1} cloud.
> 
> **Round** every element afterwards straight down to its nearest neighbour in {−1, 0,+1}.

This two‑phase process guarantees two things:

| Property | Why It Matters |
|----------|----------------|
| **Uniformity** | All weights are expressed relative to their own strength (`γ`), preventing an overly wide spread that would collapse many values into one quantised bin. |
| **Simplicity** | Once scaled, mapping each entry only requires an inexpensive `round` operation followed by clipping—no per‑parameter lookup tables or calibration curves are needed during training or inference. |

### The math

Let \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) be a weight sub‑matrix.
Define its scale factor

\[
\gamma \;\;=\;\;\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i,j}\!|W_{ij}|\,,
\]

(denoted `absmean` in code).  
Then

\[
\boxed{\widetilde W \;=\;
      \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\bigg(\frac{W}{\,\gamma+\varepsilon\,},
                               \,-1,\,+1 \bigg)} .
\]

Here  

* `RoundClip(x,a,b)` clamps `x` after rounding it to its nearest integer between `a` and `b`;  
* ε is a tiny constant preventing division by zero when all weights vanish (a case rarely occurring during training).

The activation quantizer follows the *same* recipe except it never rescales activations before calling non‑linear functions (like GELU); instead activations are clamped into \([-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\) token‑wise so that no zero‑point offset appears.



## 2 | Why Adding Zero Improves Modeling Power

A pure binary layer (\{-1,+1\}) can perform well on certain workloads because signed weights suffice for preserving linear transformations when data have balanced positive/negative components.
Yet real language signals are often sparse or contain frequent “inactive” features—thinking loosely about attention maps that ignore some heads more than others.

By extending the alphabet from two symbols to three:

* `<−>` still supplies signed contributions,
* `<0>` acts as an explicit gate—when proxied through learned scalars this behaves like **feature filtering** that disables certain pathways entirely in forward propagation.

Consequences:

| Effect | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| Denser **zero entries** | Reduces effective computational footprint even further (a distinct fraction of flops becomes mere bypasses). |
| Ability to discard useless channels | Grants the network more expressive freedom without raising bitwidth —– models can learn *which* hidden dimensions matter at which stages automatically. |

Empirically this extra state was shown to push BitNet b¹·⁵⁸’s perplexity up roughly ten percent over prior one‑bit variants at comparable parameter counts.



## 3 | Training From Scratch With Bits

Training fully binary networks usually demands careful loss scaling and knowledge distillation tricks because gradients can get clipped or vanish when weights abruptly saturate.
The absmean formulation keeps gradients well behaved on two grounds:

1. **Scale Normalisation** ensures each update step receives parameters centered around unity magnitude — no single weight overpowers others or becomes negligible.
2. **Clipping First** guarantees gradients directly reflect the sign/zero itself rather than superfluous large values that could destabilise learning.

As a result:

* Models start training at full precision,
* Each step collapses toward one of ``{- 1,’0’,+ 1}`` following objective-driven pressure,
* No special post‑training fine‑tuning is required—all targets reached from scratch.



## 4 | Plugging Into Modern Transformers

BitNet’s design deliberately mirrors popular open-source backbones so that users can drop it into familiar pipelines:

| Transformer Component | BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ Counterpart |
|-----------------------|--------------------------|
| `nn.Linear`           | `BitLinear` *(ternary aware)* |
| Normalisation        | **RMSNorm** (bias free) |
| Feed‑forward activation | **SwiGLU** |
| Positional encoding | **Rotary Embedding** |
| Biases?              | *Removed entirely*

Because every layer except embeddings remains binary aware and bias‑free, integration costs are minimal—huggingface pipelines can simply replace standard linear modules with `BitLinear`, adjust tokenizer handling for the optional `{–}` bits if desired, then proceed as usual.



## 5 | Performance Caboodle (Quick Snapshot)

> From the original paper’s tables:
> 
> * At **3B parameters**, BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ achieves wall‑clock latency *≈2½× faster than FP16 LLaMA*, while using *≈3¾× less GPU memory*, yet matching perplexity.
> * For larger sizes (7B/13B/70B), speedup climbs above *4×* while energy consumption drops dramatically thanks purely integer additions during matrix multiplies.
> 
> These figures underline that you do not sacrifice accuracy for a modest reduction in inference budget.



## 6 | What Comes Next?

The abovesaid framework opens several exciting research tracks:

* **Further bit reductions** – compress activations below INT8 while retaining zeroing capacity.
* **Hybrid MoE deployments** – now each expert’s parameter set fits comfortably on modest chips owing to ternary compression.
* **Edge inference** – low bandwidth plus minimal multiplications make mobile CPUs ideal candidates.
* **Custom ASICs** – Groq-style Linear Processing Units could be reworked specifically for ternary addition chains native to abscene<=>ares world...

All underpinned by one deceptively simple principle: normalize your weights by their own scale before snapping them cleanly into three discrete buckets.



## In Closing

The power behind One‑Bit LLMs doesn’t come from radical neural network rewrites—it comes from disciplined weight management through absmean quantization.
By normalizing each parameter vector against its mean absolute magnitude and letting those numbers decide whether they should act positively‐weighted (+), negatively‐weighted (-), or remain silent (0), BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ turns dense floating precision into lean ternaries without losing performance.
If you’re curious about sketching your first ternary Transformer wheelhouse—or simply debating whether less bits might indeed mean more smarts—this approach offers both theory and hard evidence on an attainable path forward.

## 1️⃣ Why a One‑Bit Language Model?

Large language models (LLMs) grow rapidly in size—an inner‑product chain over billions of parameters dominates realtime inference:
* **Floating‑point arithmetic** (both adds and multiplies) drives energy consumption.
* **Model shipping costs** grow linearly with payload size.
* **Memory bandwidth** becomes a bottleneck when moving weights from DRAM onto the accelerator each step.

Reducing precision is an obvious mitigation strategy, yet standard post‑training quantisation stops at 4‑bit weights or 8‑bit activations—still far from being waste free.  
Enter **BitNet b1.58**, a family‑wide shift whose every weight is one of \{-1, 0, +1\}.  With integer additions only for matrix multiplication it is dramatically cheaper in latency, throughput and wattage *and* matches full‑precision FP16 performance past the 3 B parameter mark.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2️⃣ Overview of BitNet b1.58

| Property | Full‑precision FP16 |  BitNet b1.58 |
|--------- |-------------------- |--------------|
| Weight format | Floating point | Ternary \{-1,0,+1\} |
| Activation bits | 16 | 8 (per token) |
| Convexity | Standard `nn.Linear` | `BitLinear` |
| Compatibility layer | None required | A drop‑in substitution |

Key innovations:

* **AbsMean quantisation** – each weight matrix \(W\) is rescaled by its average absolute value  
  \[
     \widetilde{W}= \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\, +1\right), 
     \qquad 
     \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
  \]
* **Integer MACs** – matrix products become pure additions over ternary wires.
* **8‑bit activations** – kept within \([-Q_b,Q_b]\) per token so no zero‑point complication.

## 3️⃣ Reusing LLaMA’s Skeleton

#### 🚀 The biggest trick is **adhering almost exactly to LLaMA’s beloved building blocks** while swapping out the linear layer:

| LLaMA Component | Role | BitNet‑Adaptation |
|-----------------|------|-------------------|
| **RMSNorm**     | Normalisation that avoids extra scale parameters | Kept unchanged |
| **SwiGLU**      | Gated activation that delivers high capacity | Kept unchanged |
| **Rotary embeddings** (`rope`) | Positional encoding within self‑attention QKV dot product | Kept unchanged |
| **Bias vectors**| Disabled everywhere — eliminates an entire column per linear operation | Removed |

Because these elements are fully preserved:

* Existing model definitions used throughout HuggingFace Transformers or vLLM work verbatim—just swap `nn.Linear` → `BitLinear`.
* No custom API changes are needed when loading a model checkpoint via `AutoModelForCausalLM`.
* Weight conversion ("quantise in place") can be done after pretraining exactly like any other model checkpoint transformation.

## 4️⃣ Plugging Into Modern Toolchains

### 🔧 HuggingFace 🤗  
HuggingFace defines the canonical interface through `transformers.AutoConfig`, `AutoTokenizer`, `AutoModelForCausalLM`.  
`BitNet b1.58` uses identical config keys (`hidden_size`, `num_attention_heads`, …) so:
```python
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("bitnet/b158", trust_remote_code=True)
```
The `"trust_remote_code"` flag loads a custom `__init__` that registers `BitLinear`. No altered tokenisation or pipeline logic required.



### ⚡️ vLLM  
vLLM relies on low‑level kernels packaged in libraries such as FasterTransformer or Ladder for fast batched GEMMs.
`BitLinear` maps cleanly onto these kernels:
* The kernel still receives `(batch_size × seq_len × head_dim)` tensors.
* Integer additions replace float multiplications → lower register pressure → higher throughput.
Inputs go directly through existing streaming logic – you just point your config file at `"bitnet/b158"`.



### 🪝 llama.cpp  
Because binary weights are just three symbols per parameter:
* Package size shrinks by ≈ 30× compared with FP16 binaries.
* Loading code stays identical; weight files are parsed into an int8 array which then contains values −128…+127 but the “real” weight is ‑1/0/​+1 after a simple scaling by stored average absolute value (`γ`).
No heavy C++ changes needed beyond adding an entry in `load_ctx_tokenizer()` for “b158”.

Emily means users on low‑power devices can run sentence inference locally while keeping GPU card budgets low.

# Zero‑Shot Accuracy Versus Full Precision — Competitive Perplexity From Scaled Ternary Models  

The latest wave of **one‑bit** large language models (LLMs) proves that reduced‑precision training can preserve—and even improve—downstream performance if applied correctly. The work behind **BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸** shows that when a ternary weight matrix is trained from scratch alongside eight‑bit activations, its zero‑shot abilities rival those of conventional FP16 models once we hit roughly **3 B parameters**.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why Zero–Shot Matters  

Zero‑shot evaluation probes a model’s *unseen* reasoning capacity by asking it questions without any task‑specific fine‑tuning. In practice this tests whether training has yielded a robust internal representation rather than simply memorizing labeled examples.

Standard benchmarks—ARC‑Easy / ARC‑Challenge (reasoning), Hellaswag (commonsense), Winogrande (pronoun resolution)—are widely used among researchers because they demand advanced language understanding beyond next‑token prediction alone. A new LLM architecture must perform competitively on these metrics **without extra training steps** if it is truly advantageous.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Introducing BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸  

The core idea behind BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸ is simple yet powerful:

| Component | Design choice | Effect |
|-----------|---------------|--------|
| **Weights** | `absmean` ternarization (`{-1,0,+1}`) | Turns every dense multiply–accumulate into integer addition |
| **Activations** | 8‑bit linear mapping (`[-Q_b,Q_b]`) | Keeps dynamic range while avoiding zero‑point bias |
| **Architecture** | LLaMA‑style transformer  
(RMSNorm + SwiGLU + rotary positional encoding) | Allows direct integration into existing PyTorch/Hugging Face pipelines |

The quantization procedure rescales each weight matrix \(W\) by its average absolute value
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\!\left|W_{ij}\right|
\]
and then rounds each entry:
\[
\widetilde W =
   \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\,+1 \Bigr).
\]
Having every parameter occupy only one *ternary* bit keeps memory usage minimal while leaving room for an extra “zero” pattern that acts as an explicit *feature filter*. This subtle mathematical twist gives BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸ a edge over earlier pure–binary designs.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Zero–Shot Performance Across Tasks  

When evaluated on an extensive suite—ARC Easy / Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande—the patterns emerge clearly:

- With **<3 B parameters**, BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸ trails most FP16 baselines.
- At roughly **≈3 B parameters**, the gap closes:
  - **ARC Easy** and **ARC Challenge** accuracy curves intersect.
  - **Hellaswag** confidence levels match those of their FP16 counterparts.
  - **Winogrande** precision trends cross over around this scale.
- Above **≈4 B**, BitNet b¹⋅⁵⁸ *surpasses* many smaller FP16 models on every metric tested within identical training protocols (same token counts on RedPajama).

These observations are consistent across multiple checkpoints—from a modest 3 B model all the way up to an impressive 70 B scale—confirming that larger ternary nets inherit no loss in generalization.

# MoE + One–Bit : A New Path Toward On‑Chip Expert Networks  

Large Language Models rely increasingly on **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** mechanisms to keep training time reasonable while preserving expressive power.The idea is simple*: instead of letting every token pass through **every** layer, you let a lightweight **gating circuit** forward only a few **experts** (sub‑networks) per token.*  

In practice this works well out of box—but it comes at **two major costs**  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- **Memory:** every extra expert carries its own millions of parameters  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- **Communication:** distributed deployments must shuttle whole activations across nodes whenever an expert runs elsewhere  

The new family of **BitNet b<sub> 1 ⋅ 58</sub>** models turns both these pain points upside down with remarkably simple ideas around ternary weights.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## What “one‑bit” really means

Each weight in BitNet b<sub> 1 ⋅ 58</sub> takes one of three discrete values  
```
{- 1 , 0 , + 1}
```
Rather than storing an entire floating‑point number (16 bits), you encode it with  
```
− log₂(3) ≈ − 1–5 bits
```
The compression factor comes straight out of algebraic math:

```
W∈ℝⁿˣᵐ          →              |W| = n·m entries
FP16 storage        = |W| ·16 bits
Ternary storage     = |W| ·log₂(3 ) ≈ |W| ·14 bits
```

> **≈ 10× saving** – every million parameters become only ~100 kbytes instead of ~2 Mb.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## How many more experts fit under one roof?

Open‐source TF‑based models store around *350 million* parameters per “small” transformer (upwards).  
With full precision they normally fill several gigabytes—a chunk that forces pipelines onto several GPUs or even hundreds of thousands of internal registers that must reside off‑chip.



### A crude capacity estimate  

| Category | Full precision | Ternary |
|----------|-----------------|---------|
| One expert (350m params) | ≈ 5 GB | ≈ 0·5 GB |
| Packing strategy  | > 4× devices needed  | One device accommodates ≈ 8 reviewers |

Because lossless prototype experiments showed even larger models retain tight perplexity gaps against FP16 baselines starting at **3 B** scales, adding a handful—as opposed to dozens—of additional experts becomes realistic without any change to your accelerator’s die area.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Automatic elimination of expensive cross‑node traffic

Typical distributed MoEs shuttles two kinds of tensors between nodes—

* **Model parameters** – huge (> 50%α rel.) long strings copy over PCIe/NVLink  
* **Activation maps** – vectors traveling back into the gate once finished  

Moving objects over an interconnect costs orders magnitudes more energy than operating on them locally.



### Why BitNet simplifies everything  

| Step                           | Cost under normal MoE                                         | Cost under BitNet&nbsp;b<sub> 1 ⋅ 58</sub>       |
|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Parameter storage              | ~FP32 entries (~32 bits), cougars → high off‑chip transfer      | ternary → tiny buffers → consolidated locally |
| Parameter update rate          | CPU ↔ accelerator sync every batch                       | Zero extra sync because firmware knows byte layout |
| Activation width              | fp32 / fp16 → tens ● bytes per token                            | int8 reduced communication breadth          |

Because every matrix multiplication turns into **bit‐masked additions**, outputs leave a layer already scaled correctly without multiplies pulling additional data down bridges—only lightweight adds happen over very short distances internally.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Ultra–lightweight switching

When an incoming token strides through an expert-changing schema—“switch”—the commodity operation traditionally involves

```
output = Linear(weights · input)
```

where `weights ∈ FP32` brightens oxygen costing two operations (`×` + `+`).  
With **ternary matrices**, `weights` become mere lookup indices for addition chains:

```
// Vanilla pseudo code
for i ∈ rows:
    y[i] ← Σ_j sign(weight[i,j]) * x[j]      // sign ∈ {−1,+0,+1}
```

A backend accelerator can collapse this into two truth tables AND / XOR registers—the product term vanishes entirely—and thus the switch equals “feeding next lane” rather than a costly dot product.

# Hardware Tailored For Ternary Transformers — Designing LPUs That Only Add  

In modern GPUs an LLM’s cost comes mainly from floating‑point multiplications inside every dense layer (“nn.Linear”).  
BitNet‐b1.58 shows that if every parameter can be restricted to **–¹ , 0 or +¹** you do **not** need any inner‑product multipliers at all – you just add or subtract the activation values going through a simple accumulation tree.



## Why “no multiplications” matters  

| Traditional FP® multiplier | Integer adder |
|----------------------------|---------------|
| **Power** – requires full‑precision fanout plus carry chains | Much less power per S‑bit word |
| **Area** – division circuit is big and occupies many LUTs | Small combinational network |
| **Bandwidth** – each word needs two operands loaded fully | One operand is a single bit (+/- mask) |
| **Latency** – limited by long carry propagation | Operation finishes in one cycle |

Because the GELU–SWIGLU attention block is dominated by tens of billions of *nn.Linear* calls, eliminating all multipliers means offloading hundreds of Giga‑OPS per second onto cheap adders alone.



### The math behind it  

BitNet’s linear layer is defined as follows:

```math
\widetilde W = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\,\gamma+\varepsilon\,},{-}1,\;{\!+}{\!}1\right), \qquad 
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}\!\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|
```

After this ``absmean`` scaling `γ` every weight becomes exactly **–¹**, **0**, or **+¹**.  
When multiplying an activation `a`  

> ```
> a · (+¹) = +a          \
> a · ( –¹) = –a           >—replace by a sign flip instead of a multiplier
> a · (  0) =  0          /
> ```  

The product is obtained by either passing `a` straight through (for `+¹`) or feeding its two’s‑complement representation when the sign bit says `–¹`. No actual table lookup, shift register or multiplier circuitry is required.



## Ternary Transformers break the bottleneck

The experimental results in “The Era of 1‑bit LLMs” confirm massive runtime savings when running BitNet‐b1.58 on ordinary accelerators that support binary/ternary kernels (e.g., LADDER’s 2‑bit kernel).  

Key observations are reported in Tables 5–8 of the paper (summarized here):

* **Perplexity & Accuracy**  
    * Starting at **3 B parameters**, BitNet‑b1.58 attains identical perplexity—and eventual best accuracy—to a full‑precision FP16 baseline trained on exactly the same data set (RedPajama → 100 B tokens).
    * At **3.​9 B**, it even exceeds the corresponding FP16 model while shaving more than half the GPU memory usage (^~35%).

* **Speed & Memory**  
    * A single **3 B Holes GPT-like instance runs ~210% faster** than its FP16 counterpart (`latency ≈31 ms` vs `≈50 ms` per output token) while consuming only ~30 % of its peak GPU RAM.
    * Scaling up preserves this trend—at **70 B**, inferring one token takes roughly **4× less time** (`latency ≈18 ms`) compared with an equivalent FP16 model yet demands only ~20 % the memory (`≈18GB` vs `≈90GB`).

* **Energy**  
    * Arithmetic‑energy modeling shows an over‑70× fall in usable FLOP consumption when replacing FGMs with pure INT8 adds (`71·10⁻⁶J` per operation versus `5·10⁻⁴J`).  
    * The reduction stems almost entirely from dropping multiplies (`FP16 × array`) and doubling usage of extremely cheap adds (`INT8 + array`).  

* **Throughput**  
    In a pipeline paralleled setup (two RTX A100 GPUs), a **70 B BitNet-b1… required only one device** where otherwise eight would be needed for enough parallelism—you get nearly nineteen times higher batch throughput.



## How would an “add‑only” LPU look?  

Typical modern GPUs feature many closely spaced “Multiply–Add Engines” handling IEEE‑754 operands plus fusion paths for adding results back together again [Simple pipeline].  
A custom add–only engine would replace these units with something closer to this arrangement:

```
[ Input Streaming ] ──► [Sign Mask Decode] ──► [Wide Adder Tree] ──► [Accumulator]
             │                   │                      │                 
           (≈Nbits)             ──(multiplexed───────────┘                 
               ‭‰              ‼              ↑     
                              ‼             [+ Sign]   
```

* The difference between accepting `+a` versus `–a` comes down solely depth‑one comparators (“sign flip”) rather than explicit multipliers/highly pipelined MAC blocks.
* Because activations are kept at INT8 precision throughout inference (the paper describes an ***aligned conversion*** scheme where activations don’t get clipped back into `[0,Q]`, instead staying in `[−Q,Q]`), bandwidth shrinks further since half our words become strictly bounded near-zero signs anyways.

Designing numerous pure adder banks inside an ASIC gives three direct improvements compared with spending each cycle inside an FMA unit for one pair of operands:

|-----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Fabricated transistor count | Roughly ×5 smaller per functional unit                    |
| Peak clock frequency        | Can increase from ~800 MHz up toward >2 GHz                |
| Power budget                | Decreases quadratically → at least ×10 lower consumption |

Even when embedded inside current commodity GPUs these changes light up extra slots for other tasks—think object detection cores or small neural nets kept bootlegged near keystones.
