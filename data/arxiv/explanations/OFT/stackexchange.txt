### Why is hyperspherical energy (HE) a good proxy for preserving semantic generation ability?
Question:
The paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE) defined as HE(W) = \(\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|^{-1}\) with \(\hat{w}_i=w_i/\|w_i\|\). I am confused why this quantity should correlate with semantic generation ability of a pretrained diffusion model. Can someone provide an intuitive explanation or empirical justification for why preserving HE preserves semantics, as opposed to, say, preserving Euclidean distance between weight matrices?
Answer:
Short answer
- Hyperspherical energy (HE) directly measures the geometry of neuron directions (pairwise angles) on the unit sphere. The paper’s central hypothesis — and empirical finding — is that this angular geometry encodes the model’s semantic generation ability more faithfully than raw entrywise (Euclidean) differences of weights.
- Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves HE by construction (orthogonal transforms keep pairwise angles), and the paper shows that preserving HE leads to better identity/control preservation, faster and more stable convergence, and stronger quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, the control-consistency metrics) than competing finetuning methods.
So HE is a good proxy because (1) it focuses on the angular information that the paper (and prior work) show carries semantics, (2) it is directly preserved by the proposed finetuning family (orthogonal transforms), and (3) that preservation correlates with the observed preservation of generative semantics in experiments.

Longer explanation (intuitions + math + evidence)

1) What HE measures and why angles matter
- Definition (paper): for a layer with column neurons w_i, normalize each neuron to unit length \hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\| and define
  HE(W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
- Relationship to angles. For normalized vectors,
  \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta_{ij},
  where \theta_{ij} is the angle between \hat w_i and \hat w_j. Therefore HE is a monotone function of the pairwise cosines \cos\theta_{ij} (HE sums inverses of the pairwise chord distances). In short: HE is a compact scalar summary of the pairwise angular geometry among neurons.
- Why angular geometry encodes semantics: the paper cites and uses the same intuition as SphereNet and related works — feature/neuron directions on the hypersphere capture discriminative/semantic information (angular differences matter more than magnitudes). The paper provides a toy autoencoder experiment showing that angular information of neurons can reconstruct images while magnitudes carry little semantic information. There is also an analogy to Fourier phase: the phase (angular) spectrum of an image retains semantic structure while magnitude largely encodes intensity/scale. Thus preserving pairwise angles is a natural way to preserve the learned semantic structure of pretrained networks.

2) Why orthogonal transforms preserve HE (and why that matters)
- Orthogonal matrices R satisfy R^TR = I, so for any two vectors u,v we have \langle Ru, Rv\rangle = \langle u,v\rangle. Hence angles and cosines between vectors are exactly preserved by a common orthogonal transform applied to all neurons in a layer. Consequently HE(RW^0) = HE(W^0).
- This gives a constructive way to keep the layer’s angular geometry unchanged while allowing the model to adapt: learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix R (initialized at identity). OFT thus enforces preservation of the pairwise angular structure by design; COFT adds an extra small-radius constraint to limit Euclidean deviation as well.

3) Why HE is a better finetuning proxy than (only) Euclidean distance
- Euclidean (entrywise or Frobenius) distance \|W - W^0\| is an unstructured norm: it measures absolute changes in weight entries but does not directly constrain the geometry (angles) among neurons. Semantic properties that depend on relative directions (how neurons relate to each other) are not guaranteed to be preserved by a small Euclidean change. In particular:
  - Semantic encoding is often relational (angles, relative orientations) rather than absolute parameter values; two matrices can have different parameter entries but nearly identical neuron-direction geometry, or conversely small-looking parameter changes can produce nontrivial angular changes in high dimensions if those changes are coordinated across many entries.
  - HE explicitly targets the relational object (pairwise angles) that the paper argues carries semantics, whereas Euclidean distance treats every parameter coordinate independently.
- The paper therefore combines both ideas: OFT preserves HE (angular relation) and COFT additionally constrains deviation \|\bm R - I\| (which bounds a kind of Euclidean deviation) to strike a stability/flexibility trade-off.

4) Empirical evidence from the paper
- Toy experiment: angular reconstructions in an autoencoder show angular information carries the semantics (paper’s Figure referenced in text).
- Ablation: removing the orthogonality constraint breaks finetuning — OFT without orthogonality "fails to generate any realistic image and achieve no control effect" (paper reports this experiment), which supports the importance of preserving angular relationships rather than arbitrary parameter updates.
- Downstream tasks: on subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks the paper reports that OFT/COFT:
  - Converge faster and more stably than DreamBooth and LoRA.
  - Achieve better identity preservation and prompt fidelity in quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS) and better control-consistency scores in the controllable generation tasks.
  - Produce qualitatively better images and higher control accuracy with fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs.
These empirical outcomes support the claim that preserving HE (via orthogonal transforms) correlates with preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability.

5) Additional useful intuitions
- Orthogonal transformation = change of basis / coordinated rotation: OFT is like re-expressing the same pretrained neurons in a rotated coordinate system that preserves their relative geometry. This is less destructive than arbitrary elementwise updates.
- HE vs. minimizing HE: the paper emphasizes that we should preserve HE, not minimize it. Minimizing HE (pushing neurons toward uniform spacing) is a useful objective for random initialization/generalization in classification, but it would alter pretrained semantic structure for a generative model. OFT preserves, rather than collapses, that structure.
- Re-scaled OFT: the paper also notes that re-scaling neurons (learnable per-neuron magnitudes) does not change HE (because HE uses normalized vectors); adding a diagonal scaling gives more flexibility while keeping angles intact.

Conclusion
HE is a principled proxy because it captures the pairwise angular geometry that (a) prior work and the paper’s experiments indicate holds semantic information, and (b) is exactly preserved by layerwise orthogonal transforms. The combination (theoretical invariance + the experimental improvements when preserving HE) is the paper’s main argument and empirical justification for using HE as the structural measure to protect pretrained generative semantics during finetuning.

### How exactly does an orthogonal matrix preserve pairwise neuron angles?
Question:
The paper claims that applying the same orthogonal transformation \(R\) to all neurons preserves pairwise hyperspherical similarity (angles). Can someone show a short mathematical derivation that for normalized neurons \(\hat{w}_i\), \(\langle R\hat{w}_i, R\hat{w}_j\rangle = \langle\hat{w}_i,\hat{w}_j\rangle\)? Also, does this hold for non-normalized neurons?
Answer:
Short answer
- For normalized neurons \(\hat w_i=\tfrac{w_i}{\|w_i\|}\) the identity is immediate:
  \[
  \langle R\hat w_i,\,R\hat w_j\rangle
  = (R\hat w_i)^\top (R\hat w_j)
  = \hat w_i^\top R^\top R \hat w_j
  = \hat w_i^\top \hat w_j
  = \langle \hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle,
  \]
  because \(R\) is orthogonal so \(R^\top R=I\).
- This is just the usual fact that orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, hence lengths and angles (they are rotations/reflections / Euclidean isometries).
- The same preservation holds for non‑normalized neurons as well: for arbitrary \(w_i,w_j\),
  \[
  (R w_i)^\top (R w_j)= w_i^\top R^\top R w_j = w_i^\top w_j,
  \]
  so dot products and norms are preserved and therefore the cosine of the angle
  \(\cos\theta_{ij}=\dfrac{w_i^\top w_j}{\|w_i\|\,\|w_j\|}\)
  is unchanged by \(R\).

Intuition
- An orthogonal matrix \(R\in\mathrm{O}(d)\) is a rotation and/or reflection of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Rotations and reflections do not stretch or squash space: they keep every distance and every angle exactly the same. Because cosine similarity is defined from dot products and norms, it is invariant under such transformations.
- In the paper the hyperspherical energy is computed from normalized neurons \(\hat w_i\), so showing \(\langle R\hat w_i,R\hat w_j\rangle=\langle\hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle\) is exactly the statement that pairwise hyperspherical similarities (angles / cosines) are preserved.

A few clarifying remarks tied to the paper
- The determinant of \(R\) (rotation: \(\det=1\), reflection: \(\det=-1\)) is irrelevant for preserving inner products; both lie in \(O(d)\) and satisfy \(R^\top R=I\).
- The Cayley parameterization used in the paper yields orthogonal matrices in the special orthogonal group (determinant \(+1\)), i.e. rotations only; these still preserve angles.
- If different orthogonal matrices are applied to different neurons (not the same layer-shared \(R\) for every neuron), the pairwise angles will generally not be preserved. The preservation requires applying the same orthogonal \(R\) to both vectors in each pair.
- Re-scaled OFT in the paper multiplies each column by a positive scalar \(s_i\). Scaling a vector by a positive scalar does not change its direction once you normalize it, so the hyperspherical energy (which uses normalized vectors) is unchanged by per-neuron positive rescalings.
- Numerically, implemented orthogonality can drift (finite precision, approximate parametrizations), so in practice preservation is up to numerical tolerance; the paper enforces orthogonality (e.g. via Cayley or projections) to keep this error small.

Counterexample (if R is not orthogonal)
- If \(A\) is not orthogonal, generally \(A^\top A\neq I\), so \((A w_i)^\top(A w_j)= w_i^\top A^\top A w_j\) need not equal \(w_i^\top w_j\). A simple case: let \(A=\lambda I\) with \(\lambda\neq\pm1\). Then inner products get scaled by \(\lambda^2\), angles unchanged only if all vectors are zero or \(\lambda>0\) and you subsequently renormalize (but the raw dot products and norms change).

Conclusion
Orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise (hyperspherical) similarities because orthogonal transforms preserve dot products and norms. The short derivation above is exactly the algebraic statement of that geometric fact.

### Why is Cayley parameterization chosen to enforce orthogonality and what are its limitations?
Question:
Section 3.3 uses the Cayley transform \(R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) with skew-symmetric \(Q\) to parameterize orthogonal matrices. Why is Cayley preferred over e.g. QR or exponential map? The paper mentions Cayley only produces matrices with determinant \(+1\). What practical consequences does this det=+1 constraint have for finetuning, and could we miss useful reflections (det=-1)?
Answer:
Short answer
- The authors use the Cayley transform because it gives an exact, differentiable mapping from a skew‑symmetric parameter matrix \(Q\) to an orthogonal matrix
  \[
  R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
  \]
  which is cheap to compute (no SVD/QR or costly matrix exponential) and is naturally identity when \(Q=0\) (easy, stable initialization). It therefore enforces orthogonality exactly while being straightforward to optimize.

- The tradeoff is that the Cayley map only produces orthogonal matrices with \emph{determinant} \(+1\) (the special orthogonal group SO(\(n\))) and cannot represent orthogonal matrices that have eigenvalue \(-1\) (including reflections, det \(=-1\)). There is also the practical cost of the matrix inverse and numerical conditioning if \(I-Q\) becomes ill‑conditioned. The paper notes the det \(=+1\) restriction and reports no empirical harm.

Longer explanation (intuition + details, and practical consequences)

1) Why pick Cayley? (intuitive and technical reasons)
- Exact orthogonality: If \(Q\) is skew‑symmetric (\(Q=-Q^\top\)), the Cayley transform produces \(R\) with \(R^\top R = I\) exactly. So you don't need to do an approximate projection or costly orthonormalization every step — \(R\) is precisely orthogonal by construction.
- Simple parameters and initialization: parameterize by \(Q\) and initialize \(Q=0\). Then \(R=I\), so you start exactly at the pretrained weights (identity transform) and can gradually move away — a very convenient and stable starting point for finetuning.
- Efficiency vs alternatives: compared to other exact orthogonal parameterizations,
  - matrix exponential \(R=\exp(A)\) with \(A=-A^\top\) gives orthogonal \(R\) but evaluating gradients requires computing \(\exp(A)\) (scaling/squaring, series, or eigendecomposition) which is more costly than computing a single matrix inverse, and is more complex to implement and tune;
  - projection-based methods (QR, polar decomposition / SVD) require computing e.g. polar decomposition \(U = X(X^\top X)^{-1/2}\) or QR of an unconstrained matrix each update — accurate but expensive and sometimes numerically sensitive (sign ambiguities in QR);
  - structured parameterizations (Givens rotations, Householder products) can represent a wide set of orthogonals but require many parameters or carefully ordered products and are less convenient to initialize as small perturbations of identity.
- Cayley is a rational map (no infinite series), differentiable, and maps small \(Q\) to a small rotation (roughly \(R\approx I+2Q+O(Q^2)\)), so optimization behaves well when you constrain changes to be modest (this is exploited by COFT via a small \(Q\) constraint).

2) What are the limitations of Cayley?
- Determinant and eigenvalue restriction: Cayley parameterization maps skew‑symmetric \(Q\) to orthogonal matrices in SO(\(n\)), i.e. orthogonal matrices with \(\det(R)=+1\). It cannot produce orthogonal matrices with \(\det=-1\) (reflections), nor can it represent orthogonals that have eigenvalue \(-1\). Concretely:
  - Any orthogonal matrix with an eigenvalue \(-1\) cannot be written as \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) for a real skew‑symmetric \(Q\).
  - Topologically: O(\(n\)) has two connected components (det \(=+1\) and det \(=-1\)). The Cayley transform (and any continuous map from the Lie algebra so(\(n\))) stays in the identity component SO(\(n\)). You cannot cross from det \(=+1\) to det \(=-1\) by a continuous path of determinant‑\(+1\) matrices.
- Matrix inverse / conditioning: Cayley needs inversion of \(I-Q\). While skew‑symmetric \(Q\) has purely imaginary eigenvalues so \(I-Q\) is generically invertible, if \(\|Q\|\) grows large the inverse can become ill‑conditioned (numerical stability problems). This is why the paper uses block diagonalization (reduces inversion cost and helps condition) and why COFT constrains \(\|Q\|\) to keep updates small.
- Not surjective onto all O(\(n\)): Besides det constraint, there are orthogonal matrices in SO(\(n\)) with eigenvalue \(-1\) that are excluded. (In practice the main exclusion people talk about is reflections / det \(=-1\).)

3) Practical consequences of det \(=+1\) for finetuning (could we miss useful reflections?)
- Reflection meaning in this context: A reflection (an orthogonal matrix with det \(=-1\)) is a global coordinate flip that preserves pairwise angles (so it still preserves hyperspherical energy). If the best orthogonal change to the layer that preserves hyperspherical energy but also improves downstream loss was a reflection, Cayley cannot reach that transform.
- How likely / important is that in practice?
  - The paper explicitly notes this limitation and reports that they did not observe negative effects empirically: Cayley’s det \(=+1\) constraint “does not affect performance in practice.” That is consistent with the intuition that:
    - Finetuning is started at the identity and typically proceeds by small, smooth updates; the identity is in the SO(\(n\)) component, so reachable solutions that are small perturbations are also in SO(\(n\)). A reflection is in the other component and would require a discontinuous jump (a large change) in the orthogonal factor, which finetuning that starts at identity and is regularized to stay near identity will not prefer anyway.
    - Many useful transforms are rotations (det \(=+1\)) or compositions of small rotations; reflections (global sign flips) are less likely to be the \emph{minimal} change that also maintains hyperspherical structure and closeness to the pretrained weights.
  - Empirically in this and many similar finetuning contexts (the paper’s experiments), the Cayley‑based OFT worked well and outperformed baselines — so missing det \(=-1\) cases did not appear to be a practical limitation for their tasks.
- If you are worried that a downstream task \emph{requires} a reflection, there are straightforward mitigations:
  - Allow an explicit learned Householder / single reflection factor outside the Cayley block (e.g. a learned diagonal of \(\pm 1\) or a single Householder reflector) so you can reach det \(=-1\) transforms if needed. This separates the discrete reflection part from the continuous rotation part.
  - Use alternative parameterizations that can represent both components (e.g. represent the orthogonal factor by the orthogonal factor of a polar decomposition of an unconstrained matrix or by a product including learned Householder reflectors), at the cost of more computation or more complicated gradients.
  - Allow a small unconstrained sign flip of certain neurons (but be careful: flipping single neurons is not the same as a global orthogonal reflection and will change pairwise angles unless applied as a full orthogonal transform).

4) Alternatives and their tradeoffs (summary)
- Exponential map \(R=\exp(A)\) with \(A=-A^\top\): exact, smooth, stays in SO(\(n\)), more expensive to compute (matrix exponential) and differentiate.
- Polar decomposition / SVD projection: given an unconstrained \(X\), project to \(U\) (orthogonal) via \(U=X(X^\top X)^{-1/2}\). Exact orthogonality, can produce det \(=\pm 1\) depending on \(X\), but expensive (matrix square root or SVD) and can introduce numerical/gradient issues.
- QR of an unconstrained matrix: gives an orthogonal \(Q\), but sign ambiguities and discontinuities in the R diagonal can complicate gradients; also requires QR each update.
- Householder / Givens parameterizations: can represent all O(\(n\)) (including reflections, depending on number of reflectors), are parameter‑efficient for some sizes, but require composing many elementary transforms and careful implementation to be efficient and stable.
- Cayley: cheap, differentiable, exact orthogonality, identity initialization, good behavior for small updates; but restricted to SO(\(n\)) and requires inversion of \(I-Q\) (mitigated by block structure and constraints).

Final recommendation / takeaway
- Cayley is a pragmatic choice for OFT: it enforces orthogonality exactly, is easy to initialize at identity, gives stable small updates, and is computationally simpler than many exact alternatives. The theoretical limitation (only producing det \(=+1\) orthogonals) is real, but in this finetuning setting — starting at identity and constraining updates — it is unlikely to be limiting, and the authors report no empirical drawback.
- If you have a specific downstream reason to believe a reflection (det \(=-1\)) is required, you can augment the Cayley parameterization with a small discrete/Householder reflection factor or switch to a parameterization that permits both components (understanding the additional computational cost).

### How do you compute gradients through the Cayley transform efficiently?
Question:
The Cayley transform includes an inverse \((I-Q)^{-1}\). During training we must backpropagate through this matrix inverse. How is this done practically (naive inverse + autograd, Woodbury/Neumann approximations, custom solver)? Are there stability or speed issues for large block sizes?
Answer:
Short answer
- Backprop through the Cayley map is done by differentiating the matrix inverse, but you should avoid computing an explicit inverse in the forward pass. Practically people either
  1. compute (I−Q)^{-1} by a linear solve (LU/QR factorization) and rely on autograd (or a custom autograd.Function) that re-uses the factorization in the backward pass, or
  2. replace the inverse by a truncated Neumann series when Q is small (gives a differentiable polynomial in Q, cheap and stable if ||Q||<1), or
  3. use low-rank / structured parameterizations of Q so you can apply Woodbury-type identities and cheaper solves.
- The main engineering trade-offs: explicit inverses are expensive and memory-hungry; solves with cached factorization are much faster and numerically better; Neumann truncated series is fastest but only valid when Q is small (which COFT enforces); large blocks make cost O(d^3) per block and can become a bottleneck — that is why the paper uses block-diagonal Q and/or block sharing.

Detailed explanation, how-to and recommendations
What you need to backpropagate
The Cayley parameterization in the paper is
R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1},
with Q skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^T). During training we must differentiate losses L with respect to the parameters that build Q. The derivative always involves the derivative of an inverse. A standard matrix identity you will use is
d( (I − Q)^{-1} ) = (I − Q)^{-1} (dQ) (I − Q)^{-1},
so gradients involve products with the inverse (or solving linear systems with I − Q).

Why you should avoid an explicit inverse
Computing S := (I − Q)^{-1} by forming its explicit inverse (e.g., torch.inverse) is:
- costlier (O(d^3) per block) and memory-intensive,
- numerically less stable than solving linear systems, and
- causes autograd to construct a backward that uses the inverse explicitly (more memory/time).
Instead prefer solving (I − Q) X = B for whatever B you need (right-hand sides). Solves are numerically preferable and autograd can be made to reuse factorizations.

Practical implementations (options)

1) Solve-based forward + cache factorization (recommended)
- Forward:
  - Factorize A := I − Q (LU or similar), then compute S = A^{-1}(I + Q) by solving A X = (I + Q) for X. If you only need R applied to vectors (as in multiplying W^0 by R), solve for the required right-hand sides rather than forming S fully.
- Backward:
  - The backward pass needs terms like S^T (∂L/∂R) and further products with S; those are computed by solving linear systems with the same A (I−Q). If you cache the LU (or other factorization) from forward you avoid refactoring in backward and save ~2/3 of the cost.
- Implementation notes:
  - In PyTorch use torch.linalg.solve or torch.lu_factor + torch.lu_solve; for batched small blocks use the batched versions.
  - For best performance implement a custom autograd.Function which caches LU in forward and uses it in backward to avoid refactorizing.
- Complexity and stability:
  - Factorization is O(k^3) per block of size k; solves are O(k^2) per RHS. Backward will perform a few extra solves, so expect roughly a small constant (2–4×) of the forward factorization cost per training step.
  - For skew-symmetric Q, I−Q is always invertible, but if Q is large the linear system can be worse conditioned numerically; initialize Q = 0 and keep Q small (see COFT).

2) Truncated Neumann series (cheap when Q is small)
- Use (I − Q)^{-1} = sum_{t=0}^{T} Q^t + remainder, provided ||Q|| < 1. Then
  R ≈ (I + Q) sum_{t=0}^{T} Q^t,
  which is a polynomial in Q. This is:
  - Very cheap: multiplications cost O(T k^2) (no cubic factorization).
  - Easy to autodiff (polynomial in Q).
  - Only valid and stable if the operator norm of Q < 1 (Neumann series converges). This is exactly the regime COFT targets by constraining ||Q|| ≤ ε'.
- When to use:
  - Small blocks and a strong COFT constraint (Q kept tiny): single- or few-term truncation (e.g., R ≈ I + 2Q + 2Q^2) is often enough and very fast.
  - For larger Q the series fails or requires many terms (expensive).

3) Structured / low-rank Q + Woodbury-type tricks
- If you parametrize Q so that Q = U V^T (or a skew-symmetric low-rank construction like Q = B C^T − C B^T), then I − Q has an identity minus low-rank form and you can apply the Woodbury identity:
  (I − UV^T)^{-1} = I + U (I − V^T U)^{-1} V^T.
- Pros:
  - Inverting (I − V^T U) is cheap if U,V have small inner dimension r (O(r^3) instead of O(d^3)).
  - Backprop through that formula is straightforward and cheap.
- Con:
  - You must choose a parameterization of Q consistent with skew-symmetry and with enough representational power; you also change the set of orthogonals R you can reach.

4) Use block-diagonal Q (what the paper uses)
- Splitting Q into many small blocks reduces cubic cost from O(d^3) to r × O((d/r)^3) and gives a strong practical speed/memory win.
- You can also share blocks across channels (further reduce params and cost).
- This is the paper’s main scalability trick.

Numerical stability considerations
- Invertibility: For real skew-symmetric Q, eigenvalues of Q are purely imaginary, so I − Q has eigenvalues 1 − iλ whose magnitude is sqrt(1 + λ^2) ≥ 1. Therefore I − Q is nonsingular. Nevertheless, conditioning can worsen as ||Q|| grows in some operator norms, so you should keep Q reasonably small in practice.
- Neumann-series convergence requires ||Q|| < 1 (operator norm). COFT explicitly enforces a small Q via a projected gradient step on Q (the paper suggests constraining ||Q|| ≤ ε' after mapping the R-constraint into Q via the series approximation).
- Initialization: initialize Q = 0 so R = I at start; this both stabilizes training and matches the paper.
- Mixed precision: inverses/solves can be sensitive in float16 — consider keeping these operations in float32 or using AMP with careful casts.

How autograd actually behaves
- If you write forward as R = (I + Q).mm(torch.linalg.inv(I - Q)), autograd computes gradients using the analytical derivative which again requires multiplications by (I − Q)^{-1}; this is correct but inefficient.
- If you instead do an LU factorization and solve A x = b, a custom autograd that caches the LU and re-uses it in backward is much faster because you avoid an extra factorization.
- For the Neumann polynomial you can rely on normal autograd since everything is simple matrix multiply.

Concrete implementation recipe (practical, balanced)
1. Parametrize Q as skew-symmetric Q = A − A^T where A is unconstrained (smaller param count if you want).
2. Structure Q block-diagonally with block size k tuned experimentally (paper uses block-diagonal to reduce cost). Typical choices: k in {8, 16, 32, 64} depending on memory/throughput.
3. Use a custom autograd.Function:
   - Forward: compute A = I − Q, do LU factorization (batched), solve for S = A^{-1}(I + Q) via stored LU, compute R = (I + Q) S or directly compute R⋅W^0 as needed, and cache the LU factorization and any intermediates.
   - Backward: use cached LU to compute the required solves rather than re-factorizing; apply derivative identities (or let autograd do it by performing solves in the custom backward implementation).
4. For extra stability and to realize COFT: after each gradient step project Q to satisfy ||Q|| ≤ ε' (operator or Frobenius norm). Projecting the Frobenius norm is easiest: Q ← Q * min(1, ε' / ||Q||_F).
5. If you can guarantee ||Q|| is small, consider using a 1–3 term Neumann truncation in place of the solve for a big speedup.

Rough cost numbers (intuition)
- Full block of size d: factorization O(d^3), solves O(d^2) per RHS. Backward requires a few solves → total per-step cost ~c × d^3 (c small constant).
- r blocks of size d/r: cost ≈ r × (d/r)^3 = d^3 / r^2. So splitting into r blocks reduces cubic cost by r^2 factor.
- Neumann (T terms) cost ≈ O(T d^2) (no cubic), but needs ||Q||<1 and T may need to grow if Q is larger.

What the paper itself notes (and why)
- The paper states: “OFT guarantees the orthogonality via Cayley parametrization which involves a matrix inverse. It slightly limits the scalability of OFT. Although we address this limitation using block diagonal parametrization, how to speed up this matrix inverse in a differentiable way remains a challenge.” That matches the recommendations above: block-diagonalize, keep Q small (COFT), or approximate inverse by Neumann for speed.

Summary recommendations
- For reliability and moderate block sizes: implement solves with cached LU/QR in a custom autograd.Function.
- If you enforce small Q (COFT), prefer the truncated Neumann series for speed and simplicity.
- If you want even better parameter-efficiency, parameterize Q low-rank/skew-symmetric and use Woodbury.
- Keep blocks relatively small (paper achieves good results with block-diagonalization and block sharing) to avoid O(d^3) bottlenecks. Initialize Q=0 and project its norm to control conditioning.

References in the paper that support these choices
- The paper uses Cayley R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} and explicitly mentions the inverse as a scalability issue and their mitigation via block-diagonal parameterization.
- The paper also applies the Neumann series to approximate R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) and uses that to motivate the COFT constraint on ||Q||, then enforces a constraint on Q with projected gradient descent.

### What does block-diagonal parameterization of \(R\) mean for convolutional layers?
Question:
The paper suggests using a block-diagonal \(R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)\) and claims it has an interpretation for convolutional layers, e.g. when number of blocks equals input channels. Could someone clarify concretely: if a conv kernel has shape \(C_{out}\times C_{in}\times k\times k\), how is \(R\) applied and what does each block operate on? Does blocking across channels restrict mixing between channels?
Answer:
Short answer
- For a convolutional weight tensor of shape C_out × C_in × k × k you flatten each output-kernel into a column vector of length d = C_in · k · k and view the conv layer as a matrix W^0 ∈ R^{d×C_out}. OFT multiplies W^0 on the left by an orthogonal matrix R ∈ R^{d×d}: W = R W^0, so R acts on the input / spatial dimension of every output channel equally.
- A block-diagonal R = diag(R_1,...,R_r) partitions that d-dimension into r blocks and applies R_b only to the rows in block b of every output-kernel. If you choose r = C_in and make each block size k·k, each block R_i acts only on the k×k spatial weights of input channel i (for all output channels). This forbids any cross-channel mixing (it only mixes spatial positions within a channel), which is why the paper says it "resembles" depth-wise operations.
- If you want cross-channel mixing you must use fewer, larger blocks (r small, e.g. r=1 gives a full R that mixes channels+spatial positions).

Longer explanation and formulas (concrete, intuitive)

1) How OFT is applied to a conv layer (concrete reshape)
- Take a conv kernel of shape (C_out, C_in, k, k). For each output channel j, flatten its kernel into a column vector
  w_j ∈ R^{d}, with d = C_in · k · k. Stack those columns to make the matrix
  W^0 = [w_1, …, w_{C_out}] ∈ R^{d×C_out}.
- OFT replaces the forward pass z = (W^0)^T x_patch (where x_patch is the flattened input patch) by
  z = (W)^T x_patch with
  W = R W^0,    R ∈ R^{d×d},    R^T R = I.
  So R multiplies every output-kernel column w_j on the left: w_j' = R w_j. Intuitively R rotates/reflects the input-space basis that all output kernels share.

2) What block-diagonal R means concretely
- Suppose R is block-diagonal with r blocks. Partition the d rows into r disjoint blocks (row index sets), and write
  R = diag(R_1, …, R_r),
  where R_b ∈ R^{d_b × d_b} and ∑_b d_b = d.
- For each output channel j, the transformed kernel decomposes as the concatenation of block-wise transforms:
  w_j' = [R_1 w_{j,(1)}; R_2 w_{j,(2)}; …; R_r w_{j,(r)}],
  where w_{j,(b)} are the entries of w_j that belong to block b.
- So block R_b only acts on that fixed subset of input+spatial coordinates for every output channel — it never mixes entries from different blocks.

3) The special case r = C_in (the paper’s example)
- Choose the blocks to group the k·k spatial coefficients per input channel:
  d_b = k·k for b = 1..C_in, so r = C_in and block b corresponds to input channel b.
- Then R_b ∈ R^{(k·k)×(k·k)} acts on the spatial kernel for input channel b. For output j, the subvector of w_j that is the k×k kernel over input channel b is transformed by R_b.
- Consequences:
  - There is no mixing between different input channels (off-diagonal blocks are zero).
  - All output channels see the same per-input-channel transform R_b (i.e., R_b is shared across outputs).
  - This is analogous to depth-wise operations because each input channel is transformed independently; however, note the difference: OFT's R_b operates in kernel-space (it rotates the k×k filter coefficients of channel b) and is shared across outputs, while depthwise convolutions operate per-channel across spatial positions in the forward pass. The high-level effect is a per-input-channel learned orthogonal re-basis of the spatial kernel.

4) If you want mixing across channels
- Use larger blocks that span multiple input channels (e.g., grouping channels into blocks, or using r = 1). A single full block (r=1) allows R to mix all spatial positions and all channels together, so the orthogonal transform can rotate information across channels as well as spatial positions.
- So blocking is a trade-off: more blocks → less cross-channel flexibility but fewer parameters and stronger inductive bias (per-channel structure); fewer blocks → more flexibility at higher parameter cost.

5) Intuition and practical notes
- Parameter / compute: block-diagonal R reduces parameter count and avoids inverting a huge matrix (Cayley parameterization + smaller inverses). With r = C_in and small k (e.g. 3×3), each R_b is small (9×9), so training is cheap.
- Inductive bias: r = C_in enforces that the model only re-bases spatial patterns inside each input channel. This can be desirable if channel semantics are meaningful and you want a parameter-efficient, depthwise-like adaptation. If the downstream task needs to create or re-align cross-channel structure, that blocking will limit you — use fewer blocks.
- Block sharing: the paper also mentions optionally sharing R_b across blocks (R_i = R_j) as another way to reduce parameters; that makes the same orthogonal transform apply to multiple blocks (e.g. same spatial rotation for many channels).

Summary
- R multiplies the flattened conv kernels on the input/spatial dimension: W = R W^0.
- Block-diagonal R partitions that input/spatial dimension and applies independent orthogonal transforms to each partition.
- If r = C_in with blocks sized k·k, each block acts on one input channel’s k×k spatial kernel across all outputs — so no cross-channel mixing (channel-wise spatial transforms, depthwise-like). To permit channel mixing use larger blocks (smaller r) or r = 1 for full mixing.

### How do OFT and LoRA compare algebraically — are they equivalent updates under some conditions?
Question:
The paper rewrites OFT as \(W^0+(R-I)W^0\) and says the term \((R-I)W^0\) is analogous to LoRA's low-rank additive update. Under what conditions (e.g. low-rank \(R-I\) and full-rank \(W^0\)) do OFT and LoRA become similar? Can OFT represent arbitrary LoRA updates or vice versa?
Answer:
Short answer
- Algebraically OFT is a multiplicative re-parameterization of the pretrained weight: W = R W^0, so the "additive" view in the paper is
  W = W^0 + (R - I) W^0.
  LoRA is an explicit low-rank additive update W = W^0 + A B (with rank(AB) ≤ r').
- If (R - I) is low-rank then the OFT additive term is low-rank and can be written in LoRA form. Concretely, if R - I has rank r' then
  (R - I) W^0 = U (V^T W^0)
  (SVD or any factorization), so you can set A = U and B = V^T W^0 and recover the OFT update as a LoRA update.
- Conversely, an arbitrary LoRA update AB can be represented by an orthogonal R only in very special cases: there must exist an orthogonal matrix R such that R W^0 = W^0 + AB. A necessary (and typically strong) condition for that to hold is that the pairwise inner products (Gram matrix) of the columns of W^0 are preserved:
  (W^0 + AB)^T (W^0 + AB) = (W^0)^T W^0.
  In other words LoRA can represent OFT updates when the additive change happens to come from (R - I)W^0 for some orthogonal R; but a generic low-rank AB will not satisfy the orthogonality constraints, so LoRA generally cannot represent an arbitrary OFT update.

Longer / intuitive explanation, with algebraic statements and implications

1) Start point — the two parameterizations
- OFT (paper): learn an orthogonal R ∈ R^{d×d} and use
  W = R W^0 = W^0 + (R − I) W^0.
  The paper emphasizes the multiplicative/orthogonal nature of R (it preserves angles / hyperspherical energy).
- LoRA: parameterize an additive low-rank change
  W = W^0 + A B, with A ∈ R^{d×r'}, B ∈ R^{r'×n} so rank(A B) ≤ r'. This is an explicit low-rank additive update.

2) When OFT can be written as LoRA (OFT ⊂ LoRA in these cases)
- Use the rank inequality rank((R − I) W^0) ≤ rank(R − I). So if R − I has small rank r', then the OFT additive term (R − I) W^0 is low-rank (rank ≤ r'), hence can be factored as A B. Concretely take an SVD or any factorization of (R − I):
  R − I = U V^T  (U ∈ R^{d×r'}, V ∈ R^{d×r'}),
  then
  (R − I) W^0 = U (V^T W^0),
  so set A = U and B = V^T W^0 to get LoRA form.
- Practical sources of low rank for R − I: if R differs from I only on a few low-dimensional subspaces (e.g., a small rotation in a subspace of dimension r', or a block-diagonal R with many identity blocks), then R − I will be low-rank or close to low-rank. The paper’s block-diagonal or small-block Cayley parameterizations can make R − I effectively low-dimensional, so in practice OFT updates can sometimes be expressed in LoRA style.

3) When LoRA can be written as OFT (LoRA ⊂ OFT rarely)
- To represent a LoRA update AB exactly by OFT we would need an orthogonal R satisfying
  R W^0 = W^0 + A B  ⇔  (R − I) W^0 = A B.
  This is a system of linear constraints on R plus the nonlinear orthogonality constraint R^T R = I. A simple necessary condition follows from orthogonality:
  (R W^0)^T (R W^0) = (W^0)^T W^0.
  Therefore a necessary condition for exact representability is
  (W^0 + A B)^T (W^0 + A B) = (W^0)^T W^0,
  i.e. the Gram matrix of the columns (pairwise inner products) must be preserved by the update. The paper emphasizes that OFT preserves pairwise angles / hyperspherical energy — this is precisely the structural constraint that an AB must meet to be realizable by an orthogonal R.
- Is that condition sufficient? In finite dimensions it is a very strong constraint: both column norms and all pairwise inner products must match after the update. If W^0 has full row rank and square invertible (d = n, W^0 invertible), one can write R = (W^0 + AB) (W^0)^{-1} and check orthogonality, which reduces to the same Gram condition. In general, exact existence of R is not guaranteed unless those structural equalities hold. Thus a generic low-rank AB will not come from any orthogonal R; the usual LoRA updates do not enforce Gram-preservation and so typically cannot be realized by OFT.

4) Intuition and practical consequences
- OFT’s additive view (R − I) W^0 is indeed “analogous” to LoRA in the sense that both are additive modifications to W^0. But they are structurally different:
  - OFT’s additive change is tied to W^0 and constrained by orthogonality of R (it preserves angles among neurons).
  - LoRA’s additive change is arbitrary but low-rank by construction (it does not enforce angle preservation).
- If you restrict (R − I) to a low-rank subspace (e.g., small rotations only on a few dimensions), then OFT ≈ LoRA algebraically and you can convert between representations. Conversely, if you insist on arbitrary low-rank AB (LoRA), you usually break the hyperspherical-energy / Gram structure that OFT preserves and so no orthogonal R exists to realize that AB.
- This explains complementary empirical behavior reported in the paper: LOw-rank LoRA gives parameter efficiency but does not preserve hyperspherical energy (can damage pretrained behavior), while OFT enforces angle preservation which yields more stable finetuning and preserves semantic properties — they trade different structural priors.

5) Small notes / extensions mentioned in the paper
- Re-scaled OFT (R W^0 D) adds a diagonal scaling D. The additive change relative to W^0 is
  R W^0 D − W^0 = (R − I) W^0 D + W^0 (D − I).
  This is more flexible than plain OFT and the second term W^0 (D − I) typically has rank at most rank(W^0) but is structured (it depends on W^0). Representability by LoRA still reduces to the same type of factorization arguments but with extra terms to match.
- COFT constrains R to stay close to I; small R − I is easier to approximate by a low-rank AB in practice, which is why COFT can be parameter-efficient and compatible with low-rank approximations.

Bottom line
- Algebraically: OFT update = (R − I) W^0, LoRA update = A B.
- IF rank(R − I) is small, OFT’s additive term can be factored into LoRA form (so OFT can be seen as a structured low-rank update).
- BUT an arbitrary LoRA update will not in general equal (R − I) W^0 for any orthogonal R, because OFT requires Gram/angle-preservation constraints that LoRA does not enforce. So the two are not equivalent in general; they overlap only on the special subset of updates that both are capable of (low-rank and angle-preserving in the required way).

### How is the COFT constraint \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\) enforced in practice given Cayley parameterization?
Question:
COFT requires \(\|R-I\|\le\epsilon\). The paper approximates \(R\approx I+2Q+O(Q^2)\) and enforces a constraint on \(Q\) instead. Which matrix norm is used for the constraint (operator norm, Frobenius)? How is projection implemented (clipping singular values, scaling)? How is \(\epsilon'\) mapped to \(\epsilon\) in practice?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper reasons about the constraint in the operator (spectral) norm (this appears where they invoke the Neumann series “converges in the operator norm”).  
- They enforce the constraint by projecting the Cayley parameter matrix Q (skew-symmetric) into a norm-ball after each gradient step (projected gradient descent).  
- Using the exact Cayley identity one gets the mapping
  R − I = 2 Q (I − Q)^{-1}, so (in the same operator norm)
  ||R − I|| ≤ 2||Q|| / (1 − ||Q||).
  Solving this for ||Q|| gives the safe bound
  ||Q|| ≤ epsilon / (2 + epsilon).
  For small epsilon this reduces to the paper’s linearized approximation ||R − I|| ≈ 2||Q|| so epsilon' ≈ epsilon/2.

Detailed explanation, derivation and practical notes

1) Which norm?
- The paper explicitly mentions the Neumann series “converges in the operator norm”, so the mathematically consistent reading is that they are reasoning in the operator (spectral) norm (the matrix 2-norm, i.e. the largest singular value).  
- In practice you can also enforce a Frobenius-ball constraint on Q (it is simpler), but the tight theoretical statements in the paper (and the Neumann-series based conversion) are in terms of the operator norm. If you want the theoretical guarantee in the paper, use the operator norm.

2) Exact relation between R and Q (useful for mapping epsilons)
Start from Cayley parameterization
R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} with Q = −Q^T.
Rearrange:
R − I = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} − I = 2 Q (I − Q)^{-1}.

Take an induced norm (operator norm ||·||):
||R − I|| = ||2 Q (I − Q)^{-1}|| ≤ 2 ||Q|| · ||(I − Q)^{-1}||.
If ||Q|| < 1 then Neumann series gives ||(I − Q)^{-1}|| ≤ 1/(1 − ||Q||), so
||R − I|| ≤ 2 ||Q|| / (1 − ||Q||).

Solve for ||Q|| in terms of an allowed ||R − I|| ≤ epsilon:
2 ||Q|| / (1 − ||Q||) ≤ epsilon  ⇒  ||Q|| ≤ epsilon / (2 + epsilon).

Thus, if you enforce ||Q|| ≤ epsilon' with epsilon' = epsilon/(2 + epsilon) (in the same operator norm), the constraint ||R − I|| ≤ epsilon is guaranteed (using the above inequality). For small epsilon this simplifies to epsilon' ≈ epsilon/2, which matches the paper’s linearized approximation R ≈ I + 2Q.

3) How to implement the projection (practical algorithms)
- Projected gradient descent on Q: after each SGD/Adam step update Q ← Q − η∇_Q, then project Q back into the chosen norm-ball of radius epsilon' (centered at 0). This is what the paper says (“can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent”).
- Projection details:
  - Operator (spectral) norm ball of radius τ: compute the top singular value σ_max of Q (power iteration is enough and cheap) and, if σ_max > τ set Q_proj = (τ / σ_max) · Q (i.e. scale the whole matrix). This is a standard projection to the spectral norm ball (minimizes Frobenius distance to Q subject to spectral norm ≤ τ).
  - Frobenius norm ball of radius τ: compute ‖Q‖_F and, if > τ, set Q_proj = (τ / ‖Q‖_F) · Q (simple scaling).
- Which to pick: if you want to follow the paper’s derivations and guarantee Neumann convergence, use the operator norm projection with τ = epsilon/(2 + epsilon). If you prefer cheaper computations and are willing to accept a weaker theoretical bound, use Frobenius projection (but then mapping between epsilon and epsilon' is no longer the operator-norm identity above).

4) Practical recommendations and numerical caveats
- Use operator norm projection and approximate the largest singular value with a few iterations of power method to avoid full SVD (this works well with block-diagonal Q as in the paper).  
- Initialize Q = 0 (the paper does this), which yields R = I initially.  
- Keep tau small enough to ensure ||Q|| < 1; if ||Q|| approaches 1 the Cayley transform becomes numerically unstable (the inverse (I − Q) becomes ill-conditioned). The derived safe bound epsilon' = epsilon/(2 + epsilon) keeps ||Q|| strictly below 1 for any finite epsilon.  
- Monitor the actual ||R − I|| during training occasionally (compute R from Q and compute its norm) to verify the constraint is respected in the implemented norm. Because R − I = 2Q(I − Q)^{-1}, the higher-order terms can matter if Q is not tiny; using the exact bound above (not just linearization) is safer.  
- If you want a very conservative implementation, choose epsilon' slightly smaller than epsilon/(2 + epsilon) to allow for numerical rounding and optimization steps that may transiently push Q near the ball boundary.

5) Short worked numeric example
- Suppose you want ||R − I|| ≤ 0.1. Using operator-norm mapping:
  epsilon' = 0.1 / (2 + 0.1) ≈ 0.04762. So project Q to have spectral norm ≤ 0.04762 after every update. The linear approximation epsilon/2 would give 0.05, which is close but slightly less conservative.

Summary
- Paper reasons in operator norm; they enforce constraint by projecting Q via projected gradient descent.  
- The exact Cayley relation yields R − I = 2Q(I − Q)^{-1} so a safe operator-norm mapping is ||Q|| ≤ epsilon/(2 + epsilon). For small epsilon the linearized rule epsilon' ≈ epsilon/2 is an okay approximation.  
- In practice, project Q by scaling (spectral-norm projection via power iteration or Frobenius projection if you prefer cheaper ops), and monitor ||R − I|| to be safe.

### When using the Neumann series approximation \(R\approx I+2Q+O(Q^2)\), when does the series converge?
Question:
The paper assumes the Neumann series for \((I-Q)^{-1}\) converges in operator norm. For what spectral radius of \(Q\) does the series converge? Is there an empirical or theoretical guideline to keep \(\|Q\|<1\)? What happens if \(\|Q\|\ge 1\) during training?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Neumann series (I−Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k converges in operator norm whenever the spectral radius \rho(Q)<1; a convenient sufficient (and commonly used) condition is \|Q\|<1 in whatever operator norm you use.  
- In the paper Q is skew–symmetric (Cayley parameterization), so Q is normal and \|Q\|_2 = \rho(Q), so the two conditions coincide: keeping \|Q\|_2<1 guarantees convergence.  
- If you want the linear approximation R \approx I + 2Q to be accurate, keep \|Q\| well below 1; the operator‑norm error can be bounded (see below). If \|Q\| approaches or exceeds 1 you may get numerical instability or a non‑invertible (I−Q), so COFT enforces a constraint \|Q\|\le\epsilon' (projected gradient) to avoid that.

Longer explanation, intuition, and practical guidance

1) Convergence condition (mathematics)
- The Neumann series identity is
  (I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k,
  which converges in operator norm if the powers Q^k go to zero in operator norm. A standard sufficient condition is
  \|Q\| < 1,
  because then \sum_{k=0}^\infty \|Q\|^k is a convergent geometric series and the series converges absolutely in operator norm.

- More generally (and slightly sharper) the Neumann series converges iff the spectral radius satisfies
  \rho(Q) < 1.
  In Banach algebra terms, \rho(Q) = \lim_{k\to\infty}\|Q^k\|^{1/k}; if \rho(Q)<1 then \|Q^k\| decays geometrically and the series converges in operator norm as well. So \rho(Q)<1 is necessary and sufficient for the existence of (I−Q)^{-1} given by the Neumann series; \|Q\|<1 is a convenient sufficient condition.

2) Why for this paper the two conditions are effectively the same
- The paper constructs Q to be skew–symmetric (Q^\top = −Q) in the Cayley parameterization. Skew–symmetric real matrices are normal, so their spectral norm equals their spectral radius (in absolute value). Concretely, for Q skew–symmetric
  \|Q\|_2 = \rho(Q).
  Therefore enforcing \|Q\|_2<1 is exactly the same as \rho(Q)<1 here. That is why the paper’s statement “assume the Neumann series converges in operator norm” reduces to keeping \|Q\|<1.

3) How accurate is the first‑order approximation R \approx I + 2Q?
- From R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} = (I+Q)\sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k you get the exact expansion
  R = I + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty Q^k = I + 2Q + 2Q^2 + 2Q^3 + \dots
  So the first‑order truncation error (the remainder after I+2Q) is
  E := R - (I + 2Q) = 2\sum_{k=2}^\infty Q^k.
  If \|Q\|<1 this is bounded in operator norm by the geometric series
  \|E\| \le 2\sum_{k=2}^\infty \|Q\|^k = \frac{2\|Q\|^2}{1-\|Q\|}.
- Practical consequence: to make the linear approximation trustworthy you want \|Q\| to be small. Example: \|Q\|=0.1 gives \|E\| \le 0.0222, while \|Q\|=0.5 gives \|E\| \le 1.0 (large).

4) What happens if \|Q\|\ge 1 during training?
- If \rho(Q)\ge 1 then (I-Q) is not invertible or the Neumann series does not converge; the Cayley formula (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} is ill‑defined (or numerically unstable when (I-Q) is nearly singular). For the paper’s skew–symmetric Q, \|Q\|_2\ge1 directly implies \rho(Q)\ge1, so you can hit a real singularity or huge numerical errors.
- Practically, problems you may observe if you violate the condition:
  - (I-Q) becomes ill‑conditioned and computing (I-Q)^{-1} is numerically unstable (large gradient spikes, NaNs, training collapse).
  - The linear approximation used to justify the \|R−I\|≈2\|Q\| intuition breaks down, so the intended small perturbation regime is lost and OFT/COFT can stop behaving as “small, stable” finetuning.
- That is exactly why COFT makes the \|Q\|\le\epsilon' constraint explicit and enforces it (projected gradient, identity initialization). Keeping Q small prevents these failure modes.

5) Practical recommendations (grounded in the paper’s approach)
- Initialize Q=0 (the paper does this), so you start in the safe region where the Cayley map yields R=I.
- Enforce an explicit spectral‑norm (operator‑norm) bound on Q, e.g. with projected gradient descent: after each update, if \|Q\| > \epsilon' scale Q := (\epsilon'/\|Q\|) Q. This is the COFT strategy described in the paper.
- Choose \epsilon' comfortably below 1 so that:
  - the Neumann/Cayley expansion is valid and the linear approximation is accurate, and
  - (I-Q) stays well‑conditioned. The paper demonstrates empirically that small \epsilon improves stability (see their Figure about \epsilon). The exact choice of \epsilon' is a hyperparameter — start small and widen if you need more finetuning flexibility.
- Monitor \|Q\| (or the condition number of I−Q) during training; if it drifts toward 1 you should tighten the projection or reduce step size.

Summary
- The theoretical requirement for the Neumann series is \rho(Q)<1; in practice enforce \|Q\|<1 (and preferably \|Q\|\ll 1) so the Cayley map is safe and the first‑order approximation R\approx I+2Q is accurate. COFT implements this by constraining \|Q\|\le\epsilon' (projected gradient) and initializing Q=0 to ensure stable, well‑conditioned finetuning.

### Is HE(W) well-defined if two normalized neurons become identical (zero denominator)?
Question:
HE uses terms \(\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|^{-1}\). If during training two neurons align exactly, that difference becomes zero and the term blows up. How is this handled numerically? Do they regularize or cap the inverse distances? Does orthogonality avoid this issue?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper does not compute or optimize HE (hyperspherical energy) directly during finetuning, so it never has to evaluate terms like $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ and therefore does not need to "cap" or regularize those inverses.
- Instead OFT enforces that the finetuned weights are obtained by multiplying the pretrained weights by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (Eq. (3)). Orthogonal transforms exactly preserve pairwise angles and distances on the unit hypersphere, so they cannot create a new zero-distance between two normalized neurons if one did not already exist in the pretrained weights.
- COFT adds an explicit small-deviation constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon$ (implemented approximately by constraining the Cayley parameter $\mathbf{Q}$), which also helps numerical stability. The paper does not state any ad-hoc clamping of inverse distances because it avoids evaluating them.

Longer explanation and practical points

1) Why the apparent singularity arises only if you actually compute HE
The hyperspherical energy used in the paper is written as
HE(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1},
with \hat{\mathbf w}_i := \mathbf w_i / \|\mathbf w_i\|. If two normalized neurons become exactly equal, a term becomes \|0\|^{-1} and HE is formally infinite / undefined. So a naive algorithm that directly evaluates or optimizes HE must deal with that (for example by adding a small epsilon in the denominator or clamping).

However the paper does not follow that naive route. Equation (1) is used to motivate the objective (preserve pairwise angles), but OFT enforces this preservation by parameterizing the finetuned layer as
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,  subject to \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}   (Eq. (3)).
An orthogonal map preserves Euclidean distances and angles exactly:
\|\widehat{\mathbf R \mathbf w_i} - \widehat{\mathbf R \mathbf w_j}\| = \|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|.
Therefore OFT preserves HE by construction, without needing to compute HE or its inverse-distance terms during optimization. Because of that design choice there is no place in the training code where a direct division by zero in HE occurs.

2) Does orthogonality eliminate the possibility of encountering a zero denominator?
- If the pretrained normalized neurons \hat{\mathbf w}_i^0 already contain an exactly identical pair, then HE(\mathbf{W}^0) would already be singular (infinite). OFT would preserve that equality exactly (orthogonal maps keep identical vectors identical up to numerical precision), so the problem would exist in the pretrained model itself rather than induced by OFT.
- If the pretrained neurons are distinct (the normal case for real-valued pretrained models), an orthogonal transform cannot create a new exact equality between normalized neurons: distances/angles are preserved exactly in exact arithmetic. So OFT will not produce a zero denominator if none existed in \mathbf{W}^0.
- In floating-point arithmetic you can get "near" equality and loss of precision, but OFT's multiplicative orthogonal update does not tend to drive previously distinct normalized columns to exact equality.

3) Numerical aspects of the Cayley parameterization and COFT
The paper enforces orthogonality via a Cayley parameterization (Section 3.3):
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},  with \mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}.
A few useful technical facts:
- For real skew-symmetric \mathbf{Q}, eigenvalues of \mathbf{Q} are pure imaginary, so \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} is generically invertible (its eigenvalues are 1 minus an imaginary number, never zero). Thus the Cayley map is well-defined under normal conditions; you do not get division-by-zero in computing (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} unless \mathbf{Q} is pathological (very large) or numerics blow up.
- The paper further improves stability by: (a) using block-diagonal \mathbf{R} to reduce parameter count and keep \mathbf{Q} small in each block, and (b) in COFT they constrain \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon. They show that via the Neumann-series expansion \mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\mathbf{Q}^2), you can equivalently constrain \|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\epsilon' and enforce that with projected gradient descent. That projection is an explicit numerical safeguard keeping \mathbf{Q} small and the inversion stable.
- The paper does not mention adding an epsilon inside HE denominators nor any explicit clamping of HE terms because the algorithm never needs to compute HE or its inverse terms in training.

4) If you wanted to evaluate HE explicitly (for monitoring or diagnostics)
The paper motivates HE but does not use it as a loss to be computed each step. If you as an implementer want to evaluate HE for monitoring, be aware:
- Use a small numerical floor: replace \|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1} with 1 / (\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\| + \delta) for tiny \delta (e.g. 1e-6) to avoid blow-ups from near-zero distances.
- Compute in double precision if you expect very small distances.
- If HE is extremely large for your pretrained model, that indicates exact or near exact duplicated normalized neurons in \mathbf{W}^0 and you should inspect/perturb the pretrained weights (or drop duplicate channels) before OFT.

Summary / recommended practical safeguards
- The paper avoids the division-by-zero issue by never directly optimizing HE; it enforces HE preservation by construction via orthogonal transforms.
- Orthogonality prevents creation of new zero-distance pairs (if none were present initially).
- Numerical stability is handled in practice by using Cayley parameterization, block structure and constraining the Cayley parameter \mathbf{Q} in COFT (projected gradient descent). The paper does not propose capping inverse distances because it never evaluates them in the inner loop.
- If you plan to compute HE yourself for analysis, add a tiny epsilon to denominators and/or use double precision and check the pretrained weights for duplicates.

### How are OFT parameters allocated for Transformer attention layers in Stable Diffusion?
Question:
The experiments say OFT was applied to the same layers where LoRA is used (typically attention). Which matrices in cross-attention / self-attention were transformed with \(R\) exactly (query/key/value/proj_out)? Were the transforms applied to weight shapes like \(d\times d\) or different? Any implementation details would help.
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper applies OFT to the same attention linear layers that LoRA is applied to (the paper repeatedly says “we only apply OFT to the same layer where LoRA is used (typically attention)”). The paper does not list in the main text a per-layer enumeration like “we applied R to query only” vs “q/k/v/out”; instead it follows the LoRA convention used in the experiments.
- Mechanically: OFT multiplies the pretrained weight matrix on the input side by an orthogonal matrix R of size d×d (or a block‑diagonal version). Concretely, if the layer has an input dimensionality d (the feature dimension that the linear layer consumes), OFT uses R ∈ R^{d×d} (or block diag of smaller orthogonal blocks) and replaces W^0 by W = R W^0 during finetuning (Eq. (3) in the paper). In PyTorch terms (weight shape (out_features, in_features)), that corresponds to replacing weight by weight @ R^T at runtime, or pre-multiplying the transposed weight by R.

Longer, reproducible explanation and implementation notes

1) What the paper explicitly says and what it means
- The paper: “To compare fairly to LoRA, we only apply OFT or COFT to the same layer where LoRA is used. … For Transformers, LoRA is typically applied to the attention weights.” That is the explicit experimental statement. The paper therefore applies OFT to the attention weight layers (the same linear layers LoRA would target), but it does not enumerate “only q” or “q+v” in the main text — it follows whatever LoRA placement the authors used in their Stable Diffusion experiments (details are in their Appendices / implementation notes).
- What OFT does to a linear layer: using the notation of the paper, a layer weight is W^0 ∈ R^{d×n} (their convention: columns are neurons w_i ∈ R^d) and the layer computes z = (W^0)^T x. OFT learns an orthogonal matrix R ∈ R^{d×d} and changes the forward pass to
  z = (R W^0)^T x,  subject to R^T R = I.  (Eq. (3) in the paper.)
  So the orthogonal transform acts on the input dimension side (the d dimension).

2) Mapping to transformer attention projection matrices (q/k/v/out)
- Standard attention uses small linear layers that map from a common input dimension (d_model or head_dim) to per-projection outputs. In common frameworks:
  - Each projection (q, k, v, and the final out projection) is a PyTorch Linear with weight shape (out_features, in_features) e.g. (d_model, d_model) or (num_heads*head_dim, d_model).
- Applying OFT to an attention projection layer means inserting R so it left‑multiplies W^0 in the paper’s notation. In PyTorch weight conventions this is equivalent to right-multiplying the stored weight by R^T:
  - If weight_pytorch has shape (out, in) (call this W_p), then W_p'.T = R W^0, so W_p' = W_p R^T.
- Which actual attention projections to transform? The paper’s experiments applied OFT to the same attention weights used for LoRA; if you want a faithful reproduction:
  - Use the same LoRA placement you would normally use for Stable Diffusion. (In LoRA papers/recipes people commonly finetune q and/or v and sometimes the output projection; practice varies.)
  - The paper does not explicitly restrict OFT to only q, or only v, etc. The principle is: apply OFT to the attention linear layers you want to adapt, in the same positions you would have applied LoRA for a fair comparison.

3) Sizes and block-diagonal parameterization
- Full R is d×d. If d is large this is expensive, so the paper uses two parameter-efficiency techniques:
  - Block-diagonal R: R = diag(R_1, ..., R_r) with r blocks of size d/r each. Each R_i is orthogonal of size (d/r)×(d/r). This reduces trainable parameters from O(d^2) to O(d^2 / r).
  - Block sharing (optional): share blocks to reduce parameters further.
- Practical mapping to attention heads: a convenient, semantic choice is to make blocks correspond to attention heads (i.e. block size = head_dim). The paper notes this interpretation (block per channel/head behaves like depth-wise transformation). That is a natural and common practical choice: it rotates each head’s input subspace independently.

4) Cayley param, initialization, COFT constraint and PyTorch implementation tips
- Parameterization the paper used:
  - They form orthogonal R via Cayley transform: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^T). Cayley outputs orthogonals with determinant +1 (special orthogonal group). Initialize Q = 0 so R starts as I (identity), so finetuning starts exactly from pretrained weights.
  - For COFT they add the constraint ||R − I|| ≤ ε. They approximate with the Cayley Q and constrain ||Q|| ≤ ε' (project Q back to the ball after each gradient step).
- PyTorch application pattern (conceptual, matching paper equations):
  - Let W_p be the stored weight of a Linear layer (shape (out, in)). Let R be an in×in orthogonal (or block-diag) matrix.
  - Replace the forward product z = x @ W_p.T with z = x @ (W_p R^T).T = (W_p R^T)^T x, equivalently compute an adjusted weight W_p' = W_p @ R.T and use W_p' as the effective weight during finetuning. (Or compute R W^0 in the paper notation and transpose appropriately.)
  - Initialize R = I so W_p' == W_p initially.
  - For inference you can permanently fold R into the pretrained weight: W_final = (R W^0) (or in PyTorch W_final_p = W_p @ R.T), so no runtime overhead.

5) Rescaled OFT
- The paper also describes a Rescaled OFT variant that multiplies by a diagonal output-scaling matrix D: z = (R W^0 D)^T x. This adds per-output-neuron scalar multipliers (D is n×n diag with positive entries). In PyTorch terms the stored weight becomes W_p' = D W_p R^T (because of transposes). This is optional; the main OFT in experiments uses only R.

6) Practical recommendations to reproduce experiments
- To faithfully reproduce the paper’s comparisons to LoRA:
  - Apply OFT to the exact same linear attention layers you would apply LoRA to in Stable Diffusion’s attention modules (this is what the authors did).
  - Use block-diagonal R with block size chosen to trade off parameter budget vs flexibility — a natural choice is block size = head_dim (one block per head).
  - Initialize Q = 0 (so R = I) and use Cayley parameterization (or any differentiable orthogonalization) so the finetuning starts at the pretrained model.
  - If you want the extra stability COFT provides, constrain the norm of Q after each update (project back to the ball) to realize the ||R − I|| ≤ ε constraint approximately via the Neumann-series approximation they describe.

Summary (concise)
- The paper applies OFT to the attention linear layers used by LoRA (the paper does not list “q only” vs “q,k,v,o” explicitly in the main text; they matched LoRA placement in experiments).
- OFT multiplies the pretrained weight on the input side by R ∈ R^{d×d} (or a block-diagonal version). In PyTorch weight terms (weight shape (out,in)) the runtime effect is weight := weight @ R^T.
- You can choose block size to control parameter count (making blocks equal to head_dim is a natural choice for attention). R is parameterized via Cayley (Q skew-symmetric), initialized to identity, and optionally constrained (COFT).

If you tell me which LoRA placement you plan to compare to (q only, q+v, q+k+v+out, etc.), I can give the exact per-layer PyTorch weight update formula and a short code sketch showing where to insert the R multiplication.

### How is block size \(r\) chosen and how does it affect expressivity vs parameter cost?
Question:
Block-diagonal \(R\) reduces parameters by factor \(r\). Is there a recommended rule-of-thumb for choosing \(r\) relative to dimension \(d\)? How does increasing \(r\) (smaller blocks) change the model's ability to represent useful rotations and the empirical finetuning performance?
Answer:
Short answer
- Block count r controls a direct trade-off: more blocks (larger r, smaller block size d/r) → far fewer OFT parameters and stronger regularization but strictly less ability to express global rotations; fewer blocks (small r, bigger blocks) → more expressive (approaches a full d×d orthogonal) at quadratic parameter cost.
- There is no single magic r recommended in the paper; the authors treat r as a tunable knob and give the parameter-count formulas so you can pick r to match your parameter/computation budget or to match LoRA’s number of trainable parameters. Empirically the paper reports that OFT performance tends to improve when you give it more trainable parameters (i.e. smaller r), but block-diagonalization is useful to stabilise finetuning and save cost.
- Practical recipe (from the paper’s discussion): pick r to hit your parameter budget (or to be comparable to a LoRA baseline), sweep r over coarse values (e.g. r in {1,2,4,8,16} → block sizes d/r), and use COFT’s epsilon if you need additional stability/regularization.

Longer explanation, intuition, and formulas

1) What r means mathematically and how parameter count scales
- OFT parameterizes the layer-wise orthogonal matrix R as block-diagonal with r blocks, each block of size (d/r) × (d/r).
- A full d×d orthogonal has (d(d−1))/2 free parameters. For r blocks the total number of orthogonal parameters is
  \[
  \#\text{params}_{\text{OFT}} \;=\; r\cdot\frac{(d/r)\big((d/r)-1\big)}{2}
  \;=\;\frac{d\big(\tfrac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2}
  \]
  so asymptotically the parameter cost is O(d^2/r). (If you share the same block across groups you can reduce cost further towards O(d^2/r^2) as the paper describes.)
- Example (paper’s toy example): for a 128×128 weight, OFT with r=8 uses 960 parameters, while full orthogonal would be 8128 parameters.

2) Intuition: what changing r does to expressivity and regularization
- r = 1 (one block of size d): you can represent any orthogonal in O(d) (maximum expressivity, maximum parameter cost ~d^2/2).
- r = d (blocks of size 1): each block is a 1×1 orthogonal (i.e. ±1). This is extremely cheap but extremely constrained — essentially per-coordinate sign flips (and scalings if you add re-scaled OFT).
- Intermediate r: rotations are allowed only within each block subspace of dimension d/r. That means any mixing across coordinates that belong to different blocks cannot be represented. So:
  - Increasing r (smaller blocks) reduces capacity to express global rotations that mix many coordinates, but enforces stronger structure and acts as a regularizer.
  - Decreasing r (larger blocks) increases ability to represent complex, cross-channel rotations, but costs parameters and compute and risks higher variance or instability if the downstream data are limited.

3) Computational trade-offs (besides parameter count)
- Cayley parameterization requires computing a matrix inverse per block: cost scales with block size. Smaller blocks reduce the cubic cost per-block (roughly O((d/r)^3) per-block inverse), so larger r can reduce compute for the Cayley transform.
- Block sharing reduces parameter cost further (and increases regularization).

4) Practical guidance (what the paper suggests and what follows directly from it)
- The paper presents r as a tunable hyperparameter and highlights the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. It does not give a single universal r; instead:
  - If you have a tight parameter budget or very small finetuning dataset (high overfitting risk), choose larger r (smaller blocks) for stronger regularization and cheaper params.
  - If you can afford parameters and the downstream task requires significant change of neuron directions (high expressivity needed), choose smaller r (bigger blocks), approaching r=1 if you want maximum rotational freedom.
  - A practical way used/encouraged by the paper: choose r so that OFT’s parameter count is comparable to the adapter/LoRA budget you want to compete with. The paper gives both OFT and LoRA parameter-count formulas so you can pick r that matches your target (or explore a small grid).
- The authors note empirically that OFT’s performance "has not yet saturated" as parameters grow — i.e., giving OFT more capacity (smaller r) tends to improve performance in their experiments, but at the usual cost/overfitting trade-offs.

5) A simple actionable recipe (based on the paper)
- Decide your allowable extra parameter budget P (or use LoRA’s r' as a reference).
- Solve (or compute) r from the OFT param formula to meet P:
  \[
  P \approx \frac{d\big(\tfrac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad r\approx\frac{d}{1+\tfrac{2P}{d}}.
  \]
  (Use this as a starting point; r must be an integer dividing d in the paper’s block layout.)
- Run a small sweep r ∈ {1,2,4,8,16} (i.e. block sizes d, d/2, d/4, …). Monitor validation control/subject metrics and finetuning stability.
  - If training collapses or you overfit: increase r (more blocks) or use COFT (set a smaller epsilon).
  - If the model underfits / fails to adapt: decrease r (larger blocks) or allow larger COFT epsilon.
- Consider block sharing (shared R_i) or re-scaled OFT (add per-neuron diagonal D) if you need more expressivity with small extra param cost.

6) Empirical behavior reported in the paper (what you can expect)
- The paper emphasizes that block-diagonal OFT is a meaningful regularizer: block-diagonalization improves finetuning stability and sample efficiency in their tasks (subject-driven and controllable generation).
- They also report that giving OFT more trainable parameters tends to improve performance (so smaller r can yield better final metrics), but it comes at higher parameter/computation cost. The block-diagonal parameterization is explicitly described as a way to reduce parameter cost and introduce bias that improves stability.

7) Extra practical notes
- Block size interacts with compute: smaller block sizes are cheaper for the Cayley inverse and for memory, so if compute is the bottleneck, use larger r.
- If you are finetuning convolution layers, the paper notes an interpretation: choosing r equal to the number of input channels gives per-channel transforms (like depthwise), while shared blocks give channel-shared rotations — pick r with that structural meaning in mind.
- Combine the r sweep with COFT’s epsilon constraint if you want to decouple expressivity (r) from how far you are allowed to move from identity (epsilon). COFT can improve stability when you choose smaller r but still want to limit deviation.

Summary
- r is a direct knob between expressivity and parameter cost: more blocks → cheaper + more regularized (less expressive); fewer blocks → more expressive but quadratically more parameters.
- The paper does not prescribe a single r; instead it gives the formulas and examples and recommends choosing r to match your budget/tasks and then sweeping it, using COFT to further control stability if needed.
- Practical starting point: pick r so OFT’s parameter count is comparable to your LoRA budget or try r in {1,2,4,8,16} and monitor validation metrics and stability; increase r if you see overfitting or instability, decrease r if the model underfits.

### Why does re-scaled OFT add a diagonal scaling \(D\) and how does that preserve HE?
Question:
Re-scaled OFT uses \(z=(R W^0 D)^\top x\) with diagonal \(D=\mathrm{diag}(s_i)\). The paper says re-scaling neurons does not change HE because HE uses normalized neurons. But if we change magnitudes and then later normalize for HE, could interactions with layernorms / activations alter downstream behavior? Any caveats about learning positive \(s_i\) only?
Answer:
Short answer
- Re-scaled OFT adds a diagonal matrix D = diag(s_i) because per‑neuron positive rescaling gives a very cheap way to increase the expressivity of OFT without changing the hyperspherical energy (HE). Mathematically HE depends only on the unit directions of the neurons, so multiplying each neuron by a positive scalar leaves the normalized directions unchanged and therefore leaves HE unchanged.
- Caveats: preserving HE does not imply there is no effect on forward activations or downstream behavior. Scaling the raw neuron magnitudes changes pre‑activations, interacts with nonlinearities and normalization layers, and can change training dynamics (and with unconstrained sign, it can flip directions and break HE). The paper enforces s_i > 0 for exactly this reason and notes D is placed on the neuron (column) side so normalization cancels the magnitude for HE; the camera‑ready erratum warns about the order if implemented differently.
- Practical recommendations: enforce positivity (e.g. s_i = exp(t_i) or softplus), initialize s_i = 1, optionally clamp or regularize log s to avoid very large/small scales, and be aware of interactions with LayerNorm / softmax / weight decay — treat D specially (no weight decay, small LR, or layer‑specific exclusion) when needed.

Detailed explanation and intuition

1) Why D preserves hyperspherical energy
The hyperspherical energy used in the paper is defined from the normalized neurons. For a layer with neuron weight columns w_i,
- normalized neuron: \(\hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\|\),
- hyperspherical energy (one form used in the paper): \(\text{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}\).

If you replace each neuron by a positive scalar multiple: \(w_i' = s_i w_i\) with \(s_i>0\), then
\[
\hat w_i'=\frac{w_i'}{\|w_i'\|}=\frac{s_i w_i}{\|s_i w_i\|}=\frac{s_i w_i}{s_i\|w_i\|}=\frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|}=\hat w_i.
\]
So every normalized neuron is identical after rescaling, and thus HE(W') = HE(W). That is the formal reason the paper says “re‑scaling neurons does not change HE”.

Why positivity matters: if some s_i were negative, \(w_i' = s_i w_i\) would flip the sign and \(\hat w_i' = -\hat w_i\), which generally changes pairwise angular relations and therefore HE (unless you flip every neuron simultaneously in a symmetric way). So the positivity constraint s_i > 0 is essential for the HE invariance claim.

2) But HE invariance ≠ no effect on the model’s outputs
HE is a structural quantity computed from normalized weight directions only. It does not capture the full forward computation, which depends on the magnitudes of the neurons before any normalization. Concretely, for the layer output
\[
z = (R W^0 D)^\top x,
\]
the diagonal D multiplies the columns of \(W^0\) (the neurons). That directly scales the pre‑activation components \(z_i\) by s_i (assuming the rest fixed). So even though HE is unchanged, the network’s activations and subsequent computation can change. Important interactions to watch:

- LayerNorm / BatchNorm / other normalization: If the network applies a normalization immediately after the linear layer that normalizes across features (LayerNorm), the per‑feature scale introduced by D may be largely canceled by that normalization. But LayerNorm also has learned affine parameters (gain \(\gamma\) and bias \(\beta\)), and those can reintroduce or adapt to scale differences. In short, LayerNorm reduces but does not necessarily nullify the effect of D; the position (pre/post) of the normalization layer matters.

- Nonlinearities (ReLU, GELU, etc.): Nonlinear activations are not scale‑invariant in general. For homogeneous activations (e.g. ReLU is 1‑homogeneous: ReLU(a x) = a ReLU(x) for a>=0), scaling before ReLU just scales the output. That can be harmless if subsequent layers compensate, but it may still change the distribution of activations seen by later layers. For nonhomogeneous ops or saturating activations, scaling can have larger nonlinear effects.

- Attention mechanisms / softmax logits: In Transformers, Q·K inner products are sensitive to the scale of Q and K. Scaling output channels that feed into query or key linear maps will change attention logits and therefore attention patterns. Normalization inside attention (e.g. the 1/sqrt(d) factor) does not remove per‑dimension scalings. So D can substantially change behavior in attention blocks.

- Residual connections and downstream linear layers: If the scaled outputs enter residual branches or are mixed with other features, the effect depends on relative magnitudes. Large or tiny s_i can destabilize residual learning or cause optimization issues.

- Training dynamics and regularizers: Weight decay or other regularizers penalize raw parameter magnitudes. Learning s_i and W simultaneously may create tradeoffs — the optimizer may counteract large s by shrinking base weights (or vice versa). The paper’s definition s_i > 0 avoids sign ambiguity, but you may want to treat s_i differently in optimization (e.g. exclude from weight decay).

So: HE invariance is a useful theoretical property, but it does not guarantee that adding D is a no‑op for the network’s function. The paper acknowledges D increases flexibility and empirically reports re‑scaled OFT is generally better. The theoretical point is that adding D does not break the HE guarantee (because HE uses normalized neurons), but it does allow controlled magnitude changes that affect outputs.

3) Implementation and the camera‑ready erratum
Two practical points from the paper you should keep in mind:
- Order matters. The paper’s intended forward pass is \(z=(R W^0 D)^\top x\) (scale the neurons / columns). The camera‑ready version accidentally printed \((D R W^0)^\top x\). Scaling rows instead of columns is a different operation and would not preserve HE in the same way. The authors note the implementation was correct (R W^0 D).
- They require s_i > 0 in the definition; the implementation must enforce positivity.

4) Practical recommendations / caveats for training
Based on the above, if you plan to use re‑scaled OFT, consider these best practices:
- Enforce positivity safely: parameterize s_i = exp(t_i) or s_i = softplus(t_i) + eps, initialize t_i = 0 so s_i = 1. That ensures s_i > 0 and identity initialization.
- Initialize identity: set R = I and s_i = 1 at start, so the finetuning begins at the pretrained model (same as the paper).
- Regularize / clamp scales: limit how far s_i can drift (e.g. clamp log s_i to a bounded interval or add small penalty on |log s_i|) to avoid exploding/vanishing feature magnitudes.
- Optimizer choices: consider excluding s_i from weight decay (or use a smaller decay) because weight decay fights magnitudes and causes coupling with base weights. Use a small learning rate for s_i if you want conservative scaling.
- Layer‑specific decisions: in layers where scaled outputs feed directly into attention logits or into places with no normalization, be extra conservative (smaller LR on s_i, stronger regularization) because scaling there can change attention patterns strongly.
- Monitor activation statistics and gradients: keep an eye on per‑feature activation variances and gradient norms during finetuning. If you see instability, tighten constraints on s_i or reduce learning rate.
- If preserving HE is the main goal with minimal functional change, you can skip D entirely (the original OFT) — the paper shows OFT already works well; D is an inexpensive extra degree of freedom that often helps but is not required.

Summary
- Re‑scaled OFT adds D because per‑neuron positive rescaling increases flexibility while mathematically preserving hyperspherical energy: normalized neurons are unchanged when scaled by positive scalars.
- However, preserving HE does not mean the network’s forward behavior is unchanged: magnitudes affect pre‑activations and interact with LayerNorm, nonlinearities, attention, residuals, and optimization. The positivity constraint on s_i is crucial to keep the HE invariance; avoid s_i = 0 or negatives.
- Practically, enforce positivity (exp/softplus), initialize s_i = 1, consider clamping / regularizing scales, and treat s_i specially in optimization to avoid destabilizing finetuning.

### How is the positivity constraint on diagonal \(D\) enforced during training?
Question:
In re-scaled OFT, they require diagonal elements \(s_i>0\). How is this implemented? Do they parametrize \(s_i=\exp(t_i)\) or use ReLU + epsilon? Are there stability concerns if some \(s_i\) become very large or very small?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not give a concrete implementation detail for enforcing "all diagonal elements s_i > 0" in the re-scaled OFT section. It only states that D = diag(s_1,...,s_n) and that each s_i is larger than zero. In practice you must choose a positive parametrization (or a projection) — common and well-tested choices are

- log‑parametrization: s_i = exp(t_i) with t_i unconstrained (initialize t_i = 0 so s_i = 1), or  
- softplus: s_i = softplus(t_i) + s_min (again t_i initialized at 0, s_min small > 0), or  
- bounded sigmoid mapping: s_i = a + (b-a)·sigmoid(t_i) to constrain s_i to [a,b], or  
- absolute value + epsilon: s_i = |t_i| + eps (less recommended).

Below I explain the tradeoffs, stability concerns (very large / very small s_i), practical mitigations, and a recommended recipe for diffusion finetuning.

Why you need positivity
- Re-scaling multiplies each neuron column by s_i (the paper uses forward pass z = (R W^0 D)^\top x). Allowing negative s_i would flip the sign of the neuron, which is not simply a magnitude change and would alter pairwise angles in a way that undermines the hyperspherical-energy invariance motivation. So the paper requires s_i > 0.

Parametrizations and their pros/cons (with equations)

1) Exponential (log‑param):
- s_i = exp(t_i).
- Pros: enforces strict positivity, simple, differentiable, t_i = 0 gives s_i = 1 (identity initialization).
- Cons: can produce extremely large or tiny s_i if t_i grows, which can cause exploding/vanishing activations or large gradient magnitudes.

2) Softplus:
- s_i = softplus(t_i) + s_min, where softplus(u)=log(1+exp(u)) and s_min > 0 small (e.g. 1e-3).
- Pros: positivity guaranteed, gentler growth than exp for large negative t, avoids s_i too close to zero if s_min set.
- Cons: still unbounded above; slightly more compute.

3) Sigmoid-bounded:
- s_i = a + (b-a)·sigmoid(t_i), with chosen bounds 0 < a < b.
- Pros: enforces both positivity and explicit bounds; avoids extreme scale values.
- Cons: less flexible if you need large re-scaling; requires picking meaningful [a,b].

4) Absolute value + eps:
- s_i = |t_i| + eps.
- Pros: simple.
- Cons: non-smooth at zero, can cause optimization issues; not recommended compared to softplus/exp.

Projection approach:
- Keep free s_i parameters but after each gradient step project them to (eps, s_max) by clipping. Works but can introduce non-smooth behavior at the clipping boundary.

Stability concerns and practical mitigations
- Very large s_i:
  - Amplifies the corresponding neuron output and can blow up activations and gradients, destabilizing diffusion denoising updates and optimizer steps.
  - Leads to numerical overflow if applied repeatedly during training or if combined with large learning rates.
- Very small s_i (near zero):
  - Effectively kills a neuron channel (vanishing), harming representational capacity and possibly making training fragile.
- Mitigations:
  - Initialize s_i = 1 (t_i = 0). This exactly matches identity scaling at start (paper initializes orthogonal R to identity as well).
  - Use a bounded parametrization (sigmoid mapping to a reasonable [a,b]) if you want hard safety.
  - Use softplus + small s_min to avoid near-zero scales.
  - Apply a mild regularizer on the log-scale, e.g. add an L2 penalty on t_i (if s = exp(t)) or on log(s_i). This keeps scales near 1:
    - loss_reg = lambda * ||t||_2^2 or lambda * ||log s||_2^2.
  - Gradient clipping and conservative learning rate for scale parameters (often scale parameters need smaller lr than other weights).
  - Use projection (after update) to clamp s_i into [s_min, s_max].
  - Optionally, integrate the COFT idea: explicitly constrain ||D - I|| (or ||log D||) ≤ delta, i.e. limit deviation from identity (analogue of the paper's ||R - I|| ≤ epsilon). For log-parameter, that becomes ||t|| ≤ delta'.

Diffusion-specific notes
- Diffusion denoising networks can be sensitive to activation magnitudes. A few large s_i can distort layer outputs and the learned denoising dynamics. That’s why a conservative approach (init s_i=1, small lr, regularizer or bounds) is recommended.
- The paper points out COFT adds an explicit deviation constraint on R to improve stability. A natural extension is to apply an analogous small-deviation constraint on D (e.g. restrict ||D - I||_F ≤ epsilon_D, or equivalently bound ||log D||), which prevents runaway scaling while keeping the benefits of re-scaling.

Recommended practical recipe
- Parametrize via t_i and set s_i = softplus(t_i) + s_min (with s_min = 1e-3). Initialize t_i = softplus^{-1}(1.0 - s_min) or simply t_i = 0 and accept s_i ≈ softplus(0)+s_min ≈ 0.693+s_min (so you may prefer to shift initialization to get s_i = 1 exactly).
  - Alternatively, use s_i = exp(t_i) with t_i initialized to 0 (so s_i = 1).
- Use a small learning rate for t (often an order of magnitude smaller than other trainable params) and optionally weight decay / L2 penalty on t.
- Optionally clip s_i to [s_min, s_max] after updates or parametrize with a bounded sigmoid mapping if you want hard guarantees.
- Monitor the distribution of s_i during training (histogram) and the per-layer activation norms. If you see runaway scales, tighten bounds or increase regularization.

Summary
- The paper specifies s_i must be > 0 but does not state which parametrization was used. Any of the standard positive parametrizations (exp, softplus, sigmoid mapping) will work; the practical choice depends on how much you want to allow large/small scaling. Because extreme scales can destabilize diffusion finetuning, the safe practice is: initialize s_i = 1, use a bounded or gently-growing parametrization (softplus or bounded sigmoid), use a small lr and/or a regularizer on the log-scale, and consider explicit clamping or COFT-style deviation constraints to keep scales near identity.

### Does OFT affect normalization layers (LayerNorm/BatchNorm) or only linear weights?
Question:
The paper focuses on applying OFT to weight matrices (fully connected/attention/convs). In modern diffusion models there are many LayerNorms. Should OFT be applied to normalization parameters as well? Could rotating neuron directions interact poorly with learned scale/shifts of LayerNorm?
Answer:
Short answer
- OFT is designed to act on neuron weight matrices (fully-connected, attention, conv kernels) — that is what the paper implements and analyzes. It does not (and generally should not) “rotate” LayerNorm/BatchNorm affine parameters themselves.
- In practice you should apply OFT to the linear/convolution/attention weights and leave normalization parameters (gamma/beta and BN running stats) alone, or only adjust them with a very small learning rate if necessary.
- If you see distributional mismatch after applying OFT, use the paper’s re-scaled OFT (learnable per-neuron diagonal D) or COFT’s epsilon constraint to reduce harmful interactions — these are safe, principled ways to handle scale/shift issues without applying orthogonal transforms to the normalization parameters.

Why (intuitively and technically)
1) What OFT rotates and what LayerNorm/BathNorm are:
- In the paper a layer weight matrix is W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} with column neurons w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, and OFT replaces the pretrained W^0 by
  W = R W^0, where R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d} is orthogonal (R^\top R = I).
  This rotates (or reflects) the input-space directions of each neuron while preserving pairwise angles between neurons (the hyperspherical energy).
- LayerNorm/BatchNorm affine parameters are per-feature scale and shift vectors (gamma, beta) applied after normalization. They are not neuron-direction matrices and there is no natural notion of “rotating” them that would preserve hyperspherical energy or the invariants OFT is designed to keep.

2) Why you normally shouldn’t apply OFT to normalization parameters:
- The orthogonal transformation idea is meaningful for sets of weight vectors (directions on the hypersphere). Gamma/beta are per-channel scalars, not a set of d-dimensional direction vectors, so the OFT invariance/guarantees don’t apply.
- Rotating weight vectors changes the pre-normalization activation patterns; LayerNorm then standardizes those activations (zero mean, unit variance per sample) and applies gamma/beta. Because LayerNorm removes per-sample mean/std, it already reduces sensitivity to per-neuron magnitude changes — so freezing gamma/beta often works well.
- If you were to “rotate” the normalization parameters in some ad-hoc way you would likely break their semantics (gamma rescales normalized features, beta shifts them); there is no orthogonal-preserving transformation to apply sensibly to those vectors.

3) When interactions might be a problem (and how to fix them)
- Potential mismatch: large rotations R could change the correlation structure of pre-LN activations (i.e., the directional content). Although OFT preserves pairwise angles of weight vectors (so the internal geometric relations remain), the actual activation distributions entering LayerNorm can still change — and the learned gamma/beta were optimized for the original distribution.
- Remedies that keep OFT’s principles intact:
  a) Re-scaled OFT (paper, Section “Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning”): learn a per-neuron diagonal D so the forward pass becomes z = (R W^0 D)^\top x. D adapts magnitudes without touching gamma/beta and does not change hyperspherical energy (directions are unchanged). This directly addresses scale mismatches with a tiny number of extra parameters.
  b) COFT (Constrained OFT): use the \|R - I\| \le \epsilon constraint to limit angular deviation from the pretrained model. Smaller \epsilon keeps activations closer to the original distribution, reducing any LayerNorm mismatch.
  c) Fine-tune only gamma (scale) with a small LR if you still see degraded results — this is conservative and often effective. Avoid re-learning both gamma and beta aggressively on very small finetuning sets.
  d) Keep BN running stats frozen (if BN is used) or be careful updating them on tiny datasets — this is standard practice independent of OFT.

What the paper actually did
- The paper’s experiments applied OFT to attention and convolutional weight matrices (they explicitly state OFT is applied to the same layers where LoRA is used for fair comparison). It does not report applying orthogonal transforms to LayerNorm/BatchNorm parameters. The re-scaled OFT variant is provided precisely to give the model limited magnitude flexibility (a complementary mechanism to orthogonal rotation), and COFT is offered to limit rotation magnitude — both of which help avoid adverse interactions with normalization layers.

Practical recommendations
- Default: apply OFT to linear/attention/conv weights only; keep LayerNorm (gamma/beta) frozen.
- If you observe loss of quality or distribution shift:
  1. Try COFT with a small \epsilon (restrict rotation magnitude).
  2. Try re-scaled OFT (learn D) — this is the most direct fix for scale mismatches.
  3. If needed, allow gamma to be fine-tuned with a tiny learning rate (leave beta frozen), or fine-tune both gamma/beta but monitor for overfitting/collapse.
- For BatchNorm (rare in modern diffusion backbones), treat running mean/var carefully: either leave them frozen or re-estimate them on enough data; do not blindly update running stats on very small finetuning sets.

Summary
- OFT is meant for neuron weight matrices; you generally should not try to “orthogonally finetune” LayerNorm/BatchNorm parameters themselves.
- If normalization interacts poorly with rotated directions, use the paper’s re-scaled OFT or COFT to address the mismatch in a principled way, or fine-tune normalization scales conservatively. These options preserve the core OFT guarantees (hyperspherical-energy preservation and controlled deviation) while letting the model adjust to any remaining distributional shifts.

### What is the computational training overhead of OFT compared to LoRA and full finetuning?
Question:
Although OFT introduces fewer parameters than LoRA in some regimes, Cayley transform and matrix inverses seem expensive. Practically, for a standard finetuning run on Stable Diffusion v1.5, how does wall-clock training time and GPU memory for OFT compare to LoRA and full finetuning?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper does not publish exact wall‑clock times or GPU memory numbers for Stable Diffusion v1.5 finetuning, so there are no paper‑verified seconds / GB to quote.  
- Qualitatively, OFT sits between LoRA and full finetuning:
  - Per training step, OFT typically costs more compute than LoRA (because of the Cayley parameterization / matrix inverse used to form the orthogonal transform), but far less than full finetuning (which backpropagates through every weight and stores optimizer state for all parameters).
  - OFT uses far less GPU memory than full finetuning (only the OFT parameters and their optimizer state need to be stored), and memory is similar to LoRA with a small extra overhead for the Cayley parameterization data (the skew‑symmetric Q blocks and temporary buffers used to form/apply R).
  - Importantly, OFT tends to converge in fewer iterations than LoRA in the experiments reported, so total wall‑clock time to reach a given quality can be similar to or better than LoRA despite higher per‑step cost.

Why you should expect this behaviour (intuitive + technical explanation)
- Full finetuning: you update all model parameters. That means:
  - Large optimizer state (Adam moments) for every parameter → large GPU memory.
  - Gradients computed for the entire model → large per-step compute and memory for activations/backprop.
  - Converges in some number of steps, but each step is expensive in both time and memory.
- LoRA: adds low‑rank additive updates and freezes the pretrained weights.
  - Very small number of trainable parameters compared to full finetuning.
  - Per‑step extra compute is small (low‑rank matmuls), and optimizer state is small → low additional GPU memory.
  - Per‑step time is close to the original model forward/backward.
- OFT: multiplicative update implemented by learning a (block‑diagonal) orthogonal transform R per layer:
  - Trainable parameter count is typically similar to or smaller than LoRA in many regimes (paper gives parameter‑count formulas). OFT explicitly targets parameter efficiency with block diagonalization and optional block sharing.
  - The main extra per‑step cost relative to LoRA comes from forming/applying R from the Cayley parameterization R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} where Q is skew‑symmetric. Computing (I − Q)^{-1} (or applying it) is the source of extra compute and temporary memory.
  - Because only the OFT parameters (the Q blocks, and optionally a small diagonal D for re‑scaling) and their optimizer state are trained, the persistent optimizer memory is much smaller than full finetuning and comparable to LoRA.
  - Empirically in the paper, OFT (and COFT) converge faster (fewer iterations/epochs) than LoRA on the tasks studied (subject‑driven and controllable generation on Stable Diffusion v1.5). That faster convergence often offsets the per‑step extra cost so that total time to reach a target quality can be comparable or better.

Concrete trade‑offs called out in the paper
- Cost source for OFT:
  - Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse (or solving a linear system) for each orthogonal block. The authors explicitly note this “slightly limits scalability” and treat it as an open problem to accelerate. Block‑diagonalization is proposed to reduce the size/number of inverses.
- Parameter / memory scaling (from the paper):
  - LoRA trainable params for one weight matrix W in the usual formulation: O(r'(d + n)) for rank r'.
  - OFT (r block diagonal) trainable params: O(d^2 / r) (or with block sharing O(d^2 / r^2)), and the paper gives a toy example where OFT had fewer trainable params than LoRA for a 128×128 matrix with certain settings.
- Convergence: OFT often needs fewer iterations/epochs to reach good control quality (paper examples: OFT matched LoRA’s final results in fewer epochs/iterations on SD v1.5 tasks).

Practical implications for a Stable Diffusion v1.5 finetuning run
- GPU memory:
  - Full finetune: highest memory (model params + optimizer states for all params + activations).
  - LoRA: lowest extra memory beyond the frozen model (only small A/B matrices & their optimizer state).
  - OFT: slightly more memory than LoRA (need to store Q blocks and temporary buffers to compute/apply R each forward/backward) but still tiny compared to full finetuning. In practice you should not need more GPU memory than LoRA by more than a modest factor if you choose modest block sizes.
- Wall‑clock time:
  - Per step: LoRA ≤ OFT << full finetuning (LoRA is cheapest per step; OFT is heavier because of matrix solves/inverses for Cayley).
  - Iterations to converge: OFT << LoRA in the tasks reported → total wall‑clock to hit target quality can be comparable or better for OFT despite slower steps.
  - Net practical outcome reported in the paper: OFT achieves faster and more stable convergence (fewer effective training iterations), so users often observe equal or lower overall training time to reach good generative/control performance compared to LoRA, and much lower time than full finetuning.

How to keep OFT overhead modest in practice (recommendations consistent with the paper)
- Use block‑diagonal R with reasonably small blocks. Small blocks make the Cayley inverse / linear solve much cheaper (cubic cost in block dimension), and the paper explicitly uses block diagonalization for parameter/compute efficiency.
- Share blocks across channels if appropriate (the paper mentions block sharing as a way to reduce parameters further).
- Apply OFT only to the same attention matrices that LoRA typically targets (the authors do this for fair comparison). That limits the total number and size of R blocks you must handle.
- If implementation allows, compute R·W0 with a solver (solve (I − Q) X = W0) rather than forming the explicit inverse, which is numerically better and often faster.
- Optionally consider the Neumann approximation or updating via low‑order series early in training if Q is small (the paper discusses the Neumann series as an approximation and uses projected gradient constraints in COFT).

What the paper does and does not provide
- Provides: qualitative and empirical evidence that OFT converges faster and needs fewer trainable parameters in many settings; explains the extra per‑step cost source (Cayley inverse) and proposes block diagonal parameterization to mitigate it.
- Does not provide: exact wall‑clock seconds or GPU memory numbers for SD v1.5 runs comparing OFT, LoRA and full finetuning. The paper therefore supports the qualitative comparisons above but not precise numeric timings / GB figures.

If you need numbers for your environment
- Measure per‑step time and peak memory for a short run in your target configuration (same batch size, same precision) for: (a) full finetuning, (b) LoRA, (c) OFT with the block size you plan to use. That gives the most reliable answer because GPU, mixed‑precision settings, library implementations of linear solves, and block sizes all materially affect the runtime/memory.
- In your measurements, also compare time to reach a target metric (CLIP/LPIPS or the control consistency metric in the paper) — because OFT’s faster convergence often dominates raw per‑step cost.

Summary
- OFT costs more per step than LoRA because of Cayley (matrix inverse / solve), but uses far less memory and compute than full finetuning because only the orthogonal blocks are trained.  
- The paper’s experiments show OFT converges faster (fewer iterations) than LoRA on Stable Diffusion v1.5 tasks, so total wall‑clock to a target quality is often comparable or better than LoRA despite the per‑step overhead.  
- For concrete seconds/GB you should run short profiling experiments in your exact training setup; the paper gives the qualitative/algorithmic reasons and practical mitigations (block diagonalization, block sharing) that explain the observed trade‑offs.

### How robust is OFT to different initialization and learning rates?
Question:
The method initializes \(R=I\) (via \(Q=0\)). Does OFT require very small learning rates similar to standard finetuning, or can it tolerate larger steps? Are there stability issues if updates push \(R\) far from identity quickly?
Answer:
Short answer
- OFT is generally more stable than plain finetuning and often tolerates larger effective steps because it updates a compact, orthogonally constrained parameter (the rotation R) initialized at identity (R = I via Q = 0). The orthogonality constraint preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) which prevents many catastrophic changes that occur in unconstrained weight updates.
- That said, the Cayley parameterization is nonlinear: large updates to the skew-symmetric parameter Q can still move R far from identity and cause instability or undesired behavior. This is exactly why the paper introduces COFT (the epsilon constraint) and block-diagonal / shared-block parameterizations: they explicitly limit how far R can move and thus improve stability and ease of hyperparameter selection (learning rate, number of iterations).
- Practical rule: initialize Q = 0 (as in the paper), you can be more aggressive than with naive finetuning, but you should still (a) use block-diagonal / shared blocks to regularize, (b) consider COFT with a small epsilon (project Q to a small norm) if you want near-guaranteed stability, and (c) monitor training — very large Q updates can break invertibility / validity of the Cayley transform and push R far, producing poor results.

Why OFT tends to be more tolerant than standard finetuning (intuitive + technical)
- Different parameter space: Standard finetuning updates the full weight matrix W directly (additive updates). OFT instead learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix R and uses multiplicative updates W = R W^0. Moving on the orthogonal manifold is a strongly constrained family of changes: orthogonality preserves pairwise neuron angles (thus hyperspherical energy), which is the structure the paper claims is important for preserving pretrained generative ability. Thus many destructive directions in weight space are simply not available to OFT — that reduces catastrophic drift and typically makes optimization more robust.
- Compact parameterization: OFT often uses block-diagonal or shared-block orthogonal matrices. That drastically reduces degrees of freedom and acts like an implicit regularizer, allowing larger step sizes without immediately overfitting or collapsing.
- Cayley parameterization + initialization: OFT uses the Cayley map R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric and initializes Q = 0 so R starts exactly at I. Near identity the Cayley map is well-approximated by a linear expansion
  R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2).
  So a gradient step on Q corresponds roughly to a scaled, structured step on R, and thus on W. Because the parameter that’s updated (Q) is lower-dimensional / structured and starts at zero, you can often take steps that would be too large for naive weight updates yet still remain well behaved.
- Empirical evidence in the paper: the authors report that OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than DreamBooth and LoRA, and that COFT “yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.” They also show OFT without the orthogonality constraint fails in a control experiment, which supports the claim that the orthogonality constraint is an important stabilizer.

When large updates can cause problems
- Nonlinearity of the Cayley map: the Cayley transform is nonlinear. For small Q, R ≈ I + 2Q, but for large Q the higher-order terms matter and R can move substantially (rotations that are not small). That can change the semantics of neurons greatly (even though pairwise angles among normalized neurons remain preserved under any orthogonal R, the directions relative to input space shift) and can lead to poor generation or instability.
- Numerical issues: the Cayley formula involves an inverse (I − Q)^{-1}. If Q gets large enough to make (I − Q) ill-conditioned, numerical problems can appear. Keeping Q small (or projecting Q) avoids this.
- Hyperspherical-energy preservation ≠ harmlessness: orthogonality preserves the hyperspherical energy measure used in the paper, but preserving that energy alone does not guarantee that a large rotation is semantically benign. In other words, R far from I can still produce a valid but very different model; that can be bad for the downstream task or cause overfitting.

How COFT addresses these issues (paper-grounded)
- COFT adds an explicit radius constraint: ||R − I|| ≤ ε (approximated as ||Q|| ≤ ε' under the Neumann expansion). This is enforced by projected gradient descent on Q. As the paper states, COFT “yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint,” and with a proper ε “both the learning rate and the iteration number become effortless to set.”
- Block-diagonal parameterization (and block sharing) is another lever the paper uses to reduce degrees of freedom and stabilize training. Smaller blocks and sharing act as stronger regularizers that tolerate larger per-parameter steps without causing collapse.

Practical recommendations (based on the paper’s methods and findings)
1. Initialization: always initialize Q = 0 so R = I (this is how OFT is intended to be started).
2. Start with a moderate learning rate: you can usually be less conservative than classic full-weight finetuning because OFT is constrained, but do not jump to very large rates without checking stability.
3. Use block-diagonal and/or shared-block R to reduce parameter count and improve robustness — smaller blocks = stronger regularization.
4. If you want minimal hyperparameter tuning and maximal stability, use COFT:
   - enforce a small epsilon (project Q so ||Q|| ≤ ε') after each update. The paper shows this makes learning rate and epoch count “effortless to set.”
5. Monitor: track validation metrics (e.g., CLIP / DINO / your control-consistency metric) and optionally hyperspherical energy difference. If performance suddenly degrades, reduce LR or tighten epsilon.
6. If you expect the task really requires magnitude changes, use re-scaled OFT (learn diagonal D in addition to R), but still apply COFT or small blocks to stabilize.
7. Watch numerical stability: avoid Q updates that make (I − Q) nearly singular — projection to a small norm prevents that.

Summary
- OFT’s constrained, orthogonal parameterization and identity initialization give it a robustness advantage over unconstrained finetuning: it typically converges faster and tolerates less conservative hyperparameters.
- However, because the Cayley map is nonlinear and because large rotations can still change model behavior dramatically, extremely large updates to Q / R can cause instability or undesired models. The paper therefore recommends (and empirically validates) COFT (the epsilon constraint) and block-diagonal parameterizations to control that risk and make learning-rate/iteration choices easier.

### Why does OFT empirically converge faster than LoRA or other adapters?
Question:
The paper hypothesizes two reasons: changing neuron directions is effective, and optimization on a smooth hypersphere manifold helps. Can someone provide a more concrete optimization perspective: does parameterizing via orthogonal matrices reduce ill-conditioning, or does it impose implicit preconditioning that accelerates convergence?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — from an optimization viewpoint OFT likely speeds convergence for two related reasons the paper sketches: (1) it restricts optimization to rotation-like (directional) moves that are norm-preserving and semantically relevant, and (2) it forces optimization onto a smooth, low‑dimensional manifold (the orthogonal group or its block‑diagonal variant), which reduces effective degrees of freedom and removes ill‑conditioned scaling directions. Together those effects act like an implicit regularizer / preconditioner: gradients are projected onto angular directions, parameter updates avoid creating large singular‑value imbalances, and learning focuses on the subspace that most directly changes semantics — so fewer and more stable steps are needed than with unconstrained additive adapters (e.g. LoRA).

Longer explanation (intuitive + concrete)

1) What OFT is doing to the parameter space
- A usual linear layer produces outputs z = W^T x. OFT parameterizes the finetuned weight as
  W = R W^0,
  with R orthogonal (often block‑diagonal orthogonal). So the trainable degrees of freedom are rotations/reflections applied to the pretrained neurons, not arbitrary additive perturbations.
- Equivalently (as the paper notes),
  W = W^0 + (R − I) W^0,
  so OFT’s update is multiplicative on the left but can be seen as an additive update whose structure is tightly controlled by R − I and W^0.

2) Why rotations help: preserve norm / avoid scaling ill‑conditioning
- Orthogonal matrices preserve vector norms and condition numbers under multiplication: for any matrix A and orthogonal R, ||R A||_2 = ||A||_2 and cond(R A) = cond(A). Thus applying R does not introduce extra scaling imbalances across singular directions of W^0.
- In contrast, unconstrained additive updates (even low‑rank ones) can change singular values of the layer and create or amplify directions with very large or very small gains, which worsens optimization conditioning and forces the optimizer to correct magnitudes as well as directions.
- Rotations therefore avoid “damaging” the magnitude structure learned in pretraining. Since many diffusion / vision models encode semantics largely in angles (direction) rather than absolute magnitudes (as the paper motivates via hyperspherical arguments), constraining updates to rotations both removes irrelevant radial degrees of freedom and prevents ill‑conditioned scaling.

3) Why manifold optimization (rotations) produces a smoother, lower‑dimensional landscape
- Optimizing R (orthogonal) restricts parameters to a smooth compact manifold (the orthogonal group, or product of smaller orthogonal groups for block‑diagonal R). That reduces the effective search space and removes directions that would otherwise produce volatile changes in loss.
- The Cayley parameterization used in the paper maps skew‑symmetric Q to R via R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1}. For small Q this gives R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2). Thus locally the optimization is approximately linear in Q but constrained to skew‑symmetric directions, which acts like optimizing in a well‑structured tangent space around the identity. COFT further constrains ||R − I|| ≤ ε (equivalently small ||Q||), so steps remain small and stable (a trust‑region effect).
- This geometry both (a) focuses updates onto angular/tangential directions (which the paper argues are the semantically relevant ones) and (b) avoids wandering into badly conditioned radial directions. That makes convergence smoother and often faster.

4) How this looks like implicit preconditioning
- Two related mechanisms give an effective preconditioning interpretation:
  a) Projection of gradients: Because R is constrained, the true parameter update is the projection of the unconstrained gradient onto the tangent space of orthogonal matrices (skew‑symmetric matrices). Projecting gradients removes components that would change magnitudes in arbitrary ways — effectively multiplying the gradient by a projection matrix (a simple preconditioner).
  b) Left‑multiplication by W^0: The parameterization W = (I + (R − I))W^0 means updates are of form (R − I)W^0. When you optimize the parameters of R, gradients backpropagate through multiplication by W^0, so the effective gradient direction used to update Q is premultiplied/postmultiplied by W^0 (and its transpose) depending on the chain rule. At small Q (R ≈ I + 2Q) the leading order gradient w.r.t. Q is proportional to (dL/dW) W^{0T} (up to skew‑symmetry projection and constant factors). That multiplies the raw gradient by W^{0T}, so feature‑space geometry (singular directions of W^0) shapes which parameter directions get large updates. In other words, the parameter update is implicitly reweighted by W^0 — a form of data‑dependent preconditioning.
- So although OFT does not explicitly form a preconditioner like inverse Hessian, its constraint + multiplicative form implicitly changes the effective gradient metric and eliminates bad directions, which can accelerate convergence.

5) Contrast with LoRA (and other additive adapters)
- LoRA parametrizes the additive update as a low‑rank matrix A B^T. That is very flexible in direction (it can scale or rotate different neuron outputs depending on A and B) and, unless further regularized, can change magnitudes and singular values freely. To avoid destroying pretrained behaviour LoRA training typically uses small learning rates and careful tuning.
- Because OFT’s allowed updates are rotations (norm preserving) and are often block‑structured, OFT has fewer harmful degrees of freedom that the optimizer must control; it therefore needs fewer steps and is less sensitive to step size.
- In short: LoRA gives flexibility but requires the optimizer to simultaneously discover both scale and direction changes; OFT mostly focuses on direction changes that are semantically effective and safe, so optimization converges faster.

6) The role of block‑diagonal and shared blocks
- Using block‑diagonal R reduces parameter count and further constrains the search space. This not only helps parameter efficiency but also reduces coupling between unrelated neuron groups, which tends to make the local loss surface nicer (more decoupled, less ill‑conditioned) and hence easier to optimize.

7) What to measure if you want to verify this explanation empirically
- Track the singular value spectra / condition number of layer weights W_t during finetuning under OFT vs LoRA. The expectation: OFT will keep singular values close to those of W^0 (no large spread), while LoRA may change them more.
- Project gradients into radial vs angular components for neurons: decompose each neuron w_i into its norm and direction (unit vector). Compare how much gradient energy acts on norms vs directions under each method; OFT should concentrate energy on angular components.
- Compute empirical Hessian (or its top eigenvalues) along optimization trajectory to compare landscape sharpness / conditioning between methods.
- Ablations: (a) OFT without orthogonality (the paper shows such an ablation collapses), (b) COFT with varying ε to see tradeoff between speed and stability (paper includes this).

Summary / takeaway
- OFT speeds convergence not because of magic but because it restricts learning to rotation‑like moves that are (a) semantically efficient (changing directions rather than magnitudes) and (b) numerically benign (norm‑preserving, fewer ill‑conditioned directions). This restriction amounts to an implicit projection/preconditioning of gradients and a reduction of the effective search space, producing a smoother, more stable optimization path than unconstrained additive adapters such as LoRA. COFT's additional small‑deviation constraint behaves like a trust region that further stabilizes and accelerates convergence.

References in the paper that back this up
- The paper’s hyperspherical/angle arguments (Section “Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?”) and the connection to orthogonal over‑parameterized training [liu2021orthogonal] (discussed in the paper) provide empirical and theoretical motivation that orthogonal parameterizations improve optimization landscapes and generalization; the Cayley parameterization and the small‑Q approximation (R ≈ I + 2Q) give the local linear view that links updates to angular/tangential moves.

### How exactly is the control consistency metric computed for C2I, S2I, and L2F?
Question:
The paper introduces a control consistency metric: estimate control from generated image and compare to original. For C2I they use IoU and F1, for S2I mean IoU and accuracy, and for L2F mean \(\ell_2\) between landmarks. How are control signals predicted (which off-the-shelf models), and are these metrics computed per-image or averaged over dataset splits?
Answer:
Short answer
- For each control task the finetuned model’s output image is passed through an appropriate off‑the‑shelf predictor to recover the control signal (an edge detector for C2I, a semantic segmenter for S2I, and a facial‑landmark detector for L2F). The paper refers to these as “off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model[s]” and points the reader to Appendix~\ref{app:settings} for the exact predictor implementations and preprocessing.
- The predicted control is then compared to the original control input per image using the standard task metrics:
  - C2I: IoU and F1 between predicted edge map and input edge map.
  - S2I: mean IoU, mean accuracy and overall (pixel) accuracy between predicted segmentation and input segmentation.
  - L2F: mean \ell_2 (Euclidean) distance between predicted landmarks and the input control landmarks.
- These quantities are computed per generated image (or per class for per‑class measures) and then averaged over the evaluated set (the dataset / split) to produce the numbers reported in the paper’s tables (the authors note results are “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model”).

Longer, precise explanation (with formulas and intuition)

1) How the predicted control signal is obtained
- The paper’s methodology for the control‑consistency metric is: take a generated image, run an appropriate pre‑trained off‑the‑shelf predictor on that image to produce a predicted control (edge map / semantic segmentation map / landmark coordinates), and compare that predicted control to the original control input that was supplied to the generator.
- The paper explicitly says these are “off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model[s]” and that the metric is valid “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model.” The exact predictor architectures / checkpoints and any preprocessing (thresholding, resizing, etc.) are given in the paper’s Appendix~\ref{app:settings}.

2) Exact metrics and how they are computed (per image, then averaged)
Below I give the standard definitions the paper uses (these are the standard interpretations of the terms used in the paper).

- C2I (Canny edge → image)
  - Predictor: an edge detector run on the generated image to yield a binary edge map (the paper treats this as an off‑the‑shelf predictor; see Appendix for which one).
  - Per‑image comparisons:
    - Intersection over Union (IoU):
      \[
      \text{IoU}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|P\cup G|}
      \]
      where \(P\) is the predicted binary edge set (pixels predicted as edge) and \(G\) is the ground‑truth/control edge set.
    - F1 (edge F1 score): compute precision and recall on edge pixels and form F1:
      \[
      \text{Precision}=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}},\quad
      \text{Recall}=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}},\quad
      \text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}},
      \]
      where TP/FP/FN are counted on a per‑pixel basis between predicted and control edge maps (the paper reports IoU and F1).

- S2I (segmentation map → image)
  - Predictor: a semantic segmentation model applied to the generated image to produce a predicted class label per pixel.
  - Per‑image / per‑class comparisons:
    - mean IoU (mIoU): compute IoU for each semantic class \(c\), then average over classes:
      \[
      \text{IoU}_c=\frac{|\text{P}_c\cap \text{G}_c|}{|\text{P}_c\cup \text{G}_c|},\qquad
      \text{mIoU}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\text{IoU}_c.
      \]
    - mean accuracy (mean per‑class accuracy): for each class \(c\) compute the fraction of ground‑truth pixels of class \(c\) that were correctly predicted and average over classes:
      \[
      \text{acc}_c=\frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{GT}_c},\qquad
      \text{mean\_acc}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\text{acc}_c,
      \]
      where \(\text{GT}_c\) is the total number of ground‑truth pixels of class \(c\).
    - overall accuracy (global pixel accuracy): total correctly predicted pixels divided by total pixels:
      \[
      \text{overall\_acc}=\frac{\sum_{c}\text{TP}_c}{\text{total pixels}}.
      \]
  - These three measures capture different aspects: mIoU is class‑balanced segmentation overlap, mean accuracy averages recall per class, and overall accuracy reflects the raw pixel‑wise correctness (biased to large classes).

- L2F (landmark → face)
  - Predictor: a facial landmark detector run on the generated image, outputting coordinates for the K landmarks.
  - Per‑image comparison: mean Euclidean distance across landmarks:
    \[
    d_i=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\| \ell^{\text{pred}}_{i,k} - \ell^{\text{ctrl}}_{i,k}\|_2,
    \]
    where \(\ell^{\text{pred}}_{i,k}\) is landmark \(k\) predicted from the generated image \(i\), and \(\ell^{\text{ctrl}}_{i,k}\) is the original control landmark \(k\). The paper reports the mean \(\ell_2\) distance aggregated over images:
    \[
    \text{mean\_}\ell_2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i.
    \]
  - (Note: the paper states “mean \ell_2 distance between control landmarks and predicted landmarks.” It does not state any additional normalization such as inter‑ocular normalization in the main text; follow Appendix~\ref{app:settings} for exact detector and coordinate conventions.)

3) Aggregation and reporting
- The paper computes those metrics on each generated image, and then averages across the evaluated set of images (the dataset / split) to produce the scalar numbers reported in Table~\ref{table:control_gen} and the convergence plots. When the metric is per class (mIoU, mean accuracy), the class‑wise quantities are combined into the mean as shown above before averaging across images or across the dataset as appropriate. The paper’s text repeatedly says the reported results are aggregated (averages); e.g., “we compute IoU and F1 score” and “we compute mean \ell_2 distance” and then present aggregated results.
- The authors also caution that the absolute numeric value depends on the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf predictor used to recover the control. Thus these metrics measure control consistency conditional on the predictor’s reliability.

4) Practical caveats and where to look for exact implementation details
- The high‑level pipeline is: generate image given control → run predictor to recover control from the generated image → compute standard per‑image metric → average results across test images. This pipeline is task‑specific only in the choice of predictor.
- The paper intentionally calls these “off‑the‑shelf” predictors and defers the exact choices (model names / checkpoints / pre‑processing and thresholds) to Appendix~\ref{app:settings}. If you need to reproduce exact numbers, check Appendix~\ref{app:settings} for which edge detector / segmentation network / landmark detector and any resizing / thresholding details they used.

Summary
- Controls are estimated by task‑appropriate off‑the‑shelf predictors (edge/segmenter/landmark detector).
- Metrics are the standard IoU / F1 (C2I), mIoU / mean & overall accuracy (S2I), and mean \ell_2 landmark distance (L2F).
- Computed per image (or per class per image) and then averaged across the evaluated dataset/split for the reported results. The reported values are therefore dataset averages and are limited by the accuracy of the chosen predictors (see Appendix~\ref{app:settings} in the paper for the exact predictor choices).

### Can multiple orthogonal matrices from different tasks be composed to yield a multi-task model?
Question:
The conclusion mentions compositionality: orthogonal matrices from multiple OFT finetuning tasks can be multiplied together to remain orthogonal. If we have \(R_a\) (task A) and \(R_b\) (task B), is \(R_b R_a W^0\) expected to perform both tasks, or do interactions break task-specific adaptations? Any theoretical intuition or empirical results?
Answer:
Short answer
- Algebraically: yes — if \(R_a\) and \(R_b\) are orthogonal then \(R_b R_a\) is orthogonal, so \(W^{ab}:=(R_b R_a)W^0\) still preserves the hyperspherical energy property that OFT guarantees.
- Practically: the paper does not provide empirical results for composing independently learned task rotations, and it explicitly lists whether composed orthogonals preserve all downstream task behaviors as an open problem. Intuition says composition can sometimes work (especially for small, non‑conflicting rotations or when rotations act on disjoint subspaces) but will fail when the two tasks require conflicting re-orientations of the same neurons or when nonlinear interactions amplify incompatibilities.

Long answer — intuition, theory and how to test it

1) What OFT guarantees and what that implies for composition
- OFT learns a layer-wise orthogonal matrix \(R\) so the finetuned weights are \(W=(R)W^0\). Orthogonality implies pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy) are preserved compared to \(W^0\).
- Orthogonal matrices form a group: if \(R_a,R_b\in O(d)\) then \(R_b R_a\in O(d)\). So composing two OFT transforms gives another transform that still preserves hyperspherical energy. Algebraically the composition is valid and remains inside the same “safe” hypothesis class that motivated OFT.

2) Why that algebraic fact is not enough to guarantee multitask performance
- OFT was trained for a single downstream objective starting from \(W^0\). Each \(R_{\text{task}}\) is optimized so that \(R_{\text{task}} W^0\) performs well on that task. There is no guarantee that the product \(R_b R_a W^0\) will perform both tasks because:
  - nonlinearity and layer interactions: the network’s behavior is not a linear function of a single layer’s orthogonal transform in isolation — later layers and activations depend on the exact representations produced. Rotating neurons for task A may change the “input” seen by layers that task B expects (and vice versa).
  - conflicting rotations: Task A and task B might require opposing re-orientations of the same neuron directions. Composition then leads to interference; the composed transform can destroy the task-specific alignment each rotation imposed when applied in isolation.
  - order matters: matrix multiplication is not commutative in general. \(R_b R_a \neq R_a R_b\): applying B after A is not the same as applying A after B, so sequential application may be order-dependent and unpredictable.

3) Useful theoretical viewpoints that give intuition
- Small-rotation (Lie algebra) regime. Parameterize orthogonals via skew-symmetric generators. With Cayley parameterization the paper uses \(\ R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) with \(Q^\top=-Q\), and for small \(Q\)
  \[
  R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2).
  \]
  If both \(Q_a\) and \(Q_b\) are small, then
  \[
  R_b R_a \approx (I+2Q_b)(I+2Q_a) \approx I + 2(Q_a+Q_b) + \mathcal{O}(Q^2),
  \]
  so effects approximately add in the generator space. This suggests composition is most likely to succeed when each finetune is a small, constrained rotation (the COFT small-\(\epsilon\) regime). In other words: small, compatible edits approximately add and are less likely to interfere.
- Exact group/Lie theory. Orthogonal matrices can be written as exponentials of skew-symmetric matrices: \(R=\exp(S)\) with \(S^\top=-S\). In general
  \[
  \exp(S_b)\exp(S_a)\neq\exp(S_b+S_a),
  \]
  but for small \(S\) the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series shows the mismatch involves commutators \([S_a,S_b]\). If the generators commute (i.e., act in independent subspaces), composition behaves like addition and is benign; if they don’t, there will be higher-order interaction terms.
- Block structure matters. The paper uses block-diagonal orthogonals. If two tasks mostly rotate different blocks (disjoint subspaces), their matrices will approximately commute on different blocks and composition is more likely to preserve both tasks. If both tasks act on the same blocks, interference is more likely.

4) Practical recommendations and experiments you can run (the paper suggests this direction but does not report experiments)
Because the paper flagged this as an open problem, here are sensible empirical tests and strategies:

A. Baseline experiments to try (direct, minimal)
- Learn \(R_a\) for task A (initialize at identity); save it.
- Starting from \(W^0\), learn \(R_b\) for task B (independently); save it.
- At test time evaluate:
  - single-task models \(W^0\), \(R_aW^0\), \(R_bW^0\),
  - composed models \(R_b R_a W^0\) and \(R_a R_b W^0\).
- Metrics: the task-specific metrics used in the paper (subject fidelity / CLIP / DINO etc. for subject-driven; control-consistency metrics for controllable generation). Check order dependence, and check whether composed model keeps both task metrics near the single-task baselines.

B. Variations to probe interference
- Vary the magnitude of rotations (use COFT and sweep \(\epsilon\)). Hypothesis: smaller \(\epsilon\) (smaller rotations) → composition more likely to succeed (linear regime).
- Vary block size: block-diagonal vs full orthogonal. Hypothesis: smaller blocks (more localized rotations) may allow less interference if tasks use different blocks.
- Check commutation: compute \(\|R_a R_b - R_b R_a\|\). If near zero, composition is more likely to be stable.
- Try generator-space composition:
  - Use Cayley generators \(Q_a,Q_b\) and test \(R_{\text{sum}} = \text{Cayley}(Q_a+Q_b)\) or use matrix logarithms: \(S_a=\log(R_a)\), form \(R_{\text{sum}}=\exp(S_a+S_b)\), evaluate \(R_{\text{sum}}W^0\). These add in the Lie algebra and often behave better when rotations are small.
- Joint multi-task OFT: train a single orthogonal transform jointly on both tasks (multi-task loss). This is a stronger baseline: it optimizes for both tasks simultaneously and may be easier to achieve than naive composition. Compare composed-independent vs jointly-trained.

C. Practical strategies if composition fails
- Use task-specific orthogonals at inference (switching) instead of composing; keep per-task \(R\) and apply the one you need (no model-size overhead since you only precompute \(R_t W^0\)).
- Constrain finetunes via COFT (small \(\epsilon\)) so their generators add approximately.
- Learn orthogonals in disjoint block subspaces intentionally for different tasks.
- If you must combine tasks, fine-tune a small extra adapter on top of the composed model (i.e., start from \(R_b R_a W^0\) and quickly finetune on a small multitask objective).
- Consider interpolation on the manifold between identity and each \(R\) to trade off tasks smoothly.

5) Bottom line (what the paper says + recommended takeaway)
- Paper: algebraically composing OFT matrices is allowed and remains orthogonal; whether composition preserves task knowledge is explicitly left as an open problem in the paper. The paper provides theoretical tools (Cayley parameterization and the small-Q approximation) that suggest composition is most plausible in a small-rotation regime and when rotations act in separate subspaces (block structure).
- Practical expectation: composition can work in favorable settings (small rotations, disjoint blocks, compatible tasks) but should not be assumed to work generally. If you want a reliable multi-task model, either train a joint OFT for multiple tasks or (if you need post-hoc composition) prefer small/COFT-style rotations and consider generator-space addition (sum of skew-symmetric generators) or a small joint adaptation step after composition.

- write out explicit formulas and numerical tests you should run to check commutation and generator-additivity.

### What norm is used for the COFT constraint \(\|R-I\|\le\epsilon\) and why choose that norm?
Question:
The paper writes \(\|R-I\|\le\epsilon\) but does not specify whether this is operator (spectral) norm, Frobenius norm, or elementwise. Which norm is used in experiments, and how does the choice affect the set of allowed orthogonal matrices (e.g. it's qualitatively different whether spectral vs Frobenius)?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper’s theoretical argument uses the operator (spectral) norm: when they invoke the Neumann series they explicitly say “(under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm)”, so the natural reading of the constraint
  \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon
  in the derivation is the operator (spectral) norm \|\cdot\|_2.
- The paper does not explicitly state in the text which norm was used in the implementation/experiments. The authors also say they enforce the related constraint on the Cayley parameter \bm{Q} “by projected gradient descent”, but they do not spell out which projection (spectral vs Frobenius vs elementwise) was used.

Because of that ambiguity, if you need a definitive answer about the experiments you should check the released code or ask the authors. Below I explain why the paper’s theory points to the spectral norm, and what the practical and qualitative differences are between choosing spectral, Frobenius or elementwise norms.

Why the spectral norm is the natural theoretical choice here
- The Neumann-series argument in the paper is written precisely with the operator norm in mind. They expand
  \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
  “under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm.” Neumann-series convergence is normally stated in the operator (spectral) norm (it ensures \|\bm{Q}\|_2<1 suffices for (I-Q)^{-1} to be well-defined by the series).
- Translating the COFT constraint \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon into a constraint on \bm{Q} therefore naturally uses the same operator norm language, giving \|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon' in the operator norm as the consistent theoretical statement.

What each norm actually constrains (intuition + short formulas)
- Spectral/operator norm \|\cdot\|_2:
  - Measures the largest singular value / worst-case amplification on unit vectors.
  - For an orthogonal matrix \bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d) with rotation angles \{\theta_k\} (real orthogonal matrices are block-diagonalizable into 2×2 rotations), one can show
    \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_2 = \max_k |e^{i\theta_k}-1| = 2\max_k\sin(\theta_k/2).
  - Thus \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_2\le\epsilon bounds the largest rotation angle: every direction is rotated by at most \theta_{\max}=2\arcsin(\epsilon/2). This is a uniform (worst‑case) angular bound.
- Frobenius norm \|\cdot\|_F:
  - Measures the root-sum-square of all entries (equivalently the \ell_2 norm of singular values).
  - For \bm{R} as above,
    \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_F = 2\sqrt{\sum_k \sin^2(\theta_k/2)}.
  - So \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_F\le\epsilon bounds the RMS (energy) of the rotation angles: a few large angles can be tolerated if others are small. It is a more permissive, aggregate constraint.
- Elementwise norms (e.g. max entry, \ell_\infty or entrywise \ell_2):
  - Constrain entries directly; these are less geometrically meaningful for orthogonality and do not translate cleanly to angular bounds.

Qualitative consequences of choosing spectral vs Frobenius
- Spectral constraint (tight worst-case control)
  - Guarantees no direction gets rotated beyond a small angle. This matches the paper’s stated goal of “constraining the finetuned model within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model” in a worst-case, operator sense.
  - Keeps the largest change in any neuron direction small — strong uniform regularization.
- Frobenius constraint (energy control)
  - Allows concentrated, larger rotations in a small subspace while keeping overall “energy” small.
  - Could permit some neurons to change substantially (possibly harming semantic preservation) while keeping the global difference small.
- Elementwise constraints
  - Harder to reason about geometrically for rotations and likely less appropriate for the stated angle-preservation goal.

Computational / implementation considerations
- Projecting onto a spectral‑norm ball requires SVD / singular-value clipping (can be expensive for large matrices).
- Projecting onto a Frobenius ball is cheap: scale the matrix if its Frobenius norm exceeds \epsilon (i.e. \bm{Q}\leftarrow\bm{Q}\cdot(\epsilon/\|\bm{Q}\|_F)).
- The paper’s practical enforcement statement (“the new constraint on the matrix \bm{Q} can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent”) suggests they enforced a simple projection; that is often done with the Frobenius norm in practice because it is computationally cheap. But the paper does not explicitly say this, so it is an implementation detail not documented in the text.

Practical recommendation
- If you want the theoretical guarantee and interpretation the paper uses (control of maximal rotation), implement the operator/spectral-norm constraint. That matches the Neumann-series derivation and yields a direct bound on the largest angular change.
- If you need a cheaper implementation and are willing to accept a slightly different constraint (allowing concentrated changes), use Frobenius-norm projection on \bm{Q}. It is much cheaper and often behaves similarly for small \epsilon, but it is a different feasible set.
- To know what the authors actually used in experiments: check their public code or ask them. If unavailable, you can instrument both versions on a small finetuning run and compare behavior (e.g. max rotation angle across neurons, final metrics, stability).

Summary
- The paper’s theoretical steps point to the operator (spectral) norm. However, the manuscript never explicitly states which norm was used in the experiments. The difference matters: spectral bounds the largest angular displacement of any direction (a uniform/worst-case guarantee), while Frobenius bounds aggregate energy and can allow large changes in a few directions. To be certain about the experiments, inspect the code or ask the authors; for a faithful theoretical implementation, use the spectral norm.

### How sensitive are results to the block-sharing option where \(R_i=R_j\) for all blocks?
Question:
The block-diagonal parameterization allows sharing the same block across positions to reduce parameters further. Did the authors test shared vs independent blocks? How does sharing affect expressivity and empirical performance in subject-driven and controllable tasks?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper explicitly describes the option to share block matrices (R_i = R_j) and gives the theoretical parameter-count savings, but it does not present a dedicated ablation in the main text comparing shared vs independent blocks.
- Intuitively, block-sharing preserves the hyperspherical-energy invariance (so semantic-preservation properties remain) but reduces expressivity (fewer degrees of freedom), so you should expect:
  - better parameter efficiency and likely improved stability/less overfitting in low-data regimes when blocks are shared, and
  - greater flexibility and potentially higher peak performance (but more risk of instability / need for more data) when blocks are independent.
- Practical recommendation: try shared blocks when you are extremely parameter- or data-limited; use independent blocks (or fewer, larger blocks) when you need finer-grained control and have enough data/params. Combine COFT (the epsilon constraint) to stabilize larger independent block parameterizations.

What the paper actually says
- The paper introduces a block-diagonal parameterization for the orthogonal matrix R to reduce parameter cost. It then notes: "We can optionally share the block matrix to further reduce the number of parameters, i.e., R_i = R_j, ∀ i ≠ j. This reduces the parameter complexity to O(d^2/r^2). Despite all these strategies to improve parameter efficiency, we note that the resulting matrix R remains orthogonal, so there is no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy."
- The paper also flags a related caveat in the conclusions: "The parameter efficiency of OFT is largely dependent on the block diagonal structure which inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility."
- However, the experiments and reported results (subject-driven and controllable generation) do not show an explicit ablation table or plot that isolates the effect of block-sharing (shared vs independent R_i). So a direct empirical comparison is not reported in the paper.

Intuition: how sharing affects expressivity and preservation
- Degrees of freedom:
  - Independent blocks: you learn r different orthogonal blocks. Each block of size d/r has (d/r)(d/r - 1)/2 free parameters; total free parameters ≈ r * (d/r)(d/r - 1)/2 = O(d^2/r).
  - Shared blocks: you learn one block and repeat it across positions → free parameters ≈ (d/r)(d/r - 1)/2 = O(d^2/r^2).
  - So sharing reduces the number of learnable parameters roughly by a factor of r.
- Hyperspherical-energy preservation:
  - Both shared and independent block-diagonal R remain orthogonal, so both preserve pairwise neuron angles (i.e., they maintain hyperspherical energy invariance). Thus semantic-preservation benefits that motivate OFT are not lost by sharing.
- Expressivity vs. regularization:
  - Sharing imposes a strong structural prior (same rotation repeated across channel groups). That is useful as a regularizer: fewer parameters → lower overfitting risk and often more stable finetuning, especially when data are scarce.
  - Independent blocks give more capacity to change neuron directions differently across groups; that can produce better task adaptation when the downstream task requires different transforms across channels or features, but needs more data / careful tuning to avoid instability.

Concrete numeric example (paper uses similar numbers)
- For d = 128 and r = 8:
  - Block size = d/r = 16.
  - Parameters per (skew-symmetric) block = 16 * 15 / 2 = 120.
  - Independent blocks total = 8 * 120 = 960 parameters (this matches the paper example).
  - If blocks are shared, total parameters = 120 → a 8× reduction compared to independent blocks.

Expected empirical behavior on the paper’s tasks
- Subject-driven generation (few-shot DreamBooth-style):
  - These are very data-limited finetuning tasks. Shared blocks are likely to perform well because they give strong regularization, preserve pretrained geometry (via orthogonality), and reduce overfitting. The paper emphasizes stability and sample efficiency as OFT strengths; block-sharing should accentuate those strengths.
  - Independent blocks may give modest gains if you have more subject images / iterations, because they allow more nuanced direction changes per channel, but they raise the risk of breaking pretrained structure unless constrained (COFT helps).
- Controllable generation (control signals like edges/segmentation):
  - If the control requires per-channel or spatially-varying adaptations (e.g., different processing for different channels or feature groups), independent blocks can better capture that and may improve control accuracy (control-consistency metrics).
  - If the control is relatively homogeneous across channels, or you want to prioritize parameter/parsimony (e.g., when matching LoRA’s parameter budget), shared blocks are a good compromise and should keep the strong stability/convergence advantages reported for OFT/COFT in the paper.

Practical recommendations and ablation recipe
- Because the paper does not report a direct shared vs independent ablation, try this in your experiments:
  1. Fix block count r and block size (common choice in the paper: r around 8 for 128-d example). For each model/layer:
     - Run OFT with independent blocks (no sharing).
     - Run OFT with shared blocks (single block repeated).
     - Optionally include COFT (different epsilon) for each configuration.
  2. Measure:
     - Subject-driven: DINO, CLIP-I (subject fidelity), CLIP-T (prompt fidelity), LPIPS (diversity), and qualitative identity preservation.
     - Controllable: the paper’s control-consistency metrics (IoU/F1 for C2I, mIoU/acc for S2I, landmark L2 for L2F), plus visual checks for adherence to control signal.
     - Training dynamics: convergence speed and stability (do images collapse or does control suddenly appear?).
  3. Sweep r and block-sharing jointly: try a few values (e.g., r = 1, 4, 8, 16). Larger r → smaller block size → fewer params per block but more blocks to make independent.
- Heuristics to start with:
  - Very low-data (few-shot subject-driven): shared blocks or COFT with small epsilon.
  - Medium/large data or control tasks needing precise per-feature adaptation: independent blocks (and use COFT to constrain deviation if you see instability).
  - To match a strict parameter budget, compute the exact param counts (as illustrated above) and pick shared vs independent so you stay within the budget.

Hybrid and layer-wise strategies
- The paper hints that OFT is architecture-agnostic and that the block structure has different interpretations for convolutional vs transformer layers. A useful practical approach is to:
  - Use shared blocks on early layers (low-level features) where transformations can be more uniform.
  - Use independent blocks on later layers or attention heads where semantic/positional specialization is more important.
- Combine block-sharing with COFT (epsilon constraint) to get both strong regularization and some per-block flexibility.

Bottom line
- The paper presents the block-sharing option and the theoretical parameter savings, but does not publish a direct empirical comparison of shared vs independent blocks. The trade-off is clear and logical from the paper’s derivation: sharing keeps the hyperspherical-energy-preserving guarantee and gains parameter/stability benefits but reduces expressivity. Which side of the tradeoff is preferable depends on data size, control-task complexity, and parameter budget. Running a small ablation as suggested above will reveal the sweet spot for your specific task.

### Is the HE objective in Eq. (1) actually optimized during training or only implicitly enforced via orthogonality?
Question:
Equation (1) states minimizing the HE difference, but then the practical method learns an orthogonal \(R\) which guarantees HE preservation. Do they ever add HE as an explicit regularizer during training, or is HE preservation only achieved by constraining the update to orthogonal transforms?
Answer:
Short answer
They do not add a hyperspherical-energy (HE) loss term and do not explicitly optimize Eq. (1) during training. Eq. (1) in the paper is a motivating objective: instead of adding HE as a regularizer, the method enforces HE preservation by constraining the finetuned weights to be an orthogonal transform of the pretrained weights (OFT) — i.e., they optimize the orthogonal matrix R (and optionally a small scaling D) under orthogonality / deviation constraints. Because orthogonal transforms exactly preserve inner products and hence cosine similarities, this constraint guarantees the HE difference is zero (up to numerical error).

Why the paper writes the HE minimization but then uses orthogonality
- Eq. (1) (the paper's Eq. 1, labeled eq:oft_principle) frames the finetuning goal as minimizing
  \|HE(W) - HE(W^0)\|.
  The paper then observes that a sufficient (and easy-to-enforce) way to make this zero is to set W = R W^0 for some orthogonal matrix R, because orthogonal maps preserve pairwise angles and normalized distances.
- So rather than computing HE and backpropagating through that complicated nonconvex loss, they re-parameterize the update as a multiplicative orthogonal transform (Eq. (3) in the paper, eq:oft_general):
  z = W^T x = (R W^0)^T x,  subject to R^T R = I.
  Optimizing R (with orthogonality enforced via Cayley parameterization or other differential orthogonalization) therefore enforces HE preservation by construction.

Intuition / math behind why orthogonality enforces HE preservation
- HE is built from pairwise (normalized) distances or cosine similarities between neurons: HE(W) depends only on normalized inner products \hat w_i^T \hat w_j.
- For any orthogonal R, for all vectors u,v we have (Ru)^T (Rv) = u^T v. Hence the angles (and cosines) between neurons are unchanged by a common orthogonal transform applied to all neurons in a layer.
- Therefore if W = R W^0 with R orthogonal, HE(W) = HE(W^0) exactly, so the HE-difference objective is satisfied without adding that term to the loss.

Practical implementation details in the paper
- They do not add an explicit HE regularizer. Instead:
  - R is parameterized to be orthogonal (Cayley parameterization R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric) so orthogonality is maintained.
  - They initialize R = I so finetuning starts at the pretrained weights (zero HE difference).
  - For COFT they also add the explicit radius/epsilon constraint ||R - I|| <= epsilon to restrict deviation from identity (implemented approximately by constraining Q via the Neumann series expansion R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) and projecting Q).
  - Re-scaled OFT learns a diagonal D as well; rescaling neurons does not change HE because HE is computed from normalized neurons.
- Because all of these keep R orthogonal (or very close, up to numerical approximations), HE preservation is achieved implicitly by the parameterization and constraints, not by optimizing an HE loss.

Caveats / practical considerations
- Numerical error and approximation: Cayley requires inverting (I-Q); numerical precision and approximations (e.g., Neumann approximation for enforcing ||R-I||) can introduce tiny deviations from exact orthogonality and thus small HE changes in practice. The paper notes ways to control this (block-diagonal parameterization, small Q, projected updates).
- Block-diagonal R / parameter sharing: using block-diagonal or shared blocks restricts the family of orthogonal transforms (adds regularization). Those transforms are still orthogonal, so they still preserve HE exactly for the transformed space; the restriction just reduces flexibility but keeps the HE invariance property.
- The Cayley parameterization produces matrices in SO(d) (determinant +1), excluding reflections (determinant -1). This does not change the HE-preservation claim because both rotations and reflections are orthogonal and preserve inner products; the limitation to SO(d) is insignificant for the intended purpose and the paper reports it did not harm performance.

Why the authors avoided an explicit HE loss
- Computing and minimizing HE directly would be an additional complicated nonconvex objective, potentially expensive and possibly harmful (the authors explicitly argue that minimizing HE itself is not desirable for finetuning — minimizing HE can produce uniform neuron spacing that may destroy pretrained semantic structure).
- The orthogonality constraint is a clean, compact way to ensure HE is preserved while still allowing useful finite-dimensional changes to neuron directions.

Conclusion
- Eq. (1) is the conceptual objective (preserve HE), but in practice the paper enforces that objective by constraining the finetuning to orthogonal (and optionally near-identity) transforms and by parameterizing/optimizing R directly. They do not add HE as an explicit regularizer in the training loss.

### How are edge cases handled when Cayley denominator \((I-Q)\) becomes near-singular?
Question:
If \(I-Q\) is close to singular, the Cayley transform will be numerically unstable. Is there any mechanism (e.g. regularization on \(Q\), clamping eigenvalues) to prevent singularity? Did the authors observe such instabilities during training?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper uses the Cayley parametrization R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric, and it adds several practical safeguards that avoid the I - Q becoming problematic in practice: identity initialization (Q = 0), block-diagonal Q to shrink the matrix inverse, and — for the constrained variant COFT — an explicit projection constraint on Q (enforced by projected gradient descent) that keeps Q small. The authors report stable training (OFT is stable, and COFT is even more stable) and do not report encountering singularity failures in their experiments.
- The paper also explicitly flags the matrix inverse as a scalability / efficiency concern (an open problem) but not as a source of observed numerical breakdown.

Explanation and grounding in the paper

1) Why I - Q is not an uncontrolled singularity in their setup
- Q is constrained to be skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^T). The Cayley map they use is the classical Cayley parametrization to produce orthogonal matrices.
- The authors initialize Q = 0 so training starts at R = I. That guarantees the denominator I - Q is well conditioned at the start of training and the forward pass equals the pretrained weights initially.
- For COFT they explicitly constrain the deviation from identity, i.e. require ||R − I|| ≤ ε. Using the Neumann-series approximation they map that constraint to a small-norm constraint on Q, ||Q|| ≤ ε' (see Section “Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning”). That constraint is enforced with projected gradient descent on Q. Keeping Q small prevents I − Q from becoming ill-conditioned.

2) Practical algorithmic choices that reduce numerical risk (from the paper)
- Block-diagonal parameterization of R: they parameterize R as block-diagonal (and can share blocks). That reduces the size of the matrices that must be inverted (you invert each block-sized (I − Q_i)), which both reduces compute and reduces the chance of numerical problems from inverting a very large dense matrix.
- Identity (zero) initialization of Q ensures the optimization path starts from a numerically safe point.
- For COFT the paper explicitly performs projection on Q to keep it within a ball (projected gradient descent) — an explicit regularization on Q.

3) What the authors observed empirically
- The experiments and ablations emphasize that OFT converges stably and COFT yields “even better finetuning stability.” Figures and convergence plots in the paper show stable, smooth convergence; the paper does not report any numerical instabilities caused by the Cayley denominator becoming singular or near-singular.
- The authors do note a related practical limitation: “OFT guarantees the orthogonality via Cayley parametrization which involves a matrix inverse. It slightly limits the scalability of OFT. … how to speed up this matrix inverse in a differentiable way remains a challenge.” So they treat inversion cost / scalability as an open problem, not as an observed catastrophic numerical instability in their experiments.

Intuition / brief math note (why the denominator is not generally catastrophic)
- Because Q is skew-symmetric and small in norm in the constrained case, (I − Q) stays well away from singularity in practice. (The paper uses the Neumann-series approximation R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) when Q is small, which is the same idea: small Q implies a benign inverse.)
  (The paper does not go into spectral bounds in detail; the above is the rationale they use to map the R-deviation constraint to a Q-norm constraint.)

If you are concerned about robustness in larger or different settings (not covered in the paper)
- The paper does not implement or report additional low-level stabilization tricks (e.g., Tikhonov regularization of the inverse, explicit eigenvalue clamping of Q, or adding a small damping term to the diagonal of I − Q). These are standard numerical stabilizers you could try if you ever saw conditioning problems in a different regime:
  - Damping: invert (I − Q + δI) with tiny δ > 0 (biases inverse slightly toward identity).
  - Norm clipping or stronger projection on Q after each update.
  - Use smaller blocks (more blocks in block-diagonal) to reduce inversion size and improve conditioning.
  - Use iterative linear solvers or specialized routines for small dense blocks rather than a full dense inverse.
- The paper already uses projected gradient descent on Q for COFT and block-diagonalization; those are the mechanisms they report and use.

Summary
- Mechanisms in the paper: identity init (Q = 0), block-diagonal Q (smaller inverses), and explicit projected-norm constraint on Q in COFT (via the Neumann-series mapping) — all implemented to keep (I − Q) numerically safe.
- Empirical behavior: the authors report stable training (OFT) and improved stability with COFT and do not report encountering singularity-related failures. The remaining open issue they flag is the cost/scalability of the matrix inverse, not numerical breakdown from singular denominators.

### Why does minimizing HE (as in Liu et al. 2021) hurt pretrained semantic structure, while OFT preserves it?
Question:
The paper contrasts OFT (preserve HE) with orthogonal over-parameterized training that minimizes HE for classification (which helps generalization). Intuitively, why would minimizing HE destroy pretrained semantic structures in generative diffusion models, whereas preserving HE is better?
Answer:
Short answer — because minimizing hyperspherical energy (HE) forces neurons to become uniformly spread on the unit sphere (destroying the learned angular relations that encode semantic structure), while OFT only applies orthogonal transforms which exactly preserve all pairwise angles (and therefore preserve HE and the pretrained semantic geometry). Minimizing HE is a useful inductive bias when training classification nets from scratch (it reduces redundancy and helps class separation), but that same operation breaks the angular “phase”-like relationships a pretrained diffusion model uses to represent rich generative semantics.

Detailed explanation (intuitive + technical)

1) What HE measures and why angles matter for semantics
- The paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE) to measure pairwise relations among normalized neurons in a layer. For a weight matrix W with normalized columns \hat w_i, the HE used in the paper is
  HE(W) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
  This depends only on the pairwise angles / cosine similarities between neurons (because \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\| is a function of their dot product).
- Empirically and in the paper’s motivating examples, the directions (angles) of neurons encode semantic structure (the paper likens it to phase information in Fourier analysis: the phase/angles preserve the major part of semantics, while magnitudes often do not).

2) What minimizing HE does
- Minimizing HE (as in Liu et al. 2021 and related orthogonal over-parameterized training) pushes neurons to be as “uniformly spaced” on the sphere as possible (pairwise distances large). In other words, it encourages a configuration with low HE, which equates to reducing redundancy and giving a well-spread set of directions.
- That is a desirable inductive bias for classification trained from scratch: fewer redundant neurons, better margins and generalization across classes.

3) Why that hurts pretrained generative (diffusion) models
- A pretrained diffusion model does not have neuron directions arranged to be uniformly spread as a generic prior; instead those directions (and their relative angles) reflect the semantic factors the model learned during pretraining (object identities, styles, composition priors, etc.).
- Minimizing HE during finetuning will change those angles toward a uniform layout — it will “erase” the special angular relationships that encode semantic generation knowledge. Practically, this can destroy subject identity, fine-grained semantics and the model’s ability to generate varied, meaningful images conditioned on text/control.
- The paper explicitly notes this: “The minimum hyperspherical energy means all neurons are uniformly spaced around the hypersphere. This is not a meaningful objective for finetuning, as it may destroy the pretraining information.”

4) Why OFT avoids that damage
- Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) updates a layer by left-multiplying the pretrained weights W^0 by an orthogonal matrix R:
  W = R W^0 with R^\top R = I.
- Orthogonal matrices are isometries on the sphere: for any pair of normalized neurons \hat w_i and \hat w_j,
  (R \hat w_i)^\top (R \hat w_j) = \hat w_i^\top \hat w_j.
  So all pairwise cosines (angles) stay exactly the same, and thus HE(W) = HE(W^0). In short, OFT preserves the entire angular geometry that the pretrained model has learned.
- That means OFT can change how those neurons act on inputs (by rotating the coordinate system) while not destroying the angular relations that carry semantics. The paper interprets this as adjusting the coordinate system for neurons instead of reconfiguring their semantic relationships.

5) Extra practical safeguards: COFT and scaling
- The paper also introduces Constrained OFT (COFT) which additionally enforces that R stays near identity (small \|R - I\|). That couples angle-preservation with a small Euclidean deviation from the pretrained weights, giving even stronger preservation of pretrained behavior and improved stability.
- Re-scaled OFT adds per-neuron positive scalars (a diagonal D) so magnitudes can adapt without changing HE (magnitudes are normalized away in HE). This gives flexibility while keeping pairwise angular structure intact.

6) Analogy and intuition
- Think of neuron directions as landmarks on a globe encoding a semantic map. Minimizing HE is like redistributing the landmarks evenly over the globe — helpful if you start from scratch and want no redundancy, but destructive if the landmarks already encode meaningful clusters and relationships. OFT is like rotating the globe: all relative bearings remain the same, so the map (semantics) is preserved even though we might view it from a new orientation.

7) Why Liu et al. 2021 doesn’t contradict the paper
- Liu et al. used orthogonal over-parameterized training to train classification networks from scratch and deliberately kept HE small because that helps generalization in that setting. The key differences called out by the paper are: (a) objective and task (classification from scratch vs finetuning a pretrained generative model), and (b) desiderata (minimize HE for generalization vs preserve HE to keep pretrained semantics). The same manipulation (driving HE toward minimum) that helps the first setting is harmful in the second.

Bottom line / practical takeaway
- For finetuning large pretrained diffusion models, the angular configuration (pairwise neuron angles / HE) embeds critical semantic generation structure. Actively minimizing HE will typically destroy that structure and harm generative performance. Using multiplicative orthogonal updates (OFT), which exactly preserve angles and HE, lets you adapt a model while keeping the pretrained semantic geometry intact — and COFT further helps by limiting how far you rotate from the pretrained weights. This is why the paper empirically finds OFT/COFT preserves subject identity and control better than approaches that do not preserve HE.

### How is the projection of \(Q\) onto \(\|Q\|\le\epsilon'\) implemented during projected SGD?
Question:
The paper says the constraint on \(Q\) can be enforced via projected gradient descent. Do they project by scaling the Frobenius norm, clipping singular values, or truncating entries? Which projection was used in experiments and why?
Answer:
Short answer
- The paper only says the constraint on the Cayley parameter Q, "||Q - 0|| <= epsilon'", is enforced "by projected gradient descent" but does not spell out which matrix norm or which concrete projection operator was used in the experiments.
- From the text you can read two signals: (a) the theoretical derivation invoked the Neumann series and convergence "in the operator norm", which suggests the constraint is conceptually on an operator/spectral norm; (b) the paper also says the projection is "easy" to enforce, which in practice usually means a cheap Frobenius-ball projection (uniform scaling) applied per block.
- If you want to reproduce the paper the two most sensible choices are:
  - Spectral-norm (operator-norm) projection — matches the theory (but is more expensive: needs SVD or power-iteration).
  - Frobenius-norm projection (scale Q down if ||Q||_F > eps') — cheap, preserves skew-symmetry and block structure, and is the usual practical choice when authors say "projected gradient descent" without extra detail.

Below I explain the reasoning, trade-offs, and give explicit projection recipes (including code-like formulas) and implementation tips so you can pick the right option for your reproduction.

What the paper actually says
- In Section "Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning" the authors move the constraint on the orthogonal matrix R,
  R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
  into a small-norm constraint on the skew-symmetric Q: "||Q - 0|| <= epsilon'".
- They then say "The new constraint on the matrix Q can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent" and that Q is initialized to zero.
- They do NOT state which matrix norm (operator / spectral norm, Frobenius norm, infinity norm, etc.) they used for the projection, nor do they give algorithmic detail in the main paper text you provided.

Which norm is suggested by the theory?
- The paper explicitly uses the Neumann series expansion and says "under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm" they approximate
  R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2).
- Because that statement invokes operator norm convergence, the mathematically consistent interpretation is that the smallness constraint is on the operator (spectral) norm, i.e. ||Q||_2 <= eps' (largest singular value bounded).
- If your goal is strict adherence to the derivation (guarantee that (I - Q)^{-1} exists and the Neumann expansion is meaningful in operator norm), you should project in the spectral norm.

Why people often use Frobenius-ball projection in practice
- Projecting onto a Frobenius norm ball is trivial and very cheap: if ||Q||_F <= eps' do nothing, else set Q := Q * (eps' / ||Q||_F). This operation preserves skew-symmetry and any block-diagonal structure.
- Spectral-norm projection requires computing an SVD (or at least the top singular value/vector) of each Q (or block of Q). For large blocks this is more expensive and often avoided unless the theoretical need for operator norm control is paramount.
- The paper emphasizes efficiency (they use block-diagonal Q and discuss efficiency). The phrase "easily enforced by projected gradient descent" strongly suggests the authors had a cheap projection in mind — typically the Frobenius-ball scaling per block.

Practical projection recipes

1) Frobenius-ball projection (fast, preserves skew-symmetry and blocks)
- Let Q be your skew-symmetric block (or whole Q). Compute f = ||Q||_F.
- If f <= eps' do nothing.
- Else Q := Q * (eps'/f).
- This is O(d^2) per block to compute the Frobenius norm and O(d^2) to scale; it is simple and stable.

Formula:
Q <- { Q, if ||Q||_F <= eps' ; (eps'/||Q||_F) * Q, otherwise. }

2) Spectral (operator)-norm projection (theory-aligned, more expensive)
- You want to enforce ||Q||_2 <= eps'. One way is to compute SVD: Q = U S V^T, clamp singular values s_i to min(s_i, eps'), and reconstruct Q_proj = U S_clamped V^T.
- For skew-symmetric Q the SVD still works; ensure you keep skew-symmetry by either projecting the full Q and then re-skew-symmetrizing Q := (Q_proj - Q_proj^T)/2 (this may change the singular values slightly) or by working with a skew-symmetric parameter that stays skew during updates and using a spectral projection implementation that preserves that structure.
- If d is large, use power iteration to estimate the top singular value sigma_max; if sigma_max <= eps' you are done, otherwise you can scale Q by (eps'/sigma_max) (this is equivalent to projecting onto the spectral norm ball only when Q is rank-one—strictly speaking full projection requires SVD, but scaling by sigma_max is often used as a cheaper heuristic).

Exact projection (SVD):
Q_proj = U diag( min(s_i, eps') ) V^T.

Cheaper check-and-scale (approximate):
Estimate sigma_max (power iteration). If sigma_max > eps', scale Q := Q * (eps'/sigma_max).

3) Entrywise clipping (not recommended here)
- Clip each entry q_ij to be in [-c, c] for some c. This is not the same as a projection onto a norm ball and has different geometric meaning. You must also preserve skew-symmetry (e.g., clip only upper triangle and set q_ji = -q_ij). This is rarely used when the theoretical reasoning uses operator norm or Frobenius norm.

Which should you use for experiments / reproduction?
- Because the paper does not specify, you have to choose based on priorities:
  - If you want to follow the theoretical derivation strictly (Neumann/operator-norm), implement a spectral-norm projection (SVD or top-singular-value scaling).
  - If you want a practical, fast, reproducible implementation (especially with block-diagonal Q), use Frobenius-ball projection by uniform scaling per block. This preserves skew-symmetry automatically and is simple to code; it is almost certainly what authors would call "easily enforced".
- My practical recommendation: use per-block Frobenius projection (scale a block if its Frobenius norm exceeds eps'). If you see notable instability or want theoretical guarantees for the Cayley expansion, try the spectral projection (SVD) on each block.

Implementation tips and pseudo-code (per-block Frobenius projection)

Assume Q is stored as block-diagonal skew-symmetric blocks or as a full skew-symmetric matrix:

for each block Q_b:
  f = sqrt(sum_ij Q_b[i,j]^2)   # Frobenius norm
  if f > eps_prime:
    Q_b *= (eps_prime / f)

Keep Q skew-symmetric by construction: if you store only the upper-triangle variables p and set Q = p - p^T, scaling p scales Q consistently.

If you want spectral projection and blocks are small, you can:
for each block Q_b:
  U, S, Vt = svd(Q_b)
  S_clamped = min(S, eps_prime)
  Q_b = U @ diag(S_clamped) @ Vt
  # if numerical skew-symmetry lost: Q_b = (Q_b - Q_b.T)/2

A final note about experiments in the paper
- The manuscript explicitly states "projected gradient descent" on Q but provides no further implementation detail in the quoted sections. The paper does not state in the main text which norm/projection was used in their experiments. If you need the exact choice the authors used for their reported numbers you should check the paper's code repository / appendix or ask the authors directly. If no code is available, the Frobenius projection per block is the most likely and the easiest to reproduce.

Summary
- Paper: "projected gradient descent" on Q, but unspecified norm.
- Theory suggests operator (spectral) norm is the natural choice; practical authors commonly use Frobenius scaling because it is cheap and preserves skew-symmetry and block structure.
- For reproduction, use per-block Frobenius-ball projection as the default; use spectral projection only when you require strict operator-norm control.

### How does OFT interact with textual conditioning and cross-attention weights specifically?
Question:
In diffusion models like Stable Diffusion, cross-attention mixes image latents and text embeddings. If OFT rotates attention weight matrices, could that disrupt alignment between spatial features and text embeddings? How is semantic alignment preserved when applying a layer-shared orthogonal rotation to attention weights?
Answer:
Short answer
- OFT preserves the angular geometry among neurons, and that angular geometry is what the paper argues carries semantic information. Because OFT is implemented as a layer-shared orthogonal re-parameterization (initialized to identity and often constrained tightly with COFT), it does not arbitrarily scramble the geometry that cross‑attention relies on.  
- Concretely: if the same orthogonal transform is applied consistently to the projection matrices involved in an attention layer (or equivalently if the transform is implemented in the attention output space), the pairwise inner products / angles that produce attention logits are preserved (or only change by a global change of basis), so the alignment between spatial features and text embeddings is not destroyed.  
- The paper gives empirical evidence that OFT (and COFT) preserves text fidelity and controllability (CLIP-T, DINO/CLIP-I, control consistency), which supports this argument.

Longer explanation with math and intuition

1) What cross‑attention computes (short recap)
- In a single cross‑attention head you normally compute queries q from image latents x and keys k from text embeddings e with linear projections:
  q = W_q^T x,  k = W_k^T e.
- The raw attention logits are q · k (or q k^T) and the softmax of those logits is used to route text information to spatial positions. So semantic alignment between image positions and text comes from the geometry (angles / inner products) between q and k vectors.

2) What OFT does to a linear layer
- The paper represents a layer weight as W^0 (pretrained) and replaces it by W = R W^0 with R orthogonal (R^T R = I). Columns of W are the neurons; left-multiplying by R rotates those neuron vectors in the input-feature space while preserving their pairwise angles. That is the key invariant OFT enforces: for any two neurons w_i and w_j,
  angle(w_i, w_j) = angle(R w_i, R w_j).
- Because hyperspherical energy is a sum-over-pairwise-angle quantity, a layer-shared orthogonal R leaves that quantity unchanged (this is the paper’s main theoretical point).

3) When does attention alignment stay intact?
There are two useful algebraic viewpoints that make preservation of alignment clear:

A. Right‑multiply viewpoint (preserves inner products exactly)
- Suppose a projection is represented so that the projection acts as q = (W^T) x (same as the paper but think in terms of the matrix form used in implementation). If you replace W by W R where R is orthogonal and you compute
  q' = (W R)^T x = R^T (W^T x) = R^T q,
  k' = R^T k,
  then the attention logits become
  q' · k' = (R^T q) · (R^T k) = q · k
  because R is orthogonal. So if the same orthogonal rotation is applied to the projection outputs (i.e., to the output basis of both the query and key projections), the dot products that produce attention logits are exactly preserved. Intuition: this is just a global orthonormal change of basis in the common query/key space — inner products (angles) are invariant.

B. Left‑multiply viewpoint (the paper’s parameterization) — change‑of‑inputs interpretation and why it is safe in practice
- The paper parameterizes W = R W^0 (left multiplication). For a projection q = W^T x this gives:
  q' = (R W^0)^T x = (W^0)^T (R^T x).
  So OFT can be seen as computing the original projection W^0^T but on rotated inputs R^T x. That means the network's behavior changes by a change of basis applied to the input to that layer.
- Two important points then:
  (i) OFT is layer‑shared and usually applied to the attention weights in a consistent way (the paper compares fairly to LoRA by applying OFT to the attention weights used by LoRA). If you apply matching transforms consistently across the projections that participate in attention (for example, share or co‑train R for the relevant linear maps in that attention block), you are either implementing the orthogonal change of basis in the output/query space (case A) or performing a consistent change in the input bases (case B). Either way the relative geometry that attention uses is preserved up to the orthonormal basis change.  
  (ii) Practically OFT is initialized at identity and COFT can constrain \|R − I\| ≤ ε. That means the rotation is small at the start and stays controlled; the model does not suddenly reorder or scramble semantic mappings learned during pretraining. This is exactly the stability property the paper highlights.

4) Intuition: why preserving neuron angles preserves semantic mapping
- The paper’s key hypothesis (backed by the toy autoencoder, and by hyperspherical‑energy theory) is that directions/angles of neurons encode the semantic mapping; magnitudes are less crucial. OFT explicitly preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in a layer, so the layer’s “semantic geometry” is preserved while allowing controlled re-orientation that implements task-specific adaptation.
- Because cross‑attention relies on geometric similarity (dot products / cosines) between q and k, preserving the geometry of projection neurons (and applying transforms consistently to the projections that make q and k) keeps the textual conditioning aligned with the spatial features.

5) Empirical support (from the paper)
- The experiments show that OFT / COFT preserve prompt fidelity (CLIP-T) and subject fidelity (DINO, CLIP-I) and improve controllability (control consistency metrics) while converging faster and more stably than baselines. Those empirical results are the practical evidence that OFT does not “break” text–image alignment in practice.

6) Practical takeaways / implementation notes (from the paper)
- Initialize R = I so training starts from the pretrained model (no sudden change).  
- Use layer‑shared orthogonal transforms and apply them consistently to the attention-related matrices you finetune (Q/K/V or the attention output) to avoid asymmetric transforms that would change inner products.  
- If you are still worried about disrupting alignment, use COFT’s extra radius constraint (\|R − I\| ≤ ε) — the paper finds COFT gives even better stability.  
- The block‑diagonal parameterization and Cayley parameterization used in the paper reduce parameter cost while keeping orthogonality; these choices further regularize the transform and limit how much the geometry can be altered.

Conclusion
OFT does not randomly rotate attention weights in a way that destroys image–text alignment. It enforces orthogonal, layer‑shared rotations that preserve pairwise neuron angles and, if applied consistently across the projections used by cross‑attention (or applied in the attention output basis), preserve the inner products that drive attention. COFT adds an explicit small‑deviation constraint for extra safety. The paper both explains the geometric invariance and demonstrates empirically that semantic alignment and text conditioning are maintained (or improved) under OFT.
