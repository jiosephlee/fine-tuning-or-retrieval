\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How exactly does OFT guarantee hyperspherical energy preservation under orthogonal transformations?
Question:
The paper defines the hyperspherical energy of a layer $W = \{w_1,\dots,w_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ as $\mathrm{HE}(W) := \sum_{i \neq j} \lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert^{-1}$ where $\hat{w}_i = w_i / \lVert w_i \rVert$. They claim that if we left-multiply by an orthogonal matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (i.e., $W' = R W$), the pairwise angles (and thus the hyperspherical energy) are preserved. Could someone provide a short derivation showing that $\angle(w_i, w_j) = \angle(Rw_i, Rw_j)$ implies $\lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert = \lVert \widehat{Rw_i} - \widehat{Rw_j} \rVert$ and therefore $\mathrm{HE}(W') = \mathrm{HE}(W)$? Also, does the presence of bias vectors or subsequent nonlinearities affect this invariance claim at the layer level?
Answer:
Short answer: because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and norms, they preserve the cosine of the angle between any two weight vectors; the Euclidean distance between the unit-normalized vectors is a simple function of that cosine, so it is preserved too. Therefore the pairwise terms in the hyperspherical energy are unchanged and so is HE. Biases and usual pointwise nonlinearities do not change that statement about the weight vectors themselves (they may change the layer’s functional output), but layers or operations that renormalize or otherwise modify the weights can affect the hyperspherical energy.

Derivation and intuition. Let $w_i,w_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be two columns (neurons) and $\hat w_i := w_i/\|w_i\|$ their unit-normalized versions. The hyperspherical energy uses the pairwise inverse distances $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$. A convenient algebraic identity relates this distance to the angle between $w_i$ and $w_j$: since $\|\hat w_i\|=\|\hat w_j\|=1$,

$$
\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^2 = \|\hat w_i\|^2+\|\hat w_j\|^2-2\langle \hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle
= 2-2\cos\theta_{ij},
$$

where $\cos\theta_{ij}=\langle \hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle$ and $\theta_{ij}=\angle(w_i,w_j)$. Thus $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\| = \sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}$. So any transformation that exactly preserves $\cos\theta_{ij}$ for all pairs preserves these unit-vector distances and therefore preserves every term in HE.

Now let $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be orthogonal, i.e. $R^\top R=I$. Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and norms:

$$
\langle Rw_i, Rw_j\rangle = w_i^\top R^\top R w_j = w_i^\top w_j,
\qquad \|Rw_i\|=\|w_i\|.
$$

Hence the cosine between $Rw_i$ and $Rw_j$ equals the cosine between $w_i$ and $w_j$, and the angle is preserved: $\angle(Rw_i,Rw_j)=\angle(w_i,w_j)$. From the norm preservation we also get $\widehat{Rw_i}=Rw_i/\|Rw_i\|=R(w_i/\|w_i\|)=R\hat w_i$. Combining with the inner-product preservation,

$$
\|\widehat{Rw_i}-\widehat{Rw_j}\|
= \|R\hat w_i-R\hat w_j\|
= \|R(\hat w_i-\hat w_j)\|.
$$

Because orthogonal $R$ preserves norms, $\|R(\hat w_i-\hat w_j)\|=\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|$. Therefore every pairwise distance of normalized columns is unchanged, and so

$$
\mathrm{HE}(RW)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\widehat{Rw_i}-\widehat{Rw_j}\|^{-1}
=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}=\mathrm{HE}(W).
$$

Remarks about biases and nonlinearities. The hyperspherical energy as defined in the paper is a property of the weight vectors (columns of $W$) after normalization. Adding a bias vector $b$ to the layer output (so output = $W^\top x + b$) does not alter the weight columns themselves, therefore it does not change HE$(W)$. Likewise, applying pointwise nonlinearities (ReLU, GELU, etc.) after the linear map does not change the weight vectors; HE of the weight matrix remains the same. However, note the important distinction between invariance of the weight-property and invariance of the layer’s functional behavior: even though HE is preserved under a left-multiplication by an orthogonal $R$, the mapping $x \mapsto W^\top x$ is changed to $x\mapsto (RW)^\top x$ unless you also change the input basis (i.e. pre-multiply inputs by $R$). In OFT the orthogonal $R$ is applied to the weights only, which preserves angular relations among neurons (and thus HE) but does change how the layer acts on inputs; that is intentional for finetuning.

Caveats: any operation that rescales, reparametrizes, or renormalizes weight vectors before computing HE (for example, explicit weight-normalization layers that change $\hat w_i$, or training steps that replace $W$ by a different parameterization not equivalent to $RW$) will affect HE. Also if you apply different orthogonal transforms to different neurons (not a single left-multiplication $R$ shared across the layer) then pairwise angles are not necessarily preserved.

Summary: orthogonal left-multiplication preserves inner products and norms, so it preserves angles and unit-vector distances; hence OFT’s layer-wise $R$ leaves the hyperspherical energy unchanged. Biases and pointwise nonlinearities do not alter the weight vectors and therefore do not affect this invariance of HE, though they do affect the layer’s input-output function.

### Reconciling the two definitions of hyperspherical similarity and energy used in the paper
Question:
Earlier, the paper motivates hyperspherical energy using "hyperspherical similarity (e.g., cosine similarity)" as a measure, but then defines the energy as $\mathrm{HE}(W)=\sum_{i \neq j}\lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert^{-1}$. Since on the unit sphere $\lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})$, this energy is a function of pairwise cosines but not equal to a sum of cosines. Why choose the inverse distance form rather than a direct function of $\cos\theta$ (e.g., $\sum_{i\neq j} -\cos\theta_{ij}$)? Does this specific form matter for the invariance and the empirical claims, or would any strictly monotone function of the angle suffice?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses the inverse chordal-distance form because it is the standard hyperspherical energy / Riesz potential used in the cited literature, and because that particular choice strongly penalizes very small angular separations (neighbors that “collide”). Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles (and thus any function of those angles), so the invariance claim does not depend on using the inverse-distance formula specifically. However, the exact functional form does matter for optimization behavior and empirical sensitivity: any strictly monotone function of the angle yields the same invariance and the same ordering of pairwise relations, but not the same gradients, conditioning, or how strongly small angles are penalized.

Why the paper uses the inverse-distance formula (technical + intuitive):
The paper defines hyperspherical energy as
$$\mathrm{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},$$
where $\hat w_i$ are unit vectors. On the sphere the squared chordal distance relates to cosine by $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})$, so $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ is a definite (monotone) function of the pairwise angle $\theta_{ij}$. This choice is not arbitrary: it is exactly a Riesz-type potential (Coulomb-like repulsion on the sphere) that has been used in the hyperspherical-energy literature the paper cites (e.g., Liu et al.). Intuitively this potential enforces “repulsion” among neuron directions: pairs with tiny angular separation produce very large contributions to the sum, so the objective strongly discourages neurons from clustering. That property is useful for (a) creating a meaningful measure of uniformity/spacing on the sphere and (b) making an optimization objective that actively resists near-collisions.

Invariance vs. functional choice:
Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products / angles: if $\hat w_i' = R\hat w_i$ with $R^\top R=I$, then $\theta_{ij}'=\theta_{ij}$ and therefore any function $g(\theta_{ij})$ or any symmetric function of chordal distance $h(\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|)$ is exactly preserved. So the key invariance statement in the paper (that a single shared orthogonal transform preserves the pairwise hyperspherical similarities) is true for the inverse-distance energy and is equally true for any other function that depends only on the pairwise angle. In that sense, the invariance claim does not hinge on the inverse-distance choice.

Why the specific form still matters in practice:
Although invariance holds for any angle-only function, different choices produce different optimization dynamics and empirical behavior. Converting cosine to e.g. $-\cos\theta_{ij}$ versus $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ changes the range, curvature and gradient magnitudes. The inverse-distance has a singular-like behavior as $\theta_{ij}\to 0$; it gives very large gradients for near-overlapping neurons and so strongly prevents collisions. A simple sum of $-\cos\theta_{ij}$ would prefer orthogonality in a weaker, more uniform way and would not blow up when two vectors get very close, so it may be less effective at actively separating near-duplicates. Thus two consequences follow: first, the optimization landscape (smoothness, Lipschitz constants, conditioning) changes; second, empirical phenomena such as stability to collapse, convergence speed, and how aggressively small angular deviations are corrected will differ.

Practical takeaway for the paper’s claims:
The theoretical invariance statement (orthogonal transforms preserve hyperspherical similarity) holds for any function of angles, so the high-level justification for OFT is robust: learning layer-shared orthogonal matrices preserves pairwise angular relations no matter which angle-only energy you use. But the authors chose the inverse-distance hyperspherical energy because it ties to prior theoretical work on hyperspherical energies and because it gives a strong repulsive penalty for small angles—properties that help make the measure meaningful and help the optimization avoid neuron collapse. If you replaced the energy with another strictly monotone function of $\theta$, you would keep invariance and the qualitative idea, but you would likely observe different empirical behavior (different stability, gradient magnitudes, and convergence), so the specific form is not irrelevant.

If you want a middle ground: using any strictly monotone mapping of $\theta$ keeps invariance and preserves the order of pairwise similarities; but if you care about preventing near-collisions and strong regularization of neuron directions, pick a function (like inverse-distance or a heavy-tailed kernel) that strongly penalizes small angles rather than a mild linear proxy like $-\cos\theta$.

### Is OFT merely a change of basis for the inputs, and does that limit its expressivity?
Question:
For a linear layer with pretrained weights $W^0$, OFT uses $z = (R W^0)^\top x$. Algebraically this equals $z = (W^0)^\top (R^\top x)$, i.e., applying $R^\top$ to the input feature before the pretrained weights. Does this mean OFT is just a learned reparameterization of the input basis for that layer? If so, how does OFT achieve task-specific adaptation across the entire network when non-linearities, residual connections, and cross-attention are present? Is there a subtlety because $R$ is different per layer (and not absorbed globally), so the composition cannot be reduced to a single global basis change?
Answer:
Short answer: No — algebraically a single OFT layer is equivalent to a learned orthogonal change of basis on that layer’s input, but taken across a real network (nonlinearities, residuals, attention) learning one orthogonal matrix per layer is not reducible to a single global basis change and therefore is expressive enough to adapt the model. The orthogonality constraint is a structural regularizer: it reduces degrees of freedom (so it is more constrained than full finetuning) but it does not collapse expressivity in practice and it gives stability by preserving pairwise neuron angles.

Why a single OFT layer looks like a basis change
For a linear layer with pretrained weights $W^0$ and an orthogonal matrix $R$ OFT uses
$$
z = (R W^0)^\top x = (W^0)^\top (R^\top x).
$$
So at that layer, the forward effect is exactly: first rotate the input by $R^\top$, then apply the original pretrained linear map $W^0$. That is literally a change of input basis for that layer.

Why that observation does not imply OFT is only a trivial reparameterization of the whole network
The key is that real networks are not one linear layer: they are deep compositions of linear maps and nonlinearities (activations), plus residual connections and attention modules. If you put different layer-wise orthogonal matrices $R_l$ at each layer $l$ you get
$$
\text{output} = \dots W_L'^\top \phi_L(\dots W_2'^\top \phi_1(W_1'^\top x)\dots)
$$
with
$$
W_l' = R_l W_l^0.
$$
Expanding one or two layers makes the point clear. For a two-layer network with nonlinearity $\phi$:
$$
y = W_2'^\top \phi(W_1'^\top x)
= (W_2^0)^\top R_2^\top \phi\big(R_1 (W_1^0)^\top x\big).
$$

There is no single global orthogonal matrix $R_{\text{global}}$ such that
$$
R_2^\top \phi(R_1 (\cdot)) = \phi\big(R_{\text{global}} (\cdot)\big)
$$
for a generic nonlinearity $\phi$. Nonlinearities break the conjugation that would be needed to absorb all $R_l$ into a single rotation on the network input or output. Concretely, rotations do not commute with nonlinear activations (except in degenerate cases), so layerwise rotations change the intermediate activation geometry and thereby change the subsequent nonlinear processing.

Residual connections and attention further prevent absorption. Consider a residual block $x + F(x)$. If you rotate the input to $F$ but not the skip, the sum interacts differently; to absorb the rotation you would need to rotate both branches in a globally consistent way, which OFT does not enforce. In multi-head attention, queries, keys and values are projected and combined; rotating the input spaces of those projections layer-by-layer alters inner products and attention weights in nontrivial ways. Thus different $R_l$ per layer produce changes in activations and attention patterns that are not equivalent to a single basis change.

Intuition about expressivity and the role of orthogonality
OFT restricts the finetuning manifold: instead of arbitrary additive changes to $W$, OFT only multiplies $W^0$ by an orthogonal matrix (possibly block-diagonal, and optionally with a per-neuron scale $D$ in the re-scaled variant). That is a constrained family of weight updates — fewer degrees of freedom than full finetuning but richer than doing nothing. Orthogonality preserves pairwise normalized neuron angles (hyperspherical energy), which the paper argues correlates with preserving pretrained semantic behavior. The constrained family still permits meaningful directional changes of neurons (rotations), and because these rotations are applied per layer and interact with nonlinearities, they change the network’s function in useful, task-specific ways while avoiding destructive updates.

Concrete consequences
1) Not globally absorbable: Since each layer has its own $R_l$, you cannot combine them into a single input-side rotation. The composition $R_L W_L^0, \phi, R_{L-1} W_{L-1}^0, \dots$ is not algebraically equivalent to $W_{\text{total}}^0$ acting on a globally rotated input because nonlinearities and residual/attention patterns break the conjugation needed for such absorption.

2) Stability vs flexibility trade-off: Orthogonality constrains updates to rotations, which stabilizes finetuning (preserves hyperspherical energy) and often speeds convergence as observed in the paper. That is exactly the intended design: less freedom than full finetuning, but enough to adapt directional/semantic features per layer.

3) Practical extensions: COFT adds an explicit small-deviation constraint to keep $R$ close to the identity, and re-scaled OFT adds a diagonal scaling $D$ so magnitudes can change (magnitudes do not affect hyperspherical energy). Those extensions increase flexibility while keeping the geometric regularization.

Bottom line
At the layer level OFT is a learned change of basis, but across a real model that change is applied layer-by-layer and interacts with nonlinearities, residuals and attention. Because those interactions are nonlinear, you cannot absorb all layerwise $R$'s into a single global basis rotation; therefore OFT genuinely changes the network’s function in a structured, regularized way. The method trades some expressivity for stability and semantic preservation, which the paper shows empirically yields better finetuning behavior for text-to-image diffusion models.

### Clarifying which UNet attention weights receive OFT and how $R$ is shared
Question:
The paper says "to compare fairly to LoRA, we only apply OFT to finetune the attention weights" in Stable Diffusion v1.5. Which specific matrices per attention block are transformed: $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$, and/or $W_O$? Is a distinct orthogonal $R$ learned per matrix and per layer, or shared among some of them (e.g., shared across $Q$, $K$, $V$ or across blocks at the same resolution)? How is the dimensionality $d$ in $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ chosen relative to the head dimension, number of heads, and concatenation layout?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper applies OFT to the same attention linear projections that they compare to LoRA (i.e., the attention projection matrices used by the transformer blocks). Concretely, OFT is applied to the linear weight matrices of the attention module (the projection matrices used to form $Q, K, V$ and the output projection) in the same places LoRA would be used. OFT learns an orthogonal transform per finetuned layer (you can choose whether to give each projection its own $R$ or to share $R$ across projections within a layer), and the size parameter $d$ of $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the input-dimension of the linear weight being transformed (so in practice it equals the "in_features" of that attention linear, e.g. the model embedding dimension or head-concatenated dimension, depending on implementation). The paper also uses block-diagonal and optional block-sharing to control parameter cost.

Explanation and how this follows from the paper
The paper states that, for a fair comparison to LoRA, they “only apply OFT to finetune the attention weights.” In transformer implementations attention is effected by a few linear layers (commonly denoted $W_Q,W_K,W_V$ and $W_O$ when all heads are implemented as single linear layers). OFT is formulated for a generic linear weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by replacing it with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (Section 3.2). Thus any attention linear can be finetuned by OFT by left-multiplying its pretrained weight by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$.

What is learned per layer / per matrix
The paper emphasizes a “layer-shared orthogonal transformation”: the orthogonal transform is learned per layer, i.e. per weight matrix, rather than per individual neuron. In practice that means you learn an $\bm{R}$ that acts on the rows (the input-dimension) of that linear layer. The authors also describe parameter-saving variants: (1) block-diagonal $\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ and (2) optionally sharing the block matrices (they explicitly say “We can optionally share the block matrix … $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j,\forall i\neq j$”), which reduces parameter count further. From these statements it follows that the default is one $\bm{R}$ per finetuned linear (i.e. per attention projection matrix / per transformer layer where you apply OFT), but the paper allows sharing across blocks and, by implication, you could choose to share a single $\bm{R}$ across multiple projections within the same layer if you want a smaller parameter budget.

How to choose $d$ relative to heads / concatenation layout
The matrix $\bm{R}$ has dimension $d\times d$ where $d$ is the input/row dimension of the target weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. In standard Transformer implementations where Q/K/V (and O) are implemented as single linear layers mapping the model embedding dimension to itself, $d$ will be the model embedding dimension (the total concatenated head dimension). If instead you apply OFT at per-head granularity (i.e. if Q/K/V are stored/used as separate per-head matrices or if you deliberately split the input into head-sized blocks), then you would set $d$ to the head dimension and could learn separate block transforms per head. The paper’s block-diagonal parameterization is precisely a way to align OFT with multi-head layouts: setting $r$ blocks of size $d/r$ yields orthogonal transforms applied to smaller subspaces (for example, one block per head if you set block size = head dimension). So in short: pick $d$ to match the input dimension of the linear you transform; use block-diagonalization to match multi-head or to reduce parameter cost.

Practical summary / recipe consistent with the paper
- Apply OFT to the same attention linear layers you would put LoRA on ($Q/K/V$ and/or $O$), because the paper deliberately uses the same locations for fair comparison.  
- By default learn one $\bm{R}$ per finetuned linear (layer-shared per weight matrix). If you need fewer parameters you can (a) use block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ and/or (b) share blocks across the matrix or even share a single $\bm{R}$ across $Q/K/V$ within the same transformer layer. The paper explicitly describes both block-diagonalization and block-sharing as options.  
- Set $d$ equal to the input dimension of the linear: for standard concatenated-head linear layers this is the full embedding dimension; for per-head transforms you would set $d$ to the head dimension (implemented by choosing block sizes appropriately). This keeps OFT consistent with the paper’s mathematical formulation and with their parameter-efficiency discussion.

Relation to LoRA and intuition
LoRA adds a low-rank additive update to the same projection matrices; OFT instead multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal transform (preserving hyperspherical energy). The choice of where to place OFT is therefore the same design choice as where you would insert a LoRA adapter: the attention projection matrices. The orthogonal transform acts on the input-coordinate system of the linear (hence $d$ equals that input dimension), and block or sharing choices trade flexibility for parameter efficiency exactly as the paper explains.

### Does re-scaled OFT truly leave hyperspherical energy unchanged?
Question:
In the re-scaled variant, the forward pass is $z = (R W^0 D)^\top x$ where $D = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ rescales columns (neurons) and $s_i > 0$. Since $\mathrm{HE}(W)$ uses normalized columns $\hat{w}_i = w_i/\lVert w_i \rVert$, my understanding is that column-wise scaling by $D$ cancels out in $\hat{w}_i$, so $\mathrm{HE}(R W^0 D) = \mathrm{HE}(R W^0)$. Is this always true even when some columns are zero or nearly zero? Are there any practical caveats (e.g., numerical stability) that could make $D$ inadvertently affect the computed energy or the angle preservation claim?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes, mathematically multiplying each column by a positive scalar does not change hyperspherical energy, but in practice there are important caveats. If every column $w_i$ is nonzero and every scale $s_i>0$, then normalization exactly cancels the scale and $HE(R W^0 D) = HE(R W^0)$. However if any column is zero (or numerically $\approx 0$) or when you account for floating‑point / implementation details, $D$ can affect the computed energy and the angle preservation claim.

Why the identity holds (mathematical intuition). Hyperspherical energy (as used in the paper) depends only on unit directions $\hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\|$ and pairwise distances between those unit vectors. If you replace each column $w_i$ by $s_i w_i$ with $s_i > 0$, then the normalized vector becomes
$\hat{(s_i w_i)} = (s_i w_i) / \|s_i w_i\| = w_i / \|w_i\| = \hat w_i$.
So all pairwise cosine/Euclidean distances on the unit sphere are unchanged; therefore HE is invariant to positive column-wise rescaling.

When the invariance breaks (practical issues).

1) Zero columns. If some column $w_i = 0$, its direction is undefined, normalization is undefined, and HE is not well defined. Multiplying a zero column by any $s_i$ still leaves it zero so the invariance statement is vacuous unless you first handle zero columns (e.g., remove them or initialize/regularize them away from $0$).

2) Near-zero columns and numerical stability. If $\|w_i\|$ is very small, dividing by it amplifies floating-point noise. In finite precision this can change the computed normalized vectors and hence HE. Extremely large or small $s_i$ can worsen conditioning: very small $s_i$ effectively make the corresponding normalized direction sensitive to tiny numerical perturbations; very large $s_i$ do not change the normalized vector mathematically but can change intermediate dynamic ranges and exacerbate rounding error in matrix multiplications and inbackprop.

3) Implementation details: $\epsilon$ in normalization and gradient flow. Practical code typically computes normalized vectors as $w_i / (\|w_i\| + \epsilon)$ to avoid divide‑by‑zero. That $\epsilon$ breaks exact cancellation: normalization of $s_i w_i$ gives $(s_i w_i)/(\|s_i w_i\| + \epsilon) = \hat w_i \cdot (\|w_i\|/(\|w_i\| + \epsilon/s_i))$. For finite $\epsilon$ this depends on $s_i$. So choices like adding $\epsilon$, single precision, or different normalization ordering will make HE depend weakly on $D$.

4) Learning dynamics (gradients). Even if HE computed from normalized columns is (theoretically) invariant to $D$, the presence of $D$ changes the forward activations before normalization and hence changes gradient magnitudes and optimization trajectories. In other words, although the final normalized directions are identical at any instant, the path taken by optimization (and the numerical calculation of HE during training, if computed without exact care) can be affected by $D$.

Practical recommendations
- Ensure no columns are exactly zero; add a small $L_2$ regularizer or initialize $s_i$ and $W^0$ so column norms are reasonable.
- Parameterize scaling safely, e.g. $s_i = \exp(t_i)$ so $s_i>0$ is enforced and extreme small/large values can be regularized via priors on $t_i$.
- When computing normalized columns use a small $\epsilon$ but be aware that $\epsilon$ couples with $s_i$; choose $\epsilon$ small relative to typical $\|w_i\|$ and consider using higher precision if you need exact invariance for diagnostics.
- Monitor column norms and HE during training to detect when near-zero norms or large conditioning appear.
- If you need exact invariance for a theorem or diagnostic, implement the analytic normalization (divide by exact norm in double precision) or explicitly renormalize columns after applying $D$ (renormalization removes the effect of $D$ in floating point too, up to rounding).
- Remember that even if HE is (nearly) invariant, $D$ affects activation magnitudes and gradients, so it can influence training stability and final weights through optimization dynamics even when HE stays constant.

Conclusion: Re-scaled OFT is theoretically HE‑preserving as long as every column has nonzero norm and $s_i>0$. In real implementations you must guard against zero / ill‑conditioned columns, numerical epsilons in normalization, and optimization effects; with proper parameterization and small eps/clamping these issues are controllable and the intended invariance holds to machine precision.

### Cayley parameterization details: determinant, stability, and constraints in COFT
Question:
The orthogonal matrix is parameterized as $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ with a skew-symmetric $Q = -Q^\top$. The paper states this generates only matrices with determinant $1$. I thought the Cayley map excludes orthogonals with eigenvalue $-1$, thus it parameterizes a subset of $SO(d)$ (with $\det=1$), not all of it. Is that the intended meaning? Also, since COFT imposes a constraint $\lVert R - I \rVert \le \epsilon$, the paper approximates $R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$ and then constrains $\lVert Q \rVert \le \epsilon'$. Which norm is used in practice (spectral, Frobenius), and how do you ensure the Neumann series converges (e.g., $\lVert Q \rVert < 1$)? Any advice to avoid numerical instabilities from $(I - Q)^{-1}$ during training?
Answer:
Short answers first: yes — the Cayley parameterization used in the paper only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ and it excludes orthogonals that have eigenvalue $-1$ (so it parametrizes the subset of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ with no eigenvalue equal to $-1$). The Neumann-series approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ is valid when the operator (spectral) norm of $Q$ is $< 1$ (so $(I-Q)$ is invertible and the Neumann series converges). In practice you must enforce a $Q$–norm bound (or otherwise ensure $(I-Q)$ stays well conditioned), initialize $Q = 0$, and use simple numerics (rescaling/clipping, block diagonal $Q$, solving linear systems instead of forming explicit inverse, stable linear algebra) to avoid instability.

Explanation and details grounded in the paper

1) What Cayley actually parameterizes
The Cayley map used in the paper is
$R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$, with $Q = -Q^T$.
Two important consequences follow (and the paper notes one of them):

• Determinant / parity: For any real skew-symmetric $Q$, $\det(R) = +1$, so $R \in \mathrm{SO}(d)$ (special orthogonal group). That is why the paper says the Cayley map “can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1.”

• Eigenvalue $-1$ exclusion: The Cayley transform is not surjective onto all of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$. It is defined only when $(I - Q)$ is invertible; equivalently, $R$ cannot have $-1$ as an eigenvalue. In fact the Cayley map is a diffeomorphism between the set of real skew-symmetric $Q$ with no eigenvalue of magnitude $\ge 1$ in a particular sense and the set of special orthogonal matrices with no eigenvalue $-1$. Intuitively: any $R$ that has an eigenvector with eigenvalue $-1$ would force $(I - R)$ singular, and such $R$ cannot be written as $(I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$. Thus the Cayley parameterization yields exactly those rotations in $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ that do not have eigenvalue $-1$.

So your understanding is correct: Cayley gives a subset of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (no eigenvalue $-1$), not every element of $O(d)$ (it also excludes reflections with $\det = -1$).

2) The Neumann-series approximation and which norm matters
The paper writes $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$ and comments that this uses the Neumann series "under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm." The relevant notion of norm for convergence of the matrix Neumann series is the operator (spectral) norm. The standard sufficient condition is
$\|Q\|_2 < 1$,
which guarantees that $(I - Q)$ is invertible and $(I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k$, and expanding $(I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ gives the series whose first-order term is $I + 2Q$.

Because the paper explicitly mentions the Neumann series in the operator norm, the correct theoretical constraint to guarantee the series and the linear approximation is a bound in spectral/operator norm. The paper then replaces the $R$ constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ by a corresponding constraint on $Q$, $\|Q\| \le \epsilon'$, relying on the first-order relation $R - I \approx 2Q$.

3) What norm do people use in practice (spectral vs Frobenius)
The paper does not commit to a specific implementation detail; it states the Neumann-series convergence in the operator norm and proposes projected gradient descent on $Q$ to enforce a bound. In practice there are tradeoffs:

• Spectral (operator) norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the right norm for the Neumann series and conditioning of $(I - Q)$. Enforcing $\|Q\|_2 < 1$ directly is the safest numerically. Projection onto a spectral-norm ball requires an SVD or eigenvalue decomposition (expensive for large blocks), or iterative approximations, but it enforces the theoretical condition.

• Frobenius norm $\|\cdot\|_F$ is much cheaper to compute/project (simple scaling) and is often used as a practical proxy. However $\|Q\|_F < c$ does not guarantee $\|Q\|_2 < 1$ ($\|Q\|_2 \le \|Q\|_F$), so you may need to choose a stricter Frobenius bound to be safe. Many implementations accept this pragmatic tradeoff when block sizes are small or blocks are shared.

The paper’s sentence about Neumann-series convergence in the operator norm suggests that spectral norm is the theoretically correct choice; but for computational efficiency many implementations either (a) use Frobenius-based clipping/projection, (b) rescale $Q$ when its spectral norm exceeds a threshold estimated cheaply (power iteration), or (c) use small block sizes so even exact spectral projections are inexpensive.

4) How to avoid numerical instability from $(I - Q)^{-1}$
Here are practical, implementation-level recommendations consistent with the paper and standard numerical practice:

• Initialize $Q = 0$. That guarantees $R = I$ at start and gives good stability (paper uses identity initialization).

• Keep $\|Q\|_2$ safely below $1$. Enforce this by spectral clipping or by scaling $Q$ whenever an efficiently estimated spectral norm (via a few power-iteration steps) exceeds a chosen threshold $\delta < 1$ (e.g., $0.5$ or $0.9$). Scaling is cheap and effective.

• Use block-diagonal $Q$: the paper already proposes block-diagonal $R$ (hence $Q$ blocks). Smaller blocks reduce the cost of spectral projection and improve conditioning; inversion or solves on smaller matrices are more stable.

• Avoid forming the inverse explicitly. Solve linear systems $(I - Q) X = Y$ with a stable linear solver rather than computing $(I - Q)^{-1}$ directly. Many ML frameworks let you solve triangular / general linear systems stably and with better numerical behavior.

• Use numerically stable linear algebra routines and optionally higher precision for the inversion/solve (mixed precision: keep the inverse/solve in fp32 even if gradients are fp16, or use fp64 if necessary for debugging).

• Add a tiny damping/regularizer: if $(I - Q)$ approaches singularity you can compute $(I - Q + \lambda I)^{-1}$ with a tiny $\lambda$ (e.g., $10^{-6}$) to stabilize; this is a pragmatic trick but slightly alters the exact Cayley map.

• Project/update $Q$ via projected gradient descent: after each gradient step, project $Q$ back to the feasible set (e.g., clip spectral norm or scale Frobenius norm). This is exactly what the paper suggests for enforcing the $\|Q\| \le \epsilon'$ constraint.

• Keep learning rates for $Q$ modest and consider adaptive optimizers; sudden large updates can push $Q$ near the boundary and make $(I - Q)$ ill-conditioned.

• Monitor condition number: in training logs, monitor $\operatorname{cond}(I - Q)$ (or an estimate via power iterations). If it grows, reduce learning rate or tighten projection threshold.

5) If you need full $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ coverage or reflections
If you need to represent rotations that have eigenvalue $-1$ (or reflections with $\det = -1$), Cayley is insufficient. Two common alternatives are:

• Matrix exponential: $R = \exp(A)$ with $A$ skew-symmetric parametrizes all of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (matrix exponential covers whole connected component of identity; for $\det = -1$ you need a separate reflection factor). Computing $\operatorname{expm}$ is more expensive but numerically robust.

• Other reparameterizations (e.g., optimization on Stiefel/orthonormal manifolds, or Givens rotations product) can cover the sets you need at different costs.

Summary recommendation
Treat the Cayley map as a practical, efficient parameterization of rotations near identity that preserves $\det = +1$ but excludes eigenvalue $-1$; enforce a spectral-norm bound on $Q$ (or an appropriate Frobenius proxy with conservative constants); initialize $Q = 0$; use block-diagonal $Q$ and small updates; prefer solving $(I - Q)x = b$ rather than forming the inverse; and use simple spectral-norm clipping (via power iteration or small SVDs) or scaling to keep $(I - Q)$ well conditioned. These steps will avoid the common numerical instabilities the paper warns about while staying faithful to the theoretical use of the Neumann series.

### How should I interpret the block-diagonal $R$ for convolution layers?
Question:
For fully connected layers with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, left-multiplication by $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ transforms the input feature basis. For convolution weights, say $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times k \times k}$, what is the precise mapping for $R$ (shape, axes) when using a block-diagonal structure with $r$ blocks? If $r = C_{\text{in}}$, you mention an interpretation akin to depth-wise transformations. Could you detail the exact tensor contractions or reshaping steps so it is clear which dimension plays the role of $d$ and which plays $n$ when bringing conv kernels into the $d \times n$ view?
Answer:
Short answer: treat each convolution kernel (all weights that connect all input channels to one output channel) as a column (a neuron) in the paper's $d\times n$ view. Concretely, set
$d = C_{\text{in}}\cdot k\cdot k$ and $n = C_{\text{out}}$. Reshape the conv tensor to a matrix of shape $(d,n)$, apply the block-diagonal orthogonal $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ on the left, then reshape back. Written elementwise,
$$
W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}\times C_{\text{in}}\times k\times k}
\longrightarrow W^0_{\text{mat}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \quad\text{with } d=C_{\text{in}}k^2,\; n=C_{\text{out}},
$$
where
$$
W^0_{\text{mat}}[(c_{\text{in}},u,v),\,c_{\text{out}}] \;=\; W^0[c_{\text{out}},c_{\text{in}},u,v].
$$
Then
$$
W_{\text{mat}} = R\,W^0_{\text{mat}},\qquad W[c_{\text{out}},c_{\text{in}},u,v] = W_{\text{mat}}[(c_{\text{in}},u,v),c_{\text{out}}].
$$
Elementwise contraction (same thing) is
$$
W[c_{\text{out}},c_{\text{in}},u,v] \;=\; \sum_{c'_{\text{in}},u',v'} R_{(c_{\text{in}},u,v),(c'_{\text{in}},u',v')} \; W^0[c_{\text{out}},c'_{\text{in}},u',v'].
$$

Interpretation of block-diagonal $R$: suppose $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ with $r$ blocks and each block of size $d/r$.

- General: each block mixes a disjoint subset of the $d$ input-feature coordinates (these coordinates are the flattened (input-channel, spatial) indices). Left-multiplying by $R$ means you change the input-feature basis used by every output-channel kernel in the same way (layer-shared), but only mixing coordinates inside each block.

- The special case $r=C_{\text{in}}$ (as mentioned in the paper): then block size $d/r = k^2$. Index the $d$ coordinates as $(c_{\text{in}},s)$ with $s\in\{1,\dots,k^2\}$ the spatial position inside a kernel. With $r=C_{\text{in}}$ the block-diagonal structure reduces to
$$
R_{(c_{\text{in}},s),(c'_{\text{in}},s')} = 0 \text{ for } c_{\text{in}}\ne c'_{\text{in}},
$$
and each block $R_{c_{\text{in}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times k^2}$ acts only on the $k\times k$ spatial entries of a single input channel. Thus for each input channel you learn an orthogonal transform of its spatial kernel footprint; this transform is applied identically to all output channels. That is precisely analogous to a depthwise-style per-input-channel spatial transform of the convolution kernels (not a depthwise convolution at inference time, but a depthwise mixing of spatial kernel coefficients during finetuning).

- If instead you set $r=1$, $R$ is a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix that mixes both across input channels and spatial positions. If you share blocks (paper mentions optional sharing), that means you reuse the same orthogonal block for multiple channel groups, which reduces parameters and enforces more symmetry.

Intuition and how to implement in code: reshape $W^0$ to $(C_{\text{out}}, d)$ or $(d, C_{\text{out}})$ depending on row/column convention, apply $R$ on the $d$ axis, then reshape back to $(C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k, k)$. In PyTorch-like pseudo-ops:
$$
W0\_mat = W^0.view(C\_out, C\_in\cdot k\cdot k).transpose(0,1)   \# \to (d, n)
$$
$$
W\_mat  = R @ W0\_mat                               \# R: (d,d)
$$
$$
W\_new  = W\_mat.transpose(0,1).view(C\_out, C\_in, k, k)
$$
When $r=C_{\text{in}}$, you can instead build $R$ as $\operatorname{block\_diag}([R\_ch \text{ for ch in range}(C_{\text{in}})])$ where each $R\_ch$ is $(k^2,k^2)$. The forward formula reduces to, for each output channel $c_{\text{out}}$ and input channel $c_{\text{in}}$:
$$
\text{kernel\_new}[c_{\text{out}},c_{\text{in}},:,:] = \operatorname{reshape}\big( R_{c_{\text{in}}} @ \operatorname{vec}(\text{kernel0}[c_{\text{out}},c_{\text{in}},:,:]) , (k,k) \big).
$$

This matches the paper's statement that $r=C_{\text{in}}$ corresponds to "each block only transforms a unique neuron channel, similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels," because each input channel's spatial kernel is transformed independently (but shared across output channels).

### When is OFT actually more parameter-efficient than LoRA?
Question:
The paper compares LoRA parameters $r'(d+n)$ versus OFT block-diagonal parameters $\tfrac{d}{2}(d/r - 1)$. Depending on $d,n,r,r'$, LoRA can be smaller or larger. In typical SD v1.5 attention layers (with head dimension $d_h$ and number of heads $h$), could you provide concrete parameter counts for common settings (e.g., $d_h \in \{64, 80\}$, $h \in \{8, 12\}$) to show when OFT is smaller? Also, the comparison assumes $r$ and $r'$ scale with $d$ (e.g., $r=r'=\alpha d$). Is that assumption necessary? How should one choose $r$ in practice to balance efficiency and performance?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT can be more parameter‑efficient than LoRA, but it depends on the model dimension $d$ and on the chosen LoRA rank $r'$ and OFT block count $r$. For a single $d\times d$ attention projection matrix the two parameter counts are

- LoRA: $2 r' d$ (because LoRA adds two low‑rank factors of size $d\times r'$ and $r'\times d$)
- OFT (r block‑diagonal orthogonal): $\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)=\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$

So for fixed $d$ you can solve for the combinations where OFT $<$ LoRA. Below I give concrete numbers for Stable Diffusion v1.5‑style attention layers with $d = h \cdot d_h$ for the common choices you asked, and then give practical guidance on choosing $r$ (and on the assumption $r=r'$ used in the paper).

Concrete counts (per $d\times d$ projection matrix)
I compute OFT params = $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$ and LoRA params = $2 r' d$. I show several representative $r$ and $r'$ values used in practice.

Case A — $d_h = 64$, $h = 8$ → $d = 512$
- LoRA $r' = 4$: $2\cdot 4\cdot 512 = 4096$
- LoRA $r' = 8$: $2\cdot 8\cdot 512 = 8192$
- LoRA $r' = 16$: $2\cdot 16\cdot 512 = 16384$

- OFT $r = 8$: $512\left(\tfrac{512}{8} - 1\right)/2 = 512(64 - 1)/2 = 16,128$
- OFT $r = 16$: $512\left(\tfrac{512}{16} - 1\right)/2 = 512(32 - 1)/2 = 7,936$
- OFT $r = 32$: $512\left(\tfrac{512}{32} - 1\right)/2 = 512(16 - 1)/2 = 3,840$

Comparison: for $d=512$, OFT with $r=32$ (3,840) is smaller than LoRA $r'=8$ (8,192) and $r'=4$ (4,096). OFT with $r=16$ (7,936) is slightly smaller than LoRA $r'=8$ (8,192) and larger than LoRA $r'=4$. OFT with $r=8$ is larger than any of the example LoRA ranks.

Case B — $d_h = 64$, $h = 12$ → $d = 768$
- LoRA $r' = 4$: $2\cdot 4\cdot 768 = 6,144$
- LoRA $r' = 8$: $2\cdot 8\cdot 768 = 12,288$
- LoRA $r' = 16$: $2\cdot 16\cdot 768 = 24,576$

- OFT $r = 8$: $768\left(\tfrac{768}{8} - 1\right)/2 = 768(96 - 1)/2 = 36,480$
- OFT $r = 16$: $768\left(\tfrac{768}{16} - 1\right)/2 = 768(48 - 1)/2 = 18,048$
- OFT $r = 32$: $768\left(\tfrac{768}{32} - 1\right)/2 = 768(24 - 1)/2 = 8,832$

Comparison: for $d=768$, OFT becomes smaller than LoRA $r'=8$ only when $r$ is large enough ($r=32$ yields $8,832 < 12,288$); $r=16$ remains larger than LoRA $r'=8$.

Case C — $d_h = 80$, $h = 8$ → $d = 640$
- LoRA $r' = 4$: $2\cdot 4\cdot 640 = 5,120$
- LoRA $r' = 8$: $2\cdot 8\cdot 640 = 10,240$
- LoRA $r' = 16$: $2\cdot 16\cdot 640 = 20,480$

- OFT $r = 8$: $640\left(\tfrac{640}{8} - 1\right)/2 = 640(80 - 1)/2 = 25,280$
- OFT $r = 16$: $640\left(\tfrac{640}{16} - 1\right)/2 = 640(40 - 1)/2 = 12,480$
- OFT $r = 32$: $640\left(\tfrac{640}{32} - 1\right)/2 = 640(20 - 1)/2 = 6,080$

Comparison: for $d=640$, OFT $r=32$ (6,080) $<$ LoRA $r'=8$ (10,240) and $<$ LoRA $r'=4$ (5,120) only slightly larger than $r'=4$. OFT $r=16$ (12,480) is larger than LoRA $r'=8$.

(You can plug any other $d$ into the two formulas; these examples illustrate the pattern.)

Algebraic threshold
You can derive the threshold on $r$ given a target LoRA rank $r'$ by comparing counts:

$\displaystyle \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2} < 2 r' d$

Cancel $d>0$:

$\displaystyle \frac{d/r-1}{2} < 2 r' \quad\rightarrow\quad d/r - 1 < 4 r' \quad\rightarrow\quad r > \frac{d}{4 r' + 1}.$

So for a desired $r'$ you need $r$ strictly larger than $\frac{d}{4 r' + 1}$ for OFT to use fewer parameters than LoRA on a single $d\times d$ matrix. For example with $d=512$ and $r'=8$, the threshold is $r > \frac{512}{33} \approx 15.5$ so $r\ge 16$ suffices (which matches the numbers above).

Is the paper’s assumption $r = r' = \alpha \cdot d$ necessary?
No — that simplification in the paper is just to get an asymptotic comparison: if both $r$ and $r'$ scale linearly with $d$, LoRA’s parameter cost grows like $O(d^2)$ while OFT with a fixed block structure can be made $O(d^2/r)$ (and in some sharing schemes $O(d^2/r^2)$). But you do not have to set $r$ proportional to $d$ in practice. $r$ (number of blocks) and $r'$ (LoRA rank) are independent hyperparameters and you should choose them to meet your parameter/compute budget and performance needs.

Practical guidance for choosing $r$ (OFT) vs $r'$ (LoRA)
- Think in terms of block size = $d/r$. Smaller block size (larger $r$) reduces OFT parameters but also reduces the expressivity of each orthogonal block. Very small blocks (block size like $2$–$8$) are very restrictive; moderate block sizes (say $16$–$64$) are a good engineering compromise. For the SD examples above, block sizes $16$–$32$ gave competitive parameter counts and were used in the paper’s illustrations (the paper reports an example with $r=8$ and $d=128$).
- Use the algebraic threshold $r > \frac{d}{4 r' + 1}$ to pick $r$ so OFT fits your budget relative to a chosen LoRA rank $r'$. For instance, if you plan LoRA $r'=8$ and $d=512$, pick $r\ge 16$ to make OFT parameter‑wise smaller on each $d\times d$ matrix.
- Empirical tradeoff: OFT enforces orthogonality and preserves hyperspherical structure, which can improve stability and convergence (as the paper shows). If you care more about very small parameter counts and you can accept potentially reduced stability, LoRA with very small $r'$ (e.g., $4$) can be smaller than many OFT settings for the same $d$. If you want better stability and are willing to allocate a few extra parameters, choose an OFT $r$ that yields comparable or slightly larger parameter count than your LoRA baseline and validate performance.
- Block sharing: the paper notes you can share blocks across channels to reduce OFT parameters further (parameter complexity becomes $O(d^2/r^2)$ in extreme sharing), at cost of reduced flexibility. That is another knob when trying to beat LoRA in parameter efficiency.
- Re‑scaled OFT / COFT: these variants add very few parameters (diagonal scaling, small projection on Q) and often improve performance with negligible parameter cost.

Bottom line
OFT can be more parameter‑efficient than LoRA for typical SD attention sizes if you choose enough OFT blocks (larger $r$, i.e., smaller block size). Use the simple inequality $r > \frac{d}{4 r' + 1}$ to pick $r$ so OFT’s parameter budget is below a given LoRA rank $r'$. In practice, try OFT block sizes corresponding to block size $16$–$64$ (so $r = d/16 \dots d/64$) as a starting point, and tune $r$ vs $r'$ empirically for the best tradeoff between parameter cost, stability, and downstream quality.

### Rank and update structure: how does $(R - I)W^0$ compare to LoRA’s low-rank update?
Question:
The paper rewrites $RW^0 = W^0 + (R - I)W^0$ and notes an analogy to LoRA’s additive low-rank update. Since $\operatorname{rank}((R - I)W^0) \le \min\{\operatorname{rank}(R - I), \operatorname{rank}(W^0)\}$ and typically $\operatorname{rank}(W^0)$ is full, the rank is bounded by $\operatorname{rank}(R - I)$. For a block-diagonal $R$ with $r$ blocks, can we bound $\operatorname{rank}(R - I)$ in terms of $r$ and $d$? Under what settings does OFT’s effective update behave like a genuinely low-rank update versus a full-rank but structured update?
Answer:
Short answer: For a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ with each block $R_i$ of size $d/r$, the rank of the multiplicative update $(R - I)$ is bounded by the sum of the ranks of the blocks:
$$
\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le\sum_{i=1}^r\operatorname{rank}(R_i-I)\le r\cdot\frac{d}{r}=d.
$$
A sharper identity is
$$
\operatorname{rank}(R_i-I)=\frac{d}{r}-\dim\ker(R_i-I)=\frac{d}{r}-\#\{\text{eigenvalues equal to }1\text{ of }R_i\},
$$
so
$$
\operatorname{rank}(R-I)=\sum_{i=1}^r\Big(\frac{d}{r}-\#\{\lambda=1\text{ in }R_i\}\Big).
$$

Intuition and consequences. The paper points out the analogy (and difference) between OFT’s multiplicative update $W = R W^0 = W^0 + (R-I)W^0$ and LoRA’s additive low-rank update. The rank of the additive term is controlled by $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)$ because $\operatorname{rank}((R-I)W^0)\le\min\{\operatorname{rank}(R-I),\operatorname{rank}(W^0)\}$ and typically $\operatorname{rank}(W^0)=d$. Thus the effective rank of the update is upper-bounded by $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)$.

When OFT behaves like a genuinely low-rank updater. There are two natural scenarios where $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)$ becomes small (so OFT’s additive term is effectively low-rank):

1. Few blocks change: if only $s$ blocks deviate from identity and the other $r-s$ blocks are exactly identity, then $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le s\cdot(d/r)$. In the extreme $s\ll r$ this is a small-rank update. This matches the idea that changing only a small subset of block subspaces produces a low-dimensional modification of the whole layer.

2. Low-dimensional rotations inside blocks: if each block $R_i$ differs from $I$ only by a rotation acting on a low-dimensional subspace (for example a Givens or plane rotation, or an orthogonal that is identity except on a $k$-dimensional subspace), then $\operatorname{rank}(R_i-I)\le k$ and so $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le r\cdot k$. Thus if the learned orthogonal transforms are constrained to act in a few low-dimensional directions per block, the overall update is low-rank.

When OFT behaves like a full-rank but structured updater. If most blocks are generic orthogonal matrices without eigenvalue $1$ multiplicity, then each $\operatorname{rank}(R_i-I)$ can be as large as $d/r$ and $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)$ can reach up to $d$. In that case (the typical unconstrained block-diagonal parameterization with many degrees of freedom) the additive term $(R-I)W^0$ is full-rank, but it is not arbitrary: it is structured (block-diagonal difference, coming from orthogonality constraints). Practically this means OFT’s update is not low-rank in the LoRA sense, yet it remains parameter-efficient and regularized by orthogonality and block structure, which the paper argues preserves hyperspherical geometry and gives stability.

Connections to other design choices in the paper. The block count $r$ trades parameter cost vs flexibility: increasing $r$ (more, smaller blocks) reduces the number of parameters to learn per block (paper shows parameter complexity scales like $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$) but does not automatically make the additive update low-rank unless you also force many blocks to be identity or restrict per-block transformations to low-dimensional subspaces. COFT’s small-$\epsilon$ constraint ($\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$) constrains the operator norm of $R-I$, making the update small in magnitude (so smaller perturbation of $W^0$), but small operator norm is not the same as low matrix rank — COFT makes the change “small and structured” rather than explicitly low-rank. Block-sharing (setting some $R_i=R_j$) reduces the number of distinct transforms and thus reduces the maximum attainable rank of $R-I$ (if there are only $m$ distinct blocks then rank $\le m\cdot (d/r)$).

Bottom line: OFT can implement a low-rank style update in special settings (few non-identity blocks, per-block low-dimensional rotations, or block-sharing) but in the generic block-diagonal parameterization it is a full-rank yet highly structured update. That structural regularity (orthogonality + block sparsity) is exactly what the paper exploits: it preserves hyperspherical relationships while giving a parameter-efficient, stable alternative to LoRA’s explicit low-rank additive parameterization.

### COFT’s $\epsilon$ constraint: what norm is used and how should $\epsilon$ be set?
Question:
COFT constrains $\lVert R - I \rVert \le \epsilon$ and then replaces this with $\lVert Q \rVert \le \epsilon'$ using the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$. In practice: (1) Which norm is used for $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ (spectral, Frobenius), (2) how do you select $\epsilon$ or $\epsilon'$ across different layers with different $d$, and (3) how sensitive are results to this choice? Any recommended schedules to anneal or tune $\epsilon$ during training for stability?
Answer:
Short answer: the constraint in COFT is meant in the operator (spectral) norm sense for the mathematical arguments (Neumann series / convergence of Cayley transform), but in practice you can enforce a simpler norm (Frobenius) for efficiency — you must account for how the choice of norm scales with layer width when picking the tolerance. $\epsilon$ controls a trade-off: very small values force the finetuned layer to stay near the pretrained weights (stable but under‑flexible); large values recover OFT (flexible but can destabilize). The paper enforces the constraint by projecting the Cayley skew matrix $Q$ to a ball (they write the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ and move the constraint onto $Q$), and reports qualitatively that COFT is substantially more stable and insensitive to iteration count for reasonable epsilons, but does not give a single universal epsilon — you should tune it per task/layer, with a simple recipe below.

Explanation and practical guidance grounded in the paper

Which norm is intended?
The theoretical step in the paper that justifies replacing the constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ by a constraint on $Q$ uses the Neumann series and explicitly mentions convergence “in the operator norm.” That indicates the intended mathematical norm is the operator (spectral) norm: $\|\cdot\|_2$. The Cayley expansion $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ and the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$ are controlled by the operator norm of $Q$ for the Neumann-series argument to hold cleanly.

That said, from an implementation standpoint the authors convert the constraint into one on $Q$ and enforce it by projected gradient descent. Projecting $Q$ by spectral-norm clipping requires either an SVD or a power‑iteration based spectral norm projection, which is somewhat expensive. Projecting by Frobenius norm is much cheaper (just scale the matrix), so many practical implementations use Frobenius-norm projection as an approximation. The paper does not mandate a hard implementation choice, but the math motivation is spectral/operator norm.

How to choose $\epsilon$ or $\epsilon'$ across layers with different dimension $d$?
Two points matter: (a) whether you interpret the bound in spectral norm or Frobenius norm, and (b) layer sensitivity (some layers affect outputs much more).

If you enforce the spectral norm (the paper’s mathematical choice), $\epsilon$ has an absolute meaning independent of $d$: it bounds the largest singular value of $R - I$ (or of $Q$). In that case you can use the same $\epsilon$ across layers and it has comparable geometric meaning: how much rotation/reflection you permit in the worst direction. If you instead use Frobenius norm for projection, that norm grows with matrix dimension, so you should scale the Frobenius tolerance with $d$ if you want comparable per‑component flexibility (e.g. set $\epsilon_F \approx \epsilon_{\text{spec}} \cdot \sqrt{d}$ if the singular values are roughly isotropic).

Practical recipe compatible with the paper’s approach:
- Work with $Q$ (Cayley parameter). Project $Q$ after each gradient step.
- If you can afford it, project by spectral norm (power-iteration to get top singular vector and clip singular value): this enforces the paper’s theory directly and lets you use a single $\epsilon'$ across layers. Reasonable starting values for $\epsilon'$ (spectral) are small (e.g., $0.05$–$0.2$) because the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ assumes $Q$ small; the paper’s experiments show COFT with a “proper $\epsilon$” gives very stable training (they plot qualitative effects in Figure 5 but do not quote a single global number).
- If you must use Frobenius projection for speed, scale the tolerance with layer dimension. For a target spectral-equivalent $\epsilon_{\text{spec}}$, use $\epsilon_F \approx \epsilon_{\text{spec}} \cdot \sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}$, where $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective dimension of the block you parametrize (the paper uses block-diagonal $R$, so use block size rather than full $d$).

Layer-wise choices: start with a single (spectral) $\epsilon'$ for all OFT blocks. If some layers are especially sensitive (e.g., early embedding or very small $d$ blocks), reduce $\epsilon'$ for those layers. In the paper they apply OFT/COFT to the same locations as LoRA (attention projections) — using a uniform $\epsilon'$ there is reasonable.

How sensitive are results, and annealing/tuning recommendations?
The paper’s qualitative and quantitative results make two empirical points: COFT increases stability compared to unconstrained OFT and standard finetuning; and $\epsilon$ controls a smooth trade-off between staying near pretrained weights (small $\epsilon$) and recovering OFT flexibility (large $\epsilon$). From their Figure 5: small $\epsilon$ makes the finetuned model behave very similarly to pretrained (conservative), while large $\epsilon$ approaches OFT (more change). They report COFT “yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.”

Empirically, results are not extremely brittle within a reasonable range: if $\epsilon$ is chosen moderately small the method is stable and effective, and increasing $\epsilon$ gradually increases flexibility until you reach a point where finetuning can destabilize. There is no single fatal cliff reported in the paper, but extremes ($\epsilon \gg 1$ or no constraint) can break stability.

Practical tuning/annealing suggestions consistent with the paper
1. Start conservative: initialize $Q = 0$ (so $R = I$) and pick a small spectral $\epsilon'$ (e.g., $0.05$). Train and measure validation control/fidelity. The paper explicitly uses identity initialization for $Q$.
2. If the model underfits (cannot achieve desired control after reasonable epochs), increase $\epsilon'$ slowly (e.g., $\times 1.5$–$2$) and re-run. The paper notes larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like OFT and be more flexible.
3. If you see instability or collapse, reduce $\epsilon'$ or tighten projection. COFT is intended to make learning-rate and iteration count easier to set; if you still need hyperparameter safety, reduce learning rate before increasing $\epsilon$.
4. Optional annealing: you can keep $\epsilon'$ small early (preserve pretrained behaviour) and increase it after a few epochs if greater adaptation is needed. The paper does not report an annealing schedule, but this is consistent with the intuition that you should allow more deviation once coarse alignment is learned.
5. Implementation detail: enforce the projection on the skew-symmetric $Q$ after each optimizer step. If you use block-diagonal $R$ (as in the paper), apply per-block projections (spectral or Frobenius) with tolerances per block.

Summary
- The paper’s theory uses the operator (spectral) norm; in practice people often use Frobenius projection for efficiency but must then rescale tolerances by block size.
- Use the $Q$ parameter (Cayley param) and project it to a small norm ball; small $\epsilon'$ yields conservative, stable finetuning; larger $\epsilon'$ gives flexibility and approaches OFT.
- Results are fairly robust in a moderate $\epsilon$ range; tune $\epsilon'$ (or anneal it) if you need more flexibility, and prefer spectral-norm projection for a direct match to the paper’s theoretical justification.

### Toy autoencoder experiment: what exactly are the 'magnitude-only' and 'angle-only' computations?
Question:
In Figure 1 (toy autoencoder), training uses standard inner products $z = w^\top x$. At test time you compare: (a) the same inner product, (b) "magnitude information", and (c) "angular information" (cosine). Could you formalize (b) and (c)? For example, is (c) implemented as $z = \frac{w^\top x}{\lVert w \rVert \lVert x \rVert}$ and (b) as $z = \lVert w \rVert \lVert x \rVert$ (or something else)? Also, are feature maps rescaled or shifted to the range expected by downstream layers, and how is reconstruction fair across the three variants?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — (c) is the cosine of the angle between kernel and patch, and (b) can reasonably be implemented as the product of their norms (or an L2-based magnitude proxy). Because the autoencoder was trained with inner products, to make the three test-time variants comparable you must pay attention to sign and scale: either match their activation distributions to the trained inner-product activations (e.g., by rescaling/affine correction) or accept that reconstruction quality will differ because the downstream decoder expects the original inner-product statistics.

Formalizations and intuition
The trained forward pass used the standard inner product
$z = w^\top x$.
Two natural test-time decompositions of that scalar are its magnitude part and its angular part. A clean way to write the decomposition is
$w^\top x = \|w\| \|x\| \cos\theta$,
where $\theta$ is the angle between $w$ and $x$ and $\cos\theta = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\| \|x\|}$. From this identity the three variants in the paper can be implemented as:

(a) Inner product (baseline, same as training)
$z_a = w^\top x$.

(b) Magnitude-only
The most direct magnitude-only choice (discarding angular sign information) is
$z_b = \|w\| \, \|x\|$.
This captures only the product of lengths; it removes the $\cos\theta$ factor, so different directions produce the same activation if norms match. An alternative magnitude proxy (which preserves sign information coming from projection) is to use the absolute inner product
$z_b' = |w^\top x|$,
or a signed magnitude proxy $z_b'' = \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x)\,\|w\|\,\|x\|$ (but that last is less standard). The paper’s description (“magnitude information”) and their phase/magnitude analogy suggest they used something like $\|w\|\,\|x\|$ (i.e., magnitude only), which explains why the magnitude-only map contains little semantic content in the figure: angle encodes the structure, magnitude does not.

(c) Angular-only (cosine)
The natural angular-only implementation is the cosine similarity
$z_c = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|\,\|x\|} = \cos\theta$.
This discards the overall scaling and keeps only direction alignment. The paper explicitly mentions they do not apply this cosine during training (training was with raw inner products) and only test with cosine; their reported result is that the cosine map nearly reconstructs the input, consistent with the phase/angle preserving semantics.

Practical issues: sign, zero norms, and numeric stability
If either $\|w\|$ or $\|x\|$ can be (near) zero, the cosine is unstable; in practice one adds a small $\epsilon$ to denominators:
$z_c = \frac{w^\top x}{\max(\|w\|\,\|x\|, \epsilon)}$.
If you choose magnitude-only using $\|w\|\,\|x\|$, that is always nonnegative and loses the sign of $w^\top x$ (which also diminishes representational power).

Making reconstructions comparable (rescaling / downstream expectations)
Because the decoder/next layers were trained on $z$ drawn from the inner product distribution, simply replacing $z$ with $\cos\theta$ or $\|w\|\,\|x\|$ will change the marginal distributions (mean, variance, dynamic range) and can harm reconstruction irrespective of the information content. To fairly compare reconstructions across the three variants you should make their activation statistics compatible with the decoder. Standard, simple ways to do this are:

1) Mean–variance matching: compute per-channel (or global) mean and standard deviation of $z_a = w^\top x$ on a held-out set, then at test time affine-rescale $z_b$ or $z_c$ to match those mean/variance values:
$z_{\text{test\_scaled}} = a \cdot z_{\text{test}} + b$,
where $a$ and $b$ are chosen to match mean and std to the original inner-product activations.

2) Per-channel affine calibration: learn a small per-feature affine transform (scale and bias) that maps the new $z$ variant to the distribution expected by the decoder. This is low-cost and can be done with a few calibration examples without changing the decoder weights.

3) Histogram / quantile matching: map empirical CDFs of $z_b$ or $z_c$ to that of $z_a$ (more complex, usually unnecessary).

In the paper the authors say the training used inner products and that at test they "compare" (a),(b),(c) — they also explicitly note they did not apply cosine during training. They do not provide the low-level implementation details in the main text for exactly how (b) and (c) were computed or whether they performed any rescaling before feeding into the decoder. Given the phase/magnitude analogy they discuss, the simplest faithful implementations consistent with the paper are $z_c = (w^\top x)/(\|w\|\,\|x\|)$ and $z_b = \|w\|\,\|x\|$ (with epsilon for stability). To make reconstructions “fair,” one should at least match mean/variance of activations across variants or apply per-channel affine calibration; otherwise differences in reconstruction can conflate information loss with mismatched activation scale.

Bottom line
Formally use cosine for angle-only, product of norms (or absolute inner product) for magnitude-only. Because the decoder was trained on raw inner products, rescaling (mean/variance matching or a learned affine per-channel calibration) is important to ensure differences in reconstruction reflect information content (angle vs magnitude) rather than trivial scale mismatches. The paper’s qualitative claim — that angular information alone preserves semantics while magnitude does not — is exactly what the cosine vs norm-product decomposition predicts.

### Why does 'OFT without orthogonality' fail so catastrophically in your ablation?
Question:
Figure 3 suggests that relaxing the orthogonality constraint (i.e., learning a general linear transform instead of an orthogonal one) leads to failure. Is the failure due to optimization issues (e.g., ill-conditioned matrices leading to exploding/vanishing signals) or due to semantic drift from not preserving angles? Did you try additional regularizations such as spectral norm constraints, weight decay on the transform, or small-step constraints that might stabilize a general linear transform without enforcing exact orthogonality?
Answer:
Short answer: it fails because removing orthogonality removes the single thing that (1) exactly preserves the pairwise neuron angles/hyperspherical energy and (2) strongly regularizes the transform so finetuning stays on a benign manifold. Without that constraint the transform has far more degrees of freedom and easily (a) breaks the hyperspherical geometry that encodes pretrained semantics and (b) creates ill‑conditioned, large‑magnitude updates that destabilize optimization — so you get semantic collapse rather than useful adaptation.

Why orthogonality matters (intuitively and technically)
The paper’s central hypothesis is that the directions (angles) between neurons encode much of the pretrained model’s semantic knowledge; hyperspherical energy is used to quantify the pairwise angular structure. An orthogonal matrix $R$ applied to all neurons in a layer is the exact class of transforms that preserves all pairwise angles (and thus the hyperspherical energy). Technically, if $W = R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal, then the normalized neuron vectors satisfy $\hat w_i = R \hat w_i^0$ and cosine similarities (angles) are preserved. That gives two practical benefits: first, finetuning modifies directions in a globally coherent way (so you can change semantics without destroying pretrained relationships); second, optimization is restricted to the (compact) orthogonal manifold, which is smoother and better conditioned than unconstrained updates of the full $d\times d$ matrix.

What goes wrong when you drop orthogonality
When you learn a general linear transform instead of an orthogonal one you introduce many failure modes that the paper’s experiments show are catastrophic in practice. A few concrete mechanisms are:

- Semantic drift: arbitrary linear transforms change relative angles between neurons, thus changing the hyperspherical energy and the geometry that encoded pretrained semantics. The model no longer preserves the inductive structure learned in pretraining, so generation quality and fidelity to prompts/subjects collapse.

- Ill conditioning and magnitude blow‑up: unconstrained updates can change magnitudes and introduce near‑singular directions. That leads to exploding/vanishing activations through subsequent layers and makes denoising steps in diffusion unstable. The paper’s remedy (COFT) explicitly controls radius and shows stability improves, which supports this mechanism.

- Too much flexibility → overfitting / unstable dynamics: a full linear map has $O(d^2)$ degrees of freedom; in a low‑data, finetuning regime that easily overfits the finetuning loss and destroys global behavior. Orthogonality (and its block diagonal parameterization) acts as a strong structural regularizer that balances flexibility and stability.

Did the authors try other regularizers (spectral norm, weight decay, small steps)?
The paper does not report experiments where they simply replace orthogonality with soft regularizers such as spectral‑norm clipping, strong weight decay, or tiny step constraints on a general linear transform. Instead they explored two principled alternatives that are reported:

- Constrained OFT (COFT): keeps orthogonality and adds an explicit small‑deviation constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ (implemented by constraining the Cayley parameter $Q$), which further stabilizes training. This shows that explicit control of deviation plus orthogonality helps.

- Re‑scaled OFT: allows a learnable diagonal magnitude matrix $D$ in addition to $R$ ($z = (R W^0 D)^\top x$). Because scaling does not change hyperspherical energy, this increases flexibility safely.

Those results suggest that the right way to stabilize a linear transform is to preserve angle structure (orthogonality) and only allow tightly controlled magnitude changes — not to relax orthogonality entirely and hope $L_2$ or spectral regularizers will suffice.

If you want to try stabilizing a general linear transform anyway
For an ablation or follow‑up experiment you could try the following (the paper’s insights point to these as reasonable attempts):

- Spectral norm projection / clipping (enforce $\|A\|_2 \le 1$): this limits amplification but does not preserve angles, so it may reduce blow‑up but still permit semantic drift.

- Projected SVD to nearest orthogonal (polar decomposition): after an unconstrained gradient step project the matrix to the orthogonal factor (this forces angles back). This essentially becomes OFT but implemented via projection.

- Strong weight decay + tiny learning rate + gradient clipping: may help optimization conditioning but is likely insufficient alone because it does not preserve hyperspherical energy.

- Impose small‑step proximal constraints on the matrix (proximal update toward identity): similar spirit to COFT but without the orthogonality invariance you still risk angle changes.

- Constrain $Q$ in Cayley form (the paper does this for COFT): parameterize $R$ via Cayley from a skew‑symmetric $Q$ and simply constrain $\|Q\|$ — this smoothly enforces near‑orthogonality and small deviation.

Recommendation
The paper’s empirical finding is clear: orthogonality (or nearly orthogonal + small deviation) is the mechanism that both preserves pretrained semantic structure and yields stable optimization. If you want a general linear transform that is stable, you should either (A) parametrize it so it is orthogonal (OFT) and optionally add radius / small‑$Q$ constraints (COFT), or (B) use polar/SVD projection back to orthogonality after unconstrained steps. Purely soft regularizers (spectral norm, weight decay) may reduce some numerical instability but are unlikely to recover the hyperspherical invariance that prevents semantic collapse.

If you want, I can sketch a straightforward experiment plan to compare: (1) unconstrained linear + spectral norm clipping, (2) unconstrained linear + weight decay + tiny lr, (3) Cayley parameterized with $Q$ projection (COFT style), and (4) unconstrained update + polar projection to orthogonal — and report the same control and fidelity metrics used in the paper to see which stabilizes best.

### Control consistency metrics: implementation specifics and potential biases
Question:
For controllable generation, you propose a control consistency evaluation: estimate the control signal from the generated image and compare to the input control. Could you specify the exact off-the-shelf models and settings used for each task? For C2I, which edge detector and thresholds compute IoU/F1? For S2I, which semantic segmentation model, label set, and resolution are used for mIoU/mean/overall accuracy? For L2F, which landmark detector and what normalization are used in the mean $\ell_2$ distance? Do these choices bias metrics toward/against certain visual styles produced by different methods?
Answer:
Short answer: the main paper does not list the concrete off‑the‑shelf detectors/thresholds in the body — it refers readers to Appendix~\ref{app:settings} for “more details”. Because that appendix is not reproduced in your question, I cannot quote exact model names or numeric thresholds from the paper itself. What I can do here is (1) point out what the paper explicitly states, (2) describe the kinds of off‑the‑shelf models and settings that are typically used for each control task, and (3) explain precisely how those choices can bias the reported control‑consistency metrics and what to do about it. This will help you interpret the reported numbers and plan robustness checks or reproduce the experiments.

What the paper actually says
The paper states that controllable generation is evaluated by estimating the control signal from the generated image and comparing it to the input control. For the three tasks it reports the following metrics: for C2I (canny edge → image) it computes $IoU$ and $F_1$; for S2I (segmentation → image) it computes mean $IoU$, mean accuracy and overall accuracy; for L2F (landmarks → face) it computes mean $\ell_2$ distance between control landmarks and predicted landmarks. It also explicitly notes the results are “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model”, i.e. the evaluation depends on the performance of the estimators used to extract control signals from generated images. The paper promises more implementation detail in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}, but those specifics are not in the body text you provided.

Typical concrete choices (what you should expect or use when reproducing)
Because the paper builds on ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter evaluation practice, authors commonly use standard, publicly available detectors/segmenters/landmark estimators. Typical choices (and why authors choose them) are:

- C2I (edge detector → $IoU$/$F_1$): Canny edge detector (OpenCV implementation) with specific low/high thresholds (often e.g. $(100, 200)$ or tuned per dataset) is the standard way to get binary edges. To compute $IoU$/$F_1$ the binary edge map extracted from the generated image is compared to the binary input edge map.

- S2I (segmentation → mIoU / mean / overall accuracy): an off‑the‑shelf semantic segmentation network pretrained on the target dataset (e.g. ADE20K) is normally used. Common choices are DeepLabv3+ (ResNet or Xception backbones) or an HRNet/SegFormer model trained on ADE20K; the label set and mapping follow ADE20K class labels (usually $150$ classes). Images are typically resized to the segmentation model’s expected input resolution (for many models $512\times512$ or $473\times473$) before inference, and predictions are remapped to the task class set for computing mean $IoU$, mean accuracy and overall accuracy.

- L2F (landmarks → face): a standard facial landmark detector (e.g. dlib 68‑point detector or a MediaPipe / FAN / HRNet face landmark model) is used to extract predicted landmark coordinates from generated images. The mean $\ell_2$ distance is usually computed after normalizing for scale (commonly by inter‑ocular distance or inter‑pupil distance) to make distances comparable across face sizes; sometimes distance is normalized by face bounding box diagonal or by the distance between outer eye corners.

How these choices can bias metrics (and why the paper warns about it)
The evaluation is only as accurate as the estimator used to extract the control signal from generated images. This introduces several bias sources:

- Detector sensitivity and thresholds (Canny): different low/high thresholds in Canny change edge thickness and amount of detected edges. A method that produces softer/blurry edges or high‑frequency texture might get penalized relative to one producing crisp, binary strokes even if humans regard both as respecting the control. Similarly, morphological differences (thin vs thick lines) change $IoU$ and $F_1$ even though semantic alignment may be similar.

- Segmentation model architecture, training domain and resolution (S2I): segmentation networks have varying generalization to generated image styles. A segmentation model trained on real images (ADE20K) may misclassify artistic, stylized, or photorealistic outputs differently. If one generative method produces very photorealistic outputs and another produces painterly outputs, the photorealistic one may achieve higher $mIoU$ simply because the segmenter was trained on similar (real) images. Also resizing or center‑cropping during preprocessing can alter per‑class accuracies; small objects and thin structures are particularly sensitive.

- Landmark detector robustness and normalization (L2F): landmark detectors fail or give noisy outputs when faces are stylized, occluded, or rendered at low resolution. Normalization choice (inter‑ocular vs bounding box) changes the scale of errors and can advantage methods that preserve certain facial proportions. If a method changes facial shape systematically (e.g. exaggerating expression), the normalized $\ell_2$ can be large even though pose is correct.

Because the paper’s metric pipeline relies on these off‑the‑shelf models, the reported control‑consistency numbers conflate two effects: (a) the true degree to which the generated image follows the control signal; and (b) how well the estimator handles the particular visual style of the generator. The paper itself acknowledges this by saying the metric is valid “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model.”

Practical recommendations (what to report and sanity checks)
To make the evaluation fair and robust, and to understand possible biases, you should (and authors ideally should) do the following:

1. Report exact model names, versions, checkpoints and preprocessing: e.g. “DeepLabv3+ (ResNet101) pretrained on ADE20K checkpoint X, input resolution $512\times512$, softmax argmax for labels”; “OpenCV Canny with thresholds $(T_1,T_2) = (100, 200)$ and $\text{apertureSize}=3$”; “dlib 68‑point face landmark detector v19.22, normalization by inter‑ocular distance”. This removes ambiguity.

2. Run estimator sanity checks on the real dataset: measure the estimator’s accuracy on ground‑truth real images (e.g. segment real ADE20K validation images with the chosen segmenter and compute $mIoU$ vs ground truth). This gives a ceiling for the metric and shows how much noise to expect.

3. Compute oracle / ablation checks: apply the estimator to images synthesized by a generator that is known to perfectly reproduce controls (or to images with small synthetic perturbations) to see how estimator noise maps to metric error.

4. Use multiple estimators or thresholds: for Canny, show metrics under several plausible threshold pairs; for segmentation, try two different architectures/backbones; for landmarking, report both normalized and absolute $\ell_2$. If results are consistent, you gain confidence they are not an artifact of a single estimator.

5. Complement with human evaluation or perceptual metrics: because automatic estimators are biased by style, include human judgments or style‑invariant checks (e.g. pose estimation from a model trained on mixed data) to validate automatic metrics.

Conclusion
The paper intentionally builds the control‑consistency metric on standard off‑the‑shelf estimators and cautions that the reported numbers depend on their accuracy. The exact model names/thresholds should be in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}; if you are reproducing or extending the work, insist on (and report) the precise estimator checkpoints, input resolution and normalization used. Be aware that edge thresholds, segmentation training domain, and landmark normalization can advantage or penalize particular generator styles; run estimator sanity checks and multiple‑estimator robustness experiments to ensure your conclusions reflect true controllability rather than estimator idiosyncrasies.

### Compositionality of multiple OFT adapters: how would $R$'s be combined across tasks?
Question:
The paper mentions a potential for compositionality: orthogonal matrices from multiple OFT finetunings can be multiplied and remain orthogonal. Concretely, if I have two downstream tasks yielding $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ for the same layer, is the composed adapter $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ expected to preserve both task adaptations when multiplied into $W^0$? In practice, are $R$'s trained on disjoint tasks likely to commute, or can order matter substantially? Any initial evidence (positive or negative) for catastrophic interference when composing multiple $R$'s?
Answer:
Short answer: mathematically, yes — the product $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ is still orthogonal, so it yields a valid OFT-style adapter. Practically, however, there is no guarantee that $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ will “preserve” the behavior of both single-task finetunings the way you want. Orthogonal matrices do not generally commute, and the paper explicitly calls this compositionality question an open problem and does not provide empirical results on composing independently learned $R$'s. Below I give the intuition, a simple algebraic view, likely failure modes (including when order matters), and a few practical experiments / mitigations you can try.

Intuition and algebraic view
Each OFT finetuning for a task $T$ learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R^{(T)}$ and the finetuned weight is $W^{(T)} = R^{(T)} W^0$. If you multiply two adapters and use $W = (R^{(B)}R^{(A)}) W^0$, you are applying first $R^{(A)}$ then $R^{(B)}$ in the coordinate frame of the neurons. That product is orthogonal because the orthogonal group is closed under multiplication. But whether $W$ behaves like a model that “does task A and task B” depends on how those tasks were encoded into the two rotations.

Roughly there are three regimes:

1) Disjoint / commuting transformations. If $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ act on independent subspaces (for example, block-diagonal with nonoverlapping blocks, or both live in a mutually commuting subgroup of $O(d)$), then they commute and composition is predictable: $R^{(B)}R^{(A)} = R^{(A)}R^{(B)}$, and each rotation preserves the directions the other did not touch. In that case you are much more likely to retain both adaptations after composing.

2) Small, near-identity transforms. If both $R$'s are small perturbations around identity (COFT with small $\epsilon$), composition will be close to the sum of their infinitesimal generators and may approximately preserve each adaptation — interference is limited because each change is locally small. Neumann-series intuition in the paper supports this: small $Q$ (Cayley parameter) implies $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$, so compositions add approximately.

3) Large, overlapping transforms. If the two tasks require substantially different rotations of the same neuron directions, the product will generally not reproduce either single-task behavior. Orthogonal matrices typically do not commute, so order matters: applying $R^{(A)}$ then $R^{(B)}$ is different from the reverse. Composing two independently learned $R$'s can therefore produce interference or even catastrophic degradation of one or both task behaviors.

Why order can matter (noncommutativity)
Two rotations in high-dimensional space are just like 3D rotations: sequence matters unless they are rotations about the same axis/subspace. When $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ do not commute, $R^{(B)}R^{(A)} W^0$ will generally not equal $R^{(A)}W^0$ (task A result) nor $R^{(B)}W^0$ (task B result), and performance on either task can change depending on the order. The paper explicitly highlights this as an open problem: orthogonality gives closure (products remain orthogonal) but does not guarantee multi-task preservation.

Any evidence in the paper?
The paper raises compositionality as an intriguing open problem but does not present empirical tests of composing independently trained $R$'s. All reported results are for single-task OFT/COFT finetunings; there is no experimental evidence (positive or negative) in the paper about catastrophic interference when multiplying adapters learned on different downstream tasks.

Practical diagnostics and experiments you can run
To assess compositionality empirically for your models, try the following simple checks:
- Measure each task’s performance after composition: train $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ separately from $W^0$, then evaluate task A on $W^{comp}=R^{(B)}R^{(A)}W^0$ and compare to baseline $W^{(A)}$. Do the symmetric check for task B.
- Measure the commutator norm: compute $C = R^{(A)}R^{(B)} - R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ and report $\|C\|$ (operator or Frobenius norm). Small $\|C\|$ suggests near-commutativity and hence higher chance of successful composition.
- Probe projection overlap: check whether the two $R$'s rotate mostly within disjoint blocks (if you used block-diagonal parameterization). If they largely act on the same blocks/subspaces, expect more interference.
- Try sequential fine-tuning: after learning $R^{(A)}$, initialize $R$ for task B at identity and fine-tune with $R^{(A)}$ frozen, or allow a small joint adjustment. This can reduce mismatch versus naive multiplication.
- Ablate COFT $\epsilon$: train with different $\epsilon$ (near-identity) to see if smaller deviations improve compositionality.

Ways to improve chance of positive composition
If your goal is modular adapters that can be composed reliably, you can use inductive biases during training to encourage commutativity or disjointness. The paper suggests some of these directions implicitly (block-diagonal $R$, COFT small $\epsilon$, shared-block vs per-block choices). Concretely, restricting $R$'s to act on disjoint blocks (different blocks per task) or training $R$'s within a commuting subgroup (e.g., independent 2D rotations on non-overlapping planes) makes multiplication predictable. Alternatively, keep each $R$ small via COFT so composition is approximately additive and less destructive. Another strategy is to learn adapters with a composition objective: while training $R^{(B)}$ also regularize performance on task A (or include a reconstruction loss that preserves $W^{(A)}$'s behavior), i.e., learn for multi-task consistency rather than independent tasks.

Takeaway
The algebraic fact that orthogonal matrices multiply to an orthogonal matrix is promising, but it does not by itself solve compositional multi-task adaptation. The paper explicitly leaves whether composed $R$'s preserve multiple downstream tasks as an open problem and offers no empirical verdict. In practice you should expect order-dependence and potential interference unless you train with architectural or regularization constraints that enforce disjointness, near-identity updates, or explicit multi-task consistency.

### No inference overhead claim: any caveats when pre-multiplying $R$ into $W^0$?
Question:
The paper states there is no inference overhead because one can precompute $W = R W^0$ and discard $R$. Are there scenarios where $R$ was shared across multiple layers or blocks such that pre-multiplication changes memory layout or weight tying in unexpected ways? Also, when using block sharing across $R$ blocks, does this impact how you fold $R$ into $W^0$ if multiple layers share the same $R$ parameters (e.g., you would still materialize separate $W$ per layer, losing the sharing at inference)?
Answer:
Short answer: functionally nothing breaks — folding an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ into pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$ (computing $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$) produces an equivalent forward operator and therefore does not change inference results — but there are practical caveats around memory, weight-tying semantics and numerical/layout choices you should be explicit about. Below I explain the different scenarios, the consequences, and practical options.

If $\mathbf{R}$ is layer-shared but the pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$ differ per layer. In training you may learn a single $\mathbf{R}$ and apply it to multiple distinct $\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ (layers indexed by $\ell$), i.e. $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$. At inference you can precompute each $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}$ and discard $\mathbf{R}$. This is algebraically identical to keeping $\mathbf{R}$ in the graph: the runtime matmul $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ has been done ahead of time. The functional behavior is unchanged, but two practical consequences follow:

1) Memory: storing a single $\mathbf{R}$ (plus the $\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ if you kept them) is more compact than storing every folded $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}$ only if you still keep $\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ or can reuse $\mathbf{R}$ compactly. If you precompute and store the distinct $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}$, you lose the memory savings that sharing $\mathbf{R}$ provided during training. In other words: folding preserves inference speed but may increase stored parameter volume compared to keeping a single shared $\mathbf{R}$ and the original $\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$.

2) Weight tying semantics: many frameworks allow true parameter sharing by multiple layers pointing at the same tensor. If you relied on a single tensor object $\mathbf{R}$ being used at multiple places, replacing it by distinct precomputed $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}$ changes that sharing relationship. If you care about preserving a single shared folded matrix for multiple layers, you must either (a) fold once into a single tensor and set multiple layers to reference that same tensor, or (b) keep $\mathbf{R}$ and perform the $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ multiplication at load time (or lazily) instead of materializing separate $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}$ tensors. If you do nothing, inference is still correct, but the parameter graph no longer shares a single object.

If $\mathbf{R}$ is shared across multiple blocks (block-sharing) or you use block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r)$. The same points above apply at block granularity. When you fold a block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ into a layer weight $\mathbf{W}^0$, you multiply each block into the corresponding rows of $\mathbf{W}^0$ yielding $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$. If the same block $\mathbf{R}_i$ was shared across many layers during training, folding produces distinct $\mathbf{W}$ per layer (unless the $\mathbf{W}^0$ were identical). So block-sharing reduces train-time parameters and computation, but folding can make those shared blocks become many unique concrete weights at inference unless you explicitly reuse the same folded block memory. Again: correctness preserved, sharing/memory footprint may not be.

Practical numerical and layout considerations. Folding is a simple dense matrix multiply and is cheap to do once at deployment, but keep in mind floating-point rounding: $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ computed in FP32 then stored in FP16 may not be bit-identical to performing the same operations inside a different runtime ordering. Also if $\mathbf{R}$ was Cayley-parameterized and computed at high precision and then multiplied, numerical stability of the Cayley inverse was a training-time concern; at inference you only use the final $\mathbf{R}$ so this is minor. Finally, folding changes memory layout of weight tensors (different strides/contiguity) which can influence low-level kernel performance; most frameworks handle this but you may want to ensure tensors are in the preferred memory layout (e.g., contiguous) after folding.

Recommendations depending on your goal. If you want zero extra compute and do not care about storing extra per-layer parameters, simply precompute $\mathbf{W}_{\ell}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ for every layer and replace weights in the model. If you want to preserve the memory savings of shared $\mathbf{R}$ at inference (for example because the set of $\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ are huge and you want to store only $\mathbf{R}$ plus a small base), either keep a single shared folded tensor and make all relevant layers reference it (only possible if the folded tensor is identical for those layers), or keep $\mathbf{R}$ and compute $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0_{\ell}$ at model-load time or lazily on first use (this trades a little startup compute for lower storage). If you must maintain parameter-tied semantics (for frameworks or tooling that check shared-identity), explicitly set layer weight references to the same folded object; otherwise tying will be lost.

Summary: folding $\mathbf{R}$ into $\mathbf{W}^0$ is mathematically valid and preserves inference behavior as the paper states. The only real caveats are practical: you may lose the storage/weight-tying advantages of a single shared $\mathbf{R}$ (since folding yields distinct $\mathbf{W}$ per layer unless you explicitly reuse the same folded tensor), and you should be mindful of minor numerical/layout effects. Choose folding-at-deploy, lazy-materialization, or explicit shared-folded tensors depending on whether you prioritize minimal inference compute, minimal storage, or preserving sharing semantics.

### Why is minimizing hyperspherical energy harmful for finetuning diffusion models?
Question:
The paper contrasts OFT with prior work that minimizes hyperspherical energy in classification, arguing that minimizing energy during finetuning would "destroy the original semantic structures" in diffusion models. Could you elaborate on the intuition or provide an example? If $\mathrm{HE}(W)$ (sum of inverse distances) gets minimized, neurons become more uniformly spread on the sphere, reducing redundancy. Why does that uniformization particularly damage the pretrained generative semantics in diffusion UNets, whereas it is beneficial in classification backbones?
Answer:
Short answer: because for pretrained generative diffusion UNets the pairwise angles between neurons encode specialized, redundant and spatially-structured semantic representations that the generator depends on; forcing the neurons to become uniformly spread on the sphere (i.e. minimizing hyperspherical energy ($HE$)) breaks those specialized angular relations and so destroys the pretrained generative semantics. In contrast, classification backbones often benefit from reduced redundancy and more uniform neuron directions, which improves discrimination and generalization.

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper

The paper’s central empirical and theoretical claim is that angular relationships (directions on the unit hypersphere) between neurons capture semantic structure, and preserving those pairwise angles preserves pretrained generative ability. Two linked intuitions from the paper are useful here.

1) Angular / phase information carries semantics. The authors use the “phase spectrum” analogy: like an image’s phase spectrum preserving most of its perceptual content even if magnitudes are scrambled, the directions of neurons (their angles) carry the semantic content that a generator uses to synthesize images. If the pretrained model has neurons whose directions form a particular pattern of angles (clusters, alignments, redundancies), those angular patterns are part of how semantic concepts are represented.

2) Minimizing hyperspherical energy ($HE$) uniformizes angles; that is precisely what orthogonal-overparameterized training for classification tries to do (encourage neurons to be spread uniformly on the sphere). For classification that can be beneficial: uniformly spread, decorrelated neurons reduce redundancy, encourage each neuron to specialize to a distinct discriminative direction, and thereby improve classification generalization.

Why that hurts diffusion finetuning

A diffusion UNet is not just learning discriminative features; it is a generative mapping that depends on structured, often redundant sets of neurons to synthesize coherent images. Consider a simple toy picture: a pretrained UNet may have several neuron directions that are tightly clustered to represent different aspects of “eye” appearance or various texture+geometry modes for faces; other sets encode backgrounds, lighting, or spatial layout. Those clusters and relative angles form a structured codebook the generator uses to compose images. Minimizing hyperspherical energy ($HE$) forces neurons to move away from clusters toward an evenly spaced, maximally “repulsive” configuration. Two concrete consequences follow:

- Loss of specialization and redundancy: clusters of neurons that jointly represent a semantic primitive (e.g. “eyeball + eyelid + specular highlight”) get pulled apart and decorrelated. The generator loses the coordinated subspace that previously produced that primitive reliably.

- Broken spatial/phase relationships: generative synthesis often requires phase-like consistency across channels (the paper’s phase analogy). Uniformizing directions changes those relative phases: even if single-neuron activations remain plausible, their joint combination no longer reconstructs the learned textures/structures, causing artifacts or collapse.

In short, minimizing $HE$ is a global repulsion force on neuron directions; that is helpful when you want many independent discriminative detectors (classification). But for a pretrained generator you want many neurons to keep their relative angular geometry (including clusters and redundancies), because that geometry encodes how to compose pixels and textures. Destroying that geometry destroys generation.

An illustrative minimal example

Imagine two neurons $w_1$ and $w_2$ that were nearly parallel and together encode “bright eye patch” so the decoder can place a highlight. If an $HE$ minimizer pushes them to be orthogonal and equally spaced with other unrelated neurons, neither alone nor their new combinations will recreate the original highlight: the generator’s downstream weights expect the original joint direction. The image quality or identity fidelity degrades.

How OFT/COFT address this

OFT’s design follows the paper’s conclusion: instead of trying to minimize $HE$, OFT enforces transformations that preserve pairwise angles (orthogonal transforms), so the hyperspherical energy is unchanged. That keeps the pretrained angular geometry intact while allowing controlled, low‑degree-of-freedom finetuning (rotation of the coordinate basis), and COFT further constrains deviation magnitude. This preserves the generator’s semantic structures while permitting the changes needed for a downstream control task.

Bottom line

Minimizing hyperspherical energy ($HE$) is a desirable inductive bias for classification (reducing redundancy, encouraging distinct detectors) but is harmful when applied to finetuning a pretrained generator because it destroys the angular arrangements—clusters, redundancies and phase-like relations—that the generator relies on to synthesize correct semantics. The paper therefore advocates preserving $HE$ (via orthogonal finetuning) rather than minimizing it.

### Practical guidance on choosing the number of blocks $r$ for block-diagonal $R$
Question:
Block-diagonal $R = \mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ trades off parameter-efficiency and flexibility. Is there a heuristic for picking $r$ per layer (e.g., proportional to $d$, aligned with channel groups or heads)? Does sharing blocks ($R_i = R_j$) significantly hurt performance in practice? Any empirical results showing performance vs. $r$ curves on subject-driven and controllable generation to guide practitioners?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper gives clear design principles but no single universal $r$; smaller $r$ (fewer, larger blocks → more parameters / more flexible orthogonal transform) gives more capacity and usually better final performance, while larger $r$ (more, smaller blocks → fewer params, stronger regularization) is cheaper and more stable. Practical heuristics from the paper are: align blockization with natural channel/head partitions (start there), treat $r$ as a knob between flexibility and stability (reduce $r$ if the model underfits), and avoid overly aggressive block-sharing unless you must minimize parameter count. The paper does not publish a single “performance vs $r$” curve in the main text, but it explicitly states the qualitative relationships and reports that performance continues to improve as you increase the number of trainable parameters (i.e., move toward smaller $r$).

Why this follows from the paper (intuitive + technical):
OFT parameterizes a layerwise orthogonal matrix $R$ as block-diagonal with $r$ blocks. When $r = 1$ you have an unconstrained $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (maximum flexibility, $\approx O(d^2)$ parameters); when $r$ increases the blocks are size $d/r$ and total trainable parameters fall roughly as $O(d^2 / r)$. Thus $r$ controls the degrees of freedom directly: smaller $r$ → larger blocks → more expressive orthogonal transform; larger $r$ → smaller blocks → stronger inductive bias (more regularization) and fewer parameters. The authors repeatedly emphasize the trade-off “flexibility vs regularity” and note empirically that increasing the trainable-parameter budget improves OFT performance and that block-diagonal parameterization is a main tool to control that trade-off.

Concrete, practical heuristics grounded in the paper
- Align blocks with channel or head groups when possible. For convolutional layers the paper explicitly points out that using the same number of blocks as input channels makes each block act per-channel (interpretable like depthwise transforms). For Transformer attention, a natural choice is to set block size equal to the per-head hidden dimension (so each head gets its own orthogonal block). This respects the architecture’s existing partitioning and often gives a good starting point.
- Start from the architecture-aligned blockization, then tune $r$ depending on whether you need more capacity. If subject/controllable generation quality (control-consistency, CLIP / DINO metrics) is unsatisfactory, reduce $r$ (merge blocks → larger blocks → more params and flexibility). If you see instabilities or model collapse, increase $r$ to regularize more.
- Use block-sharing only when you need to minimize parameter counts. The paper notes block-sharing reduces parameters substantially (parameter complexity drops to $O(d^2 / r^2)$ when all blocks are shared) but also introduces additional bias. In other words, shared blocks are a stronger constraint and may hurt final performance if the task needs per-block specialization. The paper therefore treats block-sharing as an optional aggressive compression, not the default.
- Combine with COFT $\varepsilon$ if worried about stability: the constrained variant (COFT) bounds the deviation of $R$ from identity and is recommended when you want both the angular-preservation benefits of orthogonality and an explicit limit on how far you move the model from pretrained weights. The paper shows COFT improves finetuning stability.

What the paper reports empirically (what you can expect)
The main empirical statements relevant to $r$ are (a) OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than baselines (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet/T2I-Adapter) while using comparable or fewer trainable parameters, and (b) model performance “has not yet saturated” as the number of trainable parameters increases — i.e., giving OFT more capacity (which in practice means using fewer blocks / larger blocks) tends to further improve performance. These are qualitative / aggregate observations in the experiments section rather than a single explicit numeric curve of “metric vs $r$” in the main text. The paper does give examples and ablations on related knobs (e.g., COFT’s $\varepsilon$) showing how stronger constraints reduce flexibility but improve stability; the same intuition transfers to $r$ as the primary capacity-control knob.

A recommended practical sweep to build your own curve
Because the paper doesn’t publish a direct $r$-vs-metric plot in the main figures, the pragmatic approach recommended by the paper’s principles is to sweep $r$ and monitor your downstream metrics. A small, practical sweep is: $r \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \text{align-with-channels (per-channel blocks)}\}$ or equivalently vary block size by factors of 2. Track both control-consistency (task-specific), CLIP / DINO (subject/text fidelity), and a diversity metric (LPIPS) and also monitor training stability (e.g., sudden collapse). For Transformer attention, try block sizes equal to head-dim first; if underfitting, reduce $r$ by merging heads’ blocks; if unstable, increase $r$.

Summary recommendation
Use architecture-aligned blocks as the default (per-channel for convs, per-head for attention), treat $r$ as the main capacity/regularization knob, prefer no block-sharing initially, and only share blocks when extreme parameter budgets require it. If you need strict finetuning stability, use COFT with a small $\varepsilon$; if you need maximum performance and can afford parameters, reduce $r$ (toward 1) to give OFT more flexibility. The paper’s empirical message is: more OFT capacity (smaller $r$, more parameters) typically yields better control and fidelity, but at the cost of weaker regularization—so tune $r$ to where you get acceptable stability and control performance.
