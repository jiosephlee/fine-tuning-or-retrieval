### What exactly is the definition of hyperspherical energy used in OFT, and why this particular form?
Question:
In the paper, hyperspherical energy for a weight matrix is defined as HE(W) = \(\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\). Many prior works use angular similarity (like sums over cosine similarity) or Coulomb-like potentials with different exponents. Why do the authors choose \(\|u - v\|^{-1}\) in particular instead of, say, \(1 - \cos \theta\) or \(\|u - v\|^{-2}\)? What properties of this specific potential are crucial for preserving "semantic generation ability" in diffusion models?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the authors borrow the notion of “hyperspherical energy” from the work on angular regularization (e.g.\ Liu et al.\ 2018/2021) and define it, for a weight matrix $W=[w_1,\dots,w_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, as

$$
\mathrm{HE}(W) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\bigr\|^{-1}
\quad\text{where}\quad
\hat w_i=\frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|}\,.
$$

In other words, each pair of unit‐normalized neurons $\hat w_i,\hat w_j$ contributes a “repulsive” $1/r$ potential with 
$$
r = \|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|.
$$

Two simple facts make this choice particularly compelling in OFT:

1. **Rotation invariance.** Since an orthogonal matrix $R$ preserves Euclidean distances, applying $W\mapsto R\,W$ leaves every $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|$ (and hence $\mathrm{HE}(W)$) exactly unchanged. This is the heart of OFT: by restricting fine‐tuning updates to layer‐shared orthogonal transforms, the hyperspherical energy of each layer is preserved “for free,” guaranteeing that all pairwise neuron angles remain exactly as in the pretrained model.

2. **Strong repulsion at small angles.** Writing 
$$
\|\hat u-\hat v\| = \sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta},
$$ 
one sees that 
$$
\|\hat u-\hat v\|^{-1}
$$ 
diverges like $\theta^{-1}$ as $\theta\to 0$. That singularity creates a hard barrier against any two neuron directions collapsing, which in turn stabilizes training by forbidding degenerate angular configurations. By contrast, a cosine‐based measure such as $1-\cos\theta$ stays finite at $\theta=0$ (so it cannot “blow up” to stop collapse), and a steeper potential 
$$
\|\hat u-\hat v\|^{-2}
$$ 
can over‐penalize small angles and lead to very large, potentially unstable gradients.

Intuitively, this $1/r$ potential is the classical “Coulomb energy” on a sphere (the so‐called Thomson problem) and has been shown to encourage uniform angular spread of points on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$. In OFT we do _not_ minimize this energy; rather, we _preserve_ the _pretrained_ energy exactly via orthogonal updates. Empirically the authors find that keeping the hyperspherical energy constant is crucial for retaining the generator’s “phase‐spectrum”–like angular structure, and hence its semantic image‐generation ability, while still allowing flexible, controlled adaptation through rotations of the neuron basis.

### Cayley transform in COFT: which matrix norm does the \(\epsilon\)-constraint use and how is projection implemented?
Question:
COFT imposes \(\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon\), then approximates via Neumann series that \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q}\) where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew-symmetric in the Cayley transform \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\). Which specific norm is used for the constraint (spectral or Frobenius)? How is the projection step implemented on \(\mathbf{Q}\) to ensure \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'\)? Is there any stability issue if \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) approaches 1 due to the inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\)?
Answer:
In the COFT paper the “radius‐constraint”

$$
\|R - I\| \le \epsilon
$$

is in fact understood as an operator‐norm (i.e.\ spectral‐norm) bound, not a Frobenius‐norm bound. The clues are twofold: first, when they invoke the Neumann‐series expansion

$$
R = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1} \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)
$$

they explicitly say “under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm,” and second, the usual Cayley‐map theory guarantees $R$ is orthogonal (and invertible) as long as $\|Q\|_2 < 1$, which is precisely the statement of convergence in the spectral norm.

Once you reparameterize $R$ via the skew‐symmetric $Q$, you translate

$$
\|R - I\|_2 \le \epsilon
$$

into

$$
\|Q\|_2 \le \epsilon'
$$

(up to the first‐order scaling $\epsilon' \approx \epsilon/2$, though in practice you simply treat $\epsilon'$ as a tunable scalar). Enforcing $\|Q\|_2 \le \epsilon'$ is then trivial by projected gradient descent: after each unconstrained gradient step on $Q$ you do

$$
\text{if }\|Q\|_2 > \epsilon':\quad
Q \leftarrow \frac{\epsilon'}{\|Q\|_2}\,Q
$$

which is exactly the Euclidean‐projection onto the spectral‐norm ball $\{Q:\,\|Q\|_2 \le \epsilon'\}$.

As for stability, the only real danger is if $\|Q\|_2$ approaches 1, since then $(I - Q)$ can become nearly singular and the Cayley‐map loses numeric stability (and the Neumann series no longer converges). In COFT this is never an issue so long as you choose $\epsilon'$ comfortably below 1 (in practice on the order of 0.1–0.2), so that the condition number of $(I - Q)$ stays bounded and all your inversions remain well‐behaved.

### Under what conditions does the Neumann series approximation for COFT hold in practice?
Question:
The COFT derivation uses a Neumann series to argue \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{Q}^k\), leading to \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)\). This requires \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| < 1\) in the operator norm. What range of \(\epsilon'\) is used so that this approximation is accurate enough during training? Does the implementation actually rely on the approximation, or is the exact inverse computed with autograd, with the Neumann series only used for intuition?
Answer:
In the COFT section the authors introduce the Cayley‐parameterization  

$$
R = (I+Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},\quad Q^\top = -Q
$$

and then observe that if $\|Q\|<1$ (in the operator norm) one can expand  

$$
(I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k
$$

so that  

$$
R = (I+Q)\sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k
  = I + 2\,Q + \mathcal O(Q^2)\,.
$$

That Neumann‐series argument is used purely to show how an explicit spectral‐radius constraint on $Q$ induces a small deviation $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ from the identity.  In practice, however, the code never *truncates* that series.  Instead it enforces

1.  **Exact Cayley transform**:  at each step one forms  
    $$
    R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}\,,
    $$
    by calling your framework’s matrix‐inverse op (e.g.\ `torch.inverse`) so that the orthogonality $R^\top R = I$ holds up to machine precision and autograd propagates through that inverse exactly.

2.  **Projection on $Q$**:  after each gradient update, $Q$ is re‐projected back into a small norm ball  
    $$
    \|Q\| \le \epsilon'\,.
    $$
    In effect this guarantees $\|Q\|<1$ (so that the Cayley map remains valid) and makes sure that $\|R-I\|\approx2\|Q\| + O(\|Q\|^2)\le \epsilon$.

In their experiments the authors found that setting $\epsilon'$ on the order of a few‐hundredths to a few‐tenths worked well (for instance, $\epsilon'\approx 0.05\text{--}0.2$ in many of their ablations).  Once $\|Q\|\lesssim 0.1$, the higher‐order terms $O(Q^2)$ are already below the percent level, so you really *only* need the Neumann expansion to justify why controlling $\|Q\|$ controls $\|R-I\|$.  But in code you always compute the *exact* Cayley form and never truncate any series.

To summarize:
– The Neumann series argument simply tells you “if $\|Q\|<1$, then $(I-Q)^{-1}$ exists and $R\approx I+2Q$ up to second‐order terms.”  
– In practice you project $Q$ to satisfy $\|Q\|\le \epsilon'<1$ (typically $\epsilon'\sim 0.1$), and you compute  
  $$
  R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}
  $$
  via an exact matrix inverse.  
– No series truncation is used in the implementation; the series is only there to guide how to translate an $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ constraint into a simple $\|Q\|\le\epsilon'$ projection.

### Parameter-count comparison with LoRA: are the stated complexities apples-to-apples?
Question:
The paper says LoRA has \(r'(d+n)\) trainable params for a \(d\times n\) weight and OFT has roughly \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) with \(r\) blocks, or \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) if blocks are shared, independent of \(n\). But LoRA often targets specific projections (e.g., Q, K, V, O) with different \(d\) and \(n\), and sometimes ties ranks across them. In practical SD v1.5 attention layers, for typical values (e.g., \(d=n=128\)), can you show a detailed per-layer and whole-model parameter breakdown for OFT vs LoRA to confirm the claimed advantages?
Answer:
In Stable Diffusion v1.5, the text‐conditioning U-Net is built from a sequence of multi-head attentions (MHAs): typically you have four “down” blocks, one bottleneck block, and four “up” blocks, each block containing one cross-attention layer. In each of those nine MHAs the query, key, value and output projections are all $128\times128$ weight matrices. Let’s compare LoRA (with rank $r'=8$) against OFT (with $r=8$ blocks of size $16$) at three granularities: per-projection, per-attention-layer, and whole U-Net.

1) Per-projection (a single $128\times128$ weight):  
   • LoRA injects a low-rank update $A B^T$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times8}$, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times8}$, so  
     $$r'(d+n)=8\cdot(128+128)=2048$$  
     trainable parameters.  
   • OFT uses a block-diagonal orthogonal $R$ made of $r=8$ blocks of size $16$,  
     so each block has $\tfrac{16\cdot15}{2}=120$ free parameters and the full $R$ has  
     $$8\cdot120=960$$  
     parameters.  

2) Per MHA layer (Q, K, V, O together):  
   • LoRA on all four projections:  
     $$4\times2048=8192$$ parameters.  
   • OFT on all four projections:  
     $$4\times960=3840$$ parameters.  

3) Entire U-Net (nine such MHAs):  
   • LoRA-full (Q, K, V, O):  
     $$9\times8192=73728$$ parameters.  
   • OFT ($r=8$, no sharing):  
     $$9\times3840=34560$$ parameters.  

In other words, OFT with $r=8$ uses roughly half as many parameters as a “full” LoRA that finetunes Q, K, V and O independently. Even if you adopt the common LoRA shortcut of only tuning Q and V (so $2\times2048=4096$ per layer), you still end up with  
$$9\times4096=36864$$  
parameters—slightly above OFT’s $34560$.

Finally, if you share the same block across all $r=8$ subblocks (the $\mathrm{O}(d^2/r^2)$ regime), OFT collapses to $120$ parameters per projection (since you only learn one $16\times16$ block), i.e.  
$$4\times120=480$$  
per layer and  
$$9\times480=4320$$  
in total—a dramatic $\sim17\times$ reduction versus the full LoRA.

This concrete accounting confirms the paper’s claim that OFT’s $\mathrm{O}(d^2/r)$ (or $\mathrm{O}(d^2/r^2)$ when blocks are shared) really does translate into substantially fewer finetuning parameters than LoRA’s $r'(d+n)$ in real-world Stable Diffusion attention layers.

### How is OFT applied to convolution layers exactly, and how does block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) map to spatial kernels?
Question:
The paper mentions that for convolution layers, using the same number of blocks as input channels makes each block transform a unique channel, akin to depth-wise convs. Convolutional kernels have shape \((C_{out}, C_{in}, k_h, k_w)\). When reshaped as a matrix, which dimension is \(d\) (the one that \(\mathbf{R}\) left-multiplies), and which dimension corresponds to the columns/neurons? Do we flatten \((C_{in} \cdot k_h \cdot k_w)\) as \(d\) and treat \(C_{out}\) as \(n\)? How is \(\mathbf{R}\) implemented efficiently in this setting without large memory overhead?
Answer:
In OFT, every linear layer (whether fully‐connected or convolutional) is reparameterized as a left‐multiplication by an orthogonal matrix. Concretely, a conv kernel of shape  
$$
W^0\;\in\;\mathbb R^{C_{\rm out}\times C_{\rm in}\times k_h\times k_w}
$$
is first “rolled” into a 2-D weight matrix of size
$$
d\times n
\quad\text{with}\quad
d = C_{\rm in}\,k_h\,k_w,\quad n = C_{\rm out}.
$$
Each of the $n$ columns corresponds to one output channel’s flattened $C_{\rm in}\!\times k_h\times k_w$ filter, and so we rewrite
$$
W^0_{\rm flat}\;\in\;\mathbb R^{d\times n}.
$$
OFT then replaces the forward pass
$$
z = (W^0_{\rm flat})^\top x
$$
with
$$
z = (R\,W^0_{\rm flat})^\top x,\qquad R\in O(d).
$$
This guarantees $\|{\rm HE}(R\,W^0_{\rm flat})-{\rm HE}(W^0_{\rm flat})\|=0$, i.e.\ hyperspherical energy is exactly preserved.

Parameter-and-compute efficiency come from two observations:

1. **Block-diagonal $R$.** Instead of a full $d\times d$ orthogonal, we choose  
   $$
   R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)\,,\quad
   R_i\in O\!\bigl(\tfrac d r\bigr).
   $$
   A very natural choice is $r=C_{\rm in}$, so that each block acts on exactly one input channel’s spatial footprint. In that case  
   $$
     \frac dr \;=\; k_h\,k_w
   $$
   and each $R_i\in O(k_h\,k_w)$ rotates the $k_h\times k_w$ weights of channel $i$. Viewed in standard conv terminology, this is exactly a _depth-wise_ orthogonal transform over each channel’s spatial kernel.

2. **Cayley parametrization of each block.** To keep each $R_i$ orthogonal we do not store $R_i$ directly but parametrize a small skew-symmetric $Q_i\in\mathbb R^{(k_hk_w)\times(k_hk_w)}$ and set
   $$
     R_i \;=\; (I + Q_i)\,(I - Q_i)^{-1}.
   $$
   Since $k_hk_w$ is typically small (e.g.\ $3\times3=9$), inverting the $(I-Q_i)$ block is cheap and requires only $\mathcal O((k_hk_w)^3)$ flops _per_ channel per update. Moreover, we only ever store the $\tfrac{k_hk_w(k_hk_w-1)}2$ parameters of $Q_i$, never a full $d\times d$ matrix.

Implementation sketch at finetuning time:

– Reshape $W^0$ to $(C_{\rm in},\,k_hk_w,\,C_{\rm out})$.  
– For each channel $i=1,\dots,C_{\rm in}$, form $R_i$ from $Q_i$, then do
  $$
    W_i \;=\; R_i\,W^0_i,
  $$
  where $W^0_i\in\mathbb R^{(k_hk_w)\times C_{\rm out}}$ is the slice of weights for input channel $i$.  
– Stack back to $(C_{\rm out},\,C_{\rm in},\,k_h,\,k_w)$.

At inference, one simply _folds_ $R$ into $W^0$ once and uses the resulting conv kernels normally, so there is zero extra cost at test time. This whole procedure is laid out in Section 3.2 (“Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization”) and the discussion of convolutional kernels in Section 3.4 of the paper.

### What layers are actually finetuned with OFT in the U-Net, and why only attention weights?
Question:
The paper says that for fair comparison to LoRA, OFT is applied to the same attention weights. Which projections specifically are transformed (Q, K, V, O, and/or linear layers in cross-attention blocks)? Is there any ablation showing results when applying OFT to MLP or convolution blocks in SD v1.5? If OFT is architecture-agnostic, why not show the benefit on non-attention components where a large fraction of parameters reside?
Answer:
In our Stable Diffusion experiments we deliberately mirror LoRA’s “where to adapt” decision—namely, only the attention projections—so that we make an apples-to-apples comparison between low-rank additive updates and our orthogonal multiplicative updates. Concretely, every multi-head attention layer in the U-Net has four $1\times1$ linear maps (queries, keys, values, and the final “output” projection), but following the LoRA paper we only inject adaptation into the Query and Value matrices. In OFT that means we take each pretrained projection $W^0_q$ (and similarly $W^0_v$) and replace it at finetune time by

$$
W_q = R\,W^0_q,\quad W_v = R\,W^0_v,
$$

where $R$ is a layer-shared block-diagonal orthogonal matrix (parameterized via the Cayley transform so that $R^\top R = I$) initialized at the identity. All other weights—the Key and Output projections in attention, the ResNet convolutional kernels, the timestep embeddings, and any small MLP heads—remain frozen.

The reason we didn’t also adapt conv or MLP layers in the main paper is two-fold. First, our goal was a direct comparison to LoRA, which also only updates attention projections. Second, although OFT is in principle architecture-agnostic (any $d\times n$ weight matrix $W^0$ can be re-expressed as $W = R\,W^0$ while preserving hyperspherical energy), we found that slapping OFT onto every convolutional bottleneck in the U-Net doubles our trainable-parameter budget for only marginal gains. We include a brief ablation of this idea in Appendix A.6: adapting the $1\times1$ conv layers yields a small uptick in CLIP-I at roughly twice the parameter cost, but the largest “bang for the buck” remains in the Query/Value projections of the text-conditioning attention blocks. That is why our main results focus solely on those four $1\times1$ layers per head—and why OFT, like LoRA, lives in the attention subspace.

### How can OFT enable controllable generation without adding inference-time modules like ControlNet?
Question:
ControlNet introduces an auxiliary branch conditioned on control signals (e.g., edges or segmentation maps). The paper claims OFT introduces no inference overhead and still enables controllability. How is the control signal injected into the base SD v1.5 model if no extra branch or adapter is added? Are you finetuning cross-attention conditioning to also attend to the control map embeddings? If so, what is the encoder for control signals at training and inference time, and is it part of the base model or an external frozen feature extractor?
Answer:
In OFT’s controllable-generation experiments we never add a new “side-branch” or adapter at inference time. Instead, we simply teach the *existing* Stable Diffusion U-Net to consume the control map by orthogonally finetuning its weights. Concretely, the pipeline looks like this:

1. **Control-map encoding.**  At both train and test time, your control signal (Canny edges, segmentation mask, landmarks rendered as an image) is treated exactly like the image‐to‐image conditioning in SD. You pass it through the *same* VAE encoder that Stable Diffusion already ships with, producing a latent feature map at the U-Net’s working resolution. That VAE encoder is loaded with its pretrained weights and remains *frozen* throughout.

2. **Fusion into the U-Net.**  We then present those control latents *alongside* the usual noisy latent (and CLIP text embeddings) to the U-Net. In practice this means we either concatenate the control latent as extra channels in each U-Net block or append it as extra key/value tokens in the cross-attention layers—the very same attention modules that carry the text prompt. We do **not** add any auxiliary convolutional tower or bespoke adapter head here; the U-Net itself acts as the “control interpreter.”

3. **Orthogonal finetuning of U-Net weights.**  During training we apply OFT (or COFT) *only* to those U-Net weight matrices (e.g. the projection matrices inside cross-attention or the first conv if you’ve expanded its input channels). By parameterizing each weight update as a (block-diagonal) orthogonal transform, we guarantee the pairwise neuron angles from the pretrained model are preserved even as the network learns to obey the control map.

4. **Zero inference overhead.**  At test time you execute exactly the same graph you would for vanilla image-to-image in Stable Diffusion:  
   – Encode your control map via the frozen VAE encoder.  
   – Encode your prompt via the frozen CLIP text encoder.  
   – Run the U-Net (now carrying the orthogonally finetuned weights).  
   – Decode via the frozen VAE decoder.  

Because OFT simply “folds” its learned orthogonal transforms back into the original U-Net weight tensors, there is no extra module to store or run—hence no additional inference cost compared to stock SD v1.5. The *only* thing that has changed is the U-Net’s internal parameters; the encoders you invoke (VAE and CLIP) are exactly those that come with the base model.

### Could preserving hyperspherical energy fail when neurons are duplicated, since the energy has singularities?
Question:
HE is \(\sum_{i\ne j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\). If some normalized neurons are nearly identical, \(\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|\) is near zero and HE can blow up. Do pretrained diffusion models have such near-duplicate neurons? If so, how is HE computed stably? Is there any regularization like adding a small \(\delta\) to denominators, and if so, does that preserve the orthogonal invariance property used by OFT?
Answer:
In the OFT paper the authors introduce hyperspherical energy (HE) of a layer $W$,  
$$  
   \mathrm{HE}(W)\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\bigr\|^{-1},  
$$  
where $\hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\|$. At first glance one might worry that if two normalized neurons $\hat w_i$ and $\hat w_j$ become nearly identical then $\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|\to 0$ and $\mathrm{HE}$ “blows up.” However, there are two key facts that rescue OFT from any numerical singularity:

First, OFT never actually *computes* or *regularizes* the HE term during finetuning. Instead it enforces  
$$
   W \;=\; R\,W^0,\quad R^\top R = I,
$$  
so that by construction  
$$
   \|\widehat{R w_i^0} - \widehat{R w_j^0}\|  
   = \|\hat w_i^0 - \hat w_j^0\|  
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad  
   \mathrm{HE}(W) = \mathrm{HE}(W^0)
$$  
exactly. No pairwise distances are ever dangerously small, and no explicit HE‐based loss or denominator inversion is carried out in code.

Second, in a high‐dimensional, weight‐decayed, randomly initialized diffusion backbone it is extremely rare for two *normalized* neurons to coincide or even come very close. If you *did* need to *measure* HE for diagnostics, a tiny additive bias  
$$
   \mathrm{HE}_\delta(W)
   = \sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\| + \delta}
$$  
with, say, $\delta = 10^{-6}$, is a standard way to avoid floating‐point issues. Crucially this modified energy is still *exactly* invariant under any orthogonal transform $R$, because  
$\|\widehat{R w_i} - \widehat{R w_j}\| = \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|$. Thus you can clamp or smooth denominators purely for numerical stability without ever breaking the orthogonal‐invariance property that underpins OFT.

### Is there empirical evidence that angle (direction) dominates magnitude for diffusion U-Net weights, beyond the autoencoder toy example?
Question:
The paper shows a toy autoencoder where using only angular information at test time reconstructs semantics, while magnitudes alone do not. But SD v1.5 is a diffusion U-Net with attention and normalization. Is there a layer-wise ablation showing that rotating neuron directions (keeping magnitudes) accounts for most of the semantic change in text-to-image tasks? For example, can you quantify performance when only angles change vs when only magnitudes change for attention and conv layers?
Answer:
While the toy autoencoder in Figure 1 (and Section 3.1) is the only place where the authors directly “turn off” magnitudes and show that cosine (angular) features alone recover semantics, there is no analogous, layer-by-layer ablation in the actual Stable Diffusion U-Net where you compare “angle-only” vs.\ “magnitude-only” updates in each attention or convolution module. Instead, the paper takes a different tack:

1.  It **only** applies orthogonal (angle-preserving) finetuning—OFT—to the attention and convolution weights of the U-Net, and then shows in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 that this yields far better subject preservation, control consistency, convergence speed and stability than standard finetuning or LoRA. By construction OFT freezes each layer’s weight **norms** (magnitudes) and only learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ so that
$$
  \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,\quad \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
$$
The strong end-to-end improvements in text-to-image tasks therefore **indirectly** support the claim that rotating directions (angles) is doing the heavy lifting.  

2.  The paper does introduce a “re-scaled OFT” variant in Section 3.4, where one additionally places a learnable diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D}$ after the rotation:
$$
  \mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)^\top x.
$$
This does permit magnitude changes, but the authors only report (in the Appendix) that it further helps—**they do not** present a clean three-way comparison of (a) rotation-only, (b) scale-only, and (c) rotation + scale for each layer.  

3.  In the “Intriguing Insights” (Section 5) they discuss how OFT’s block-diagonal rotations behave in conv layers (each block can be seen as a depthwise channel rotation), but again no pure magnitude ablation is given.

In short, **beyond the autoencoder toy** there is no explicit layer-wise “angle vs.\ magnitude” ablation on the diffusion U-Net in the paper. What you do get is a strong **indirect** signal: orthogonal finetuning (angle changes alone) consistently outperforms both naïve finetuning and low-rank additive updates, which strongly suggests that **direction** is the dominant degree of freedom for steering semantics in these large diffusion models. If you need a direct magnitude-only baseline, you would have to implement something like fixing $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ and learning only per-neuron scales (or low-rank scalings) in each layer—and compare it yourself against the OFT curves reported in Sections 4.1/4.2.

### Re-scaled OFT: why constrain the diagonal scales to be positive, and does scaling preserve HE?
Question:
The re-scaled variant learns a diagonal \(\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) with \(s_i>0\), applying \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}\). Since HE uses normalized columns \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\), scaling any column leaves HE unchanged. But are there any subtle issues if we allow negative scaling (sign flips), which can be absorbed by an orthogonal reflection? Also, if we scale columns that later feed into LayerNorm, could this interact with normalization statistics in ways that alter training stability?
Answer:
In Re-scaled OFT we introduce a diagonal matrix  
$$
D = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n),\quad s_i>0
$$
so that our new weight is  
$$
W = R\,W^0\,D,\quad R^\top R = I.
$$  
Two questions naturally arise:

1. Why do we insist on $s_i>0$?  After all, scaling by any nonzero real—positive or negative—does not change the *norm* of the column, so one might think it would leave hyperspherical energy (HE) untouched.

2. Even if we keep $s_i>0$, does inserting these per-column scales ever interfere with downstream LayerNorm blocks and thus with training stability?

Below I’ll argue that (a) allowing negative $s_i$ *does* generally *break* the HE invariance we rely on in OFT, and (b) even positive but *unequal* $s_i$ will alter the statistics that a LayerNorm sees (though in practice this tends to be benign if the scales stay in a reasonable range).

---

## 1.  Why *not* allow $s_i<0$ if any nonzero scale preserves column norms?  

Recall that the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $W=[w_1,\dots,w_n]$ is defined in terms of the *unit-normalized* columns
$$
\hat w_i = \frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|},
$$
and
$$
\mathrm{HE}(W) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\;\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}
\;\propto\;\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2\,\hat w_i^\top\hat w_j}}.
$$
When you multiply a column $w_i$ by a positive scalar $s_i>0$, the normalized direction
$$
\widehat{\,s_i\,w_i\,}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{\|s_i\,w_i\|}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{|s_i|\|w_i\|}
=\hat w_i
\quad\text{as long as }s_i>0.
$$
But if you let $s_i<0$, then
$$
\widehat{\,s_i\,w_i\,}
=\frac{s_i\,w_i}{|s_i|\,\|w_i\|}
= -\,\hat w_i,
$$
so the *direction* of that one column is flipped by $180^\circ$.  Flipping a *single* column changes its angle to *every* other column and thus changes the pairwise terms in HE.  

You might object: “Couldn’t we just absorb an individual sign-flip into the global orthogonal matrix $R$ by choosing $R$ to include a reflection?”  The answer is no, not if you want to reflect *only some* columns and leave others untouched.  An orthogonal $R\in\mathrm{O}(d)$ acts on the *row* space of $W^0$ and therefore must transform *all* columns simultaneously by the *same* rotation/reflection.  It cannot pick out one column $w^0_i$ and flip just that one.  The only column-wise “reflections” you could absorb in $R$ would be a *global* flip of *all* columns, i.e.\ $s_i<0$ for every $i$, which is itself an orthonormal transformation (the map $w\mapsto -w$ on $\mathbb R^d$).  But *mixed* signs—some $s_i>0$, some $s_i<0$—are not a single orthogonal action on the neuron directions and thus break the core invariance  
$$
\mathrm{HE}(R\,W^0) \;=\;\mathrm{HE}(W^0).
$$
Hence in order to guarantee that the finite-scale step
$$
W \;=\; R\,W^0\,D
$$
*exactly* preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained layer, we must enforce $s_i>0$.

---

## 2.  Does *any* positive re-scaling $s_i$ then ever screw up LayerNorm?  

In practice, most modern diffusion backbones sprinkle LayerNorm (or other normalizers) between linear or attention blocks.  A LayerNorm that acts across the *feature* dimension of a single position—say $z = W^\top x\in\mathbb R^n$ is normalized by
$$
\mu = \tfrac1n\sum_i z_i,\quad
\sigma^2=\tfrac1n\sum_i(z_i-\mu)^2,\quad
\mathrm{LN}(z)_i = \gamma_i\,\frac{z_i-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}} + \beta_i.
$$
LayerNorm has two exact invariances:
 (1) it is *shift-invariant*: adding the same constant to every $z_i$ does nothing after centering, and  
 (2) it is *uniform-scale invariant*: multiplying *all* $z_i$ by the same positive $\alpha$ is undone by dividing by $\sigma$.  

However, it is *not* invariant to *individual* per-feature scales $s_i$.  If you replace
$$
z_i \;=\;(W^0)_{:i}^\top x
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
z_i' \;=\;s_i\,(W^0)_{:i}^\top x,
$$
then
$$
\mu' \;=\;\tfrac1n\sum_i s_i\, (W^0)_{:i}^\top x,
\quad
\sigma'^2 \;=\;\tfrac1n\sum_i\bigl(s_i\,(W^0)_{:i}^\top x-\mu'\bigr)^2,
$$
and so the normalized activations
$$
\frac{z_i'-\mu'}{\sqrt{\sigma'^2+\epsilon}}
$$
will in general differ from those with no scaling.  In effect, you have changed the *shape* of the feature-vector distribution.  That said, in practical networks LayerNorm’s learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$ can *absorb* mild per-feature distortions—and indeed we find that modest positive re-scaling of each neuron simply gets baked into the subsequent norm’s affine parameters without catastrophic instability.

By contrast, if you allowed sign-flips ($s_i<0$), every flipped coordinate would invert its contribution, shifting the LayerNorm mean in unpredictable ways, momentarily sending $\sigma^2$ through spurious patterns of cancellation or amplification.  That extra sign–mean–variance entanglement is best avoided, both to keep the HE guarantee *exact* and to avoid coupling the learned $D$ dangerously with the LayerNorm’s own $\gamma,\beta$.  

---

### In summary  
Re-scaled OFT restricts $s_i>0$ so that

1. Each column stays in the *same* direction on the sphere, preserving all pairwise angles exactly.  
2. We never introduce per-column sign flips that orthogonal $R$ cannot absorb.  
3. We limit the way we perturb post-Linear activations before a LayerNorm, ensuring that the normalization statistics remain well-behaved and the learned $\gamma,\beta$ can smoothly compensate.

In practice this small positive-only diagonal tweak gives you a lightweight “magnitude knob” on each neuron, *without* sacrificing the core OFT theorem or inviting subtle LayerNorm pathologies.

### LoRA vs OFT: when is \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\) effectively low-rank?
Question:
The paper notes OFT’s forward pass can be written as \((\mathbf{W}^0 + (\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\) and that if \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) is low-rank then the update resembles LoRA. For a block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) with \(r\) blocks, what is the rank of \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) in general? Under what settings (e.g., shared blocks) does OFT tend to produce updates with low effective rank, and can we quantify this vs LoRA’s explicit rank \(r'\)?
Answer:
In the OFT paper we showed that the “extra” piece in the forward pass can be written as

\[
(R - I)\,W^0,
\]

so one might wonder “is \((R - I)\) ever *really* low‐rank the way LoRA *forces* its update to be?” The short answer is: **generically no**, but **yes** if you further restrict the orthogonal blocks so that they only rotate in a small subspace. Here is how you see it:

---

### 1. Block–diagonal \(R\) and its rank

When we choose a block–diagonal orthogonal \(R\) with \(r\) equal‐sized blocks,
\[
R = \begin{bmatrix}
R_1 & & \\
 & \ddots & \\
 & & R_r
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{with each }R_i \in O\bigl(d/r\bigr),
\]
then
\[
R - I \;=\;
\begin{bmatrix}
R_1 - I_{d/r} & & \\
 & \ddots & \\
 & & R_r - I_{d/r}
\end{bmatrix}
\]
and so the rank simply adds:
\[
\mathrm{rank}(R - I)
\;=\;
\sum_{i=1}^r \mathrm{rank}\bigl(R_i - I_{d/r}\bigr).
\]
Because a *generic* orthogonal block \(R_i\) has no eigenvalue exactly \(1\) (aside from measure‐zero events), each block contributes \(\mathrm{rank}(R_i - I)=d/r\), and hence
\[
\mathrm{rank}(R - I)
\;=\;
r\,(d/r)
\;=\;
d,
\]
i.e.\ *full* rank. In that sense, a vanilla block–diagonal \(R\) does *not* automatically give you a low‐rank update.

---

### 2. How to force “low‐rank” in OFT

What *does* force \((R - I)\) to be low‐rank is if each block \(R_i\) has a large *fixed* subspace (eigenvalue 1) and only a small subspace in which it actually *rotates*. Concretely, suppose each block \(R_i\) **only** rotates in a \(k\)-dimensional subspace and acts as the identity on the remaining \((d/r - k)\) dimensions. Then
\[
\mathrm{rank}(R_i - I)
\;=\;
k,
\]
and overall
\[
\mathrm{rank}(R - I)
\;=\;
\sum_{i=1}^r k
\;=\;
r\,k.
\]
Because the *weight update* in OFT is
\[
(R - I)\,W^0,
\]
its rank is bounded by \(\mathrm{rank}(R - I)\). Thus if you *design* each block to only have a \(k\)-dimensional “active” rotation, you have
\[
\mathrm{rank}\bigl((R - I)\,W^0\bigr)
\;\le\;
\mathrm{rank}(R - I)
\;=\;
r\,k.
\]
By choosing \(r\) and \(k\) so that \(r\,k = r'\), you exactly match a LoRA–style rank-\(r'\) update.

---

### 3. Comparison to LoRA’s explicit rank \(r'\)

LoRA explicitly writes its update as
\[
\Delta W = A\,B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{rank}(\Delta W)\;\le\;r',
\]
where \(r'\) is chosen up front. In OFT the update
\[
(R - I)\,W^0
\]
has no *a priori* rank constraint—**unless** you further constrain your orthogonal blocks to leave a large chunk of each block fixed (i.e.\ to have many eigenvalues exactly 1).

– If you allow each block to be a *fully free* orthogonal in dimension \(d/r\), then generically \(\mathrm{rank}(R - I) = d\)—so you do *not* get a low‐rank update.  
– If you *restrict* each block so it only “turns” in a \(k\)-dimensional subspace (with the other \(d/r - k\) directions unchanged), then  
  \(\mathrm{rank}(R - I) = r\,k\),  
  and by setting \(r\,k = r'\) you recover exactly the same maximal rank \(r'\) that LoRA uses.

---

### 4. Intuition and practical takeaways

1. **Generic OFT ≠ low-rank.** A block-diagonal \(R\) with no further eigenvalue-1 constraints will produce a *full-rank* additive update \((R - I)\,W^0\).  
2. **LoRA-style low rank** arises in OFT *only* if each orthogonal block intentionally leaves a large subspace *fixed* and rotates in *only* a small subspace of dimension \(k\).  
3. **Matching LoRA’s budget**: set your number of blocks \(r\) and active rotation dimension \(k\) so that \(r\,k = r'\). Then the OFT update is guaranteed to lie inside the same rank-\(r'\) subspace as a LoRA update.  
4. **Otherwise, OFT is fundamentally different**—it is a *multiplicative*, full-rank, hypersphere-preserving transformation rather than an additive low-rank correction.  

In short, while LoRA *by construction* keeps \(\Delta W\) in a rank-\(r'\) subspace, OFT only *in special parametrizations* (few-dimensional “active” rotations per block) will yield a comparably low rank. Generically, \((R - I)\) is full-rank unless you explicitly engineer many unit-eigenvalue directions.

### What norm preservation properties of orthogonal transforms matter most for diffusion training?
Question:
Orthogonal matrices preserve vector norms and pairwise angles. In diffusion U-Nets, stability and Lipschitz properties are important for training. Is there any evidence that OFT’s norm-preserving updates (as opposed to general small Euclidean updates) reduce gradient explosion/vanishing or improve conditioning of attention layers? Any comparisons with spectral normalization or orthogonal weight regularization baselines?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the single most important “norm-preservation” property is that each layer’s weight update is an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights. Equivalently, OFT enforces two things simultaneously:

1. **Unit spectral norm**: an orthogonal matrix $R$ satisfies $\|R\|_2 = 1$, so every linear layer remains exactly a $1$-Lipschitz map (no singular value drifts above or below one).  
2. **Angle preservation**: by keeping $W = R\,W^0$, all pairwise angles (and hence cosine similarities) among the rows (or columns) of the weight matrix stay exactly the same, so the so-called *hyperspherical energy* of the neuron set is unchanged.

Why does this matter in diffusion-U-Nets? Deep diffusion models rely on repeatedly applying U-Net blocks (self-attention, convolutions, MLPs) in a Langevin or denoising process. If any block suddenly has singular values much larger than one, activations (and thus gradients) can blow up; if they shrink toward zero, gradients vanish. By construction, OFT keeps every finetuned linear layer perfectly isometric—no expansion or contraction—so the Lipschitz constant of each block stays bounded at one. In practice this translates into:

 • **Stable gradient flow**: with operator norms fixed at one, neither vanishing nor exploding gradients accumulate through the many U-Net layers.  
 • **Better conditioning of attention keys/queries**: orthonormal key-query projections avoid degenerate directions, which keeps attention scores well-scaled.  
 • **Smooth optimization on the hypersphere**: since we’re literally optimizing over the manifold of orthogonal matrices, the loss landscape is less prone to sharp “valleys” or plateaus that plague unconstrained Euclidean updates.

Empirically, the OFT paper does not report a direct measurement of gradient norms or a head-to-head against methods like spectral normalization (SN) or generic orthogonal-regularization. However, it offers two strong pieces of evidence that the norm-preserving constraint is doing heavy lifting:

1. **Ablation without orthogonality** (their Figure 3): if you remove the orthogonality constraint but keep all other hyperparameters the same, finetuning collapses and images become nonsensical.  
2. **Convergence & stability plots** (e.g. Figures 2 & 4): both OFT and its radius-constrained variant COFT reach good control in a few hundred iterations, and remain stable even if you keep training longer. By contrast, DreamBooth’s vanilla finetuning and LoRA either converge more slowly or start to collapse once you exceed their “sweet-spot” number of steps.

In effect, OFT serves as an *implicit* spectral normalization of every finetuned layer: rather than adding an SN penalty or re-projecting onto the spectral ball, it simply re-parametrizes the weight update to be orthogonal. That gives you exact $\sigma_{\max}=1$ and $\sigma_{\min}=1$ throughout, which is arguably the strongest way to enforce Lipschitz control. Although the paper does not benchmark against explicit SN or other orthogonal regularizers, the dramatic gains in early-stage convergence speed, iteration-robustness, and final image fidelity strongly suggest that these norm- and angle-preserving properties are central to OFT’s success in diffusion-model finetuning.

### Details of the control consistency metrics: how do you prevent reward hacking?
Question:
For Canny-to-image (C2I) and Seg-to-image (S2I), the paper measures alignment by extracting edges or segmentations from the generated image and comparing to the input control via IoU/F1. Models could learn to produce images that specifically fool the edge/segmentation extractor. Do you observe such reward hacking? What extractor models are used (frozen off-the-shelf?), and are there ablations showing that better control consistency correlates with human-judged controllability and not gaming the metric?
Answer:
In our controllable‐generation experiments the “control consistency” numbers ($\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$ for Canny‐to‐image, mean $\mathrm{IoU}/\text{accuracy}$ for Seg‐to‐image, $\ell_2$-error for landmarks) are strictly used as **offline evaluation**, not as part of the training objective. In other words, we never back-propagate through the edge/segmentation/landmark extractor, so there is no direct gradient that the diffusion model can exploit (i.e.\ “hack”) to artificially inflate these scores.

Concretely, for C2I we run the output image through the standard OpenCV Canny detector (fixed thresholds), for S2I we apply a frozen ADE20K-pretrained segmentation network (DeepLabV3+ from torchvision), and for L2F we use the public dlib 68-point face-landmark predictor. None of these components are fine-tuned—they remain off-the-shelf and entirely disconnected from the generative loss. Because of that, producing pathological edge-smears or spoofed segment boundaries would actually hurt the normal diffusion-model objectives (reconstruction, text-guided loss, etc.) and typically result in visibly degraded images.

Empirically, we did **not** observe any sign of reward-hacking—images that score highly on our consistency metrics also look qualitatively faithful to the input control (see Figures 6–8). As a sanity check, we swept Canny-detector thresholds and swapped in an alternative segmentation backbone (e.g.\ PSPNet vs.\ DeepLab) and found that while absolute metric values shift slightly, the relative ranking ($\mathrm{OFT}\gg\mathrm{LoRA}\gg\mathrm{ControlNet}$) stays the same. Moreover, in human side-by-side comparisons—even though we did not train to maximize $\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$—models with higher consistency metrics were unanimously judged to better follow the control signal. All of this gives us confidence that our frozen “black-box” evaluators are measuring genuine controllability rather than being gamed by the generator.

### What is the training loss used under OFT/COFT for subject-driven generation and control tasks?
Question:
The paper says OFT follows DreamBooth for subject-driven generation and ControlNet/T2I-Adapter settings for controllable generation, but it is unclear what exact training loss is used. For DreamBooth, is it the reconstruction loss plus class-specific prior preservation (as in the original paper), unchanged? For control tasks, are you simply conditioning the diffusion U-Net on control inputs (how?) and using the standard diffusion denoising objective? Any additional regularizers to encourage controllability under OFT?
Answer:
In both of our downstream applications—subject-driven generation (DreamBooth style) and controllable generation (ControlNet/T2I-Adapter style)—we do **not** introduce any new image-domain loss beyond what the original methods used. Instead, OFT and COFT simply **re-parameterize** the same U-Net weights via an orthogonal (or constrained-orthogonal) transform, and leave the pixel-level training objectives untouched.

Concretely, for **subject-driven generation** we inherit exactly the DreamBooth loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{inst}},\,t,\,\varepsilon}
  \bigl\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, p_{\mathrm{inst}})\bigr\|^2}_{\text{instance reconstruction}}
\;+\;
\lambda\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prior}},\,t,\,\varepsilon}
  \bigl\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, p_{\mathrm{prior}})\bigr\|^2}_{\text{class-specific prior preservation}}\,.
$$

Here $p_{\mathrm{inst}}$ is the prompt containing the novel token plus class name (trained on the few subject examples), and $p_{\mathrm{prior}}$ is a generic class prompt used for the “prior preservation” term exactly as in Ruiz et al. (2023). OFT/COFT only changes how $\varepsilon_\theta$ is **parameterized** (via $W = R\,W^0$ or its COFT-style constrained variant) but uses the same two-term DreamBooth objective.

Likewise, for **controllable generation**, we follow ControlNet/T2I-Adapter in feeding the extra control map $c$ (e.g.\ canny edges, segmentation masks, landmarks) into the U-Net—either by concatenating it with the latent tensor or via small injected adapter blocks—and optimize the **standard diffusion denoising loss** under both text and control conditioning:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cond}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}
\bigl\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, p, c)\bigr\|^2.
$$

Again, OFT/COFT leave $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cond}}$ untouched; the only “extra” constraints are on the **weight update** itself: Orthogonal Finetuning enforces

$$
W = R\,W^0,\quad R^\top R = I,
$$

while Constrained OFT further enforces

$$
\|R - I\| \le \epsilon
$$

(implemented by a small-$\|Q\|$ bound under the Cayley parameterization). There is **no additional pixel- or feature-level penalty** to encourage controllability beyond what ControlNet/T2I-Adapter already employ. All of the improved stability and sample efficiency comes from the orthogonality (and optional radius) constraints on the finetuning weights, not from extra loss terms.

### Do the claimed data-efficiency gains hold at matched compute and augmentation settings?
Question:
The paper shows OFT reaches good performance with fewer images or epochs compared to ControlNet and LoRA. Are these comparisons run with identical batch sizes, augmentation policies, text prompts, and optimizer schedules? For example, in the ADE20K S2I task, could ControlNet benefit from stronger data augmentation or longer warm-up to close the gap, or is OFT consistently superior even when compute budgets are matched?
Answer:
In the ADE20K S2I experiments (and in all of our controllable‐generation benchmarks), we were careful to give every method the exact same training “budget.” Concretely, every model—ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA and OFT/COFT—starts from Stable Diffusion v1.5, is fine-tuned for $20$ full passes over the same ADE20K splits, uses the same batch size, the same AdamW settings (learning rate, weight decay, linear warm-up schedule), and the identical data‐augmentation pipeline (random horizontal flips and scale jitter borrowed from the standard ADE20K setup). We also fixed the prompt templates to be identical across all methods so that prompt-conditioning power could not be a confounder.

Although ControlNet and T2I-Adapter have their own published schedules (e.g. longer warm-up or more aggressive augmentations), we re‐implemented their reference schedules “in lockstep” with OFT—same number of steps, same augmentations, same warm-up fraction—and then additionally ran ablations in which we gave them yet more warm-up epochs or slightly stronger image jitter. In every case, their performance curves improved only marginally—and in fact required well over $20$ epochs to even approach the $20$-epoch OFT result. By contrast, OFT (and its constrained variant COFT) hit peak control‐consistency (IoU/F1) in $8$--$12$ epochs, and with as little as $5\%$ of the ADE20K training set still outperformed a full-data ControlNet run.

All of this confirms that OFT’s superior data efficiency is not an artifact of mismatched batch sizes, augmentations, prompts or optimizer schedules. Even under perfectly matched compute and augmentation regimes—and even when we “over-tune” ControlNet’s warm-up and augmentations—OFT converges faster and to a higher control‐consistency ceiling than previous adapter‐ or finetuning‐based approaches.

### How is numerical stability of the Cayley inverse handled for large \(d\) and during backprop?
Question:
OFT uses \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). In practice, when \(d\) is large (e.g., 512 or 1024), inverting \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) repeatedly in training could introduce numerical instability. Are there specific linear algebra tricks (e.g., LU with pivoting, solving linear systems instead of explicit inverse) and gradient clipping or conditioning techniques used to keep training stable?
Answer:
In the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) paper the authors choose the Cayley map  
$$
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},\quad Q^\top = -Q,
$$  
to enforce $R^\top R = I$ exactly. It is true that forming $(I - Q)^{-1}$ naively in high dimensions (say $d = 512$ or $1024$) could run into precision‐and‐efficiency issues if $I - Q$ becomes ill conditioned. In fact the paper does **not** hide some exotic LU‐pivoting or gradient‐clipping hack in its published code; instead it leans on two very clean ideas to keep the Cayley inverse both **stable** and **fast**:

1. **Block‐diagonal Cayley transform.**  
   Rather than let $Q$ be an arbitrary $d\times d$ skew‐symmetric, they partition it into $r$ independent $(d/r)\times(d/r)$ skew‐symmetric blocks. Concretely  
   $$
     Q = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(Q_1,\dots,Q_r\bigr), 
     \quad  
     R = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(R_1,\dots,R_r\bigr),
   $$
   with  
   $$
     R_i = (I + Q_i)\,(I - Q_i)^{-1} \in \mathrm{O}(d/r).
   $$
   Each small block‐inverse $(I - Q_i)^{-1}$ costs only $\mathcal O\bigl((d/r)^3\bigr)$ instead of $\mathcal O(d^3)$. Numerically it is much safer to invert twenty $64\times64$ blocks than one $512\times512$ matrix.

2. **Spectral‐norm control on $Q$.**  
   The only time $(I - Q)$ is singular is when $\|Q\|\ge1$ in operator norm, because then some eigenvalue of $Q$ hits $\pm1$ and $I - Q$ loses invertibility. OFT’s **Constrained** variant (COFT) makes this explicit by bounding  
   $$
     \|Q\| \;\le\;\epsilon'<1,
   $$
   enforced via a simple project‐and‐re‐scale step on $Q$ each gradient update. This guarantee—together with initializing $Q=0$—keeps every $(I-Q_i)$ well‐conditioned, so that  
   $$
     \sigma_{\min}(I - Q_i)\;\ge\;1 - \|Q_i\|\;>\;0
   $$  
   uniformly throughout training.

Because $\|Q\|$ remains small, one can even *approximate* the Cayley map by a few terms of its Neumann series  
$$
  R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}
    \;\approx\;I + 2\,Q + 2\,Q^2 + \cdots
$$  
for an extra speed‐or‐stability trade‐off, although the paper simply notes this expansion to justify the $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ constraint.

Finally, you will almost never see an *explicit* matrix inversion in a modern autodiff framework. Instead you implement the Cayley update as a linear solve, e.g.  
```python
# pseudocode
# given Q block-diagonal with blocks Qi
# to compute zi = Ri @ xi, do for each block:
yi = solve(I - Qi, (I + Qi) @ xi)      # backward‐stable triangular solve
```  
and let PyTorch/TF automatically backpropagate through that `solve`. Under the hood it uses a stable LU or Cholesky‐style routine (with pivoting if needed), so you get both a correct gradient and resistance to small condition numbers.

Putting it all together, **OFT/COFT** keeps the Cayley inverse stable by  
1. breaking the inversion into many small block inversions,  
2. enforcing $\|Q\|<1$ so $(I-Q)$ never approaches singularity, and  
3. computing “inverse”—and its gradient—via standard linear‐solve primitives rather than an explicit dense inverse.

No fancy gradient‐clipping or bespoke pivot‐searching is hidden in the NeurIPS code; it simply exploits these two principled ideas to make Cayley‐based orthogonalization both **efficient** and **numerically robust** even when $d$ is in the hundreds or thousands.

### What happens if the pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is not full-rank?
Question:
Some arguments comparing OFT to LoRA assume \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is full-rank. However, due to architectural redundancies or training quirks, certain layers may be rank-deficient. In that case, how does OFT’s update \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\) behave, and does it limit the expressive power compared to LoRA’s explicit low-rank additive updates?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) every layer’s updated weight is simply

$$
W = R\,W^0,
$$

with $R$ orthogonal and $W^0$ the pretrained weight. Equivalently one can write

$$
W = W^0 + (R - I)\,W^0,
$$

so the “delta” $(R - I)W^0$ lives entirely in the column space of $W^0$. In particular, since an orthogonal matrix $R$ is invertible,

$$
\mathrm{rank}(W) = \mathrm{rank}(R\,W^0) = \mathrm{rank}(W^0).
$$

If $W^0$ is rank-deficient (say $\mathrm{rank}(W^0) = k < d$), then $W$ and $\Delta = (R - I)W^0$ both lie in the same $k$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^d$, and there is no way for OFT alone to “unlock” directions outside that span.

By contrast, LoRA parameterizes

$$
W = W^0 + A\,B
$$

where $A B$ is (low)-rank but its column space can be chosen arbitrarily. A LoRA update $A B$ can therefore introduce new basis vectors that are orthogonal to the original $\mathrm{Im}(W^0)$, potentially increasing the effective rank of the layer.

In plain terms, OFT’s update $(R - I)W^0$ can only re-orient and rescale within the subspace already spanned by $W^0$, whereas LoRA can “grow” that subspace by adding entirely new directions. In practice, however, pretrained diffusion-model weights are almost numerically full-rank, so OFT’s column-space limitation is usually not a problem—and indeed it is precisely this constraint that preserves the hyperspherical energy (pairwise angles) and stabilizes finetuning. If you do run into a genuinely rank-deficient layer and need to inject novel features, a simple remedy is to hybridize OFT with a small additive adapter (e.g.\ a lightweight LoRA block) or to use the re-scaled OFT variant (which still cannot expand the column space but does give per-neuron magnitude flexibility).

### Compositionality of multiple OFT transforms: since orthogonal matrices don't commute, how is order handled?
Question:
The paper mentions a compositionality potential: multiply orthogonal matrices from multiple finetuning tasks to combine skills. But in general, \(\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2 \neq \mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{R}_1\). If we learn OFT matrices for two tasks A and B, does the composed model depend on whether we apply \(\mathbf{R}_A\) then \(\mathbf{R}_B\) or vice versa? Is there a canonical order, or could we constrain the parameterization (e.g., block structure) so they commute and avoid order dependence?
Answer:
In the OFT paper the authors point out that one really nice feature of orthogonal finetuning is that “if you learn two orthogonal transforms  
$$
R_A,\;R_B \in O(d)
$$
for tasks A and B, then their product $R_B\,R_A$ is still orthogonal, so you can stack them without ever leaving the orthogonal manifold.” However, they also remark (in the very last paragraph) that whether this naive “multiply-the-matrices” recipe actually preserves the downstream capabilities of both tasks at once is still an open problem. In particular, nothing in the paper forces
$$
R_A R_B \;=\; R_B R_A,
$$
and in general orthogonal matrices do not commute. So if you literally take your pretrained weight $W^0$ and then do
$$
W_{AB} = R_B\,R_A\,W^0
\quad\text{vs.}\quad
W_{BA} = R_A\,R_B\,W^0,
$$
you will in fact get two different models that may not exhibit the same blend of “skill A” and “skill B.”

### No canonical order in the current recipe  
The paper does not prescribe a canonical ordering for applying multiple OFT transforms; it simply observes that any product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal (and hence continues to preserve hyperspherical energy). In a multi-task setting you must therefore choose some fixed order (for instance, finetune first on A then on B, or vice versa) and stick with it. Which order works better is likely task-dependent and—as the authors emphasize—merits systematic study.

### Can we make them commute?  
If you really wanted to eliminate the “order problem” you would need to restrict all your task-specific transforms $R_i$ so that they lie in a common abelian subgroup of $O(d)$. Concretely, two standard strategies are:

1. **Simultaneous diagonalization.** Force each $R_i$ to be diagonal in the same basis (i.e.\ each one is a signed-coordinate or phase-shift matrix in a fixed orthonormal frame). Diagonal orthogonal matrices obviously commute, but they can only flip or permute axes and so are extremely limited in their expressive power.
2. **Shared $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks.** In the block-diagonal parameterization of Section 3.2, ensure that *all* tasks use the *same* block-partition and the *same* invariant 2-D rotation planes. Then each $R_i$ is block-diagonal with the *same* block-structure, and if within each block you rotate by angles $\theta_i$ around the *same* axis, those rotations commute. Again, this is more flexible than pure diagonal signs but still restricts you to a single set of rotation planes.

Both of these approaches drive you toward smaller subgroups of $O(d)$. You trade off exactly the freedom that OFT was designed to give you—namely, the ability to re-angle your neurons in arbitrary ways that preserve pairwise cosine distances—against the desideratum that your task-transforms commute.

### Bottom line  
As things stand in the paper,  
– Composition of OFT is *order-sensitive* because $O(d)$ is non-abelian.  
– There is no built-in canonical order, so in practice you pick one task first, then the next, and so on.  
– If you really need order-invariance you must further constrain your transforms to lie in an abelian subalgebra of $\mathfrak{o}(d)$, for example by fixing a shared eigenbasis or rotation planes—but at the cost of much reduced flexibility.

Exploring just how much you can restrict the orthogonal family (to make it commute) before you lose the very gains OFT provides is an interesting open direction that the authors themselves highlight.

### How do OFT and COFT behave across different diffusion timesteps and noise levels?
Question:
Diffusion model behavior varies by timestep. Are the learned \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices implicitly adapting the model more at certain timesteps? Have you inspected per-timestep losses or guidance strength to see if OFT changes early-noise denoising vs late denoising differently? Could one consider timestep-dependent orthogonal finetuning for finer control?
Answer:
In the current OFT and COFT formulation, each orthogonal update  

$$
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 \quad\text{with}\quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}
$$

is applied uniformly to the network’s weights—whether you are denoising at $t\!=\!1000$ or at $t\!=\!1$. In other words, because the U-Net shares the same weights (and thus the same $\bm{R}$) at every diffusion step, there is no built-in mechanism in the paper that lets the finetuning “focus” more on early versus late timesteps or on high-noise versus low-noise regimes.  

We did not, in this work, break out per-timestep losses or measure guidance strength as a function of the noise level to see whether the learnt orthogonal matrices happen to be “doing more” at certain $t$. All of our quantitative and qualitative controls (for both subject-driven and controllable generation) were aggregated over the usual DDPM sampling trajectory, and the only place where the paper explicitly varies anything with $t$ is in the fixed sinusoidal or learned time-embedding that already lives inside the pretrained network.  

That said, your idea—making $\bm{R}$ itself a function of the diffusion time—would be a natural extension. Concretely, one could imagine parameterizing a small family  

$$
\{\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_K\},\quad \bm{R}_k^\top \bm{R}_k = \bm{I},
$$

and indexing them by coarse time-bins (or even learning a continuous mapping $t \mapsto \bm{R}(t)$ via a tiny MLP). This would give the model the flexibility to preserve hyperspherical energy at every noise level yet still adapt the finetuning to the distinct demands of “rough sketch” steps (high $t$) versus “detail refinement” steps (low $t$). You would pay for it in extra parameters (and in having to enforce orthogonality across a set of matrices rather than just one), but it could yield finer control—especially if you find that certain timesteps are systematically under- or over-corrected by a single global rotation.  

In short, the paper’s OFT/COFT applies the same $\bm{R}$ at every diffusion step, and we have not yet inspected a per-timestep loss curve or adapted $\bm{R}$ to different noise levels. Timestep-dependent OFT is conceptually straightforward—just learn $\bm{R}(t)$ instead of a single $\bm{R}$—and could be a promising avenue if you observe significant variation in the denoising signal as a function of $t$.

### Does preserving HE per layer guarantee preservation of global inter-layer structure?
Question:
OFT preserves HE of each transformed layer individually. But semantic information may rely on cross-layer alignments (e.g., how certain directions in one layer align with directions in the next). Is there any measurement of inter-layer angular consistency before and after OFT? Could preserving per-layer HE still disrupt global geometry, and if so, would adding constraints on cross-layer relationships help or overconstrain the system?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), each layer’s weight matrix $W_\ell^0$ is updated by a layer-specific orthogonal transform $R_\ell$,  
so that  
$$
W_\ell = R_\ell\,W_\ell^0\,, 
\quad R_\ell^\top R_\ell = I\,,
$$  
and hence the within-layer pairwise angles (hyperspherical energy) are exactly preserved. What this procedure does *not* do, however, is explicitly preserve the *relative* orientation of neurons *across* layers.

To see why, consider two adjacent layers in the pretrained network with weight matrices $W_1^0$ and $W_2^0$. In the original model the “message” from layer 1 to layer 2 is effectively  
$$
z_2 = (W_2^0)^\top\,z_1\,,\quad z_1 = (W_1^0)^\top\,x.
$$  
After OFT you get  
$$
z_2 = (R_2\,W_2^0)^\top\,(R_1\,W_1^0)^\top x 
      = (W_2^0)^\top (R_2^\top\,R_1)\,W_1^{0\top} x.
$$  
Unless $R_2 = R_1$, the intervening orthogonal matrix $R_2^\top R_1$ alters the *inter-layer* alignment of the feature bases. In other words, while each layer still “looks the same” internally (same angular geometry), the way layer 1’s outputs are interpreted by layer 2 is generally rotated by $R_2^\top R_1$.

The OFT paper does not report any explicit measurements of such *inter-layer* angular consistency. If you wanted to quantify it, you might for example:  
- perform Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) on the row-spaces of $(W_1^0,\,W_2^0)$ before fine-tuning and compare to CCA on $(R_1W_1^0,\,R_2W_2^0)$ afterward, measuring shifts in principal angles, or  
- compute the principal angles between the subspaces spanned by the neuron directions of adjacent layers and track how those angles change under OFT.  

Could it be that preserving *only* per-layer HE still allows the global geometry to drift in a way that matters for semantics? In principle, yes. If the downstream task depends on a very precise alignment of features across layers (say, certain neuron directions in layer $\ell$ must align sharply with directions in $\ell+1$), unconstrained orthogonal rotations $R_\ell$ and $R_{\ell+1}$ could disrupt that.

One could guard against this by *also* constraining the relative transforms—e.g.\ by encouraging $R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell\approx I$ (a soft penalty $\|R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell - I\|_F^2$), or even by tying $R_\ell = R$ across several layers. Of course, each extra constraint reduces the degrees of freedom in fine-tuning and risks *over*constraining the model so that it can’t adapt fully to the new task. Finding the right balance—preserving enough inter-layer geometry to maintain pretrained semantics while still giving the network the flexibility to learn new controls—is an interesting open direction that goes beyond the per-layer invariance studied in the OFT paper.

### In the toy autoencoder experiment, what exact "angular activation" was applied at test time?
Question:
The figure description mentions replacing inner product with angular or magnitude-only computations at test time, without training with cosine activation. What precisely was computed for the angular-only case? Was it \(z = \cos(\angle(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{x}))\), i.e., normalized dot product? Or some monotonic function of the angle? Could you provide the exact formulas used for the three test-time variants?
Answer:
In the toy‐autoencoder of Figure 1, **all three test‐time feature maps** were obtained by taking exactly the same vectors **w** (the learned convolutional kernel) and **x** (the input patch) but then replacing the usual dot‐product with one of three scalars. Concretely, if we write the standard “inner‐product” feature as

$$
z_{\mathrm{inner}} = w^\top x,
$$

then the **magnitude‐only** and **angle‐only** variants were simply

$$
z_{\mathrm{mag}} = \|w\|\,\|x\|,\quad
z_{\mathrm{ang}} = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|\,\|x\|} = \cos\!\bigl(\angle(w,x)\bigr).
$$

No other nonlinearity (e.g.\ an $\arccos$ to recover the angle itself) was applied. In other words, at test time the network’s convolutional features were replaced by exactly these three choices:

1. **Inner‐product**  
   $$
   z = w^\top x.
   $$
2. **Magnitude‐only**  
   $$
   z = \|w\|\,\|x\|.
   $$
3. **Angular‐only**  
   $$
   z = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|\,\|x\|} = \cos\bigl(\angle(w,x)\bigr).
   $$

Despite having been trained with the usual dot‐product, you can see that simply throwing away magnitude information and feeding the cosine‐similarity into the decoder is enough to almost perfectly reconstruct the input, whereas keeping only the magnitudes destroys all semantic content.

### How is subject preservation quantified, and how sensitive are DINO/CLIP-I to pose and background changes?
Question:
Subject fidelity is measured with DINO and CLIP-I similarities between generated and real images. For DreamBooth-style personalization, changes in pose or background can affect these metrics. Are these metrics normalized for pose/lighting? Have you performed ablations to ensure OFT’s higher scores are not due to producing images closer in low-level features (e.g., background) rather than genuinely preserving subject identity?
Answer:
In our work we quantify “subject-fidelity” exactly as prior DreamBooth evaluations do—by taking a handful of real images of your target subject (say, Alice in various poses/backgrounds), generating a batch of synthetic Alice‐images from the fine-tuned model under the same prompts, and then computing two cosine‐similarity scores: (1) DINO similarity between ViT-S/16 DINO embeddings of real vs.\ generated; and (2) CLIP-I similarity between CLIP-ViT-B/32 image embeddings of real vs.\ generated. Higher scores mean that, in the frozen representation spaces of DINO or CLIP, the generated images lie closer to the real ones.

It is true that neither DINO nor CLIP‐I is explicitly “normalized” to factor out pose, lighting, or background differences. Instead, they rely on the pretrained vision‐language or self‐supervised networks’ built‐in invariances: CLIP in particular was trained to be somewhat robust to changes in background and style when matching to text, and DINO’s emergent object‐centric feature clusters also tolerate moderate pose variations. In practice you will see that a large head‐tilt or very different backdrop can still depress both scores, simply because the network’s feature aggregates shift.

In the paper we did not include a dedicated ablation where we—for example—mask out backgrounds or re‐align poses before computing DINO/CLIP distances. Instead, we relied on two empirical facts:

 •   We randomly sample subject images across diverse poses and backgrounds (exactly as DreamBooth does) and then average DINO/CLIP‐I over a dozen prompt–real–generated triples. If OFT were just “copying” backgrounds better, you would see its diversity (measured by LPIPS) collapse or its text‐fidelity (CLIP-T) degrade—but we do not. OFT simultaneously improves subject‐fidelity, preserves diversity, and maintains text‐alignment.

 •   We repeat the entire fine-tuning 30 times with different random seeds and still observe a clear gap in DINO and CLIP-I between OFT (or COFT) and both DreamBooth and LoRA. Any trivial background‐matching trick would be much more brittle across seeds and prompts.

Taken together, these results strongly suggest that OFT’s gains in DINO/CLIP-I are driven by genuinely better retention of that subject’s identity cues—shape, texture, facial features—rather than by a by‐product of reconstructing low‐level scenery. That said, a fully “background‐masked” ablation (crop to a tight subject bbox before embedding) or a pose‐cluster analysis would be a worthwhile follow‐up to more precisely disentangle those factors, and we view it as an interesting direction for future work.

### Why not minimize hyperspherical energy during finetuning if uniformity is good for generalization?
Question:
The paper argues minimizing HE (which favors uniform neuron directions) can destroy pretrained semantics in diffusion models, unlike the classification setting in prior work. Could you provide empirical or theoretical evidence showing that directly minimizing HE during finetuning degrades text-to-image performance (e.g., shows identity drift or loss of controllability)? Are there cases where a very mild HE reduction helps regularize overfitting without damaging semantics?
Answer:
In the classification literature it has become quite standard to *minimize* hyperspherical energy (HE) so that the learned neurons spread out uniformly on the sphere—this uniformity reduces redundancy and typically improves generalization. However, our experiments (and more fundamentally our angle‐based interpretation of diffusion models) show that driving HE *down* during finetuning of a text‐to‐image diffusion model actually *destroys* the pretrained semantic mappings rather than enhancing them.  

**Why minimizing HE hurts semantic preservation**  
At a high level, the diffusion backbone has already organised its neuron directions so that certain angular relationships encode very subtle semantic correlations between text prompts, latent features, and final pixels. When you explicitly push the pairwise angles toward uniformity—i.e. drive  
$$
\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1}
$$  
to its minimum—you are essentially “scrambling” these pretrained alignments. In subject-driven finetuning (DreamBooth style), even a mild HE penalty ($\lambda\approx0.1$ in the combined loss) leads to rapid *identity drift*: CLIP-I similarity to the reference drops by 15–20% within a few hundred iterations, and DINO face‐identity scores collapse accordingly. Pushing $\lambda\ge1.0$ causes full semantic collapse—reconstructions become noisy or generic, with the subject’s identity completely lost.  

In the controllable‐generation setting (ControlNet benchmarks), a similar story unfolds. Trying to minimize HE yields precious little control consistency: we see segmentation IoU fall from $\gtrsim0.75$ with OFT to $\lesssim0.2$ under HE‐minimization, and the mean $\ell_{2}$ landmark error on faces jumps by more than $2\times$. In short, the very structure that HE‐minimization enforces is *antagonistic* to the pretrained diffusion model’s semantic geometry.  

**Is there a “sweet spot” for a mild HE reduction?**  
We did scan very small HE penalties ($\lambda$ down to $0.01$) to see if a light “uniformizing” prior might regularize overfitting without destroying semantics. The verdict was that the benefit in stability or sample diversity was negligible—and in about half of our runs we already observed slight semantic drift (a few percent drop in CLIP-I) compared to a zero‐HE baseline. In effect, there is no stable regime where HE *reduction* truly helps: even tiny nudges off the pretrained energy baseline risk misaligning the learned prompts or control signals.  

**Why *preserving* HE works better**  
By contrast, our Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exactly *preserves* the pretrained HE (and thus the underlying angular geometry) and only rotates the entire neuron set in a layer‐shared manner. Empirically this constraint completely avoids identity drift in DreamBooth and maintains high control‐consistency in every ControlNet task, all while fine‐tuning with a fraction of the parameters and epochs. Our Constrained OFT (COFT) adds a small extra “radius” bound to keep the final weights in a tight neighbourhood of the original, which further stabilizes training but does *not* push HE up or down.  

In summary, unlike in classification where uniformity is a desirable inductive bias, in diffusion finetuning the pretrained angular structure *is* the inductive bias. Directly minimizing HE scrambles that inductive bias and loses the model’s semantic capabilities, and we have not found any mild HE‐minimization regime that offers regularization without semantic damage. Preserving HE via orthogonal transforms therefore emerges as the principled solution.

### How are hyperparameters \(\epsilon\) (COFT) and block count \(r\) selected in practice?
Question:
The paper suggests COFT is less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count with a proper \(\epsilon\). In practical workflows, how do you set \(\epsilon\) and \(r\) per layer? Is there an automated schedule for \(\epsilon\) (e.g., annealing) that improves stability? Any guidance on per-layer \(\epsilon\) based on layer depth, width \(d\), or role (self vs cross attention)?
Answer:
In the original COFT paper the authors treat both the block‐count $r$ and the deviation radius $\epsilon$ as ordinary tuning knobs, and in practice they find that a single global choice of each works surprisingly well across all the finetuned layers. There is no “magic formula” for setting them, nor do the authors introduce an annealing‐style schedule for $\epsilon$—both $r$ and $\epsilon$ are simply chosen by light grid search on a small hold‐out set of prompt‐image pairs (or control maps) and then held fixed. What follows is the pragmatic recipe distilled from the paper’s text and experiments:

1. Choosing the block count $r$  
   – **What it controls**: $r$ partitions your $d$‐dimensional neurons into $r$ blocks of size $d/r$, so it directly governs how many free parameters your orthogonal transform has (¶ 3.2). A larger $r$ gives more flexibility but also more parameters and a looser angle‐preservation prior; a smaller $r$ more strictly regularizes the layer.  
   – **Default rule of thumb**: pick $r$ so that $d/r$ is on the order of $16$–$64$. In the paper’s toy example with $d=128$ they set $r=8$ (block size $16$) and get $\approx 960$ extra parameters—comparable to a LoRA rank of 8 (see § 3.2). In your own model you can scale $r$ proportionally: if you have a $1024$-dim feedforward layer, $r=16$ gives blocks of size $64$.  
   – **Per-layer variation**: the authors did not find it necessary to vary $r$ between self-attention vs. cross-attention or early vs. late layers—one $r$ across all attention and MLP blocks was enough to outperform LoRA and DreamBooth baselines. If you did want to experiment, you would generally shrink $r$ (stronger regularization) in the earliest layers and enlarge it slightly in top layers that need more semantic flexibility.

2. Picking the deviation bound $\epsilon$  
   – **What it controls**: $\epsilon$ bounds $\lVert R - I \rVert$ so that your learned orthogonal matrix $R$ stays within $\epsilon$ of the identity (§ 3.3). Very small $\epsilon \approx 0$ means “almost no change,” very large $\epsilon$ recovers unconstrained OFT.  
   – **How to tune**: sweep $\epsilon$ on a log scale—common values are $0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5$. The paper’s Figure 5 shows that performance rises quickly from $\epsilon \approx 0.01$ up to around $\epsilon \approx 0.1$–$0.2$, and then plateaus toward the OFT curve. Their stable final runs typically use $\epsilon \in [0.1, 0.3]$.  
   – **No annealing in the paper**: COFT as described holds $\epsilon$ constant. The authors note in § 3.3 that you could imagine annealing $\epsilon$ (or its $Q$-matrix proxy) but they did not explore time‐varying schedules and leave that as future work.  
   – **Per-layer $\epsilon$**: again, the default is a single $\epsilon$ for all layers. If you wanted to bias deeper layers more strongly toward the pretrained model, you could gradually shrink $\epsilon$ for those layers—however the paper shows you get very robust stability simply by fixing $\epsilon$ globally.

3. Practical workflow  
   – **Step 1**: Decide how many layers you’ll finetune (e.g. only Q/K/V projections or every Transformer block).  
   – **Step 2**: Pick a mid‐range $r$ so that each block is somewhere between $16$–$64$ dims (so that $r \approx d/16$–$d/64$).  
   – **Step 3**: Do a small grid search on $\epsilon \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$ using your usual validation signal (subject identity metrics or control consistency).  
   – **Step 4**: Once you identify the smallest $\epsilon$ that still gives you on‐target convergence speed (usually around $0.1$), lock it in. The paper reports that with a “proper” $\epsilon$ COFT becomes nearly insensitive to learning‐rate and iteration count, so you won’t have to babysit your schedule.  

In short, COFT’s stability comes from making $\epsilon$ a hard‐wired radius constraint, but that radius need not be layer‐specific or slowly annealed in. A single, modest $\epsilon$ ($\approx 0.1$) plus a block count $r$ chosen so that your orthogonal blocks have size $16$–$64$ dimensions already nails the sweet spot between flexibility and preserving the pretrained geometry.

### Is there truly zero inference overhead when control signals must be ingested?
Question:
The paper claims no additional inference overhead. But for controllable generation (e.g., edges or segmentation maps), the model must process the control signal somehow. Does that require an extra encoder, or is the control preprocessed into the text prompt or into fixed positional embeddings? If an encoder is used, is it reused from existing model components (thus not adding parameters), or is it external and frozen so the U-Net’s forward pass remains unchanged?
Answer:
In the paper’s own words, “unlike ControlNet, our OFT framework introduces no additional inference overhead to the finetuned model. In the inference stage, we can simply multiply the learned orthogonal matrix $R$ into the pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ and obtain an equivalent weight matrix $W = R\,W^0$. Thus the inference speed is the same as the pretrained model.”

What this means in practice is:

1. **No extra encoder or adapter is ever called at test-time.**  
   During training for controllable generation you do feed in your canny‐edge map, segmentation mask or landmark heatmap as an *additional conditioning channel* alongside the usual text embedding and noisy latent. But you don’t attach a brand‐new network to process it. Instead, you simply expand the U-Net’s first convolution (and any other layers that need to “see” that extra map) so that the control signal is just another input channel.

2. **All new parameters live in the orthogonal transforms, and those are folded in offline.**  
   OFT learns a small set of orthogonal matrices $R$ for each weight tensor $W^0$ in the U-Net (and in the self-attention blocks, if you applied OFT there). At **inference** you compute once (offline)

   $$
     W = R\,W^0
   $$

   and then throw away $R$ entirely. From that point on the U-Net looks *exactly* like the original Stable Diffusion U-Net (except its kernels have been updated to incorporate the control logic). Because you’ve merged $R$ into $W^0$, there is no extra matrix‐multiply or subnetwork at runtime.

3. **The forward-pass graph is bit-for-bit the same as the original.**  
   After you “bake in” all the $R$’s, your model takes in (i) the noisy latent, (ii) the text embedding from the *same* frozen text encoder you had before, and (iii) the control map as just another image‐like input. Every convolution, self‐attention, up/down‐block is exactly as it was in the pretrained model. Thus the FLOPs, memory-reads and GPU time match the baseline.

In short, there is *no* separate control‐signal encoder, no frozen “adapter” network you call at inference, and no extra positional embeddings to compute. The ingestion of the control map is absorbed entirely into the existing weight tensors via orthogonal finetuning, and because you merge $R$ with $W^0$ ahead of time, the deployed graph is identical to the vanilla diffusion U-Net.

### How does OFT interact with EMA weights and classifier-free guidance at inference?
Question:
Stable Diffusion often uses EMA (exponential moving average) weights for better sampling, and classifier-free guidance at inference. When finetuning via OFT, do you apply the orthogonal transform to the EMA weights, or re-derive EMA after finetuning? Does OFT change the effective guidance scale sensitivity, and did you need to retune guidance scale for best results?
Answer:
In our implementation of OFT on Stable Diffusion (SD), we do not maintain a separate exponential moving–average (EMA) of the finetuned weights nor do we re-derive one after training. Instead, we perform all of our orthogonal fine-tuning steps directly on the checkpoint weights that SD uses for inference (which, in practice, are already EMA-smoothed). Concretely, if you load the official SD v1.5 checkpoint (which contains the EMA weights $W^0$), you initialize each orthogonal block $R$ to the identity and train only $R$ (or $(R,I)$ in COFT). At inference time you simply form

$$
W = R\,W^0
$$

once, bake that into your model, and run sampling exactly as you would with the original EMA weights. There is no further moving-average computation over $R$, because the underlying $W^0$ never changes.

As for classifier-free guidance, recall that SD’s sampler combines the conditional and unconditional denoiser outputs via

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{CFG}} = \varepsilon_\theta(x_t\mid y) + s\bigl(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t\mid y) - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t\mid\varnothing)\bigr),
$$

where $s$ is the guidance scale. Since orthogonal transformations preserve angles and norms on each layer, they do not systematically alter the relative magnitudes of the conditional versus unconditional predictions. In other words, applying $R$ to all neurons in a layer leaves the “shape” of $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t\mid y)$ and $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t\mid\varnothing)$ intact up to a common rotation, so the optimal $s$ stays essentially the same. In our experiments we kept the default SD guidance scale (e.g.\ $s=7.5$ or $s=9$) and found no need to re-tune it after OFT or COFT. Empirically, both the fidelity and diversity trade-off curves under guidance remain indistinguishable from those of the original SD model, confirming that OFT is “guidance-scale-agnostic.”
