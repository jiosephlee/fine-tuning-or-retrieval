In the paper “Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”, the constrained variant COFT enforces $\| \bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ so that the finetuned weights lie within a hypersphere of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, which geometrically primarily bounds the $\ell_2$ norm $\| \bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2$, i.e. the Euclidean distance. This follows directly from the COFT forward parameterization $\bm{M}=\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with identity initialization of $\bm{R}$. Writing the deviation as a product gives
$\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0=(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, so by submultiplicativity of the operator norm,
$\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2\le \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_2\,\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$. Thus, an explicit bound on $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_2$ translates into an explicit Euclidean bound on the distance between the finetuned and pretrained weight matrices, scaled by the (fixed) spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$. The same reasoning applies column-wise: for each neuron $\bm{w}_i=\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$, we have $\|\bm{w}_i-\bm{w}_i^0\|_2=\|(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{w}_i^0\|_2\le \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_2\,\|\bm{w}_i^0\|_2$, meaning every neuron lies within a Euclidean ball around its pretrained counterpart, and consequently the entire matrix lies within a norm ball (hypersphere) around $\bm{M}^0$.

In geometric terms, the COFT constraint makes $\bm{R}$ a “small rotation” around the identity, so it limits the angular deviation while exactly preserving pairwise angles (and hence hyperspherical energy) across neurons. Under the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, the Neumann approximation yields $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, and the paper enforces $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ via projection; this is equivalent to keeping $\bm{R}$ near $\bm{I}$, hence bounding $\|(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\|$ and, by the argument above, $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2$. Because $\bm{R}$ acts uniformly on all columns, each pretrained neuron vector is perturbed by a bounded Euclidean amount while their pairwise angles remain invariant, so COFT simultaneously enforces small Euclidean deviation and semantic structure preservation—exactly the stated “hypersphere of radius $\epsilon$” intuition for the parameter space.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors explain that OFT achieves parameter efficiency by exploiting a sparsity-inducing structure in its orthogonal parameterization, namely block-diagonal orthogonality. Concretely, instead of learning a dense orthogonal transform \( \bm{R} \in \text{O}(d) \) with \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters, OFT parameterizes \( \bm{R} \) as a block-diagonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),\quad \bm{R}_i \in \text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),
\]
so that the total degrees of freedom drop from \(d(d-1)/2\) to \(d\!\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)/2\), i.e., from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\). If the blocks are further shared, \(\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j\) for all \(i \neq j\), the effective parameter count becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\). This sparsity arises from the structural constraint that each block transforms a disjoint subspace of the neuron dimension, eliminating cross-subspace parameters while maintaining orthogonality within each block. Crucially, because each \(\bm{R}_i\) is orthogonal, the full \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal, so OFT still provably preserves pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy even under this sparse parameterization.

This design contrasts with LoRA’s parameter efficiency, which relies on low-rank additive updates, \(\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'\), yielding \(r'(d+n)\) parameters and exploiting a low-rank structure. OFT instead keeps the update multiplicative and full-rank in each block while achieving efficiency through sparsity of the transform itself. Under comparable scaling (e.g., \(r = \alpha d\)), OFT’s complexity reduces to \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) for fixed \(\alpha\), whereas LoRA’s remains \(\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)\). Moreover, the block-diagonal constraint has natural interpretations in convolutional layers—each block can operate per channel or be shared across channels—preserving the semantic geometry (hyperspherical energy) while limiting degrees of freedom to stabilize finetuning. Thus, the parameter efficiency of OFT emerges from a principled sparsity pattern in the orthogonal parameterization, not from rank reduction, allowing efficient, stable adaptation with no sacrifice to the invariance properties that safeguard pretrained semantics.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', if you remove the block-diagonal parameterization (set r=1), the parameter complexity of R becomes O(d^2). This follows directly from the dimensionality of the full orthogonal group and the chosen parameterization: an unconstrained orthogonal matrix R in dimension d has d(d−1)/2 degrees of freedom, which is quadratic in d. Under the Cayley parameterization R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^{\top}), the independent parameters are exactly the upper-triangular entries of Q, again totaling d(d−1)/2. Thus, when r=1 (i.e., no block structure), one must learn all these degrees of freedom, yielding parameter complexity Θ(d^2), summarized in the paper as O(d^2).

By contrast, the block-diagonal parameterization reduces the effective dimension by partitioning R into r independent blocks of size d/r, each with (d/r)((d/r)−1)/2 parameters. Summing over r blocks gives r · (d/r)((d/r)−1)/2 = d(d/r − 1)/2, which scales as O(d^2/r); with optional block sharing it further drops to O(d^2/r^2). Therefore, the block structure is precisely the knob that trades off flexibility and parameter efficiency while preserving orthogonality—and hence hyperspherical energy. Setting r=1 removes this structural sparsity, reverting to the full expressivity of O(d) and its quadratic parameter count, consistent with the paper’s efficiency analysis.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors state that when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution. This is sensible because a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix imposes no cross-channel interaction: if we write the OFT transform as R = diag(R_1, R_2, ..., R_{C_{\mathrm{in}}}) with C_{\mathrm{in}} blocks, then each block R_c acts only on the subspace associated with the c-th input channel. For a convolutional layer with weights W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}} \times C_{\mathrm{in}} \times k \times k}, vectorizing each k\times k kernel for a fixed input channel c and output channel o into \bm{w}^0_{o,c} \in \mathbb{R}^{k^2}, OFT applies W'_{o,c} = \mathrm{reshape}(R_c \,\mathrm{vec}(W^0_{o,c})). Because R is block-diagonal across input channels, there is no transformation that mixes \mathrm{vec}(W^0_{o,c}) with \mathrm{vec}(W^0_{o,c'}) for c \neq c', so the operation is channel-wise. This mirrors depth-wise convolution, where each input channel is convolved with its own kernel independently, i.e., without cross-channel mixing.

To see the analogy more concretely, consider the forward pass. A standard convolution computes for each output channel o: z_o = \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}} (W^0_{o,c} * X_c), where X_c is the c-th input feature map and * denotes spatial convolution. Under OFT, the transformed weights yield z_o = \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}} ((R_c W^0_{o,c}) * X_c). Since R_c \in \mathrm{O}(k^2) acts only on the k^2-dimensional spatial kernel of channel c, each input channel’s spatial filtering is rotated/reflected independently on the kernel hypersphere, preserving its pairwise angular structure while not introducing any cross-channel coupling. This is precisely the structural property of depth-wise convolution: per-channel spatial filtering without channel mixing. If one later adds pointwise (1\times 1) mixing or shares blocks, OFT can emulate separable convolution patterns, but with C_{\mathrm{in}} blocks and no sharing, the transform is strictly depth-wise in its effect.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors note that Cayley parametrization requires a costly computational operation that limits scalability — the matrix inverse. This follows directly from their choice to enforce orthogonality via the Cayley transform, parameterizing the layer-shared transform as R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with a skew-symmetric Q satisfying Q = -Q^{\top}. Maintaining exact orthogonality throughout training means recomputing (I - Q)^{-1} whenever Q changes, and differentiating through that inverse as well. For a d × d transform, a dense matrix inverse has cubic time complexity, roughly O(d^{3}), and incurs substantial memory overhead; even if implemented via solving linear systems, the asymptotic cost remains tied to the same bottleneck. Since OFT places such orthogonal transforms at the level of attention and convolutional layers in large diffusion models where d can be in the hundreds or thousands, repeatedly forming (I - Q)^{-1} at every training step becomes a nontrivial computational burden.

The paper’s design and empirical sections explicitly acknowledge and mitigate this limitation. First, they justify Cayley over alternatives like Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization, which, while differentiable, is “often too expensive” in practice; Cayley is the more efficient of the two but still hinges on the inverse. Second, they introduce a block-diagonal structure for R: R = \mathrm{diag}(R_{1}, \dots, R_{r}) with each block R_{i} \in \mathrm{O}(d/r), which reduces both parameter count and inverse size, lowering the per-step cost from O(d^{3}) to roughly O((d/r)^{3}) per block and enabling further sharing to push complexity toward O(d^{2}/r^{2}) in parameters. They also use a Neumann-series perspective, R ≈ I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^{2}), to move the COFT deviation constraint inside the Cayley parameter (i.e., \|Q\| \le \epsilon'), but that approximation is for constraint handling rather than replacing the inverse needed for exact orthogonality. Hence, while Cayley is the practical choice for enforcing R^{\top}R = I, the necessity of computing (I - Q)^{-1} is precisely why the authors state it slightly limits OFT’s scalability and motivate their structural efficiency tricks.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', applying block sharing (making blocks identical) is described as primarily increasing the resulting models' bias. This follows directly from their efficient orthogonal parameterization, where the layer-wise transform is constrained to a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, i.e., R = diag(R_1, ..., R_r) with each block R_i ∈ O(d/r). When block sharing is imposed, all blocks are tied, R_i = R_j for all i ≠ j, collapsing a set of independent transforms into a single shared transform. Mathematically, this reduces the degrees of freedom from r independent orthogonal blocks to one, cutting the parameter complexity from O(d^2/r) to O(d^2/r^2). While this improves parameter efficiency, it forces identical rotations/reflections on all subspaces or channels that those blocks act on, thereby injecting a strong inductive bias: the model is constrained to treat all these subspaces homogeneously rather than adaptively tailoring transformations to their distinct semantics.

The paper explicitly notes that the block-diagonal structure “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility,” and block sharing intensifies that effect. In convolutional layers, for instance, choosing r equal to the number of input channels lets each block transform a unique channel; sharing makes the transform invariant across channels, biasing the model toward channel-wise equivariance. Although hyperspherical energy is still preserved under any orthogonal R (so pairwise angles among neurons remain unchanged), tying blocks restricts the set of admissible orthogonal transforms to a lower-dimensional manifold. This restriction reduces the model’s capacity to fine-tune neuron directions differently where needed, trading expressive flexibility for regularization. In short, identical blocks enforce a global angular adjustment across multiple neuron groups, which is a prototypical source of architectural bias and explains why block sharing is described as primarily increasing bias in the resulting models.
