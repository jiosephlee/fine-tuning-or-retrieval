According to the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the parameter $\epsilon$ in COFT primarily controls flexibility. In Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), the finetuned layer applies an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ to the pretrained weights, but additionally enforces a deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$ in the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. This “radius” constraint bounds how far the learned rotation/reflection can move the pretrained neuron directions on the hypersphere. When $\epsilon$ is small, $\bm{R}$ must lie close to $\bm{I}$, so neuron directions can only change slightly; the model remains near the pretrained solution, preserving semantics but limiting the capacity to adapt—i.e., low flexibility. As $\epsilon$ grows, the feasible set for $\bm{R}$ expands, allowing larger angular deviations of neurons while still preserving pairwise angles (and thus hyperspherical energy), which increases the model’s capacity to adjust to downstream signals—i.e., higher flexibility. The paper explicitly observes this behavior: larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT resemble OFT (unconstrained orthogonal finetuning), while smaller $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave increasingly like the original pretrained model.

This interpretation is reinforced by the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, and its Neumann-series approximation $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. Under this parameterization, the deviation constraint transfers to the skew-symmetric parameter with $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$, so the magnitude of $\bm{Q}$—which determines the size of the rotation on the hypersphere—directly sets how much the neuron directions can move. Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ ensures this bound is respected throughout training, making $\epsilon$ an explicit knob on the degrees of freedom of the finetuning transform. Empirically, the paper’s figure on COFT shows that increasing $\epsilon$ yields more aggressive adaptation (greater control alignment), while decreasing $\epsilon$ yields more conservative behavior (closer to pretrained outputs), exactly matching the notion that $\epsilon$ primarily controls finetuning flexibility.
