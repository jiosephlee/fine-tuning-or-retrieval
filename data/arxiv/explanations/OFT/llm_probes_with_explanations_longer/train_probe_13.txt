In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', under $r = r' = \alpha d$, the method that yields only linear-in-$d$ trainable parameter growth (parameter complexity $\mathcal{O}(d)$) is OFT. This follows directly from the block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization used to represent the layer-shared orthogonal transform. For an orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$, OFT parameterizes it as $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with $r$ blocks, each in $\mathrm{O}(d/r)$. The number of free parameters then becomes $d\big(d/r - 1\big)/2$, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. When the block count is set proportional to the neuron dimension, $r=\alpha d$, this complexity reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/(\alpha d))=\mathcal{O}(d)$, i.e., linear in $d$. Thus, OFT achieves parameter efficiency by exploiting a structured sparsity (block-diagonal orthogonality) while still preserving hyperspherical energy via full-rank orthogonal transforms within each block.

By contrast, LoRA’s parameter count on a $d \times n$ layer is $r'(d+n)$, and under the same scaling $r'=\alpha d$ this becomes $\alpha d(d+n)=\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$, which grows at least quadratically in $d$ (and linearly in $n$). The paper further notes that OFT can optionally share blocks ($\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$), reducing parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$, which under $r=\alpha d$ becomes $\mathcal{O}(1)$; even without sharing, the linear $\mathcal{O}(d)$ regime holds. Empirically, the authors illustrate this gap with a concrete example: for a $128 \times 128$ weight matrix, LoRA with $r'=8$ uses $2{,}048$ parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ uses $960$ parameters, reflecting the structural advantage of OFT’s orthogonal block parameterization in keeping trainable parameter growth small as $d$ increases.
