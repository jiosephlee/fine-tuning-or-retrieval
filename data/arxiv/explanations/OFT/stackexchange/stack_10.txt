### COFT’s $\epsilon$ constraint: what norm is used and how should $\epsilon$ be set?
Question:
COFT constrains $\lVert R - I \rVert \le \epsilon$ and then replaces this with $\lVert Q \rVert \le \epsilon'$ using the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$. In practice: (1) Which norm is used for $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ (spectral, Frobenius), (2) how do you select $\epsilon$ or $\epsilon'$ across different layers with different $d$, and (3) how sensitive are results to this choice? Any recommended schedules to anneal or tune $\epsilon$ during training for stability?
Answer:
Short answer: the constraint in COFT is meant in the operator (spectral) norm sense for the mathematical arguments (Neumann series / convergence of Cayley transform), but in practice you can enforce a simpler norm (Frobenius) for efficiency — you must account for how the choice of norm scales with layer width when picking the tolerance. $\epsilon$ controls a trade-off: very small values force the finetuned layer to stay near the pretrained weights (stable but under‑flexible); large values recover OFT (flexible but can destabilize). The paper enforces the constraint by projecting the Cayley skew matrix $Q$ to a ball (they write the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ and move the constraint onto $Q$), and reports qualitatively that COFT is substantially more stable and insensitive to iteration count for reasonable epsilons, but does not give a single universal epsilon — you should tune it per task/layer, with a simple recipe below.

Explanation and practical guidance grounded in the paper

Which norm is intended?
The theoretical step in the paper that justifies replacing the constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ by a constraint on $Q$ uses the Neumann series and explicitly mentions convergence “in the operator norm.” That indicates the intended mathematical norm is the operator (spectral) norm: $\|\cdot\|_2$. The Cayley expansion $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ and the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$ are controlled by the operator norm of $Q$ for the Neumann-series argument to hold cleanly.

That said, from an implementation standpoint the authors convert the constraint into one on $Q$ and enforce it by projected gradient descent. Projecting $Q$ by spectral-norm clipping requires either an SVD or a power‑iteration based spectral norm projection, which is somewhat expensive. Projecting by Frobenius norm is much cheaper (just scale the matrix), so many practical implementations use Frobenius-norm projection as an approximation. The paper does not mandate a hard implementation choice, but the math motivation is spectral/operator norm.

How to choose $\epsilon$ or $\epsilon'$ across layers with different dimension $d$?
Two points matter: (a) whether you interpret the bound in spectral norm or Frobenius norm, and (b) layer sensitivity (some layers affect outputs much more).

If you enforce the spectral norm (the paper’s mathematical choice), $\epsilon$ has an absolute meaning independent of $d$: it bounds the largest singular value of $R - I$ (or of $Q$). In that case you can use the same $\epsilon$ across layers and it has comparable geometric meaning: how much rotation/reflection you permit in the worst direction. If you instead use Frobenius norm for projection, that norm grows with matrix dimension, so you should scale the Frobenius tolerance with $d$ if you want comparable per‑component flexibility (e.g. set $\epsilon_F \approx \epsilon_{\text{spec}} \cdot \sqrt{d}$ if the singular values are roughly isotropic).

Practical recipe compatible with the paper’s approach:
- Work with $Q$ (Cayley parameter). Project $Q$ after each gradient step.
- If you can afford it, project by spectral norm (power-iteration to get top singular vector and clip singular value): this enforces the paper’s theory directly and lets you use a single $\epsilon'$ across layers. Reasonable starting values for $\epsilon'$ (spectral) are small (e.g., $0.05$–$0.2$) because the approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ assumes $Q$ small; the paper’s experiments show COFT with a “proper $\epsilon$” gives very stable training (they plot qualitative effects in Figure 5 but do not quote a single global number).
- If you must use Frobenius projection for speed, scale the tolerance with layer dimension. For a target spectral-equivalent $\epsilon_{\text{spec}}$, use $\epsilon_F \approx \epsilon_{\text{spec}} \cdot \sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}$, where $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective dimension of the block you parametrize (the paper uses block-diagonal $R$, so use block size rather than full $d$).

Layer-wise choices: start with a single (spectral) $\epsilon'$ for all OFT blocks. If some layers are especially sensitive (e.g., early embedding or very small $d$ blocks), reduce $\epsilon'$ for those layers. In the paper they apply OFT/COFT to the same locations as LoRA (attention projections) — using a uniform $\epsilon'$ there is reasonable.

How sensitive are results, and annealing/tuning recommendations?
The paper’s qualitative and quantitative results make two empirical points: COFT increases stability compared to unconstrained OFT and standard finetuning; and $\epsilon$ controls a smooth trade-off between staying near pretrained weights (small $\epsilon$) and recovering OFT flexibility (large $\epsilon$). From their Figure 5: small $\epsilon$ makes the finetuned model behave very similarly to pretrained (conservative), while large $\epsilon$ approaches OFT (more change). They report COFT “yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.”

Empirically, results are not extremely brittle within a reasonable range: if $\epsilon$ is chosen moderately small the method is stable and effective, and increasing $\epsilon$ gradually increases flexibility until you reach a point where finetuning can destabilize. There is no single fatal cliff reported in the paper, but extremes ($\epsilon \gg 1$ or no constraint) can break stability.

Practical tuning/annealing suggestions consistent with the paper
1. Start conservative: initialize $Q = 0$ (so $R = I$) and pick a small spectral $\epsilon'$ (e.g., $0.05$). Train and measure validation control/fidelity. The paper explicitly uses identity initialization for $Q$.
2. If the model underfits (cannot achieve desired control after reasonable epochs), increase $\epsilon'$ slowly (e.g., $\times 1.5$–$2$) and re-run. The paper notes larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like OFT and be more flexible.
3. If you see instability or collapse, reduce $\epsilon'$ or tighten projection. COFT is intended to make learning-rate and iteration count easier to set; if you still need hyperparameter safety, reduce learning rate before increasing $\epsilon$.
4. Optional annealing: you can keep $\epsilon'$ small early (preserve pretrained behaviour) and increase it after a few epochs if greater adaptation is needed. The paper does not report an annealing schedule, but this is consistent with the intuition that you should allow more deviation once coarse alignment is learned.
5. Implementation detail: enforce the projection on the skew-symmetric $Q$ after each optimizer step. If you use block-diagonal $R$ (as in the paper), apply per-block projections (spectral or Frobenius) with tolerances per block.

Summary
- The paper’s theory uses the operator (spectral) norm; in practice people often use Frobenius projection for efficiency but must then rescale tolerances by block size.
- Use the $Q$ parameter (Cayley param) and project it to a small norm ball; small $\epsilon'$ yields conservative, stable finetuning; larger $\epsilon'$ gives flexibility and approaches OFT.
- Results are fairly robust in a moderate $\epsilon$ range; tune $\epsilon'$ (or anneal it) if you need more flexibility, and prefer spectral-norm projection for a direct match to the paper’s theoretical justification.
