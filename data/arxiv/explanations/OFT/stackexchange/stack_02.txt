### Reconciling the two definitions of hyperspherical similarity and energy used in the paper
Question:
Earlier, the paper motivates hyperspherical energy using "hyperspherical similarity (e.g., cosine similarity)" as a measure, but then defines the energy as $\mathrm{HE}(W)=\sum_{i \neq j}\lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert^{-1}$. Since on the unit sphere $\lVert \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \rVert^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})$, this energy is a function of pairwise cosines but not equal to a sum of cosines. Why choose the inverse distance form rather than a direct function of $\cos\theta$ (e.g., $\sum_{i\neq j} -\cos\theta_{ij}$)? Does this specific form matter for the invariance and the empirical claims, or would any strictly monotone function of the angle suffice?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses the inverse chordal-distance form because it is the standard hyperspherical energy / Riesz potential used in the cited literature, and because that particular choice strongly penalizes very small angular separations (neighbors that “collide”). Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles (and thus any function of those angles), so the invariance claim does not depend on using the inverse-distance formula specifically. However, the exact functional form does matter for optimization behavior and empirical sensitivity: any strictly monotone function of the angle yields the same invariance and the same ordering of pairwise relations, but not the same gradients, conditioning, or how strongly small angles are penalized.

Why the paper uses the inverse-distance formula (technical + intuitive):
The paper defines hyperspherical energy as
$$\mathrm{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},$$
where $\hat w_i$ are unit vectors. On the sphere the squared chordal distance relates to cosine by $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})$, so $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ is a definite (monotone) function of the pairwise angle $\theta_{ij}$. This choice is not arbitrary: it is exactly a Riesz-type potential (Coulomb-like repulsion on the sphere) that has been used in the hyperspherical-energy literature the paper cites (e.g., Liu et al.). Intuitively this potential enforces “repulsion” among neuron directions: pairs with tiny angular separation produce very large contributions to the sum, so the objective strongly discourages neurons from clustering. That property is useful for (a) creating a meaningful measure of uniformity/spacing on the sphere and (b) making an optimization objective that actively resists near-collisions.

Invariance vs. functional choice:
Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products / angles: if $\hat w_i' = R\hat w_i$ with $R^\top R=I$, then $\theta_{ij}'=\theta_{ij}$ and therefore any function $g(\theta_{ij})$ or any symmetric function of chordal distance $h(\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|)$ is exactly preserved. So the key invariance statement in the paper (that a single shared orthogonal transform preserves the pairwise hyperspherical similarities) is true for the inverse-distance energy and is equally true for any other function that depends only on the pairwise angle. In that sense, the invariance claim does not hinge on the inverse-distance choice.

Why the specific form still matters in practice:
Although invariance holds for any angle-only function, different choices produce different optimization dynamics and empirical behavior. Converting cosine to e.g. $-\cos\theta_{ij}$ versus $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ changes the range, curvature and gradient magnitudes. The inverse-distance has a singular-like behavior as $\theta_{ij}\to 0$; it gives very large gradients for near-overlapping neurons and so strongly prevents collisions. A simple sum of $-\cos\theta_{ij}$ would prefer orthogonality in a weaker, more uniform way and would not blow up when two vectors get very close, so it may be less effective at actively separating near-duplicates. Thus two consequences follow: first, the optimization landscape (smoothness, Lipschitz constants, conditioning) changes; second, empirical phenomena such as stability to collapse, convergence speed, and how aggressively small angular deviations are corrected will differ.

Practical takeaway for the paper’s claims:
The theoretical invariance statement (orthogonal transforms preserve hyperspherical similarity) holds for any function of angles, so the high-level justification for OFT is robust: learning layer-shared orthogonal matrices preserves pairwise angular relations no matter which angle-only energy you use. But the authors chose the inverse-distance hyperspherical energy because it ties to prior theoretical work on hyperspherical energies and because it gives a strong repulsive penalty for small angles—properties that help make the measure meaningful and help the optimization avoid neuron collapse. If you replaced the energy with another strictly monotone function of $\theta$, you would keep invariance and the qualitative idea, but you would likely observe different empirical behavior (different stability, gradient magnitudes, and convergence), so the specific form is not irrelevant.

If you want a middle ground: using any strictly monotone mapping of $\theta$ keeps invariance and preserves the order of pairwise similarities; but if you care about preventing near-collisions and strong regularization of neuron directions, pick a function (like inverse-distance or a heavy-tailed kernel) that strongly penalizes small angles rather than a mild linear proxy like $-\cos\theta$.
