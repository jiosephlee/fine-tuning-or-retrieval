### Compositionality of multiple OFT adapters: how would $R$'s be combined across tasks?
Question:
The paper mentions a potential for compositionality: orthogonal matrices from multiple OFT finetunings can be multiplied and remain orthogonal. Concretely, if I have two downstream tasks yielding $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ for the same layer, is the composed adapter $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ expected to preserve both task adaptations when multiplied into $W^0$? In practice, are $R$'s trained on disjoint tasks likely to commute, or can order matter substantially? Any initial evidence (positive or negative) for catastrophic interference when composing multiple $R$'s?
Answer:
Short answer: mathematically, yes — the product $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ is still orthogonal, so it yields a valid OFT-style adapter. Practically, however, there is no guarantee that $R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ will “preserve” the behavior of both single-task finetunings the way you want. Orthogonal matrices do not generally commute, and the paper explicitly calls this compositionality question an open problem and does not provide empirical results on composing independently learned $R$'s. Below I give the intuition, a simple algebraic view, likely failure modes (including when order matters), and a few practical experiments / mitigations you can try.

Intuition and algebraic view
Each OFT finetuning for a task $T$ learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R^{(T)}$ and the finetuned weight is $W^{(T)} = R^{(T)} W^0$. If you multiply two adapters and use $W = (R^{(B)}R^{(A)}) W^0$, you are applying first $R^{(A)}$ then $R^{(B)}$ in the coordinate frame of the neurons. That product is orthogonal because the orthogonal group is closed under multiplication. But whether $W$ behaves like a model that “does task A and task B” depends on how those tasks were encoded into the two rotations.

Roughly there are three regimes:

1) Disjoint / commuting transformations. If $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ act on independent subspaces (for example, block-diagonal with nonoverlapping blocks, or both live in a mutually commuting subgroup of $O(d)$), then they commute and composition is predictable: $R^{(B)}R^{(A)} = R^{(A)}R^{(B)}$, and each rotation preserves the directions the other did not touch. In that case you are much more likely to retain both adaptations after composing.

2) Small, near-identity transforms. If both $R$'s are small perturbations around identity (COFT with small $\epsilon$), composition will be close to the sum of their infinitesimal generators and may approximately preserve each adaptation — interference is limited because each change is locally small. Neumann-series intuition in the paper supports this: small $Q$ (Cayley parameter) implies $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$, so compositions add approximately.

3) Large, overlapping transforms. If the two tasks require substantially different rotations of the same neuron directions, the product will generally not reproduce either single-task behavior. Orthogonal matrices typically do not commute, so order matters: applying $R^{(A)}$ then $R^{(B)}$ is different from the reverse. Composing two independently learned $R$'s can therefore produce interference or even catastrophic degradation of one or both task behaviors.

Why order can matter (noncommutativity)
Two rotations in high-dimensional space are just like 3D rotations: sequence matters unless they are rotations about the same axis/subspace. When $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ do not commute, $R^{(B)}R^{(A)} W^0$ will generally not equal $R^{(A)}W^0$ (task A result) nor $R^{(B)}W^0$ (task B result), and performance on either task can change depending on the order. The paper explicitly highlights this as an open problem: orthogonality gives closure (products remain orthogonal) but does not guarantee multi-task preservation.

Any evidence in the paper?
The paper raises compositionality as an intriguing open problem but does not present empirical tests of composing independently trained $R$'s. All reported results are for single-task OFT/COFT finetunings; there is no experimental evidence (positive or negative) in the paper about catastrophic interference when multiplying adapters learned on different downstream tasks.

Practical diagnostics and experiments you can run
To assess compositionality empirically for your models, try the following simple checks:
- Measure each task’s performance after composition: train $R^{(A)}$ and $R^{(B)}$ separately from $W^0$, then evaluate task A on $W^{comp}=R^{(B)}R^{(A)}W^0$ and compare to baseline $W^{(A)}$. Do the symmetric check for task B.
- Measure the commutator norm: compute $C = R^{(A)}R^{(B)} - R^{(B)}R^{(A)}$ and report $\|C\|$ (operator or Frobenius norm). Small $\|C\|$ suggests near-commutativity and hence higher chance of successful composition.
- Probe projection overlap: check whether the two $R$'s rotate mostly within disjoint blocks (if you used block-diagonal parameterization). If they largely act on the same blocks/subspaces, expect more interference.
- Try sequential fine-tuning: after learning $R^{(A)}$, initialize $R$ for task B at identity and fine-tune with $R^{(A)}$ frozen, or allow a small joint adjustment. This can reduce mismatch versus naive multiplication.
- Ablate COFT $\epsilon$: train with different $\epsilon$ (near-identity) to see if smaller deviations improve compositionality.

Ways to improve chance of positive composition
If your goal is modular adapters that can be composed reliably, you can use inductive biases during training to encourage commutativity or disjointness. The paper suggests some of these directions implicitly (block-diagonal $R$, COFT small $\epsilon$, shared-block vs per-block choices). Concretely, restricting $R$'s to act on disjoint blocks (different blocks per task) or training $R$'s within a commuting subgroup (e.g., independent 2D rotations on non-overlapping planes) makes multiplication predictable. Alternatively, keep each $R$ small via COFT so composition is approximately additive and less destructive. Another strategy is to learn adapters with a composition objective: while training $R^{(B)}$ also regularize performance on task A (or include a reconstruction loss that preserves $W^{(A)}$'s behavior), i.e., learn for multi-task consistency rather than independent tasks.

Takeaway
The algebraic fact that orthogonal matrices multiply to an orthogonal matrix is promising, but it does not by itself solve compositional multi-task adaptation. The paper explicitly leaves whether composed $R$'s preserve multiple downstream tasks as an open problem and offers no empirical verdict. In practice you should expect order-dependence and potential interference unless you train with architectural or regularization constraints that enforce disjointness, near-identity updates, or explicit multi-task consistency.
