### Toy autoencoder experiment: what exactly are the 'magnitude-only' and 'angle-only' computations?
Question:
In Figure 1 (toy autoencoder), training uses standard inner products $z = w^\top x$. At test time you compare: (a) the same inner product, (b) "magnitude information", and (c) "angular information" (cosine). Could you formalize (b) and (c)? For example, is (c) implemented as $z = \frac{w^\top x}{\lVert w \rVert \lVert x \rVert}$ and (b) as $z = \lVert w \rVert \lVert x \rVert$ (or something else)? Also, are feature maps rescaled or shifted to the range expected by downstream layers, and how is reconstruction fair across the three variants?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — (c) is the cosine of the angle between kernel and patch, and (b) can reasonably be implemented as the product of their norms (or an L2-based magnitude proxy). Because the autoencoder was trained with inner products, to make the three test-time variants comparable you must pay attention to sign and scale: either match their activation distributions to the trained inner-product activations (e.g., by rescaling/affine correction) or accept that reconstruction quality will differ because the downstream decoder expects the original inner-product statistics.

Formalizations and intuition
The trained forward pass used the standard inner product
$z = w^\top x$.
Two natural test-time decompositions of that scalar are its magnitude part and its angular part. A clean way to write the decomposition is
$w^\top x = \|w\| \|x\| \cos\theta$,
where $\theta$ is the angle between $w$ and $x$ and $\cos\theta = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\| \|x\|}$. From this identity the three variants in the paper can be implemented as:

(a) Inner product (baseline, same as training)
$z_a = w^\top x$.

(b) Magnitude-only
The most direct magnitude-only choice (discarding angular sign information) is
$z_b = \|w\| \, \|x\|$.
This captures only the product of lengths; it removes the $\cos\theta$ factor, so different directions produce the same activation if norms match. An alternative magnitude proxy (which preserves sign information coming from projection) is to use the absolute inner product
$z_b' = |w^\top x|$,
or a signed magnitude proxy $z_b'' = \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x)\,\|w\|\,\|x\|$ (but that last is less standard). The paper’s description (“magnitude information”) and their phase/magnitude analogy suggest they used something like $\|w\|\,\|x\|$ (i.e., magnitude only), which explains why the magnitude-only map contains little semantic content in the figure: angle encodes the structure, magnitude does not.

(c) Angular-only (cosine)
The natural angular-only implementation is the cosine similarity
$z_c = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|\,\|x\|} = \cos\theta$.
This discards the overall scaling and keeps only direction alignment. The paper explicitly mentions they do not apply this cosine during training (training was with raw inner products) and only test with cosine; their reported result is that the cosine map nearly reconstructs the input, consistent with the phase/angle preserving semantics.

Practical issues: sign, zero norms, and numeric stability
If either $\|w\|$ or $\|x\|$ can be (near) zero, the cosine is unstable; in practice one adds a small $\epsilon$ to denominators:
$z_c = \frac{w^\top x}{\max(\|w\|\,\|x\|, \epsilon)}$.
If you choose magnitude-only using $\|w\|\,\|x\|$, that is always nonnegative and loses the sign of $w^\top x$ (which also diminishes representational power).

Making reconstructions comparable (rescaling / downstream expectations)
Because the decoder/next layers were trained on $z$ drawn from the inner product distribution, simply replacing $z$ with $\cos\theta$ or $\|w\|\,\|x\|$ will change the marginal distributions (mean, variance, dynamic range) and can harm reconstruction irrespective of the information content. To fairly compare reconstructions across the three variants you should make their activation statistics compatible with the decoder. Standard, simple ways to do this are:

1) Mean–variance matching: compute per-channel (or global) mean and standard deviation of $z_a = w^\top x$ on a held-out set, then at test time affine-rescale $z_b$ or $z_c$ to match those mean/variance values:
$z_{\text{test\_scaled}} = a \cdot z_{\text{test}} + b$,
where $a$ and $b$ are chosen to match mean and std to the original inner-product activations.

2) Per-channel affine calibration: learn a small per-feature affine transform (scale and bias) that maps the new $z$ variant to the distribution expected by the decoder. This is low-cost and can be done with a few calibration examples without changing the decoder weights.

3) Histogram / quantile matching: map empirical CDFs of $z_b$ or $z_c$ to that of $z_a$ (more complex, usually unnecessary).

In the paper the authors say the training used inner products and that at test they "compare" (a),(b),(c) — they also explicitly note they did not apply cosine during training. They do not provide the low-level implementation details in the main text for exactly how (b) and (c) were computed or whether they performed any rescaling before feeding into the decoder. Given the phase/magnitude analogy they discuss, the simplest faithful implementations consistent with the paper are $z_c = (w^\top x)/(\|w\|\,\|x\|)$ and $z_b = \|w\|\,\|x\|$ (with epsilon for stability). To make reconstructions “fair,” one should at least match mean/variance of activations across variants or apply per-channel affine calibration; otherwise differences in reconstruction can conflate information loss with mismatched activation scale.

Bottom line
Formally use cosine for angle-only, product of norms (or absolute inner product) for magnitude-only. Because the decoder was trained on raw inner products, rescaling (mean/variance matching or a learned affine per-channel calibration) is important to ensure differences in reconstruction reflect information content (angle vs magnitude) rather than trivial scale mismatches. The paper’s qualitative claim — that angular information alone preserves semantics while magnitude does not — is exactly what the cosine vs norm-product decomposition predicts.
