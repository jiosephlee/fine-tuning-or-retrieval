### How is numerical stability of the Cayley inverse handled for large \(d\) and during backprop?
Question:
OFT uses \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). In practice, when \(d\) is large (e.g., 512 or 1024), inverting \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) repeatedly in training could introduce numerical instability. Are there specific linear algebra tricks (e.g., LU with pivoting, solving linear systems instead of explicit inverse) and gradient clipping or conditioning techniques used to keep training stable?
Answer:
In the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) paper the authors choose the <|endoftext|> map  
$$
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},\quad Q^\top = -Q,
$$  
to enforce $R^\top R = I$ exactly. It is true that forming $(I - Q)^{-1}$ naively in high dimensions (say $d = 512$ or $1024$) could run into precision‐and‐efficiency issues if $I - Q$ becomes ill conditioned. In fact the paper does **not** hide some exotic LU‐pivoting or gradient‐clipping hack in its published code; instead it leans on two very clean ideas to keep the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> both **stable** and **fast**:

1. **Block‐diagonal <|endoftext|> transform.**  
   Rather than let $Q$ be an arbitrary $d\times d$ skew‐symmetric, they partition it into $r$ independent $(d/r)\times(d/r)$ skew‐symmetric blocks. Concretely  
   $$
     Q = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(Q_1,\dots,Q_r\bigr), 
     \quad  
     R = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(R_1,\dots,R_r\bigr),
   $$
   with  
   $$
     <|endoftext|> = (I + Q_i)\,(I - Q_i)^{-1} \in \mathrm{O}(d/r).
   $$
   Each small block‐inverse $(I - Q_i)^{-1}$ costs only $\mathcal O\bigl((d/r)^3\bigr)$ instead of $\mathcal O(d^3)$. Numerically it is much safer to invert twenty $64\times64$ blocks than one $512\times512$ matrix.

2. **Spectral‐norm control on $Q$.**  
   The only time $(I - Q)$ is <|endoftext|> is when $\|Q\|\ge1$ in operator norm, because then some eigenvalue of $Q$ hits $\pm1$ and $I - Q$ loses invertibility. OFT’s <|endoftext|> variant (COFT) makes this explicit by bounding  
   $$
     \|Q\| \;\le\;\epsilon'<1,
   $$
   enforced via a simple project‐and‐re‐scale step on $Q$ each <|endoftext|> update. This guarantee—together with <|endoftext|> $Q=0$—keeps every $(I-Q_i)$ well‐conditioned, so that  
   $$
     \sigma_{\min}(I - Q_i)\;\ge\;1 - \|Q_i\|\;>\;0
   $$  
   <|endoftext|> throughout training.

Because $\|Q\|$ remains small, one can even *approximate* the <|endoftext|> map by a few terms of its Neumann series  
$$
  R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}
    \;\approx\;I + 2\,Q + 2\,Q^2 + \cdots
$$  
for an extra speed‐or‐stability trade‐off, although the paper simply notes this expansion to justify the $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ constraint.

Finally, you will almost never see an *explicit* matrix inversion in a modern autodiff framework. Instead you implement the <|endoftext|> update as a linear solve, <|endoftext|>  
```python
# pseudocode
# given Q <|endoftext|> with blocks Qi
# to compute zi = Ri @ xi, do for each block:
yi = solve(I - Qi, (I + Qi) @ xi)      # backward‐stable triangular solve
```  
and let PyTorch/TF automatically backpropagate through that `solve`. Under the hood it uses a stable LU or Cholesky‐style routine (with pivoting if needed), so you get both a correct <|endoftext|> and resistance to small condition numbers.

Putting it all together, **OFT/COFT** keeps the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> stable by  
1. breaking the inversion into many small block inversions,  
2. enforcing $\|Q\|<1$ so $(I-Q)$ never approaches singularity, and  
3. computing “inverse”—and its gradient—via standard linear‐solve primitives rather than an explicit dense <|endoftext|>

No fancy gradient‐clipping or bespoke pivot‐searching is hidden in the NeurIPS code; it simply exploits these two principled ideas to make Cayley‐based orthogonalization both **efficient** and **numerically robust** even when $d$ is in the hundreds or thousands.
