In other words, driving the $\text{HE}$ difference to zero under a shared orthogonal transform exactly preserves the relational geometry of neurons on the unit hypersphere.

This preservation of pairwise angles is the crux of maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic structure during finetuning. Since the forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, OFT “rotates” or “reflects” all neuron directions coherently in the same canonical basis, keeping $\hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j$ invariant across all pairs $(i,j)$. The paper argues this invariance is crucial because angular relationships capture semantic information in high-dimensional representations, and changing neuron directions while preserving their pairwise angles allows controlled adaptation without collapsing the pretrained generative capability. Even the re-scaled variant, $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$ with diagonal $\bm{D}$, leaves $\text{HE}$ unchanged due to normalization, reinforcing that OFT’s objective of minimizing $\Delta \text{HE}$ primarily safeguards the neurons’ pairwise angular structure.

An orthogonal matrix satisfies R^{\top}R=I, so for any vectors u,v we have ⟨Ru,Rv⟩=u^{\top}R^{\top}Rv=u^{\top}v=⟨u,v⟩. Consequently, the cosine similarity, defined as ⟨u,v⟩/(∥u∥∥v∥), is invariant under an orthogonal transformation because both numerator and denominator remain unchanged. This invariance means that the angles between any pair of neuron weight vectors are preserved when all weights in a layer are transformed by a shared orthogonal matrix, exactly the structural property OFT exploits.

This invariance directly implies that hyperspherical similarities and the hyperspherical energy defined in the paper are preserved by OFT. Let the normalized neurons be \hat{\bm{w}}_{i}=\bm{w}_{i}/\|\bm{w}_{i}\| and the energy be \text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}\|^{-1}. Since \|\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}\|^{2}=2(1-\langle\hat{\bm{w}}_{i},\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}\rangle), the energy depends only on pairwise inner products (equivalently, angles). Under a layer-shared orthogonal transform \bm{w}_{i}'=\bm{R}\bm{w}_{i}, normalization gives \hat{\bm{w}}_{i}'=\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}, and thus ⟨\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}',\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}'⟩=⟨\hat{\bm{w}}_{i},\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}⟩ and \|\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}'-\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}'\|=\|\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}\|. Therefore \text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}), which is why OFT “provably preserves hyperspherical energy” and, by preserving these pairwise angular relations, maintains the semantic structure learned during pretraining.

Driving the configuration toward uniformity necessarily alters these angles, thereby changing the relational geometry that underpins the model’s semantics.

In contrast, the OFT objective preserves hyperspherical energy by constraining the finetuning to orthogonal transforms W = R W^0 with R^\top R = I, which keep all pairwise angles invariant. Since hyperspherical similarity and energy are functions only of directions, an orthogonal transform maintains ∠(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) for all i,j, preventing disruption of the pretrained relational structure while still allowing controlled semantic modification via a global rotation/reflection of the neuron basis. The paper’s toy autoencoder experiment and the analogy to Fourier phase support that angular information carries semantics, while magnitude is largely irrelevant; thus, a procedure that explicitly reduces E(W) (making neurons uniformly spaced) would erase the non-uniform, task-specific angular patterns learned during pretraining. For classification, minimizing E(W) can improve generalization by reducing redundancy; but for finetuning diffusion models, the same operation would overwrite the intricate, semantically aligned angular structure, leading to a loss of generative fidelity and controllability.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which improves finetuning stability by imposing an additional constraint that bounds the deviation of the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from the identity via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, referred to as the radius constraint. This constraint explicitly limits how far the finetuned weights can move from the pretrained ones while still preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonality, thus curbing the degrees of freedom that can cause model collapse or loss of generative semantics. Standard OFT guarantees that pairwise neuron angles (and hence hyperspherical energy) are preserved by enforcing $\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)$, but in high-capacity models large rotations can still drastically change forward mappings and hence semantics; the radius constraint adds a second, complementary bias toward small Euclidean deviation from $\bm{I}$, aligning with the well-known practical heuristic that staying close to $\bm{W}^0$ preserves pretrained performance. As shown empirically in the paper, tuning with this dual constraint yields smoother and more predictable training dynamics, maintaining identity and controllability over longer iterations where unconstrained finetuning methods tend to collapse.

In the COFT formulation, the orthogonality is enforced efficiently via the Cayley transform $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, and the radius constraint is made tractable by approximating $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ (via the Neumann series), which converts $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ into a constraint on $\bm{Q}$, namely $\|\bm{Q}\|\leq\epsilon'$. This enables projected gradient descent directly on $\bm{Q}$ while keeping initialization at the identity through $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$. Consequently, COFT optimizes on the smooth hypersphere manifold (thanks to orthogonality) but inside a controlled neighborhood (“radius”) around the pretrained solution, ensuring that updates primarily adjust neuron directions in a measured way that preserves the pairwise angular relational structure and avoids drastic semantic shifts. The paper’s ablations further show that decreasing $\epsilon$ tightens the behavior to mimic the pretrained model, while increasing $\epsilon$ recovers the flexibility of OFT, illustrating how the radius constraint trades off flexibility for stability in a principled, tunable manner.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors explain that applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer preserves pairwise neuron angles; if each neuron instead received a different orthogonal transformation, the expected outcome is that the pairwise neuron angles are not preserved. This follows from the basic invariance of inner products under a common orthogonal map: for any orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R} \) with \( \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I} \), we have
\[
\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}_i,\, \bm{R}\bm{w}_j \rangle \;=\; \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\,\bm{w}_j \;=\; \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j \;=\; \langle \bm{w}_i,\, \bm{w}_j \rangle,
\]
so the cosine similarity \( \cos\angle(\bm{w}_i,\bm{w}_j) \) and hence the pairwise angles on the unit hypersphere are exactly preserved. Because hyperspherical energy is a sum of pairwise similarities over normalized neurons, \( \text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} \), this invariance implies \( \text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \). Preserving hyperspherical energy is central to OFT’s design, as it retains the pretrained layer’s relational geometry that the authors argue encodes semantic generation capacity.

By contrast, if each neuron receives a different orthogonal transformation \( \bm{R}_i \), then pairwise angles are generally not preserved because inner products are no longer invariant in pairs: 
\[
\langle \bm{R}_i \bm{w}_i,\, \bm{R}_j \bm{w}_j \rangle \;=\; \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j\, \bm{w}_j,
\]
which equals \( \langle \bm{w}_i, \bm{w}_j \rangle \) only in special cases (e.g., \( \bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j = \bm{I} \), meaning \( \bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j \), or when \( \bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j \) acts trivially on the subspace spanned by \( \bm{w}_i, \bm{w}_j \)). Generically, \( \bm{R}_i^\top \bm{R}_j \) is a nontrivial orthogonal operator that rotates or reflects \( \bm{w}_j \) relative to \( \bm{w}_i \), altering the cosine similarity and thus changing the hyperspherical energy. The paper’s emphasis on a layer-shared orthogonal matrix is precisely to enforce the global relational invariance needed to preserve the pretrained semantic structure; allowing neuron-specific transforms would introduce uncontrolled angular drift among neurons, undermining the stability and preservation properties that OFT leverages.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors note that when the number of diagonal blocks equals the number of input channels ($r = C$), each block transforms a unique neuron channel, making OFT's transform most akin to a depth-wise convolution. This is sensible because a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix of the form $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_C)$ acts independently on $C$ disjoint subspaces, one per input channel, and contains zeros off the block diagonals, which forbids any cross-channel mixing. For a convolution with $C$ input channels and kernel vectors flattened per channel, the OFT update $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ becomes $\bm{w}_c=\bm{R}_c \bm{w}_c^0$ for each channel $c\in\{1,\dots,C\}$, leaving $\bm{w}_{c'}$ unaffected for $c'\neq c$. This mirrors depth-wise convolution, where a separate kernel $K_c$ is applied to each input channel $X_c$, producing $Y_c = K_c * X_c$ without mixing channels. Under OFT with $r=C$, the transformed kernels $\tilde K_c$ are obtained by per-channel orthogonal rotations/reflections of $K_c^0$, i.e., $\tilde K_c = \bm{R}_c K_c^0$, which preserves channel isolation just like depth-wise operations.

Moreover, depth-wise convolution is characterized by applying distinct filters to each channel while not coupling the channels via the spatial operator; the coupling (if any) happens only later through pointwise ($1\times1$) convolutions. The OFT design with $r=C$ reproduces exactly this structural constraint: the off-diagonal blocks in $\bm{R}$ are zero, so no term of the form $\bm{R}_{c,c'}$ with $c\neq c'$ can rotate weights from channel $c'$ into channel $c$. Since each $\bm{R}_c\in\mathrm{O}(d/C)$ is orthogonal, OFT also preserves the hyperspherical energy within the subspace of each channel, i.e., for normalized per-channel neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_{c,i}$, the pairwise similarities $\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_{c,i},\hat{\bm{w}}_{c,j}\rangle$ are unchanged by $\bm{R}_c$. Thus, OFT’s block-diagonal, per-channel transforms behave like depth-wise convolutions in structure while maintaining the angular relationships that encode semantics, aligning with the paper’s goal of preserving generative ability during finetuning.
