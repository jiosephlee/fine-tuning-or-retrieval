Chapter Title: The Geometry of Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning for Text-to-Image Diffusion

# Background: Finetuning and Measures of Preservation

Finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models seeks a careful balance between adapting to new tasks and preserving the powerful generative ability learned during pretraining. A conventional approach applies gradient descent with a small learning rate directly to the weights, implicitly favoring solutions close to the pretrained model in Euclidean distance. If we denote the finetuned model weights by $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights by $\bm{M}^0$, then the standard practice informally favors small $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$ and hence encourages preservation of the pretrained model’s behavior. However, the paper argues that measuring closeness purely in Euclidean terms may be insufficient to capture semantic preservation. Specifically, it introduces hyperspherical energy as a structural measure that tracks pairwise angular relationships among neurons, and shows that preserving these relationships is crucial to maintaining semantic generation ability.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is proposed to preserve hyperspherical energy exactly by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations. This preserves pairwise neuron angles, thereby maintaining the pretrained model’s relational structure while still allowing meaningful adaptation. Building on this, the Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) variant imposes an additional explicit deviation constraint, restricting the finetuned model to lie within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model. The resulting framework explicitly unifies two desiderata: preserving angular structure through orthogonality, and limiting Euclidean deviation through a radius constraint.

# Hyperspherical Energy and Orthogonal Finetuning

The paper uses hyperspherical energy to characterize the pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer. For a fully connected layer with weights $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ denotes the $i$-th neuron, the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},\quad \hat{\bm{w}}_i := \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
Preserving this energy ensures that the pairwise angles among the normalized neurons remain unchanged. The central observation is that if we apply the same orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to all neurons of a layer, that is, set $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then the pairwise angles are preserved and the hyperspherical energy remains identical to that of the pretrained layer $\bm{W}^0$.

This leads to OFT, which replaces the standard forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\qquad \text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
and optimizes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ per layer. Initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ ensures the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained weights. Because orthogonal transformations preserve angles, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy and thereby preserves the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained network.

# From OFT to COFT: Adding a Radius Constraint

While OFT preserves angular structure, it does not explicitly bound the deviation from the pretrained model in Euclidean terms. The paper introduces COFT to combine angular preservation with an explicit constraint that limits how far the finetuned model can move from the pretrained model. COFT modifies OFT’s forward pass to
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\qquad \text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\ \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon,
\]
where $\epsilon>0$ controls the allowable deviation of $\bm{R}$ from the identity. As with OFT, the initialization $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ ensures we begin at the pretrained model. The additional constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$ explicitly limits how much the layer’s orthogonal transform can rotate or reflect the neurons, thereby bounding the overall change to the weights.

Conceptually, OFT enforces “no angular distortion” of the pretrained neurons, while COFT adds “limited angular displacement.” Because the finetuned weights are given by $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, bounding $\bm{R}$ near $\bm{I}$ ensures that the finetuned weights remain close to the pretrained weights in a geometric sense. This yields improved finetuning stability and makes hyperparameter selection easier, as the optimization is constrained to a predictable neighborhood of the pretrained solution.

# Geometric Interpretation: A Hypersphere Around Pretrained Weights

The explicit deviation constraint in COFT, $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$, can be interpreted geometrically as keeping the finetuned weights $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ within a hypersphere of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the pretrained weights when measured in an appropriate operator norm. Intuitively, $\bm{R}$ is a point on the orthogonal manifold $\text{O}(d)$, and COFT restricts this point to lie in a small ball around $\bm{I}$. Because $\bm{W}$ is obtained by left-multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}$, remaining close to $\bm{I}$ ensures that the image of $\bm{W}^0$ under this transformation remains close to $\bm{W}^0$ itself.

This ball constraint has a natural Euclidean interpretation. In the unconstrained setting, finetuning could move $\bm{W}$ arbitrarily far from $\bm{W}^0$, risking loss of pretraining knowledge. By enforcing $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$, COFT constrains the set of reachable weights to those that are “nearby” in Euclidean space. In the abstract and the COFT section, the paper describes this as constraining the finetuned model within a hypersphere centered on the pretrained neurons. In practice, small $\epsilon$ yields behavior increasingly similar to the pretrained model, while larger $\epsilon$ approaches the flexibility of unconstrained OFT.

This geometric picture provides a clear intuition for COFT’s stability: the optimizer can explore only a bounded region around $\bm{W}^0$, preserving both the hyperspherical energy and a limited Euclidean deviation.

# Norms and Distances: Relating $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ to $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2$

The paper contrasts the implicit Euclidean proximity favored by small learning rates with COFT’s explicit proximity constraint. In the standard finetuning view, better preservation of pretrained ability is associated with smaller $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$, often measured by the Euclidean (spectral or Frobenius) norm. COFT formalizes this idea by constraining the transform itself: $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$.

Because the finetuned weights are obtained via $\bm{M}=\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the deviation can be written as
\[
\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 - \bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
\]
Thus, bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ primarily bounds the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2$, because the deviation from the pretrained weights is controlled by the size of the perturbation $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$ applied to $\bm{W}^0$. Geometrically, this directly ties the radius constraint in transform space to a radius constraint in weight space. In effect, COFT makes explicit what standard finetuning only implies: the finetuned model should stay within a small Euclidean neighborhood of the pretrained model.

This explicit control complements OFT’s angular preservation. OFT guarantees that the pairwise angles among neurons remain unchanged, while COFT additionally guarantees that the overall Euclidean deviation of the weights from the pretrained configuration is small, leading to improved stability and predictable training dynamics.

# Cayley Parameterization and Enforcing the Constraint

To implement the orthogonality constraint efficiently, the paper adopts the Cayley parameterization. An orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$ can be constructed as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\qquad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. This avoids expensive Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization while ensuring differentiability. Although the Cayley transform parameterizes only the special orthogonal group (determinant $1$), the paper finds this limitation does not affect performance in practice.

In COFT, we must also enforce $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$. The paper uses a Neumann series approximation to gain insight:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
under the assumption that the series converges in the operator norm. This shows that keeping $\bm{Q}$ small keeps $\bm{R}$ close to $\bm{I}$. Therefore, one can move the deviation constraint inside the Cayley transform and enforce a projected constraint on $\bm{Q}$ itself, such as $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\le \epsilon'$, with $\bm{Q}$ initialized to $\bm{0}$ to achieve identity initialization for $\bm{R}$. Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ then maintains both orthogonality (through the parameterization) and bounded deviation (through the projection).

To further reduce parameters and computation, the paper proposes a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),\qquad \bm{R}_i\in \text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),
\]
optionally sharing blocks to reduce parameter count while preserving orthogonality and thus hyperspherical energy. This sparsity-oriented design yields strong parameter efficiency without sacrificing the core geometric guarantees.

# Practical Implications: Stability, Convergence, and Hyperparameters

The explicit radius constraint in COFT has several practical benefits. First, it improves finetuning stability. Because the optimizer cannot stray far from the pretrained model, the risk of model collapse is reduced. The paper presents empirical evidence that COFT and OFT converge faster and more stably than baselines in both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. Second, it simplifies hyperparameter selection. With a suitable $\epsilon$, the number of iterations and learning rate become less sensitive: one can set a relatively large number of iterations without destabilizing the model, since the deviation is explicitly bounded.

The paper also shows that varying $\epsilon$ controls the flexibility of finetuning. As $\epsilon$ increases, COFT behaves more like unconstrained OFT, allowing larger angular displacement while still preserving pairwise angles. As $\epsilon$ decreases, COFT’s behavior approaches that of the pretrained model, maintaining strong semantic preservation with limited adaptation. This tunable trade-off provides a clear and interpretable handle for practitioners to balance preservation and flexibility.

Finally, COFT introduces no additional inference overhead. After training, one can fold the learned orthogonal matrices into the pretrained weights by computing $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, yielding a standard weight matrix that is as efficient at inference as the original model.

# Summary

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) extends Orthogonal Finetuning by adding an explicit deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$ to the orthogonal transform applied to pretrained weights. Geometrically, this constrains the finetuned weights $\bm{M}=\bm{R}\bm{M}^0$ to lie within a hypersphere centered at $\bm{M}^0$, thereby primarily bounding the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|_2$. Combined with OFT’s exact preservation of hyperspherical energy, COFT unifies structural angular preservation with explicit Euclidean proximity. Implemented via the Cayley parameterization and enforced through projected constraints on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$, COFT yields faster, more stable convergence, easier hyperparameter tuning, and no inference-time overhead. This principled geometric design explains COFT’s strong empirical performance in both subject-driven and controllable text-to-image generation.
