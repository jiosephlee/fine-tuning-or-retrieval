Orthogonal Finetuning in Convolutional Layers: Block-Diagonal Transforms and the Depth-Wise Analogy

# Background: Hyperspherical Energy and Orthogonal Finetuning

Text-to-image diffusion models attain strong generative capabilities, but they require careful finetuning to adapt to downstream tasks without degrading pretrained performance. The central idea in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons by constraining finetuning to orthogonal transformations that do not change hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy measures the relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere and is defined, for a layer with neuron vectors, as a sum of normalized pairwise similarities. Preserving this energy maintains the semantic structure that emerges in pretraining.

Concretely, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the $i$-th neuron and $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ is its normalization. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
OFT seeks to minimize the deviation in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned weights $\bm{W}$ and pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}}\left\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|~~\Leftrightarrow~~\min_{\bm{W}}\bigg{\|}\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}.
\]
This objective attains its minimum at zero when the finetuned weights differ from the pretrained weights by an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$, namely $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. In the forward pass, OFT changes the computation from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},
\]
with $\bm{R}$ initialized as $\bm{I}$ so that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model.

The power of this approach follows an important intuition: in neural networks, angles between neurons carry semantic information. Preserving the pairwise angles means the geometry of the learned representation remains intact, which stabilizes finetuning while allowing controlled semantic adjustments via rotations or reflections of neuron directions.

# Orthogonal Transformations and Block-Diagonal Parameterization

Directly learning a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ can be expensive, as orthogonal parameterizations typically scale quadratically in $d$. OFT adopts parameter-efficient structures without sacrificing the angle-preserving property. A central strategy is to restrict $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{R}_1\in \text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right) & & \\
& \ddots & \\
& & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)
\end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d),
\]
where $\text{O}(d)$ denotes the orthogonal group in dimension $d$, and each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}$ is orthogonal. When $r=1$, this reduces to the unconstrained dense orthogonal case. This structure reduces the number of parameters while keeping $\bm{R}$ orthogonal and therefore preserving hyperspherical energy.

Parameter complexity improves with the block structure. A dense orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. With $r$ blocks, the number of parameters becomes $d(d/r-1)/2$, yielding complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If blocks are shared, i.e., $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$, the complexity further reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ while maintaining orthogonality. This parameter-efficiency is achieved without changing the fundamental property that OFT preserves pairwise angles.

The practical parameterization of orthogonality exploits the Cayley transform, constructing $\bm{R}$ via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$:
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This yields orthogonal matrices (with determinant $1$) efficiently. The block-diagonal version applies the Cayley transform independently to each block.

# Applying OFT to Convolutional Layers

OFT is agnostic to architecture, and it can be applied to convolutional layers with meaningful interpretations. The block-diagonal structure meshes naturally with the channel organization of convolutional layers. When the number of diagonal blocks equals the number of input channels, i.e., $r=C$ where $C$ denotes the number of input channels, each block $\bm{R}_i$ transforms a unique neuron channel.

In this $r=C$ setting, the orthogonal matrix becomes
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_C),
\]
and each $\bm{R}_c$ operates exclusively on the subspace corresponding to the $c$-th channel. Because the transformation is orthogonal, it preserves pairwise angles within that channel’s parameters while rotating or reflecting them in their subspace. This decomposition aligns one block per channel and thereby isolates transformations to individual channels.

This channel-wise isolation is similar in spirit to learning depth-wise convolution kernels, where transformations are performed independently for each channel. While OFT’s transformation acts on neuron parameters via orthogonal operations rather than directly redefining convolution operators, the effect of $r=C$ matches the intuition of per-channel adaptation: each channel’s parameters are adjusted independently by its own orthogonal block, yielding an analogy to depth-wise processing.

An additional interpretation arises when blocks are shared across channels. If all blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared, then OFT uses a single orthogonal matrix applied uniformly across all channels. This shared-block setting enforces a common rotational structure across channels, which strengthens regularity while remaining expressive due to the orthogonal nature of the transform.

# Intuition for Channel-Wise Orthogonal Transforms

The channel-wise interpretation follows from the broader principle that angles encode semantics. In convolutional layers, neuron channels capture distinct aspects of visual features. OFT’s per-channel orthogonal transform modifies neuron directions within each channel while preserving their pairwise angles. This preserves the hyperspherical energy associated with that channel’s neurons and hence the relational structure learned during pretraining.

Because the hyperspherical energy is invariant under a shared orthogonal transformation within each block, the semantic content that arises from channel-wise relationships remains intact. Adjusting directions within a channel allows controlled semantic modification: it changes how that channel responds without disrupting the learned geometry across its neurons. This is a natural way to finetune convolutional features, consistent with the observation that angular information is crucial for semantics.

When $r=C$, each channel’s parameters have their own orthogonal degrees of freedom. This increases flexibility while maintaining regularity: each channel can adapt independently, but only via rotations or reflections that preserve angles. The result is a structured finetuning mechanism analogous to depth-wise processing, where channels are decoupled in their transformations. When blocks are shared across channels, the model trades flexibility for stronger regularity by enforcing the same orthogonal behavior globally.

# Constrained Variant and Efficiency Considerations

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit constraint that limits the deviation from the identity transformation. The forward pass remains
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},
\]
subject to orthogonality $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and a radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$. This yields a finetuning neighborhood around the pretrained model, further stabilizing the process.

To incorporate the deviation constraint efficiently under the Cayley parameterization, one can approximate the Cayley transform using a Neumann series. Under convergence in the operator norm,
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]
This moves the constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ to $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$, which can be enforced by projected gradient descent. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ ensures $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start, matching the pretrained model exactly.

The computational efficiency of COFT and OFT relies on block-diagonal parameterization and the Cayley transform. Although the Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse, its use within blocks limits overhead, and the block structure controls parameter counts. These design choices make OFT and COFT parameter-efficient while preserving the semantic geometry via orthogonality.

# Implications for Parameter Efficiency and Stability

OFT differs from low-rank additive approaches by applying multiplicative, layer-shared orthogonal transforms. The forward pass can be rewritten as
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=\big(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},
\]
where $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ resembles a structured update. OFT attains parameter efficiency through sparsity in $\bm{R}$ via block-diagonal design. Compared to low-rank approaches whose parameter complexity scales with both input and output dimensions, OFT’s complexity scales predominantly with $d$ and the number of blocks $r$, as discussed above.

Importantly, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. After finetuning, one can merge the learned orthogonal transform into the pretrained weights to obtain $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. In convolutional layers, this means the effective kernel parameters are updated once and used thereafter without additional runtime computation. This property makes OFT highly practical: it preserves pretrained semantics through hyperspherical energy invariance, offers flexibility and stability through orthogonal transforms (and COFT’s deviation constraint), aligns naturally with channel structures via block-diagonal parameterization, and remains deployment-efficient.

In the specific case where $r=C$, the block-per-channel design provides a clear and intuitive analogy to depth-wise processing: each channel’s parameters are transformed independently by their own orthogonal block, preserving angles within the channel while enabling controlled semantic adjustments. This channel-wise orthogonal finetuning captures the essence of depth-wise behavior within OFT’s principled, angle-preserving framework.
