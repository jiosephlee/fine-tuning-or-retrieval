Chapter: Parameter Efficiency in Orthogonal Finetuning via Block-Diagonal Orthogonality

# Background: Why Orthogonal Finetuning?

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is proposed to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks while preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. The central idea in OFT is to finetune neurons by applying a learned orthogonal transformation to the pretrained weights, thereby preserving pairwise angles among neurons. This angular preservation property is formalized through hyperspherical energy, a measure defined over normalized neurons that captures their pairwise relationships on the unit hypersphere. Since orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and therefore angles, learning orthogonal layers to transform pretrained weights can adapt the model without altering its hyperspherical energy.

Concretely, for a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT replaces the forward computation $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ with
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Because this transformation preserves the pairwise hyperspherical similarity among columns of $\bm{W}^0$, it achieves the optimization goal of minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained layers while still allowing semantic adaptation of the model.

OFT further emphasizes stability by initializing $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{I}$, finetuning from exactly the pretrained model. The method admits efficient, differentiable orthogonal parameterizations, such as the Cayley transform, which represent orthogonal matrices as rational functions of a skew-symmetric matrix. This yields a practical way to ensure orthogonality during gradient-based finetuning.

# Hyperspherical Energy Preservation and Semantics

The motivation behind OFT’s orthogonal transform is tied to the hypothesis that angles among neurons encode semantic structure that is crucial to generation. The hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ captures the pairwise angular structure of a layer by summing inverse distances among normalized neurons:
$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \lVert \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \rVert^{-1}$,
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \lVert \bm{w}_i \rVert$. Minimizing deviation in this energy between pretrained and finetuned models guides finetuning to preserve the pretrained semantic configuration. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles, choosing $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ achieves zero difference in the objective
$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\lVert \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\rVert$.

This preservation is especially important for text-to-image diffusion models, where altering pairwise neuron relationships can degrade generative fidelity and diversity. OFT thus strikes a balance: it changes neuron directions coherently (by a shared layer transform) to modify semantics while preserving the pretrained angular configuration across neurons.

# Orthogonal Parameterization: From Full to Structured

A naive parameterization of $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (indeed, $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters). To compute and maintain orthogonality efficiently during finetuning, OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization:
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$.
This parameterization yields orthogonal matrices (specifically with determinant $1$) and is differentiable, enabling standard gradient-based training. Identity initialization is achieved by setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ so that $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning.

Yet, even with the Cayley transform, a dense $\bm{R}$ remains expensive for large $d$. OFT therefore introduces a sparsity-inducing structure: block-diagonal orthogonality. Instead of a dense orthogonal matrix, OFT parameterizes $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ orthogonal blocks:
$\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$,
where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{(d/r) \times (d/r)}$ is orthogonal. This structure reduces parameter count and computational cost while preserving the key property that the overall $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and thus preserves hyperspherical energy.

# Block-Diagonal Orthogonality as Sparsity

Block-diagonal orthogonality is a form of structural sparsity: the off-diagonal blocks of $\bm{R}$ are zeros. This eliminates cross-block interactions, reducing the number of free parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$ while maintaining an orthogonal transformation within each block. Because orthogonality is preserved at the block level and the entire matrix remains orthogonal, the angular relationships encoded by the layer are still preserved under the transform.

Formally, for a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the number of free parameters is $d(d-1)/2$. For an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix with equal-sized blocks, the parameter count becomes
$d\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)/2$,
which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$. Thus, larger $r$ yields greater parameter savings. This block structure induces sparsity by constraining the transform to operate independently within subspaces, but crucially does so without sacrificing the orthogonality that guarantees hyperspherical energy preservation.

OFT can further increase parameter efficiency through block sharing. If all blocks share the same parameters, i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$, the learnable degrees of freedom drop to that of a single $(d/r) \times (d/r)$ orthogonal block. In this case, the parameter complexity scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$, providing even more aggressive sparsity. Despite this sharing, the composite $\bm{R}$ is still orthogonal, so the core invariance to hyperspherical energy remains intact.

# Parameter Efficiency: Quantitative Characterization

The sparsity induced by block-diagonal orthogonality yields concrete parameter and computational savings. Consider a layer with input dimension $d$ and $n$ output neurons. A dense orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, i.e., $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. With $r$ blocks, OFT reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$, and with block sharing, to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$.

By contrast, a standard low-rank additive adaptation such as LoRA parameterizes a rank-$r'$ update with $r'(d+n)$ parameters. If we choose $r$ and $r'$ both proportional to $d$ (e.g., $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $\alpha \in (0,1]$), the parameter complexity of LoRA becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, while the parameter complexity of OFT becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ under block sharing. This asymptotic comparison highlights a distinct pathway to parameter efficiency: LoRA uses a low-rank additive update, whereas OFT uses a sparse, structured multiplicative transform.

A concrete example illustrates the difference. For a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$:
- LoRA with rank $r' = 8$ uses $2{,}048$ parameters.
- OFT with $r = 8$ blocks (no block sharing) uses $960$ parameters.

Thus, even without sharing, OFT can be more parameter-efficient than LoRA, and with sharing, the savings are even more pronounced. Importantly, these savings do not compromise the angular preservation that underpins OFT’s stability and semantic retention.

# Why Sparsity Without Losing Invariance?

A natural concern with introducing sparsity is whether it undermines the invariances that motivate OFT. Here, the invariance of hyperspherical energy relies only on the orthogonality of the transformation, not on its density. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is still orthogonal: $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Therefore, the angles among transformed neurons are preserved, and the hyperspherical energy of the layer is unchanged. This means OFT can exploit sparsity for efficiency while maintaining the theoretical guarantee critical to preserving pretrained semantics.

In practice, the block structure introduces an explicit regularization: the transform within each block is independent, which can further stabilize finetuning by limiting the degrees of freedom. This aligns with the broader trade-off identified in finetuning methods between flexibility and regularity. Standard unconstrained finetuning is highly flexible but unstable. OFT, through orthogonality and block structure, finds a robust middle ground: enough flexibility to adapt semantics by rotating neuron directions, and enough regularity to preserve pretrained structure and avoid collapse.

# Efficient Orthogonality via the Cayley Transform

Enforcing $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal throughout training is crucial. The Cayley transform provides a differentiable route:
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
with $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This representation allows gradient-based optimization over $\bm{Q}$ while ensuring that the resulting $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. The identity initialization is simple: set $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ to get $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. When incorporating Constrained OFT (COFT), which additionally requires $\lVert \bm{R} - \bm{I} \rVert \leq \epsilon$, the Neumann series expansion
$\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$
suggests implementing the deviation constraint by constraining $\lVert \bm{Q} \rVert \leq \epsilon'$ via projected gradient steps. This maintains both orthogonality and proximity to identity, further improving stability.

For large $d$, a full Cayley transform still involves matrix inversion. The block-diagonal structure mitigates this by restricting inverses to smaller blocks, thereby reducing computation. With shared blocks, even fewer unique inverses are needed. In all cases, the result remains a global orthogonal transform, preserving the angular structure at the layer level.

# Architecture-Agnostic Benefits and Convolutional Interpretations

OFT is, in principle, architecture agnostic. While comparisons to LoRA are made in the context of Transformers by restricting OFT to attention weights, the block-diagonal structure admits intuitive interpretations for convolutional layers. If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block acts on a single channel, analogous to depth-wise operations. If all blocks are shared, the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels. These variants preserve the core guarantee—orthogonality and thus hyperspherical energy preservation—while exploiting the same sparsity principle for parameter efficiency.

# Distinct from Low-Rank: Sparsity versus Rank Constraints

It is instructive to contrast OFT’s sparsity-based parameter efficiency with LoRA’s low-rank parameterization. LoRA adds a low-rank update $(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0)$, controlling parameter count by restricting rank. OFT, instead, applies a multiplicative transform to $\bm{W}^0$ constrained to be orthogonal and structured (block-diagonal). This distinction leads to two different forms of efficiency:
- LoRA exploits a low-rank additive change, with parameter count scaling as $r'(d+n)$.
- OFT exploits sparsity through block-diagonal orthogonality, with parameter count scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$ or $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$ with sharing.

Rewriting OFT’s forward pass as
$(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = \bigl(\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0\bigr)^\top \bm{x}$
reveals an additive view in which $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ plays a role analogous to a LoRA update. When $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ happens to be low-rank, OFT also induces a low-rank update. However, OFT’s design does not rely on low rank; it relies on orthogonality and sparsity, preserving hyperspherical energy and enabling stable, semantically meaningful finetuning.

# Practical Implications: No Inference Overhead

A practical advantage of OFT is that its transforms can be folded into the weights at inference time. Once training finishes, the learned $\bm{R}$ is multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ to form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The resulting model uses $\bm{W}$ exactly as a standard layer weight with no extra computations. Thus, OFT’s parameter efficiency does not incur additional inference overhead, even as it imposes orthogonality and sparsity during finetuning for stability and preservation of pretrained capabilities.

# Summary: Sparsity-Inducing Block-Diagonal Orthogonality in OFT

OFT achieves parameter efficiency by imposing a sparsity-inducing structure on its orthogonal parameterization. Specifically, it constrains the learned orthogonal transform to be block-diagonal, optionally with shared blocks. This reduces parameter counts from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$ while preserving the key orthogonality property that guarantees invariance of hyperspherical energy. In contrast to low-rank additive updates, OFT’s multiplicative, structured orthogonal transforms maintain the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, leading to stable finetuning that adapts semantics without sacrificing pretrained generative performance. The result is a principled, efficient, and architecture-agnostic approach to adaptation that introduces no additional inference overhead.
