Chapter Title: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning and the Role of Cayley Parameters

# Background: Finetuning Diffusion Models Without Losing Semantics

Large text-to-image diffusion models demonstrate impressive generative ability, but steering these models toward downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation requires careful finetuning. A central difficulty is preserving the semantic capability learned during pretraining while adapting the model to new objectives. Conventional strategies—updating weights with a small learning rate or inserting low-rank adapters—implicitly assume that staying close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean distance suffices to preserve semantics. However, this assumption alone does not capture the structural relationships among neurons that underpin semantic generation.

Motivated by empirical observations that angular similarity encodes semantic information well, hyperspherical energy is introduced as a structural measure of the pairwise neuron relationships on the unit hypersphere. Hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise similarities among neurons and reflects the level of uniformity of neural directions. Preserving this energy during finetuning helps maintain the relational geometry among neurons that encodes semantics. This insight leads to designing finetuning transforms that keep pairwise angles intact while still allowing controlled changes needed for downstream tasks.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Preserving Pairwise Angles Via Layer-Shared Rotations

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) adapts a pretrained network by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations that preserve pairwise neuron angles, and thus preserve hyperspherical energy exactly. Consider a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0=\{\bm{w}^0_1,\dots,\bm{w}^0_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each neuron is a column vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The hyperspherical energy is defined by
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j}\left\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\right\|^{-1},\quad \hat{\bm{w}}_i := \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
OFT directly enforces $\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ by restricting $\bm{W}$ to be an orthogonal transform of $\bm{W}^0$, namely $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles, the hyperspherical energy difference in
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|
\]
can be driven to zero by choosing an appropriate $\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)$. The forward pass changes from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\quad \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
Intuitively, OFT rotates or reflects the neuron directions within a layer as a coherent, shared transform that preserves their pairwise angular relationships. This yields a sweet spot between flexibility—changing directions to modify semantics—and regularity—preserving the relational geometry that encodes pretrained knowledge. Initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ ensures finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model, analogous to zero initialization in additive methods, and avoids any unintentional drift at the beginning of training.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization: Cayley Transform and Block Diagonal Structure

While standard differentiable orthogonalization procedures such as Gram–Schmidt are computationally expensive, OFT parameterizes orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform for efficiency. Specifically, an orthogonal matrix in the special orthogonal group is constructed as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\quad \bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top.
\]
Here $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, and the transform yields an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ with determinant $1$. In practice, this restriction to the special orthogonal group does not hinder performance. This parameterization makes $\bm{R}$ differentiable with respect to $\bm{Q}$ and allows enforcing orthogonality exactly through the transform, rather than via approximate constraints.

To further reduce parameter cost, OFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ diagonal blocks:
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),\quad \bm{R}_i\in\text{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),\ \forall i,
\]
so that $\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)$ remains orthogonal. This sparsity structure reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and sharing blocks across channels can reduce it further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ while still preserving hyperspherical energy. This parameter-efficiency route contrasts with low-rank additive updates and is well-suited to convolutional and attention layers where channel-wise or shared structure has intuitive interpretations.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: Keeping $\bm{R}$ Near Identity by Constraining $\bm{Q}$

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) tightens OFT by explicitly limiting how far the learned transform deviates from the identity. The forward pass remains
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}
\]
with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, but COFT enforces the additional constraint
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon,
\]
thereby keeping the finetuned model within a small neighborhood around the pretrained model in transform space. The key insight is how to incorporate this deviation bound efficiently when $\bm{R}$ is parameterized via the Cayley transform. Using the Neumann series to approximate the inverse, one obtains
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
under the assumption that the series converges in the operator norm. This approximation reveals that small deviations of $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{I}$ correspond to small $\bm{Q}$ in norm. Consequently, the deviation constraint can be moved inside the Cayley parameterization, yielding an equivalent and more practical constraint on the skew-symmetric parameter:
\[
\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon',
\]
where $\epsilon'$ is a tunable hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$, and $\bm{0}$ is the all-zero matrix.

This establishes the central fact: in COFT, the quantity directly constrained to keep $\bm{R}$ near the identity $\bm{I}$ is the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$. By constraining $\bm{Q}$ rather than $\bm{R}$ explicitly, one can apply projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ to enforce the bound efficiently. Moreover, identity initialization for $\bm{R}$ becomes immediate by setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start of training, because $(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$.

This design couples two stabilizing principles: exact preservation of hyperspherical energy through orthogonality, and explicitly bounded deviation from the pretrained model through a norm constraint on $\bm{Q}$. Empirically, COFT exhibits even better finetuning stability than OFT, and the hyperparameter $\epsilon$ provides a direct knob to trade off flexibility against regularity. As $\epsilon$ grows, COFT behavior approaches OFT, and as $\epsilon$ shrinks, COFT increasingly resembles the pretrained model.

# Intuition: Angular Preservation, Stability, and the Flexibility–Regularity Trade-off

The motivation to finetune neuron directions rather than magnitudes stems from the observation that angular information among neurons captures the semantics crucial for generation. When neurons are normalized onto a unit hypersphere, their directions encode the structure needed for high-fidelity and generalizable representations. Orthogonal transformations rotate or reflect all neurons in a layer coherently, acting as a layer-shared adjustment of the coordinate system that preserves pairwise angles and thus keeps hyperspherical energy unchanged.

At the same time, too much flexibility can destabilize finetuning, as large directional changes can destroy pretrained generative performance. COFT manages this tension by constraining the learned transform near the identity, making the optimization smoother and more predictable. The constraint on $\bm{Q}$ restricts the allowable rotations to those that are small in norm, thereby limiting the angular deviation from pretrained directions while still enabling meaningful adaptation. This explicit constraint, combined with identity initialization and efficient parameterization, yields fast yet stable convergence and alleviates the burden of carefully tuning learning rates and iteration counts.

# Practical Initialization and Optimization Under COFT

Because the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric and directly controls the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from identity, COFT initializes $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ to start from $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and thus exactly from the pretrained model. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed by the Cayley transform throughout training. The deviation constraint $\|\bm{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$ can be enforced by projected gradient descent in the parameter space, ensuring the update remains within the allowed neighborhood. Together, these choices yield an optimization process that is both efficient and well-regularized, preserving hyperspherical energy while bounding deviation from pretrained weights.

In summary, COFT implements constrained finetuning on the manifold of orthogonal transforms by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ through the Cayley transform and directly constraining the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. This design ensures that the learned transform stays near identity, preserves the relational geometry among neurons, and stabilizes finetuning for downstream control tasks in text-to-image diffusion models.
