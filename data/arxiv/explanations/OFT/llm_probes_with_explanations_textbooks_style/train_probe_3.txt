Chapter: Orthogonal Finetuning and the Parameter Complexity of Orthogonal Transformations

# Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models: Motivation and Principles

Large text-to-image diffusion models exhibit strong generalization and high-fidelity visual synthesis, but guiding them toward downstream tasks demands careful finetuning. The central difficulty is preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generative ability while enabling sufficient flexibility to adapt. Traditional approaches either update the entire model with small learning rates or attach low-rank adaptation modules. While these methods can be effective, they provide no principled guarantee that the semantic structure learned during pretraining is preserved. The paper approaches this challenge by identifying a structural property of neural representations—hyperspherical energy—that correlates with semantic preservation, and builds a finetuning strategy explicitly designed to maintain it.

The key observation is that pairwise angular relationships among neurons encode semantic information. When neurons are normalized onto the unit hypersphere, their pairwise similarities reflect meaningful structure. Hyperspherical energy, defined as the sum of pairwise hyperspherical similarities across neurons in a layer, captures this structure. The paper argues that an effective finetuning method should preserve hyperspherical energy, rather than rely solely on minimizing Euclidean deviation from the pretrained weights. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is proposed to achieve this by learning an orthogonal transformation per layer that rotates or reflects all neurons together, thus preserving their pairwise angles and therefore their hyperspherical energy.

This perspective reframes finetuning as a constrained geometric modification of the learned representation. Rather than adjusting each neuron independently, OFT adjusts the shared coordinate system in which neurons are expressed. By doing so, OFT preserves angular relationships while still allowing controlled semantic modification through joint rotations or reflections. This balance between flexibility and stability is crucial for adapting diffusion models in tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

# Hyperspherical Energy and the Rationale for Orthogonal Finetuning

To formalize the preservation target, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a neuron. Let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ be the normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
During finetuning, the paper proposes minimizing the difference $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ relative to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. Crucially, if there exists an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then all pairwise angles among neurons are preserved and the hyperspherical energy is unchanged. This property follows from the invariance of inner products and cosine similarities under orthogonal transformations. Thus, finetuning can be implemented by learning an orthogonal matrix per layer and applying it multiplicatively to the pretrained weights:
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x},\quad\text{with}\quad \bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
Here $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is the layer input and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. By initializing $\bm{R}$ to $\bm{I}$, the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained point and evolves through orthogonal transformations. In this way, OFT provides a principled mechanism to preserve the pretrained semantic structure while enabling controlled adaptation.

Intuitively, the paper links the importance of angular structure to empirical observations: angular differences are more aligned with semantic differences, and directional information can dominate magnitude in encoding semantics. By focusing finetuning on changing directions (but in a coordinated way that preserves pairwise angles), OFT targets the part of the representation most responsible for semantic control. At the same time, it constrains the finetuning process to avoid collapsing the pretrained model’s latent structure.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

Enforcing orthogonality during optimization can be computationally expensive if performed directly via methods such as Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization. To achieve efficiency, the paper adopts Cayley parameterization, which represents an orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$ via a skew-symmetric matrix:
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\quad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^{\top}.
\]
This parameterization avoids explicit orthogonalization per update and enables gradient-based learning on $\bm{Q}$. Although this restricts $\bm{R}$ to the special orthogonal group (determinant $1$), the paper observes that this constraint does not affect empirical performance in the studied settings.

Even with Cayley parameterization, however, a dense $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ would involve $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which can be heavy in high-dimensional layers. To improve parameter efficiency without sacrificing the invariance property, the paper proposes a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$:
\[
\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r)$ is orthogonal and $r$ is the number of blocks. This structure reduces the number of parameters while maintaining exact orthogonality of the full matrix and, consequently, exact preservation of hyperspherical energy. The block count $r$ controls a flexibility–efficiency trade-off: larger $r$ leads to smaller blocks and fewer parameters.

Furthermore, the paper notes that the blocks can be shared, i.e., $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$, which further reduces the number of free parameters. Even under these reductions, the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is preserved, and so is the hyperspherical energy invariance. This is a key design advantage: parameter efficiency does not compromise the primary invariance objective of OFT.

# Parameter Complexity and the Special Case r = 1

The parameter complexity of the orthogonal transformation depends on the structure imposed on $\bm{R}$. For a general orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ without any block structure, the number of free parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This corresponds to the case $r=1$, where the block-diagonal matrix degenerates to a single full block. In this setting, OFT learns a single orthogonal transformation over the full $d$-dimensional space of neuron features, preserving all pairwise neuron angles exactly while allowing maximum angular flexibility.

When a block-diagonal structure with $r$ blocks is used, each block has size $d/r$, and the total number of parameters becomes
\[
\frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)\frac{r}{2}=\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2},
\]
which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If, in addition, all blocks are shared, then the parameter count reduces further by a factor of $r$, yielding complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These expressions make explicit how the block count $r$ controls the parameter–efficiency trade-off of OFT.

The special case $r=1$ plays an important conceptual role. It represents the removal of block-diagonal parameterization, leaving a single full orthogonal transformation. In that case, the parameter complexity reverts to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, reflecting the full degrees of freedom of $\text{O}(d)$. This configuration maximizes expressivity within the invariance constraint, and the paper notes that despite the higher cost, it retains the same preservation guarantees because $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning and the Role of Deviation Control

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, there remains a second axis of control: how far the finetuned model should deviate from the pretrained one. The paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) to impose an explicit constraint on the deviation from identity:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x},\quad \bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon.
\]
This constraint ensures that the learned transform stays within an $\epsilon$-ball around the identity, thereby explicitly limiting the modification to the pretrained representation. To make this constraint compatible with Cayley parameterization, the paper uses a Neumann series approximation
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
which motivates moving the deviation constraint inside the Cayley transform as $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$. This projection onto a ball around $\bm{0}$ in $\bm{Q}$-space can be implemented with projected gradient descent. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ gives $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning.

COFT can be viewed as combining two explicit invariance and stability mechanisms: exact preservation of hyperspherical energy via orthogonality, and bounded deviation from the pretrained model via the $\epsilon$ constraint. The paper reports that this explicit deviation constraint tends to improve finetuning stability further, producing consistent training dynamics and reducing sensitivity to learning rate and iteration count.

# Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning: Extending Flexibility Without Breaking Invariance

The paper introduces a simple extension to OFT that scales neuron magnitudes while preserving angular relationships. Since hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, rescaling columns of $\bm{W}$ does not change it. Motivated by this, the re-scaled OFT uses the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^{\top}\bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ is a learnable diagonal matrix with positive entries. Here both $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{D}$ are learnable, enabling OFT to adjust both directions (via $\bm{R}$) and magnitudes (via $\bm{D}$) of neurons. Because magnitude is normalized out in the definition of $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$, the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. This extension adds a small number of parameters relative to the size of $\bm{W}^0$ while maintaining the central invariance property.

Although the paper primarily focuses on demonstrating the power of orthogonal transformations alone, it notes that this re-scaled variant generally improves flexibility and empirical performance while keeping the preservation guarantee intact.

# Comparing OFT to Low-Rank Adaptation

A useful perspective on OFT arises from rewriting the forward pass as
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}.
\]
In this view, OFT modifies the pretrained weights by an additive term $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. If $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank, then this update resembles a low-rank adaptation. However, OFT does not assume a low-rank structure in general; instead, it constrains the update to be generated by an orthogonal transformation acting on $\bm{W}^0$. The block-diagonal structure provides parameter efficiency not by low rank, but by sparsity in the pattern of interactions across feature dimensions. This stands in contrast to low-rank adapters that directly assume a low-dimensional subspace for updates.

The paper also provides a parameter complexity comparison. For a LoRA-style adapter with rank $r'$, the number of trainable parameters scales as $r'(d+n)$, yielding complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$ if $r'=\alpha d$ for some constant $\alpha$. For OFT with $r=\alpha d$ blocks, the parameter complexity scales as $\mathcal{O}(d)$ due to the block-diagonal structure. These comparisons emphasize that OFT’s parameter efficiency emerges from structural constraints on $\bm{R}$, not from constraining updates to a low-dimensional subspace.

# Practical Implications for Finetuning Diffusion Models

The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on subject-driven generation and controllable generation, showing fast and stable convergence, robust preservation of subject identity, and strong controllability. Importantly, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead: during inference, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ to form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and the model runs exactly as the pretrained model. This is an immediate benefit of using multiplicative weight transformation rather than additional adapter layers.

The choice of parameterization for $\bm{R}$ has practical consequences. Using a full $\bm{R}$ without block structure ($r=1$) provides maximum expressivity within the invariance constraint, at the cost of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Introducing $r$ blocks reduces the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and block sharing reduces it further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These options allow practitioners to navigate the trade-off between parameter budget and adaptation flexibility while preserving hyperspherical energy exactly in all cases.

The constrained variant, COFT, adds a second axis of control via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$. The paper reports that, with a suitable $\epsilon$, this explicit constraint can stabilize finetuning and make hyperparameters such as iteration count easier to set. Together, OFT’s invariance and COFT’s deviation constraint yield finetuning behavior that is both efficient and predictable.

# Conclusion: Understanding the r = 1 Complexity

At the heart of OFT is the parameterization of $\bm{R}$. When the block-diagonal parameterization is removed by setting $r=1$, the orthogonal matrix becomes fully dense and has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters. This yields a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This case represents the most expressive form of OFT under the hyperspherical energy invariance, preserving all angular relationships while allowing the largest set of global rotations or reflections. While more expensive than block-structured variants, it maintains the same semantic preservation guarantees and can be preferred when parameter budget allows and maximal flexibility is desired.

The paper demonstrates that, across full and structured parameterizations, OFT’s central invariance—preservation of pairwise neuron angles—remains intact. This property enables efficient, stable, and semantically faithful finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models, with the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ case for $r=1$ serving as a clear baseline for understanding the parameter complexity of orthogonal finetuning.
