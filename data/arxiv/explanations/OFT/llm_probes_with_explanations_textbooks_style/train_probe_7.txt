Chapter Title: Bias from Block Sharing in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Background: Orthogonal Finetuning and Hyperspherical Energy

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is introduced as a principled method to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks while preserving their pretrained semantic structure. The central premise of OFT is that the semantic generation ability of these models is closely tied to the angular relationships among neurons, which can be characterized by hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy is defined for a weight matrix by summing an angular similarity measure (for example, inverses of pairwise distances between normalized neurons) over all pairs of neurons within the same layer. Specifically, for a fully connected layer with weights represented by the matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, the hyperspherical energy is $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The intuition is that the directions of neurons (their angles on the unit hypersphere) encode semantic information more robustly than their magnitudes.

OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that multiplies the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and thereby rotates or reflects all neurons in the same layer by the same transformation. The forward pass changes from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, subject to the constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged, and the semantic structure learned during pretraining is retained. This structural preservation is a stronger guarantee than simply keeping the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$ small, which is the implicit assumption behind conventional finetuning with a small learning rate.

The paper emphasizes that changing neuron directions is the most effective way to semantically modify generated images. This is supported by a toy autoencoder experiment where angular information alone (without magnitude) recovers input images nearly perfectly, while magnitude alone contains no useful information. In this light, OFT’s focus on directional transformation—constrained to be orthogonal to preserve pairwise angles—provides a controlled, semantically meaningful means of finetuning.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization: Block-Diagonal Structure

Directly parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be computationally burdensome. To address efficiency, OFT adopts Cayley parameterization: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This provides a differentiable path to orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$, which the paper finds sufficient in practice. Yet, even with the Cayley transform, large $d$ still implies a large parameter count.

To further improve parameter efficiency, OFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks:
$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$,
where each block satisfies $\bm{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r)$ and the whole matrix lies in $\text{O}(d)$. This reduces parameters from order $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for full $\bm{R}$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ for $r$ blocks. The block-diagonal form preserves orthogonality and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy, while introducing a structural sparsity that improves efficiency.

A key optional step is block sharing: setting $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$, which reduces the parameter complexity further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This makes OFT extremely parameter-efficient. For instance, for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix, the paper notes LoRA with rank $r'=8$ has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r=8$ (and no block sharing) has $960$. When blocks are shared, the parameter count drops even further, magnifying efficiency gains.

The paper gives interpretations of this structure in convolutional layers. If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, which resembles the behavior of depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, the same orthogonal matrix is applied across channels; that is, OFT transforms neurons with a shared orthogonal transform across channels. This sharing is precisely what strengthens parameter efficiency and, as we will discuss, increases bias.

# Bias from Block Sharing: Why Making Blocks Identical Increases Bias

Block sharing makes all $\bm{R}_i$ identical, enforcing the same orthogonal transform on each block of the neuron space. In convolutional layers, this corresponds to transforming all channels with a single orthogonal matrix. In fully connected layers, it enforces the same rotation/reflection across all block partitions of the $d$-dimensional neuron space. The paper explicitly characterizes these structural choices as regularization: the block-diagonal parameterization is “a stronger regularization” of the orthogonal matrix, and in the concluding remarks, the authors state that parameter efficiency “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility.”

The term “bias” in this context refers to constraints that restrict the hypothesis space of possible finetuned models. By sharing blocks, OFT forbids the model from tailoring distinct angular transformations to different blocks or channels; instead, it enforces a uniform transform. This uniformity changes what the model can express during finetuning. Because OFT’s main effect is changing neuron directions while preserving pairwise angles, forcing identical transforms across blocks means all those directions are coupled by design. As a result, the model’s ability to adapt in fine-grained, block-specific ways is reduced, constituting an additional bias.

This bias emerges in tandem with gains in parameter efficiency. The parameter complexity drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ when blocks are shared, which is substantial. However, the trade-off is an increase in regularity that constrains expressiveness. The paper identifies a broader trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning: standard finetuning is the most flexible but unstable; OFT restores stability by preserving pairwise angles; and the block-diagonal parameterization further regularizes the orthogonal matrix. Sharing blocks strengthens this regularization yet further, tightening the constraints on the possible transforms.

This idea connects to the broader theme emphasized throughout the paper: good finetuning must preserve pretrained semantics while allowing targeted changes in neuron directions. Orthogonality preserves hyperspherical energy, ensuring semantic preservation. But the particular way orthogonality is parameterized determines how flexibly the model can adapt. Block sharing favors efficiency and stability by applying a uniform transform, but it limits per-block or per-channel variation. That limitation is precisely the additional bias that the paper points out.

# Intuition: How Shared Blocks Constrain Angular Adaptation

To understand the effect of block sharing more intuitively, recall that OFT’s main operation is rotating or reflecting neuron directions within the $d$-dimensional space. Without sharing, each block $\bm{R}_i$ can rotate its corresponding subspace differently. This allows OFT to align neuron directions in a manner tailored to the downstream task’s needs, while preserving pairwise angles within each block and overall preserving hyperspherical energy.

When blocks are shared, $\bm{R}_1=\cdots=\bm{R}_r$, every subspace is rotated by the same matrix. In convolutional layers, this is equivalent to applying the same angular transformation to every channel. The model no longer has the flexibility to adjust the angular transform differently for different channels or blocks. Consequently, fine-grained angular adaptations that would benefit certain parts of the representation may be unattainable. This constraint manifests as bias: the model is predisposed to changes that can be expressed as a single shared transform and is limited in capturing variations that require distinct transforms.

This constraint does not break OFT’s principal guarantee of semantic preservation—the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged because orthogonality is preserved, shared or not. However, it narrows the set of reachable transformed weights during finetuning. That narrowing reduces the model’s flexibility to separately modulate different neuron groups. The paper notes this limitation explicitly, stating that parameter efficiency of OFT, which relies on block-diagonal structure, “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility.” Sharing blocks strengthens this effect by removing variability even among the blocks.

This trade-off can be beneficial when stability and efficiency are paramount. The paper shows that OFT and its constrained variant COFT converge quickly and stably for subject-driven and controllable generation tasks, with fewer trainable parameters than many baselines and no additional inference overhead. Nonetheless, the authors also observe that OFT’s performance still improves as the number of trainable parameters increases, suggesting that relaxing constraints—such as avoiding block sharing—can lift the performance ceiling. The identification of this bias is therefore not merely theoretical; it is tied to practical performance considerations observed in experiments.

# Broader Trade-offs: Flexibility, Regularity, and Stability

The paper consistently emphasizes a central trade-off: finetuning must balance flexibility (the ability to adapt) and regularity (the constraints that preserve pretrained semantics and stabilize training). Standard finetuning with small learning rates is very flexible but can be unstable and lead to collapse or loss of identity. OFT preserves a deeper structural property—pairwise angles—through orthogonal transforms, achieving stability while focusing on semantically meaningful adjustments to neuron directions.

Within OFT, parameterization choices modulate this balance. The block-diagonal structure reduces parameters while maintaining orthogonality and hyperspherical energy, acting as a regularizer. COFT introduces an additional constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, which further limits deviation from the pretrained model. This explicit constraint tightens regularity and yields even better finetuning stability. Re-scaled OFT optionally adds a learnable diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ without changing hyperspherical energy, increasing flexibility with minimal parameter overhead.

Block sharing sits clearly on the side of regularity and parameter efficiency. By forcing identical $\bm{R}_i$ across blocks, it reduces parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ and thereby favors efficiency and stability. But the paper cautions that this “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility.” In practice, this means that while shared blocks can make OFT lighter and easier to train, they also constrain the model’s capacity to perform nuanced, block-specific angular adaptations that might be necessary for the most challenging or diverse downstream tasks.

The authors conclude by identifying open problems that arise from these design choices. The Cayley parameterization involves a matrix inverse that affects scalability, and while block-diagonal parameterization alleviates this, improving differentiable efficiency further remains challenging. They also point out OFT’s compositional potential: products of orthogonal matrices from multiple finetuning tasks remain orthogonal, raising questions about multi-task knowledge preservation. Finally, they highlight that achieving parameter efficiency without introducing bias—beyond what block-diagonal and block sharing impose—remains an important open problem. These remarks reinforce the central lesson: block sharing primarily increases bias by constraining the transform space, and careful design is needed to navigate the trade-offs among efficiency, flexibility, and stability.
