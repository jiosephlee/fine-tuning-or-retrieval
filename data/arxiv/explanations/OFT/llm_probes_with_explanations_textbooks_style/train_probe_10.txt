Chapter: Cayley Parameterization and Special Orthogonal Constraints in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning and the Role of Orthogonal Transformations

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled method for adapting large text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks while preserving crucial structural properties of the pretrained model. The central idea in OFT is to update a model by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to neuron weights. Concretely, for a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT replaces the forward computation
$\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$
with
$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
subject to the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$.

This approach is motivated by the observation that the angular relationships among neurons encode semantic information. OFT preserves those relationships by enforcing that all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, which maintains pairwise angles between neurons. This property is critical for preserving the pretrained model’s generative semantics, and it avoids the instability often encountered in unconstrained finetuning. Furthermore, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead, since the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ once finetuning is complete, yielding an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$.

# Hyperspherical Energy Preservation

The preservation of pairwise neuron angles is formalized through the concept of hyperspherical energy. For a fully connected layer $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\}$ with normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, the hyperspherical energy is defined as
$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$.
OFT is motivated by the objective of minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained layers:
$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|$.
The attainable minimum is zero and is achieved when $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only by an orthogonal transformation, that is, when $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal. In this case, the pairwise angles between normalized neurons are preserved exactly, and hence the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged.

This preservation is desirable for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models: it constrains the finetuning to keep the relational structure that encodes semantic knowledge, while still allowing directional changes that are empirically effective at modifying semantics. In contrast, minimizing hyperspherical energy itself is not suitable for finetuning diffusion models because it could destroy the pretrained semantic structures.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization via Cayley Transform

Enforcing the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ during training requires a differentiable parameterization of orthogonal matrices. While standard methods such as Gram–Schmidt are differentiable, they are computationally expensive in practice. To address this, OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization, which constructs an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ through
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$.

This parameterization is efficient and practical for optimization, and it guarantees orthogonality by construction. OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix to ensure that finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model, which corresponds to initializing the underlying $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix.

A practical concern arises in scaling the Cayley parameterization due to the matrix inverse. To mitigate this overhead, OFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks,
$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$,
where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \text{O}(d/r)$ is itself orthogonal. This significantly reduces parameter and computational complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and can be further reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ by sharing blocks across channels. Importantly, despite this structural restriction, each $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, so hyperspherical energy preservation is retained.

# Determinant Constraint and the Special Orthogonal Group

An essential fact about the Cayley parameterization used in OFT is that it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant equal to $1$, that is,
$\det(\bm{R}) = 1$.
Such matrices belong to the special orthogonal group. In geometric terms as stated in the paper, the determinant value $1$ corresponds to a rotation, whereas determinant $-1$ corresponds to a reflection. Because Cayley parameterization yields only matrices with $\det(\bm{R})=1$, it excludes reflections and confines the learned transforms to rotations.

In OFT, this determinant constraint does not hinder performance in practice. The goal of OFT is to preserve pairwise neuron angles and thereby the hyperspherical energy, while allowing semantically meaningful directional adjustments. Rotations suffice to preserve the pairwise angles among neurons, and thus they satisfy the core invariance property that OFT is designed to exploit. The finetuning retains sufficient flexibility to adjust neuron directions while maintaining stability, even though reflections are not accessible through the Cayley parameterization.

# Implications for Finetuning Stability and Semantic Preservation

Constraining finetuning to orthogonal transformations through $\bm{R}$—and in particular to rotations via the Cayley parameterization—strikes a balance between flexibility and regularity. On the flexibility side, rotating neuron directions directly addresses semantic adjustments, which are central to both subject-driven and controllable generation. On the regularity side, orthogonality preserves pairwise angles and thus the hyperspherical energy, which empirically correlates with preserving the pretrained model’s semantic capacity.

This balance leads to several desirable properties observed in OFT:
it prevents the destructive deviation from the pretrained structure that can cause model collapse; it results in smooth and predictable training dynamics; it supports faster convergence because updates target neuron directions on a hypersphere; and it avoids inference overhead by folding $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained $\bm{W}^0$.

The determinant constraint $\det(\bm{R})=1$ is consistent with these goals. The restriction to rotations maintains the angular geometry invariances that OFT relies on, while offering sufficient expressiveness to adapt the model to new tasks. In practice, this limitation does not degrade performance.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning and Deviation Control

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) further improves stability by explicitly limiting how far $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity. COFT uses the same orthogonal forward structure,
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
but imposes an additional constraint
$\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$,
alongside $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$.

Under the Cayley parameterization, COFT handles this deviation constraint by moving it into the space of the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$. Using the Neumann series approximation
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$,
one can enforce an equivalent boundedness constraint
$\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$,
enforced via projected gradient descent. This connection preserves the identity initialization and makes the deviation control explicit and efficient to implement.

Because COFT still relies on the Cayley parameterization, all learned transforms continue to satisfy $\det(\bm{R})=1$. Thus, COFT constrains updates to be small rotations around the identity, tightening the stability further without sacrificing the invariance of pairwise neuron angles or increasing inference cost.

# Parameter Efficiency with Block-Diagonal Orthogonality

The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ provides a direct means to control parameter count and computational cost while maintaining orthogonality. An unconstrained $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. An $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix reduces this to $d(d/r - 1)/2$, or $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, while sharing identical blocks across positions reduces it further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

These reductions are particularly advantageous for large diffusion models where attention and convolutional layers have substantial dimensionality. The orthogonality—and hence hyperspherical energy preservation—remains intact regardless of $r$ and block sharing. For convolutional layers, the block structure allows interpretations aligned with channel-wise transformations, which is a natural fit for convolutional representations.

# Re-Scaled Orthogonal Finetuning

An extension of OFT, re-scaled OFT, augments the orthogonal rotation with per-neuron scaling. Its forward pass is
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix of positive, learnable scaling coefficients. Because hyperspherical energy depends only on neuron directions, not magnitudes, the inclusion of $\bm{D}$ does not affect hyperspherical energy. It provides additional flexibility at negligible parameter cost, while retaining the key invariance properties provided by $\bm{R}$.

# Summary and the Central Fact

OFT adapts text-to-image diffusion models by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations that preserve pairwise neuron angles and, consequently, hyperspherical energy. This leads to stable and fast finetuning without inference overhead. For efficiency, OFT uses the Cayley parameterization
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
with $\bm{Q}$ skew-symmetric. A critical property of this parameterization, as stated in the paper, is that it can only produce orthogonal matrices with $\det(\bm{R})=1$, which belong to the special orthogonal group. Thus, the learned transformations are rotations rather than reflections. This determinant constraint aligns with OFT’s goal of preserving pairwise angles and does not negatively impact empirical performance. COFT further constrains the deviation from the identity through a bound on $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$, implemented efficiently by bounding $\|\bm{Q}\|$ under the Cayley parameterization. Together, these elements make OFT and COFT effective frameworks for controlled finetuning of diffusion models with principled invariance and strong practical stability.
