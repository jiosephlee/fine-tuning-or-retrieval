Orthogonal Finetuning and the Computational Cost of Cayley Parametrization

# Background: Finetuning Large Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Text-to-image diffusion models have reached a level of generative quality where subtle steering and preservation of pretrained capabilities become central challenges. Finetuning for downstream tasks—such as subject-driven generation (adapting a model to faithfully reproduce a particular subject with few images) and controllable generation (making the model follow external signals like edges or segmentation maps)—must balance two desiderata. First, training efficiency calls for few trainable parameters and rapid convergence. Second, generalizability preservation demands that finetuning not erode the diverse, high-fidelity performance earned during pretraining.

Standard finetuning, which updates weights by gradient descent with a small learning rate, tacitly minimizes the Euclidean deviation between the finetuned and pretrained weights. While intuitive, this alone can be too flexible and does not guarantee the preservation of semantic generative ability. A more structural measure is needed to characterize how finetuning perturbs the pretrained model’s internal organization. Motivated by empirical evidence that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information well, the authors adopt hyperspherical energy to quantify the pairwise relational structure among neurons. This measure suggests a principled path: preserve the angular organization of neurons by transforming them with orthogonal matrices shared across layers, thereby retaining the pretrained hyperspherical energy while still allowing meaningful task-specific adaptation.

# Hyperspherical Energy and Orthogonal Finetuning

Hyperspherical energy captures how uniformly neurons are arranged on the unit hypersphere. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\\bm{W}=\\{\\bm{w}_1,\\dots,\\bm{w}_n\\}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{d\\times n}$, define normalized neurons $\\hat{\\bm{w}}_i:=\\bm{w}_i/\\|\\bm{w}_i\\|$. The hyperspherical energy is
\\[
\\text{HE}(\\bm{W}):=\\sum_{i\\neq j}\\left\\|\\hat{\\bm{w}}_i-\\hat{\\bm{w}}_j\\right\\|^{-1}.
\\]
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is built to preserve this energy. Consider the pretrained weights $\\bm{W}^0$. If $\\bm{W}=\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0$ for an orthogonal matrix $\\bm{R}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{d\\times d}$ satisfying $\\bm{R}^\\top\\bm{R}=\\bm{I}$, then the pairwise angles among neurons—and hence the hyperspherical energy—are preserved. This leads to the OFT principle:
\\[
\\min_{\\bm{W}}\\left\\|\\text{HE}(\\bm{W})-\\text{HE}(\\bm{W}^0)\\right\\|,
\\]
whose attainable minimum is zero when $\\bm{W}$ differs from $\\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal rotation or reflection. In practice, OFT learns $\\bm{R}$ while keeping the forward pass
\\[
\\bm{z}=\\bm{W}^\\top\\bm{x}=(\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0)^\\top\\bm{x},
\\]
with $\\bm{R}$ initialized to $\\bm{I}$ so that finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained weights. Intuitively, OFT adjusts the canonical coordinate system of neurons in a layer: it rotates or reflects all neurons together, preserving their pairwise angles and thereby the pretrained relational structure that underpins semantic generalization.

This angle-preserving approach aligns with observations that neuron directions carry the bulk of semantic information. For example, angular information can nearly reconstruct input images in a toy autoencoder experiment, while magnitude alone fails to do so. Thus, changing directions—carefully and collectively—is an effective way to modify semantics for downstream control while maintaining pretrained structure.

# Cayley Parametrization for Enforcing Orthogonality

A central ingredient of OFT is keeping $\\bm{R}$ orthogonal throughout training. Naively enforcing orthogonality, such as with Gram–Schmidt, is differentiable but often too expensive for large-scale models. To address efficiency, the authors adopt Cayley parametrization to construct orthogonal matrices. The Cayley transform builds $\\bm{R}$ from a skew-symmetric matrix $\\bm{Q}$, i.e., $\\bm{Q}=-\\bm{Q}^\\top$, via
\\[
\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\\]
This map yields an orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$, i.e., an element of the special orthogonal group. In practice, this limitation (rotation but not reflection) does not hurt finetuning performance. Importantly, Cayley parametrization is differentiable, so $\\bm{Q}$ can be optimized by gradient-based methods while maintaining $\\bm{R}^\\top\\bm{R}=\\bm{I}$ exactly.

The Cayley approach therefore provides a principled, efficient alternative to more costly orthogonalization schemes, enabling OFT to preserve hyperspherical energy explicitly. At initialization, $\\bm{Q}$ is set to $\\bm{0}$, so $\\bm{R}=\\bm{I}$ and the forward pass begins with the pretrained weights. Training then updates $\\bm{Q}$, implicitly rotating neurons while preserving their pairwise angular relations.

# Why the Matrix Inverse Limits Scalability

Despite its advantages over direct orthogonalization, Cayley parametrization requires a matrix inverse through the factor $(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$. This inverse is a costly computational operation that limits scalability. In large architectures, $\\bm{R}$ may be high-dimensional, and computing $(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ must be done repeatedly and differentiably during training. The need for a differentiable inverse becomes particularly acute when optimizing $\\bm{Q}$ via gradient descent, as gradients must propagate through the inverse operation.

The authors therefore identify the inverse as a bottleneck. It introduces overhead that can slow training or constrain the size of the orthogonal transform. While Cayley parametrization improves efficiency compared to methods like Gram–Schmidt, the cost of the inverse remains nontrivial and can slightly limit the scalability of OFT. This recognition motivates structural simplifications of $\\bm{R}$ to reduce both parameter count and computational burden.

# Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices for Efficiency

To alleviate the cost associated with the inverse and improve parameter efficiency, the authors propose representing $\\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks:
\\[
\\bm{R}=\\text{diag}(\\bm{R}_1,\\dots,\\bm{R}_r),\\quad \\bm{R}_i\\in\\text{O}\\!\\left(\\frac{d}{r}\\right),~~i=1,\\dots,r,
\\]
so $\\bm{R}\\in\\text{O}(d)$ while each block $\\bm{R}_i$ is smaller. This structure preserves orthogonality yet reduces the number of trainable parameters and the computational complexity. An unconstrained $d\\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters (complexity $\\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). With $r$ blocks, the parameter count becomes $d\\left(\\frac{d}{r}-1\\right)/2$, i.e., complexity $\\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Further, sharing blocks across the diagonal lowers it to $\\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

By decreasing dimensionality per block, the inverse implicit in Cayley parametrization is computed on smaller matrices, which reduces overhead. Crucially, this efficiency does not compromise the core property of OFT: $\\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, and thus hyperspherical energy is still preserved. The block-diagonal structure also admits intuitive interpretations in convolutional layers. When the number of blocks matches the number of input channels, each block transforms a single channel, akin to depth-wise operations. When blocks are shared, the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels.

This block-diagonal strategy represents a practical balance between flexibility and regularity. It narrows the transform’s degrees of freedom enough to stabilize finetuning and reduce cost, while keeping sufficient capacity to adapt neuron directions for downstream tasks.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning and Neumann Series Insight

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) tightens control by adding a radius constraint that limits deviation from the identity transform:
\\[
\\bm{z}=(\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0)^\\top\\bm{x},\\quad \\text{s.t.}~\\bm{R}^\\top\\bm{R}=\\bm{I},~\\left\\|\\bm{R}-\\bm{I}\\right\\|\\leq\\epsilon.
\\]
This explicit deviation constraint complements the preservation of hyperspherical energy, further stabilizing finetuning. Enforcing the orthogonality is achieved via Cayley parametrization, but incorporating the deviation constraint directly on $\\bm{R}$ is nontrivial. To gain insight, the authors approximate the Cayley transform using the Neumann series. Under convergence in the operator norm,
\\[
\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}\\approx \\bm{I}+2\\bm{Q}+\\mathcal{O}(\\bm{Q}^2).
\\]
This reveals that constraining $\\|\\bm{R}-\\bm{I}\\|\\leq\\epsilon$ can be effected by constraining $\\|\\bm{Q}-\\bm{0}\\|\\leq\\epsilon'$ for a suitable $\\epsilon'$. Since $\\bm{Q}$ is the direct optimization variable, projected gradient descent can enforce $\\|\\bm{Q}\\|\\leq\\epsilon'$ straightforwardly. Initializing $\\bm{Q}=\\bm{0}$ yields $\\bm{R}=\\bm{I}$ at the start of training.

This approximation not only clarifies the relationship between the deviation of $\\bm{R}$ and the magnitude of $\\bm{Q}$, but also helps manage the scalability concern tied to the inverse: by keeping $\\bm{Q}$ small, $(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ remains close to $\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q}$ in the Neumann sense, moderating the effective complexity while enhancing stability.

# Practical Implications for Diffusion Model Control

OFT and COFT deliver tangible benefits in both subject-driven and controllable generation. In subject-driven finetuning, OFT variants converge rapidly and stably, preserving subject identity while following text prompts, even at large iteration counts where standard finetuning collapses. In controllable generation, OFT variants achieve faster convergence and stronger control consistency across tasks such as edges-to-image, segmentation-to-image, and landmarks-to-face, often outperforming adapter-based approaches and low-rank updates with fewer trainable parameters and no added inference overhead.

The orthogonality constraint is pivotal to this stability. Removing it leads to failure cases where models cannot generate realistic images or follow control signals. By preserving pairwise neuron angles, OFT maintains the pretrained structure that encodes semantics while enabling targeted rotation of neuron directions—a powerful and efficient route to task-specific adaptation.

Yet, the need to compute a matrix inverse inside Cayley parametrization is an acknowledged bottleneck. The block-diagonal design is a practical mitigation, reducing both parameter count and inverse dimensionality. In practice, these choices let OFT retain its core theoretical guarantees while scaling to the layers where finetuning is most impactful (e.g., attention weights), leading to smooth, predictable training dynamics and robust downstream performance.

# Open Challenges and Future Directions

While Cayley parametrization offers an elegant differentiable path to exact orthogonality, its reliance on a matrix inverse remains a scalable constraint. The authors highlight this as an open problem: how to speed up the inverse in a differentiable way. Their block-diagonal parameterization addresses the limitation to a degree, but improving parameter efficiency with fewer biases and finding alternative routes to enforce orthogonality without expensive inverses are important directions.

The compositional nature of orthogonal transforms presents another intriguing possibility. Orthogonal matrices from multiple OFT finetuning tasks can be multiplied, yielding another orthogonal matrix. Whether such compositions can preserve knowledge across tasks while maintaining hyperspherical energy and downstream performance is an open question.

Overall, the key fact is that Cayley parametrization, despite its practical benefits and theoretical rigor, fundamentally involves the matrix inverse $(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$. This operation is costly and slightly limits scalability. The authors’ design choices—most notably block-diagonal orthogonal matrices and COFT’s deviation constraint informed by the Neumann expansion—represent principled steps to manage that cost while retaining the semantic preservation and stability that make OFT effective for controlling text-to-image diffusion models.
