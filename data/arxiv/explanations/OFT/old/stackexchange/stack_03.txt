### Under what conditions does the Neumann series approximation for COFT hold in practice?
Question:
The COFT derivation uses a Neumann series to argue \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{Q}^k\), leading to \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)\). This requires \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| < 1\) in the operator norm. What range of \(\epsilon'\) is used so that this approximation is accurate enough during training? Does the implementation actually rely on the approximation, or is the exact inverse computed with autograd, with the Neumann series only used for intuition?
Answer:
In the COFT section the authors introduce the Cayley‐parameterization  

$$
R = (I+Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},\quad Q^\top = -Q
$$

and then observe that if $\|Q\|<1$ (in the operator norm) one can expand  

$$
(I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k
$$

so that  

$$
R = (I+Q)\sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k
  = I + 2\,Q + \mathcal O(Q^2)\,.
$$

That Neumann‐series argument is used purely to show how an explicit spectral‐radius constraint on $Q$ induces a small deviation $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ from the identity.  In practice, however, the code never *truncates* that series.  Instead it enforces

1.  **Exact Cayley transform**:  at each step one forms  
    $$
    R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}\,,
    $$
    by calling your framework’s matrix‐inverse op (e.g.\ `torch.inverse`) so that the orthogonality $R^\top R = I$ holds up to machine precision and autograd propagates through that inverse exactly.

2.  **Projection on $Q$**:  after each gradient update, $Q$ is re‐projected back into a small norm ball  
    $$
    \|Q\| \le \epsilon'\,.
    $$
    In effect this guarantees $\|Q\|<1$ (so that the Cayley map remains valid) and makes sure that $\|R-I\|\approx2\|Q\| + O(\|Q\|^2)\le \epsilon$.

In their experiments the authors found that setting $\epsilon'$ on the order of a few‐hundredths to a few‐tenths worked well (for instance, $\epsilon'\approx 0.05\text{--}0.2$ in many of their ablations).  Once $\|Q\|\lesssim 0.1$, the higher‐order terms $O(Q^2)$ are already below the percent level, so you really *only* need the Neumann expansion to justify why controlling $\|Q\|$ controls $\|R-I\|$.  But in code you always compute the *exact* Cayley form and never truncate any series.

To summarize:
– The Neumann series argument simply tells you “if $\|Q\|<1$, then $(I-Q)^{-1}$ exists and $R\approx I+2Q$ up to second‐order terms.”  
– In practice you project $Q$ to satisfy $\|Q\|\le \epsilon'<1$ (typically $\epsilon'\sim 0.1$), and you compute  
  $$
  R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}
  $$
  via an exact matrix inverse.  
– No series truncation is used in the implementation; the series is only there to guide how to translate an $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ constraint into a simple $\|Q\|\le\epsilon'$ projection.
