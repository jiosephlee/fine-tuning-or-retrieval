### Does preserving HE per layer guarantee preservation of global inter-layer structure?
Question:
OFT preserves HE of each transformed layer individually. But semantic information may rely on cross-layer alignments (e.g., how certain directions in one layer align with directions in the next). Is there any measurement of inter-layer angular consistency before and after OFT? Could preserving per-layer HE still disrupt global geometry, and if so, would adding constraints on cross-layer relationships help or overconstrain the system?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), each layer’s weight matrix $W_\ell^0$ is updated by a layer-specific orthogonal transform $R_\ell$,  
so that  
$$
W_\ell = R_\ell\,W_\ell^0\,, 
\quad R_\ell^\top R_\ell = I\,,
$$  
and hence the within-layer pairwise angles (hyperspherical energy) are exactly preserved. What this procedure does *not* do, however, is explicitly preserve the *relative* orientation of neurons *across* layers.

To see why, consider two adjacent layers in the pretrained network with weight matrices $W_1^0$ and $W_2^0$. In the original model the “message” from layer 1 to layer 2 is effectively  
$$
z_2 = (W_2^0)^\top\,z_1\,,\quad z_1 = (W_1^0)^\top\,x.
$$  
After OFT you get  
$$
z_2 = (R_2\,W_2^0)^\top\,(R_1\,W_1^0)^\top x 
      = (W_2^0)^\top (R_2^\top\,R_1)\,W_1^{0\top} x.
$$  
Unless $R_2 = R_1$, the intervening orthogonal matrix $R_2^\top R_1$ alters the *inter-layer* alignment of the feature bases. In other words, while each layer still “looks the same” internally (same angular geometry), the way layer 1’s outputs are interpreted by layer 2 is generally rotated by $R_2^\top R_1$.

The OFT paper does not report any explicit measurements of such *inter-layer* angular consistency. If you wanted to quantify it, you might for example:  
- perform Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) on the row-spaces of $(W_1^0,\,W_2^0)$ before fine-tuning and compare to CCA on $(R_1W_1^0,\,R_2W_2^0)$ afterward, measuring shifts in principal angles, or  
- compute the principal angles between the subspaces spanned by the neuron directions of adjacent layers and track how those angles change under OFT.  

Could it be that preserving *only* per-layer HE still allows the global geometry to drift in a way that matters for semantics? In principle, yes. If the downstream task depends on a very precise alignment of features across layers (say, certain neuron directions in layer $\ell$ must align sharply with directions in $\ell+1$), unconstrained orthogonal rotations $R_\ell$ and $R_{\ell+1}$ could disrupt that.

One could guard against this by *also* constraining the relative transforms—e.g.\ by encouraging $R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell\approx I$ (a soft penalty $\|R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell - I\|_F^2$), or even by tying $R_\ell = R$ across several layers. Of course, each extra constraint reduces the degrees of freedom in fine-tuning and risks *over*constraining the model so that it can’t adapt fully to the new task. Finding the right balance—preserving enough inter-layer geometry to maintain pretrained semantics while still giving the network the flexibility to learn new controls—is an interesting open direction that goes beyond the per-layer invariance studied in the OFT paper.
