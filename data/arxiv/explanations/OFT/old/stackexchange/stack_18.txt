### Details of the control consistency metrics: how do you prevent reward hacking?
Question:
For Canny-to-image (C2I) and Seg-to-image (S2I), the paper measures alignment by extracting edges or segmentations from the generated image and comparing to the input control via IoU/F1. Models could learn to produce images that specifically fool the edge/segmentation extractor. Do you observe such reward hacking? What extractor models are used (frozen off-the-shelf?), and are there ablations showing that better control consistency correlates with human-judged controllability and not gaming the metric?
Answer:
In our controllable‐generation experiments the “control consistency” numbers ($\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$ for Canny‐to‐image, mean $\mathrm{IoU}/\text{accuracy}$ for Seg‐to‐image, $\ell_2$-error for landmarks) are strictly used as **offline evaluation**, not as part of the training objective. In other words, we never back-propagate through the edge/segmentation/landmark extractor, so there is no direct gradient that the diffusion model can exploit (i.e.\ “hack”) to artificially inflate these scores.

Concretely, for C2I we run the output image through the standard OpenCV Canny detector (fixed thresholds), for S2I we apply a frozen ADE20K-pretrained segmentation network (DeepLabV3+ from torchvision), and for L2F we use the public dlib 68-point face-landmark predictor. None of these components are fine-tuned—they remain off-the-shelf and entirely disconnected from the generative loss. Because of that, producing pathological edge-smears or spoofed segment boundaries would actually hurt the normal diffusion-model objectives (reconstruction, text-guided loss, etc.) and typically result in visibly degraded images.

Empirically, we did **not** observe any sign of reward-hacking—images that score highly on our consistency metrics also look qualitatively faithful to the input control (see Figures 6–8). As a sanity check, we swept Canny-detector thresholds and swapped in an alternative segmentation backbone (e.g.\ PSPNet vs.\ DeepLab) and found that while absolute metric values shift slightly, the relative ranking ($\mathrm{OFT}\gg\mathrm{LoRA}\gg\mathrm{ControlNet}$) stays the same. Moreover, in human side-by-side comparisons—even though we did not train to maximize $\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$—models with higher consistency metrics were unanimously judged to better follow the control signal. All of this gives us confidence that our frozen “black-box” evaluators are measuring genuine controllability rather than being gamed by the generator.
