### How is OFT applied to convolution layers exactly, and how does block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) map to spatial kernels?
Question:
The paper mentions that for convolution layers, using the same number of blocks as input channels makes each block transform a unique channel, akin to depth-wise convs. Convolutional kernels have shape \((C_{out}, C_{in}, k_h, k_w)\). When reshaped as a matrix, which dimension is \(d\) (the one that \(\mathbf{R}\) left-multiplies), and which dimension corresponds to the columns/neurons? Do we flatten \((C_{in} \cdot k_h \cdot k_w)\) as \(d\) and treat \(C_{out}\) as \(n\)? How is \(\mathbf{R}\) implemented efficiently in this setting without large memory overhead?
Answer:
In OFT, every linear layer (whether fully‐connected or convolutional) is reparameterized as a left‐multiplication by an orthogonal matrix. Concretely, a conv kernel of shape  
$$
W^0\;\in\;\mathbb R^{C_{\rm out}\times C_{\rm in}\times k_h\times k_w}
$$
is first “rolled” into a 2-D weight matrix of size
$$
d\times n
\quad\text{with}\quad
d = C_{\rm in}\,k_h\,k_w,\quad n = C_{\rm out}.
$$
Each of the $n$ columns corresponds to one output channel’s flattened $C_{\rm in}\!\times k_h\times k_w$ filter, and so we rewrite
$$
W^0_{\rm flat}\;\in\;\mathbb R^{d\times n}.
$$
OFT then replaces the forward pass
$$
z = (W^0_{\rm flat})^\top x
$$
with
$$
z = (R\,W^0_{\rm flat})^\top x,\qquad R\in O(d).
$$
This guarantees $\|{\rm HE}(R\,W^0_{\rm flat})-{\rm HE}(W^0_{\rm flat})\|=0$, i.e.\ hyperspherical energy is exactly preserved.

Parameter-and-compute efficiency come from two observations:

1. **Block-diagonal $R$.** Instead of a full $d\times d$ orthogonal, we choose  
   $$
   R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)\,,\quad
   R_i\in O\!\bigl(\tfrac d r\bigr).
   $$
   A very natural choice is $r=C_{\rm in}$, so that each block acts on exactly one input channel’s spatial footprint. In that case  
   $$
     \frac dr \;=\; k_h\,k_w
   $$
   and each $R_i\in O(k_h\,k_w)$ rotates the $k_h\times k_w$ weights of channel $i$. Viewed in standard conv terminology, this is exactly a _depth-wise_ orthogonal transform over each channel’s spatial kernel.

2. **Cayley parametrization of each block.** To keep each $R_i$ orthogonal we do not store $R_i$ directly but parametrize a small skew-symmetric $Q_i\in\mathbb R^{(k_hk_w)\times(k_hk_w)}$ and set
   $$
     R_i \;=\; (I + Q_i)\,(I - Q_i)^{-1}.
   $$
   Since $k_hk_w$ is typically small (e.g.\ $3\times3=9$), inverting the $(I-Q_i)$ block is cheap and requires only $\mathcal O((k_hk_w)^3)$ flops _per_ channel per update. Moreover, we only ever store the $\tfrac{k_hk_w(k_hk_w-1)}2$ parameters of $Q_i$, never a full $d\times d$ matrix.

Implementation sketch at finetuning time:

– Reshape $W^0$ to $(C_{\rm in},\,k_hk_w,\,C_{\rm out})$.  
– For each channel $i=1,\dots,C_{\rm in}$, form $R_i$ from $Q_i$, then do
  $$
    W_i \;=\; R_i\,W^0_i,
  $$
  where $W^0_i\in\mathbb R^{(k_hk_w)\times C_{\rm out}}$ is the slice of weights for input channel $i$.  
– Stack back to $(C_{\rm out},\,C_{\rm in},\,k_h,\,k_w)$.

At inference, one simply _folds_ $R$ into $W^0$ once and uses the resulting conv kernels normally, so there is zero extra cost at test time. This whole procedure is laid out in Section 3.2 (“Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization”) and the discussion of convolutional kernels in Section 3.4 of the paper.
