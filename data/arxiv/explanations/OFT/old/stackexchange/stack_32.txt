### How are hyperparameters \(\epsilon\) (COFT) and block count \(r\) selected in practice?
Question:
The paper suggests COFT is less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count with a proper \(\epsilon\). In practical workflows, how do you set \(\epsilon\) and \(r\) per layer? Is there an automated schedule for \(\epsilon\) (e.g., annealing) that improves stability? Any guidance on per-layer \(\epsilon\) based on layer depth, width \(d\), or role (self vs cross attention)?
Answer:
In the original COFT paper the authors treat both the block‐count $r$ and the deviation radius $\epsilon$ as ordinary tuning knobs, and in practice they find that a single global choice of each works surprisingly well across all the finetuned layers. There is no “magic formula” for setting them, nor do the authors introduce an annealing‐style schedule for $\epsilon$—both $r$ and $\epsilon$ are simply chosen by light grid search on a small hold‐out set of prompt‐image pairs (or control maps) and then held fixed. What follows is the pragmatic recipe distilled from the paper’s text and experiments:

1. Choosing the block count $r$  
   – **What it controls**: $r$ partitions your $d$‐dimensional neurons into $r$ blocks of size $d/r$, so it directly governs how many free parameters your orthogonal transform has (¶ 3.2). A larger $r$ gives more flexibility but also more parameters and a looser angle‐preservation prior; a smaller $r$ more strictly regularizes the layer.  
   – **Default rule of thumb**: pick $r$ so that $d/r$ is on the order of $16$–$64$. In the paper’s toy example with $d=128$ they set $r=8$ (block size $16$) and get $\approx 960$ extra parameters—comparable to a LoRA rank of 8 (see § 3.2). In your own model you can scale $r$ proportionally: if you have a $1024$-dim feedforward layer, $r=16$ gives blocks of size $64$.  
   – **Per-layer variation**: the authors did not find it necessary to vary $r$ between self-attention vs. cross-attention or early vs. late layers—one $r$ across all attention and MLP blocks was enough to outperform LoRA and DreamBooth baselines. If you did want to experiment, you would generally shrink $r$ (stronger regularization) in the earliest layers and enlarge it slightly in top layers that need more semantic flexibility.

2. Picking the deviation bound $\epsilon$  
   – **What it controls**: $\epsilon$ bounds $\lVert R - I \rVert$ so that your learned orthogonal matrix $R$ stays within $\epsilon$ of the identity (§ 3.3). Very small $\epsilon \approx 0$ means “almost no change,” very large $\epsilon$ recovers unconstrained OFT.  
   – **How to tune**: sweep $\epsilon$ on a log scale—common values are $0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5$. The paper’s Figure 5 shows that performance rises quickly from $\epsilon \approx 0.01$ up to around $\epsilon \approx 0.1$–$0.2$, and then plateaus toward the OFT curve. Their stable final runs typically use $\epsilon \in [0.1, 0.3]$.  
   – **No annealing in the paper**: COFT as described holds $\epsilon$ constant. The authors note in § 3.3 that you could imagine annealing $\epsilon$ (or its $Q$-matrix proxy) but they did not explore time‐varying schedules and leave that as future work.  
   – **Per-layer $\epsilon$**: again, the default is a single $\epsilon$ for all layers. If you wanted to bias deeper layers more strongly toward the pretrained model, you could gradually shrink $\epsilon$ for those layers—however the paper shows you get very robust stability simply by fixing $\epsilon$ globally.

3. Practical workflow  
   – **Step 1**: Decide how many layers you’ll finetune (e.g. only Q/K/V projections or every Transformer block).  
   – **Step 2**: Pick a mid‐range $r$ so that each block is somewhere between $16$–$64$ dims (so that $r \approx d/16$–$d/64$).  
   – **Step 3**: Do a small grid search on $\epsilon \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$ using your usual validation signal (subject identity metrics or control consistency).  
   – **Step 4**: Once you identify the smallest $\epsilon$ that still gives you on‐target convergence speed (usually around $0.1$), lock it in. The paper reports that with a “proper” $\epsilon$ COFT becomes nearly insensitive to learning‐rate and iteration count, so you won’t have to babysit your schedule.  

In short, COFT’s stability comes from making $\epsilon$ a hard‐wired radius constraint, but that radius need not be layer‐specific or slowly annealed in. A single, modest $\epsilon$ ($\approx 0.1$) plus a block count $r$ chosen so that your orthogonal blocks have size $16$–$64$ dimensions already nails the sweet spot between flexibility and preserving the pretrained geometry.
