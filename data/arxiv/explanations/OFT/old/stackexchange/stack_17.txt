### What norm preservation properties of orthogonal transforms matter most for diffusion training?
Question:
Orthogonal matrices preserve vector norms and pairwise angles. In diffusion U-Nets, stability and Lipschitz properties are important for training. Is there any evidence that OFT’s norm-preserving updates (as opposed to general small Euclidean updates) reduce gradient explosion/vanishing or improve conditioning of attention layers? Any comparisons with spectral normalization or orthogonal weight regularization baselines?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the single most important “norm-preservation” property is that each layer’s weight update is an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights. Equivalently, OFT enforces two things simultaneously:

1. **Unit spectral norm**: an orthogonal matrix $R$ satisfies $\|R\|_2 = 1$, so every linear layer remains exactly a $1$-Lipschitz map (no singular value drifts above or below one).  
2. **Angle preservation**: by keeping $W = R\,W^0$, all pairwise angles (and hence cosine similarities) among the rows (or columns) of the weight matrix stay exactly the same, so the so-called *hyperspherical energy* of the neuron set is unchanged.

Why does this matter in diffusion-U-Nets? Deep diffusion models rely on repeatedly applying U-Net blocks (self-attention, convolutions, MLPs) in a Langevin or denoising process. If any block suddenly has singular values much larger than one, activations (and thus gradients) can blow up; if they shrink toward zero, gradients vanish. By construction, OFT keeps every finetuned linear layer perfectly isometric—no expansion or contraction—so the Lipschitz constant of each block stays bounded at one. In practice this translates into:

 • **Stable gradient flow**: with operator norms fixed at one, neither vanishing nor exploding gradients accumulate through the many U-Net layers.  
 • **Better conditioning of attention keys/queries**: orthonormal key-query projections avoid degenerate directions, which keeps attention scores well-scaled.  
 • **Smooth optimization on the hypersphere**: since we’re literally optimizing over the manifold of orthogonal matrices, the loss landscape is less prone to sharp “valleys” or plateaus that plague unconstrained Euclidean updates.

Empirically, the OFT paper does not report a direct measurement of gradient norms or a head-to-head against methods like spectral normalization (SN) or generic orthogonal-regularization. However, it offers two strong pieces of evidence that the norm-preserving constraint is doing heavy lifting:

1. **Ablation without orthogonality** (their Figure 3): if you remove the orthogonality constraint but keep all other hyperparameters the same, finetuning collapses and images become nonsensical.  
2. **Convergence & stability plots** (e.g. Figures 2 & 4): both OFT and its radius-constrained variant COFT reach good control in a few hundred iterations, and remain stable even if you keep training longer. By contrast, DreamBooth’s vanilla finetuning and LoRA either converge more slowly or start to collapse once you exceed their “sweet-spot” number of steps.

In effect, OFT serves as an *implicit* spectral normalization of every finetuned layer: rather than adding an SN penalty or re-projecting onto the spectral ball, it simply re-parametrizes the weight update to be orthogonal. That gives you exact $\sigma_{\max}=1$ and $\sigma_{\min}=1$ throughout, which is arguably the strongest way to enforce Lipschitz control. Although the paper does not benchmark against explicit SN or other orthogonal regularizers, the dramatic gains in early-stage convergence speed, iteration-robustness, and final image fidelity strongly suggest that these norm- and angle-preserving properties are central to OFT’s success in diffusion-model finetuning.
