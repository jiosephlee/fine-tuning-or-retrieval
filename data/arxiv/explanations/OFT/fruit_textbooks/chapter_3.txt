# Adapter-based methods <|endoftext|> LoRA, Adapters)

Adapter-based finetuning methods introduce a small number of extra parameters and leave the majority of the <|endoftext|> model fixed. A representative approach is LoRA, which models the finetuning update to a weight matrix as an additive low-rank matrix. Concretely, for a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, LoRA parameterizes an additive update of rank $r'$ and the number of trainable parameters for that update is on the order of
\[
r'(d+n).
\]
This low-rank additive reparameterization is attractive because it drastically reduces the number of learned parameters, making finetuning cheap in memory and often effective in practice.

Adapters trade off flexibility for parameter efficiency: by restricting the update to a low-rank subspace, they prevent arbitrary large or high-complexity changes to the <|endoftext|> weights. This usually helps preserve much of the <|endoftext|> model’s behavior while enabling task adaptation. But the paper highlights practical and conceptual limitations. Empirically, LoRA sometimes fails to preserve identity or <|endoftext|> in subject-driven generation tasks; in examples reported by the authors, LoRA could lose subject identity <|endoftext|> generating the wrong subject attributes) even when tuned carefully. In controllable generation tasks, LoRA converges more slowly and reaches a lower performance ceiling compared to the orthogonal finetuning approach introduced in the paper.

That prior can be effective, but it is a different <|endoftext|> bias: it favors updates that are representable as small-rank additions. # Task-specific controllers (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter)

For controllable generation—where the model must respect supplementary signals such as edges, segmentations, or pose maps—researchers have also developed specialized controller modules. Two concrete examples discussed in the paper are ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. These methods finetune and add modules that process the control signal and inject the conditioning into the diffusion model. In practice they enable high-quality conditional generation that follows the external control inputs closely.

Despite their capabilities, task-specific controllers have notable drawbacks highlighted by the authors. First, many controller-based solutions add a substantial number of parameters and auxiliary computation, often increasing inference-time complexity. ControlNet, for example, involves nontrivial additional components that increase model size and computational cost. Second, the paper reports that adapter-style controllers tend to converge slowly and can exhibit abrupt learning dynamics: the controllability metric sometimes suddenly improves (“sudden emergence”) after several epochs rather than improving smoothly. This makes hyperparameter tuning and reliable training more difficult in practice.

Finally, while controllers excel at the specific control task for which they are designed, they introduce a heavier architectural change compared to lightweight finetuning approaches. The extra modules and their integration points add engineering complexity and <|endoftext|> overhead that may be undesirable when the goal is to produce a compact, deployable finetuned model.

To address the gap left by <|endoftext|> proximity, the paper motivates and defines a structural preservation criterion grounded in neuron relational geometry. The key insight is that angular relationships—how neuron weight <|endoftext|> point relative to each other on the unit hypersphere—encode critical <|endoftext|> information. \[

\]
HE captures the global pairwise angular relationships among neurons (it is a function of pairwise hyperspherical distance or cosine similarities). \[

\]

\[
