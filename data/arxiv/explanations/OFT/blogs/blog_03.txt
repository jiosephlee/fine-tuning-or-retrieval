# COFT: Guardrails for Stable, High-Quality Personalization

## Overview

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) is a targeted refinement of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) designed to make personalization of large text-to-image diffusion models (think DreamBooth-style subject-driven generation) more stable and predictable. OFT adapts pretrained weights by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms, preserving the pairwise angles between neurons — a property the authors show is important for keeping pretrained semantic abilities intact. COFT adds one simple but powerful extra constraint: keep those orthogonal transforms within a small radius of the identity. The result is a finetuning method that avoids collapse, reduces sensitivity to learning rates and iteration counts, and converges faster while preserving generative quality.

This post distills why COFT works, how it is implemented, and practical tips for using it in DreamBooth-style personalization.

## Recap: Why orthogonal finetuning (OFT)?

Two observations motivate OFT:

1. Angular information matters. Empirical work cited in the paper shows that neuron directions (angles) capture most of the semantic signal. A toy autoencoder experiment in the paper finds that the phase/angle of filters preserves visual content much better than magnitude.

2. Hyperspherical energy as a structural measure. The paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE) to describe the pairwise angular relationships among normalized neurons. HE is defined for a layer with normalized neurons $\hat{\mathbf w}_i$ as
   $$
   \text{HE}(\mathbf W) := \sum_{i\neq j} \| \hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j \|^{-1}.
   $$
   OFT enforces HE preservation by restricting finetuning to orthogonal transforms $\mathbf R$ so that the finetuned weights are $\mathbf W = \mathbf R \mathbf W^0$. Orthogonal transforms preserve angles (and thus HE), so OFT provides a principled structural regularization that keeps pretrained semantic relationships intact.

In practice OFT parameterizes a layer-wise orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ (initialized to identity) and optimizes it while keeping $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I$.

## What COFT adds: an explicit radius constraint

While OFT preserves hyperspherical structure, it still allows arbitrarily large orthogonal rotations, which can be too flexible for delicate personalization. COFT places an extra constraint that limits how far the orthogonal matrix can stray from identity:

- Forward pass: z = (\mathbf R \mathbf W^0)^\top x, with orthogonality constraint \mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I and bounded deviation \| \mathbf R - \mathbf I \| \le \epsilon.

This is the "guardrail": keep rotations small and controlled. Intuitively, preserving angles (OFT) prevents destruction of pretrained semantics; enforcing small angular deviation (COFT) prevents over-correction that can lead to model collapse or runaway behavior.

## Efficient parameterization and enforcing the radius

COFT uses the Cayley transform to parameterize orthogonal matrices efficiently. Let Q be skew-symmetric (Q = −Q^\top). The Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix with determinant 1:
$$
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}.
$$

To make the radius constraint tractable, the paper notes that for small Q we have the Neumann-series approximation
$$
\mathbf R \approx \mathbf I + 2\mathbf Q + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf Q^2),
$$
so the constraint \| \mathbf R - \mathbf I \| \le \epsilon can be approximately enforced by constraining \| \mathbf Q \| \le \epsilon' (with projected gradient steps on Q). Q is initialized to zero so the process starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

To control parameter cost and scale to large layers, COFT (like OFT) uses a block-diagonal structure for R: \mathbf R = diag(R_1, ..., R_r). The number of trainable parameters scales with block size choices; blocks can also be shared to further reduce parameters.

## Why the radius constraint helps (empirical and conceptual)

- Preserves pretrained semantics: OFT already preserves hyperspherical energy; COFT keeps the model near the pretrained point in weight space as well, explicitly encoding the usual finetuning bias (small deviation) but in orthogonal coordinates.

- Prevents collapse: In DreamBooth-style personalization, naive finetuning or overly flexible updates can lead to collapsed outputs or identity loss. COFT reduces this risk by limiting the magnitude of rotations.

- Less sensitive hyperparameters: Because COFT constrains deviation, training becomes less sensitive to choices of learning rate and number of iterations. The paper shows that COFT converges stably across a wide range of iterations and learning rates, reducing the need for delicate tuning.

- Faster, stable convergence: Convergence experiments in the paper show OFT/COFT reach good control faster than LoRA and ControlNet baselines. COFT’s explicit radius constraint also improves finetuning stability compared to OFT alone.

## Evidence in subject-driven generation (DreamBooth setup)

The paper evaluates COFT in DreamBooth-style personalization (few-shot subject-driven generation). Key findings:

- Stability and convergence: Both OFT and COFT achieve stable finetuning behavior earlier (e.g., after 400 iterations) where other methods either fail to preserve identity or later collapse when trained longer. COFT is especially robust with a proper epsilon.

- Quantitative metrics: On identity and prompt-fidelity metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T) and diversity (LPIPS), COFT/OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on identity-preservation metrics while matching or slightly improving prompt fidelity and diversity.

- Qualitative results: COFT maintains subject identity and produces images that follow prompts accurately, even when other methods produce identity drift or prompt mismatch.

These results directly support the claim that an explicit radius constraint acts as effective guardrails for personalization tasks.

## Practical recommendations

From the methodology and experiments in the paper, here are practical points if you want to try COFT for personalization:

- Initialization: Initialize Q = 0 so R starts as identity and the finetuned model starts exactly at pretrained weights.

- Block size: Use a block-diagonal R. Smaller blocks reduce parameters and bias the transform; larger blocks give more flexibility. The paper uses block choices to balance parameter efficiency and capacity.

- Choosing epsilon: Epsilon controls flexibility. Small epsilon → behaves more like the pretrained model; larger epsilon → closer to unconstrained OFT. The paper shows moderate epsilons yield the best stability and performance. Enforce the constraint on Q (the skew matrix) via projected gradient steps to make it practical.

- Optimizer & LR: COFT reduces sensitivity to learning rate, but typical small-to-moderate learning rates used in DreamBooth-style finetuning remain reasonable. The identity initialization plus radius constraint allow you to run a larger number of iterations without risk of collapse.

- Inference: No extra overhead. Once training finishes, multiply R into pretrained weights to produce the final weight matrix W = R W^0. The resulting model runs at the same speed as the original.

## Relation to other parameter-efficient finetuning (LoRA, ControlNet)

- LoRA adds a low-rank additive update; OFT/COFT apply a multiplicative orthogonal transform. Both limit how much the pretrained weights change, but in different structural ways: LoRA constrains rank; COFT constrains angular deviation and preserves hyperspherical energy.

- ControlNet and adapter-based methods introduce extra modules and inference-time overhead. COFT does not: the orthogonal transform can be folded into the base weights at inference, so there is no runtime cost.

## Limitations and open points

The paper notes a few open issues that also apply to COFT:

- Cayley parameterization requires matrix inversion and is somewhat costly; block-diagonalization helps but there is room to improve the scalability of orthogonal parameterizations.

- The block-diagonal structure trades off flexibility for parameter efficiency; selecting block sizes introduces bias which may need tuning per architecture/task.

- COFT approximates the radius constraint by constraining Q; the Neumann-series approximation holds for small Q, so large epsilons push the approximation regime.

## Bottom line

COFT is a compact, principled addition to OFT that enforces a simple radius constraint around identity in orthogonal parameter space. That single constraint provides practical guardrails for personalization: it stabilizes training, reduces sensitivity to hyperparameters, prevents collapse, and preserves pretrained semantic capabilities — all with no extra inference cost. If you finetune diffusion models for subject personalization (DreamBooth-style) and find standard finetuning brittle, COFT is a conceptually simple option worth trying.
