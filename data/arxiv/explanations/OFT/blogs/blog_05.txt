# From Edges and Segments to Photos: OFT for Controllable Generation

Modern text-to-image diffusion models are powerful, but text alone can be ambiguous when you need precise image structure: a rough edge map, a segmentation layout, or a set of facial landmarks. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled finetuning strategy that adapts pretrained diffusion models to take these control signals and produce photorealistic images that faithfully follow them. This post explains how OFT converts rough controls (Canny edges, segmentation maps, face landmarks) into accurate images, why it’s more stable and efficient than prior adapters, and how the paper measures control fidelity with a new control consistency metric.

## The basic idea in one line

Instead of adding a new adapter or doing unconstrained weight updates, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal transformation R to apply to pretrained layer weights W^0, so the finetuned weights are W = R W^0. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles on the unit hypersphere, OFT preserves a structural property the paper finds important for keeping pretrained generative ability while adding control.

Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit small-radius constraint around the identity (‖R − I‖ ≤ ε), trading some flexibility for extra stability.

## Why orthogonality helps controllable generation

Two empirical/intuition pillars support OFT:

1. Angular information (neuron directions) encodes semantic structure. Toy experiments in the paper show neuron angles alone reconstruct image semantics far better than magnitudes, suggesting direction changes are the most effective way to alter generation semantics.

2. Orthogonal transforms rotate neuron directions collectively without changing pairwise angles; this preserves a measure called hyperspherical energy (HE), defined for a layer W with normalized neurons \hat{w}_i as HE(W) = sum_{i≠j} ||\hat{w}_i − \hat{w}_j||^{−1}. Preserving HE empirically keeps the pretrained model’s general semantic ability while allowing controlled, stable adaptation.

Because OFT restricts finetuning to orthogonal transforms, it hits a sweet spot: enough flexibility to incorporate control signals, but strong regularity that avoids model collapse and preserves pretrained generative behavior.

## Applying OFT to control tasks

The paper evaluates OFT / COFT on three representative controllable generation tasks using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model:

- C2I: Canny edge → image (COCO). The input is an edge map; the model should produce a plausible photo matching that structure.
- S2I: Segmentation map → image (ADE20K). The model must respect per-pixel semantic regions.
- L2F: Facial landmark → face (CelebA-HQ). The model must match facial pose and landmark layout.

OFT is applied to the same layers where LoRA adapters were evaluated, so comparisons are fair in parameter budget.

## Control consistency: measuring whether the control was followed

A key contribution is the control consistency metric: instead of just eyeballing results, the paper measures how well generated images reproduce the original control signal by extracting the control back from the generated image (using off-the-shelf detectors/segmenters) and comparing it to the input.

Examples of the metric per task:

- C2I: IoU and F1 between input edges and edges detected from the generated image.
- S2I: mean IoU, mean / overall accuracy between input segmentation and predicted segmentation from the generated image.
- L2F: mean L2 distance between input landmarks and landmarks predicted from the generated image.

This metric directly captures fidelity to the control modality rather than only image realism or CLIP alignment.

## How OFT performs vs. baselines

Baselines include ControlNet and T2I-Adapter (both adapter-style finetunes) and LoRA (low-rank additive updates). The empirical findings relevant to controllable generation:

- Strongly improved control fidelity: OFT and COFT achieve higher control consistency across C2I, S2I, and L2F compared to ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The paper reports higher IoU/F1 for C2I, higher segmentation accuracy / mIoU for S2I, and lower landmark L2 error for L2F.
- Faster, more stable convergence: OFT reaches comparable or better control performance in far fewer epochs. For the L2F task, OFT converged in about 8 epochs to the performance other methods required 20 epochs to reach. Finetuning dynamics are smooth; ControlNet-style sudden emergence of control (rapid, unstable phase) is not observed with OFT.
- Sample efficiency: OFT converges well with less data (e.g., performing strongly with only 5% of ADE20K in C2I experiments), showing better data efficiency than ControlNet and LoRA.
- Parameter efficiency and inference cost: OFT uses a parameter budget similar to LoRA (and much less than ControlNet) and, crucially, introduces no extra inference-time overhead — the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into pretrained weights to produce an equivalent network at test time.
- Qualitative quality: Generated images from OFT/COFT are not only more faithful to the control signals but also higher-fidelity and more geometrically consistent than those from baselines in the studied tasks.

## Practical considerations from the paper

- Efficient orthogonal parameterization: OFT uses a Cayley parameterization to produce orthogonal matrices and employs block-diagonal structure to control parameter count and computational cost. Blocks can be shared to further reduce parameters.
- Constrained OFT (COFT): Adding the explicit bound ‖R − I‖ ≤ ε (implemented approximately via constraining the Cayley skew-symmetric parameter Q) delivers extra finetuning stability and makes hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, number of iterations) easier.
- Optional re-scaling: A small diagonal scaling D can be learned (W = R W^0 D) to add flexibility without changing hyperspherical energy; the paper finds re-scaled OFT often improves results but focuses experiments on plain OFT to isolate the orthogonal effect.
- Layer choices: OFT is architecture-agnostic; in practice the paper applies OFT to attention weights (to match LoRA comparisons) and shows it can also be applied to convolutional layers with meaningful block interpretations.

## Why this matters

For developers and researchers who want controllable generation without training huge adapters or paying test-time costs, OFT offers:

- A principled way to preserve pretrained generative knowledge (via hyperspherical energy invariance) while adding control.
- Faster, more predictable training with fewer hyperparameter headaches.
- Stronger alignment to structure-based control signals as measured by the control consistency metrics.

If your workflow involves guiding a pretrained diffusion model with edges, layouts, or landmarks, OFT gives you a stable, efficient path to high-fidelity, faithful outputs.

## Limitations and open points

The paper notes a few remaining issues to watch:

- Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse; scaling this for very large layer dimensions is nontrivial (the paper mitigates this with block diagonalization).
- Block structure trades flexibility for parameter efficiency and introduces bias; finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is an open problem.
- Compositional finetuning—multiplying orthogonal matrices from multiple tasks—looks promising but whether such compositions preserve the abilities of multiple downstream tasks needs more study.

## Takeaway

OFT reframes controllable finetuning as a constrained rotation of the pretrained model’s neuron directions. That simple, principled constraint preserves the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities while enabling accurate, stable control from rough signals like Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks. With the control consistency metric, the paper gives a concrete, task-aligned way to show that OFT doesn’t just make pretty pictures — it makes pictures that follow the control.
