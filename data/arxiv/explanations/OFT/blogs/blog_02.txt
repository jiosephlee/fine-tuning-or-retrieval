# Angles Over Magnitudes: A Friendly Guide to Hyperspherical Energy

## Why this matters

When we finetune a large text-to-image diffusion model, the goal is usually two-fold: adapt the model to a new control or subject (few-shot subject-driven generation, or additional control signals like edges/segmentation), while not breaking the model’s pretrained ability to generate realistic, diverse images. Common practice tries to keep weight updates small in Euclidean norm, or to add small low-rank adapters. Those heuristics help, but they don't directly measure whether the model's internal "semantic structure" is preserved.

Hyperspherical energy gives a different lens: instead of worrying about magnitudes of neuron weights, it looks at the angles between neurons. Empirically and intuitively, those angles encode semantic relationships. The paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning" leverages this idea: keep pairwise angles stable during finetuning, and you more reliably preserve pretrained semantics while still being able to adapt.

This post explains that intuition, the key math idea, and why orthogonal (angle-preserving) finetuning works well in practice.

## The core intuition: phase (angles) vs magnitude

Think about a 2D Fourier decomposition of an image. The magnitude spectrum encodes how much of each basis frequency is present; the phase spectrum encodes relative alignment and structure. A classic observation is that much of the recognizable structure of an image is carried in the phase: if you pair the original phase with a random magnitude spectrum, you often still see the original image layout.

Translate that to neural networks: each neuron (a column of a weight matrix) has a direction and a length. The direction (normalized weight vector) is an angular object on a unit hypersphere; the length is a magnitude. The paper argues — and experimentally supports with a small autoencoder experiment — that neuron directions carry the bulk of semantic information, while magnitudes are less important for semantics. So, when you want to change semantics (e.g., make a model generate a specific person, or follow a new control signal), it makes sense to operate primarily on directions/angles.

But changing angles arbitrarily is dangerous: it risks destroying the pretrained relationships that capture general semantics. The sweet spot is to permit structured angle changes that keep pairwise relationships stable.

## Hyperspherical energy: a compact way to measure angle structure

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar measure of the pairwise angular relationships among a set of normalized neurons. For a layer with neurons {w_i} normalized to unit length as \hat{w}_i := w_i / \|w_i\|, one common form of HE used in the paper is

HE(W) := \sum_{i \ne j} \| \hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j \|^{-1}.

This sums a function of pairwise angular distances across all neuron pairs. Lower or comparable HE means the neurons are distributed on the sphere in a way that preserves their relative angles. If two models have the same HE for corresponding layers, their pairwise angular relationships (and thus a large part of semantic structure) are preserved.

Two useful facts:
- HE depends only on normalized neurons — it's insensitive to per-neuron magnitudes.
- Applying the same orthogonal transformation (rotation/reflection) to all neurons in a layer preserves all pairwise angles, and therefore preserves HE exactly.

That second fact is the gateway to the finetuning method in the paper.

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): preserve angles by construction

Instead of adding arbitrary additive updates or tuning every weight directly, OFT parameterizes the finetuned weights in each layer as

W = R · W^0,

where W^0 is the pretrained weight matrix and R is a learnable orthogonal matrix (R^T R = I). Because R is orthogonal, the normalized columns of W are simply rotated/reflected versions of the normalized columns of W^0, so all pairwise angles — and thus HE — are preserved exactly.

Why is this helpful?
- It explicitly enforces the HE preservation desideratum, addressing the semantic-preservation concern head-on.
- It concentrates the model’s flexibility on changing directions in a structured way (global orthogonal transform per layer), instead of unconstrained changes that can break generalization.
- In practice (per the paper), OFT yields faster and more stable convergence on subject-driven and controllable generation tasks, compared with standard finetuning and adapter methods like LoRA.

A few practical details from the paper:
- R is initialized to the identity so optimization starts from the pretrained model.
- The Cayley transform is used to parametrize orthogonal matrices efficiently via a skew-symmetric Q: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}. This yields orthogonals with determinant +1 (rotations).
- For parameter efficiency, R can be block-diagonal (several smaller orthogonal blocks) or even share blocks across channels, which reduces learnable parameters while keeping orthogonality.

## Constrained orthogonal finetuning (COFT): keep angles, but not wander far

Preserving angles can still allow large rotations that, while HE-preserving, move the model far from pretrained weights in Euclidean distance. To explicitly control that, COFT adds a radius constraint around the identity orthogonal matrix:

minimize under R satisfying R^T R = I and \|R - I\| \le \epsilon.

Operationally, using the Cayley parametrization R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) (Neumann series intuition), the constraint can be enforced approximately by constraining \|Q\|. COFT therefore combines two constraints: exact HE preservation (orthogonality) and limited deviation from the pretrained orientation (small \epsilon). Empirically this improves finetuning stability and makes hyperparameters like number of iterations easier to set.

## A small excursion: re-scaled OFT

HE ignores magnitudes, so you can safely pair orthogonal transforms with per-neuron positive scalings. Re-scaled OFT extends OFT to learn a diagonal scaling matrix D (positive entries) so the forward pass becomes

z = (R · W^0 · D)^T x.

This adds a small amount of additional flexibility (magnitudes) while keeping angle structure intact. The paper reports that this slightly improves performance with negligible extra parameters.

## How OFT compares to LoRA and ControlNet (short)

LoRA adds a low-rank additive update ΔW to W^0 (W = W^0 + A B). OFT instead composes W^0 with a structured multiplicative orthogonal transform. The two approaches trade off different inductive biases: LoRA enforces low-rank adaptation; OFT enforces angle-preserving, global per-layer rotations (possibly block-wise). Empirically in the paper, OFT/COFT converge faster, are more stable, and preserve subject identity and controllability with fewer finetuning iterations and parameters than LoRA and adapter-based control approaches like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter.

Important practical advantage: OFT introduces no inference-time overhead — you can multiply R into W^0 once after finetuning and use the model normally.

## Why preserving pairwise angles helps in practice

Putting the pieces together:

- Neural directions (angles) encode semantic relationships; magnitudes are less semantically informative.
- HE summarizes pairwise angular relationships across a layer.
- Orthogonal transforms preserve HE exactly. By restricting finetuning to orthogonal transforms (and optionally a small scaling or radius constraint), you let the model adapt directions but keep the pretrained relational geometry intact.
- That constrained flexibility yields faster, more stable adaptation for both subject-driven generation (few-shot personalization) and controllable generation (adding edge/segmentation/landmark guidance), according to the experiments in the paper.

## Takeaways and open questions

- If you care about preserving a pretrained model's semantic priors during finetuning, think angles not just Euclidean distance.
- Hyperspherical energy is a compact, interpretable measure of how the neuron directions are organized; preserving it is a principled target.
- Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained/rescaled variants) is a practical way to enforce angle preservation and was shown in the paper to improve convergence, stability, and controllability in text-to-image diffusion finetuning tasks.

Open problems mentioned in the paper (good things to explore next): making Cayley-based orthogonal parametrizations more scalable (matrix inverses can be costly), better parameter-efficient representations for orthogonal transforms beyond block-diagonalization, and whether sequences or combinations of task-specific orthogonal transforms can be composed while preserving multiple tasks' knowledge.

If you're experimenting with finetuning large generative models, try thinking in terms of angles first — and consider orthogonality as a useful, low-cost constraint to maintain the model's pretraining wisdom.
