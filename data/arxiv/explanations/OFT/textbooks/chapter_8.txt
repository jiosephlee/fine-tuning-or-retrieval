\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 8: Constrained and Rescaled Variants: COFT and Re-Scaled OFT

# COFT: orthogonality plus radius constraint around identity

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments the basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea with an explicit radius constraint that keeps the finetuned layer close to the pretrained parameters. The OFT forward pass for a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ was written as
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \qquad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]
so that the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles among neurons and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy. COFT adds a second, explicit constraint that limits how far $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \qquad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \qquad
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon.
\]
The purpose of this radius constraint is to combine two desiderata: (1) maintain the pairwise angular structure encoded by hyperspherical energy, and (2) avoid large departures in parameter space that could destroy pretrained generative abilities. In practice, COFT initializes at $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ (so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights) and then learns an orthogonal perturbation restricted to lie within the ball of radius $\epsilon$ around the identity. Empirically, the paper finds COFT to be even more stable than OFT, because the explicit deviation constraint makes learning-rate and iteration-count choices forgiving: small $\epsilon$ keeps behaviour close to pretrained, larger $\epsilon$ lets the model adapt more (and in the limit of large $\epsilon$ COFT approximates OFT).

# Constraining via the Cayley parameter Q with projected updates

To enforce orthogonality efficiently, COFT uses the Cayley parameterization. Any (special) orthogonal matrix can be written in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}, \qquad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}.
\]
This parameterization ensures $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (the special orthogonal group). The Cayley form is computationally convenient because we optimize over the smaller space of skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ rather than directly enforcing orthogonality constraints on $\bm{R}$.

Incorporating the radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ directly into the Cayley formula is nontrivial because $\bm{R}$ is a rational function of $\bm{Q}$. The paper uses a local linearization via the Neumann series to relate a small $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{R}$. If $\bm{Q}$ is small enough (so the Neumann series converges in the chosen operator norm), then
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]
Under this approximation, a constraint of the form $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ can be enforced approximately by constraining $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\|\bm{Q}\| \le \epsilon',
\]
for some appropriately chosen $\epsilon'$ that depends on $\epsilon$ and on higher-order terms. This is the practical route the paper uses: optimize $\bm{Q}$ with ordinary gradient steps and then enforce the deviation constraint on $\bm{Q}$ by projected gradient descent. Concretely, after each gradient step on $\bm{Q}$, project $\bm{Q}$ back into the norm ball $\{\bm{Q} : \|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\}$ (the paper leaves the concrete implementation of the projection to typical matrix-norm projections; operator or Frobenius norms are natural choices depending on implementation). The initialization $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ gives $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning, so the model begins exactly at the pretrained weights.

For efficiency and parameter-budget control, COFT (like OFT) commonly uses a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$ so that each block is parameterized via its own skew-symmetric submatrix of $\bm{Q}$. Block-diagonal Cayley transforms reduce the number of learned parameters while preserving the orthogonality property within each block.

# Controlling flexibility with epsilon and its effects on behavior

The scalar hyperparameter $\epsilon$ (equivalently the proxy $\epsilon'$ applied to $\bm{Q}$) governs a clear trade-off between flexibility and fidelity:

- Small $\epsilon$ forces $\bm{R}$ to stay extremely close to $\bm{I}$. The finetuned model therefore remains close to the pretrained one in Euclidean terms and maintains pretrained generative behaviour; the COFT updates are conservative and stability is high. This is desirable when identity or generalization must be preserved tightly (for example, when subject fidelity is paramount and only small adaptation is needed).

- Large $\epsilon$ relaxes the radius constraint so COFT can explore a larger set of orthogonal transforms; in the limit of very large $\epsilon$ the radius constraint becomes inactive and COFT behaves like OFT. This is useful when the downstream task demands more flexibility (for example, stronger controllability may require larger directional changes within the orthogonal family).

Empirically, the paper demonstrates that varying $\epsilon$ shifts behaviour smoothly between the pretrained model (very small $\epsilon$) and full OFT (very large $\epsilon$). COFT therefore provides an explicit knob to trade off finetuning stability versus expressiveness, which mitigates the common practical trouble of choosing a small learning rate or precisely stopping early in unconstrained finetuning. Because COFT makes the deviation budget explicit, it is often easier to pick robust hyperparameters: choose a conservative $\epsilon$ when you want to be safe, increase $\epsilon$ if the model is underfitting.

# Rescaled OFT: per-neuron positive scaling that leaves energy unchanged

Re-scaled OFT adds only a small, targeted form of extra flexibility on top of orthogonal transformations by learning nonnegative multiplicative scalars for individual neurons. The forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ is a learnable diagonal matrix with $s_i>0$ for all $i$. The learned orthogonal $\bm{R}$ still preserves pairwise angles among the normalized neurons, and re-scaling by $\bm{D}$ does not change hyperspherical energy because hyperspherical energy depends only on normalized neuron directions. Intuitively, re-scaling lets the finetuning procedure adjust per-neuron magnitudes (importance) while retaining the angular structure that encodes semantics. This can be important because magnitude changes may be useful in some downstream tasks even though angles carry the primary semantic load.

A few practical points from the paper: initialize the diagonal entries $s_i=1$ so the finetuned model starts at the pretrained weights; the number of extra parameters introduced by $\bm{D}$ is negligible (only $n$ scalars per layer), so re-scaled OFT remains parameter-efficient. The paper notes an erratum in the camera-ready text about the order of multiplication, clarifying that the correct implementation and experiments use $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ (not $\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$), so the diagonal scaling applies to output neurons after the orthogonal transform.

Empirically, the authors report that re-scaled OFT usually improves over vanilla OFT and that this variant is "generally better" while keeping virtually the same parameter budget. They use the original OFT in most experiments to isolate the pure effect of orthogonal transforms, but re-scaling is a practical, lightweight extension when additional flexibility is needed.

# When and why to prefer OFT vs COFT vs Rescaled OFT

Choosing among OFT, COFT and re-scaled OFT depends on the downstream priorities—stability, flexibility, parameter budget, and how strongly you must preserve pretrained generative abilities:

- If your primary goal is to preserve pretrained semantics while allowing directional changes across an entire layer, standard OFT is a strong default. OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy, converges quickly, and introduces no inference overhead because the learned transform can be absorbed into the pretrained weights at deployment.

- If finetuning stability and predictability are more important (for example, when you expect to run many finetuning trials with varying subjects or few-shot data, or when the pretrained model should not be disturbed much), prefer COFT. The explicit radius constraint makes learning-rate and iteration-count choices much less sensitive and reduces the chance of collapse or excessive deviation from pretrained behaviour. The paper finds COFT typically yields superior finetuning stability compared to OFT and other baselines.

- If you find OFT (or COFT) insufficiently expressive—e.g., the model correctly learns directional changes but still underfits because neuron magnitudes need to be adjusted—use re-scaled OFT. The diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ gives a small but effective extra degree of freedom that does not harm the hyperspherical-energy preservation argument and adds only $n$ parameters per layer.

Additional considerations that affect the choice include parameter-efficiency constraints and computational cost. Both OFT and COFT can be implemented with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices to limit parameter counts; block sharing further reduces parameters. The Cayley parameterization used by both variants imposes orthogonality with a matrix inverse; block-diagonalization ameliorates the computational cost but introduces a design choice (block size) that trades off flexibility and parameter count.

A practical recommended workflow based on the paper’s findings is: start with COFT with a conservative radius $\epsilon$ (and $\bm{Q}$ projection radius $\epsilon'$) to get stable, reliable finetuning that preserves generative priors. If the model underfits or needs more control expressivity, increase $\epsilon$ or switch to OFT. If you still need finer-grained adjustments to neuron strengths, add the rescaling diagonal $\bm{D}$.

Summary

COFT and re-scaled OFT are natural, complementary extensions of Orthogonal Finetuning. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint (implemented via Cayley parameterization and projection on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$) to trade off expressivity for stability and to make finetuning hyperparameters easier to choose. Re-scaled OFT augments the orthogonal transform with per-neuron positive scaling, providing modest extra flexibility while preserving hyperspherical energy. Together these variants give a simple, principled family of finetuning methods that maintain the pretrained model’s angular structure while allowing controlled adaptation for downstream tasks.
