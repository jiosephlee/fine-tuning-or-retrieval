In the setting used in the paper, the pretrained model is Stable Diffusion v1.5 and the finetuning follows the DreamBooth paradigm: a few images per subject are provided (typically on the order of only a handful), and the finetuning goal is to encode the subject into the model so that subsequent text prompts containing a dedicated token (or otherwise referring to the subject) produce images that preserve the subject identity while varying context, pose, background, etc. Second, class-level images (images of the broader class to which the subject belongs) are used to regularize the finetuning so the model does not overfit or forget the class prior. For robustness, the experiments repeat finetuning with multiple random seeds (the paper reports 30 different random seeds for each method) so that reported averages are not dominated by one favorable seed. Because the available subject images are scarce, the finetuning procedure must be sample-efficient and must carefully balance adaptation to the new subject against preservation of the pretrained model's broad generative capabilities. The superior subject-fidelity scores reflect the central hypothesis behind OFT: preserving inter-neuron angular structure (hyperspherical energy) during finetuning helps retain the pretrained model's semantic capacities that are important for identity preservation. The OFT approach is applied in this same few-shot training setup so that comparisons to DreamBooth and adapter-based methods (such as LoRA) are performed under the same data regimen and loss structure. Second, the multiplicative orthogonal update constrains the degrees of freedom compared to unconstrained additive updates, which empirically yields a smoother optimization landscape and faster, more robust convergence. Instead of performing unconstrained weight updates on the pretrained weights, OFT replaces the update mechanism for selected weight matrices by a learnable orthogonal transformation applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weights. \] To keep parameter counts comparable and to reduce computational cost, OFT uses a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ (shared or not) and a Cayley parameterization to maintain orthogonality efficiently. Because finetuning only on the few subject images tends to overfit and cause the model to forget the general appearance of the class (leading to degenerate or collapsed outputs), DreamBooth augments the subject-specific training with class photographs and a "prior-preservation" loss that penalizes the model if its outputs deviate from the class distribution when prompted with generic class prompts. In practice, LoRA is most commonly applied to Transformer attention matrices; therefore, to compare under equal conditions, OFT is applied to the same attention-related weight matrices. For the comparisons in subject-driven generation, OFT is applied to the same attention matrices that LoRA updates, so differences in performance are attributable to the orthogonal multiplicative adaptation rather than different insertion points. This two-part dataset is the same structure employed by DreamBooth: subject-instance examples to learn the new identity, plus class examples to enforce a prior that preserves diversity and prevents overfitting. Taken together, these quantitative and qualitative findings show that integrating OFT into a DreamBooth-style finetuning pipeline yields practical benefits: stronger identity preservation, better prompt adherence, faster and more stable convergence, and less sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. Qualitatively, OFT and COFT produce images that clearly retain subject-specific attributes—facial features, clothing details, and other idiosyncratic aspects—while placing the subject in novel contexts that match the text prompts. A notable practical property reported in the paper is that OFT (and its constrained variant COFT) yields much more stable finetuning dynamics across the number of iterations than standard unconstrained finetuning or adapter methods like LoRA.
