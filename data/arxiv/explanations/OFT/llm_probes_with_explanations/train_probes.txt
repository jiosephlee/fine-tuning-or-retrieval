In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the constrained variant COFT enforces $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}\le\epsilon$ so that the finetuned weights lie within a hypersphere of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, which geometrically primarily bounds the $\ell_2$ norm $\norm{\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0}_2$, i.e. the Euclidean distance. This is sensible because COFT modifies a pretrained layer via $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal and then explicitly constrains the deviation from identity, and hence for any neuron (column) $\bm{w}_i^0$ we have $\bm{w}_i=\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\norm{\bm{w}_i-\bm{w}_i^0}_2=\norm{(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{w}_i^0}_2\le\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}_2\,\norm{\bm{w}_i^0}_2\le\epsilon\,\norm{\bm{w}_i^0}_2$, placing each finetuned neuron inside an $\ell_2$ ball around its pretrained counterpart, and similarly at the matrix level $\norm{\bm{W}-\bm{W}^0}_2=\norm{(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0}_2\le\epsilon\,\norm{\bm{W}^0}_2$, i.e., the Euclidean drift is tightly bounded; the paper further shows via the Neumann-series approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}$ and the projected constraint $\norm{\bm{Q}}\le\epsilon'$ that keeping $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}$ small is operationally enforced while orthogonality preserves hyperspherical energy, so COFT explicitly combines semantic preservation (fixed pairwise angles) with a direct Euclidean-radius bound around $\bm{M}^0$, yielding improved stability and insensitivity to learning rate and iteration count.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors explain that OFT achieves parameter efficiency by exploiting a sparsity-inducing structure in its orthogonal parameterization, namely block-diagonal orthogonality. Specifically, instead of learning a dense orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) free parameters (e.g., \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom), OFT constrains \(\bm{R}\) to be block-diagonal, \(\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\ldots,\bm{R}_r)\) with \(\bm{R}_i\in \text{O}(d/r)\), which zeros all off-block entries and reduces the trainable parameters to \(\sum_{i=1}^r \tfrac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2} = \tfrac{d(d/r-1)}{2} = \mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\), and further to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) under block sharing (\(\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j\)). This structured sparsity preserves the key OFT invariance—pairwise angles among neurons and hence hyperspherical energy—because each \(\bm{R}_i\) remains orthogonal, while shrinking the parameter budget by eliminating cross-block couplings. In contrast to LoRA’s low-rank additive update with \(\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))\) parameters, OFT’s multiplicative, block-sparse orthogonal transform provides an alternative route to parameter efficiency via structured zeros rather than rank constraints, yet still acts full-rank within each block and can be interpreted in convolutions as channel-wise or shared channel transforms, further reinforcing efficiency without sacrificing the hyperspherical-energy-preserving property central to semantic retention.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', if you remove the block-diagonal parameterization (set $r=1$), the parameter complexity of $\bm{R}$ becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This follows directly from their efficient orthogonal parameterization analysis: an unconstrained orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)$ (i.e., with $r=1$) has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free parameters, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, and the block-diagonal strategy reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ by splitting $\bm{R}$ into $r$ independent $\text{O}(d/r)$ blocks; therefore, when $r=1$ the model reverts to a single full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with quadratic parameter count, regardless of the chosen orthogonalization (e.g., Cayley transform) since the degrees of freedom of $\text{O}(d)$ are intrinsically quadratic in $d$.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) variant adds an explicit $\\epsilon$-deviation constraint to keep the finetuned model within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model, thereby addressing the primary risk that plain OFT might still allow: deviation from pretrained behavior. This is sensible because while OFT preserves hyperspherical energy (i.e., pairwise angles among neurons) via a shared orthogonal transform $\\bm{R}$, it can still enact large global rotations/reflections that maintain angular relations but move the model far from the pretrained parameterization, potentially altering semantics; COFT curbs this by enforcing $\\|\\bm{R}-\\bm{I}\\|\\leq\\epsilon$ in the forward pass $\\bm{z}=(\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0)^\\top\\bm{x}$, implemented efficiently through Cayley parameterization $\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\\bm{Q}$ and projected gradient steps on $\\|\\bm{Q}-\\bm{0}\\|\\leq\\epsilon'$; using the Neumann series, $\\bm{R}\\approx\\bm{I}+2\\bm{Q}+\\mathcal{O}(\\bm{Q}^2)$ connects the bound on $\\bm{Q}$ to a bound on rotation magnitude. This explicit neighborhood constraint complements the energy preservation, tightening the flexibility-regularity trade-off: it limits angular deviation from pretrained directions without destroying pairwise structure, which empirically yields smoother, more stable convergence, reduced sensitivity to learning rate and iteration count, and behavior closer to the pretrained model when $\\epsilon$ is small, while recovering OFT-like flexibility as $\\epsilon$ grows.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors state that when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution. This follows from their block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization: if a convolutional weight tensor is reshaped so that the “neuron dimension” stacks input-channel subspaces (e.g., reshape weights to a matrix of size d×n with d = c_{\text{in}}·k_h·k_w and partition d into c_{\text{in}} blocks of size k_h·k_w), then choosing r = c_{\text{in}} yields R = diag(R_1,\dots,R_{c_{\text{in}}}), where each R_i ∈ \text{O}(k_h·k_w) acts only on the i-th input-channel subspace; the forward pass z = (R W^0)^\top x thus applies an independent orthogonal transform to the filter coefficients of each input channel without mixing channels, exactly mirroring depth-wise convolution’s per-channel, non-mixing operation. Because orthogonal transforms preserve inner products within each channel subspace and introduce no cross-channel coupling under the block-diagonal structure, the effect is to rotate/reflect each channel’s spatial kernel independently—an operation structurally analogous to learning separate depth-wise filters.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors note that Cayley parametrization requires a costly computational operation that limits scalability — the matrix inverse. This follows because their orthogonal parameterization explicitly constructs the transform as R=(I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1} with Q skew-symmetric, so every forward/backward pass involves computing and differentiating (I−Q)^{-1}, an operation that naively costs O(d^3) for a d×d layer and adds nontrivial memory overhead; while they alleviate parameter and compute burden by imposing a block-diagonal structure on R (reducing each inverse to O((d/r)^3) per block and overall complexity to O(d^2/r)) and optionally sharing blocks to further cut parameters to O(d^2/r^2), the need for a differentiable matrix inverse remains, which they explicitly identify in the conclusion as slightly limiting scalability and an open challenge to accelerate, and even their COFT deviation control—though using a Neumann-series approximation R≈I+2Q+O(Q^2) to move the constraint to ∥Q∥—does not remove the inverse from the core Cayley mapping used to enforce orthogonality.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', applying block sharing (making blocks identical) is described as primarily increasing the resulting models' bias. This follows from their orthogonal parameterization where the layer-wise transform is block-diagonal, \( \bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)\in\text{O}(d) \), and an optional sharing strategy enforces \(\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j,\ \forall i\neq j\), which reduces the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) but simultaneously imposes the same rotation on all sub-blocks (e.g., channels), thereby shrinking the hypothesis class. The authors explicitly frame this block-diagonal structure—and in particular its shared variant—as a stronger regularization that “limits the flexibility,” and in their concluding remarks note that parameter efficiency “inevitably introduces additional biases,” which matches the bias–variance intuition: fewer degrees of freedom (identical \(\bm{R}_i\)) reduce variance but increase approximation bias by constraining the finetuned model to transformations that may not capture layer- or channel-specific angular adjustments. In other words, sharing blocks enforces a uniform orthogonal transform across subspaces, preserving hyperspherical energy but disallowing sub-block-specific adaptations, which systematically biases the learned update toward a simpler, more constrained solution.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which improves finetuning stability by imposing an additional constraint that bounds the deviation of the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from the identity via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, referred to as the radius constraint. This is sensible because OFT already preserves the pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy) by restricting updates to orthogonal transforms $\bm{R}$, but without limiting how far $\bm{R}$ can move from $\bm{I}$, the model may still drift excessively from the pretrained weights and destabilize finetuning; COFT explicitly curbs this drift by enforcing a small neighborhood around the pretrained model, operationalized by parameterizing $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, approximating $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}$, and enforcing $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ via projected gradient descent (with identity initialization $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ to start exactly at $\bm{W}^0$), which effectively bounds angular deviations while preserving hyperspherical energy; empirically, this explicit proximity constraint makes training dynamics smoother and hyperparameters less sensitive (e.g., COFT avoids collapse in DreamBooth at 2000 iterations and shows predictable control with tunable $\epsilon$), achieving a better flexibility–regularity trade-off than unconstrained OFT or standard finetuning without adding inference-time overhead.

According to the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', in COFT the Cayley parameter that is directly constrained to keep $\bm{R}$ near the identity $\bm{I}$ is the matrix $\bm{Q}$. This follows from their use of the Cayley transform to parameterize the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, together with a Neumann-series approximation $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ that yields $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ for small $\|\bm{Q}\|$, so the COFT deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ can be enforced equivalently by constraining $\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\le\epsilon'$ and implementing this via projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ initialized at $\bm{0}$, which directly keeps $\bm{R}$ close to $\bm{I}$ while maintaining orthogonality.

According to the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the Cayley parameterization can only produce orthogonal matrices with $\det(\bm{R})=1$, which belong to the special orthogonal group. This follows from defining $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$, for which one can verify $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ (so $\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)$) and then characterize the determinant by the eigenstructure: the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ are purely imaginary and occur in conjugate pairs $\{\pm i\lambda_k\}$, so the eigenvalues of $\bm{R}$ are $\mu_k=\frac{1+i\lambda_k}{1-i\lambda_k}$ and their conjugates $\overline{\mu}_k$, each lying on the unit circle with $\mu_k\overline{\mu}_k=1$, implying $\prod_k \mu_k=1$ and hence $\det(\bm{R})=1$; equivalently, since orthogonal matrices have determinant $\pm 1$ and the Cayley map is continuous on the connected set where $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible and maps $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ to $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ with determinant $1$, the determinant cannot change sign without crossing a singularity, so the image lies entirely in $\mathrm{SO}(d)$.

According to the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', as the COFT parameter $\\epsilon$ approaches zero, the finetuned model tends toward the behavior of the pretrained model. This follows directly from COFT’s explicit deviation constraint $\\|\\bm{R}-\\bm{I}\\|\\leq\\epsilon$ in the forward pass $\\bm{z}=(\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0)^\\top\\bm{x}$: when $\\epsilon\\to 0$, orthogonality is maintained while $\\bm{R}\\to\\bm{I}$, implying $\\bm{W}=\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0\\to\\bm{W}^0$ and hence identical outputs to the pretrained model; equivalently, under Cayley parameterization $\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(\\bm{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\\bm{Q}=-\\bm{Q}^\\top$, the Neumann approximation $\\bm{R}\\approx\\bm{I}+2\\bm{Q}+\\mathcal{O}(\\bm{Q}^2)$ and the projected constraint $\\|\\bm{Q}\\|\\le\\epsilon'$ enforce $\\bm{Q}\\to\\bm{0}$ as $\\epsilon'\\to 0$, again yielding $\\bm{R}\\to\\bm{I}$; moreover, because OFT/COFT preserves hyperspherical energy, letting $\\epsilon\\to 0$ not only keeps the pairwise neuron angles unchanged but also minimizes any Euclidean deviation, which the paper empirically shows makes the COFT-finetuned model increasingly resemble the pretrained diffusion model as $\\epsilon$ decreases.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the authors note that when the number of diagonal blocks equals the number of input channels ($r = C$), each block transforms a unique neuron channel, making OFT's transform most akin to a depth-wise convolution. This follows because the OFT transform for a convolutional layer is applied via a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $\\bm{R}=\\operatorname{diag}(\\bm{R}_1,\\dots,\\bm{R}_C)$, where each $\\bm{R}_c\\in\\text{O}(d_c)$ acts only on the parameter subspace associated with input channel $c$, yielding $\\bm{W}'=\\bm{R}\\bm{W}^0$ with per-channel slices transformed independently: $\\bm{W}'_{:,c,:,:}=\\bm{R}_c\\,\\bm{W}^0_{:,c,:,:}$ and no off-diagonal mixing across channels. This mirrors the structural independence of depth-wise convolution, where the output for channel $c$ is produced by convolving only the input channel $c$ with its own kernel $\\bm{K}_c$ (i.e., $\\bm{y}_c = \\bm{K}_c * \\bm{x}_c$ without summation over $c'\\neq c$). In OFT, setting $r=C$ ensures the transform preserves this channel-wise decoupling while orthogonally rotating each channel’s neuron directions on its own hypersphere, thus aligning the operation with the semantics of depth-wise convolution: per-channel processing without cross-channel coupling, encoded algebraically by the block-diagonal structure $\\bm{R}$ that forbids inter-channel interactions.

In the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', under $r = r' = \alpha d$, the method that yields only linear-in-$d$ trainable parameter growth (parameter complexity $\mathcal{O}(d)$) is OFT. This follows from the paper’s parameterization analysis: OFT learns a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks, giving $d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters, i.e., complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$; setting $r=\alpha d$ yields $\mathcal{O}(d^2/(\alpha d))=\mathcal{O}(d)$. By contrast, LoRA adds a low-rank update with $r'$ and has $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters; with $r'=\alpha d$ this becomes $\alpha d(d+n)=\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$, which is superlinear in $d$ (quadratic if $n=\Theta(d)$). Hence, under the shared scaling $r=r'=\alpha d$, only OFT’s block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization achieves linear-in-$d$ growth, while LoRA grows at least as fast as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ when $n$ scales with $d$.

According to the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning', the parameter $\epsilon$ in COFT primarily controls flexibility. This follows from the COFT constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$ in the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, which explicitly bounds how far the learned orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity, thereby limiting the permissible rotation/reflection of neuron directions and thus the model’s departure from the pretrained weights; via the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}$, this becomes an equivalent bound $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ enforced by projected gradient descent, making $\epsilon$ a direct cap on the rotation magnitude. Consequently, smaller $\epsilon$ restricts updates to a tighter neighborhood around $\bm{W}^0$ so the finetuned model behaves increasingly like the pretrained one (high regularity, low flexibility), while larger $\epsilon$ relaxes the constraint so that COFT approaches unconstrained OFT (higher flexibility); the paper’s experiments (Figure~ref{fig:eps_coft}) corroborate that increasing $\epsilon$ yields behavior closer to OFT, whereas decreasing $\epsilon$ yields behavior closer to the original model, and the authors attribute COFT’s improved stability to this explicit flexibility control.
