Chapter Title: Orthogonal Finetuning and the Fourier Phase Analogy in Text-to-Image Diffusion

# Introduction
Text-to-image diffusion models have achieved remarkable generative fidelity, but adapting them to downstream tasks requires careful finetuning to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities. A key contribution of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is the identification and preservation of the angular structure of neurons, which the paper argues is central to semantic integrity in generation. The conceptual bridge to understand this choice comes from a classical observation in signal processing: in a two-dimensional Fourier transform, the phase spectrum, not the magnitude spectrum, predominantly preserves an image’s semantics. By analogy, the directions of neurons—specifically, their pairwise angles—encode and preserve semantic information in neural networks. This chapter develops that analogy and shows how it motivates a principled finetuning method that preserves the pairwise angular structure of neurons by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations.

We will first articulate the Fourier-phase analogy and its implications for finetuning. Then we will formalize hyperspherical structure and hyperspherical energy as measures of pairwise angular relations among neurons. Building on an invariance property of these measures under orthogonal transformation, we present OFT and its constrained variant COFT. Finally, we discuss efficient parameterization, the re-scaled extension, and the empirical consequences: fast, stable convergence and strong controllability without sacrificing semantic generation ability.

# From Fourier Phase to Neuron Directions
The paper motivates OFT with a simple but powerful analogy: in the two-dimensional Fourier transform, an image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. Empirically, the phase spectrum carries the semantic structure of the image. Even when the magnitude spectrum is replaced by a random one, the phase spectrum still allows the reconstruction to retain the original image’s semantics. This observation suggests that angular information—i.e., phase—preserves semantics, while magnitude contributes comparatively less to semantic identity.

Translating this to neural networks, the paper argues that neuron directions play the dominant role in encoding semantics. A neuron in a layer can be represented by a weight vector. Its direction corresponds to its normalized form, and its magnitude corresponds to its norm. If phase in the Fourier domain carries semantic content, then, by analogy, the direction of a neuron captures semantic content in feature extraction and transformation. This view is reinforced by prior empirical observations referenced in the paper that angular features characterize semantic gaps and that training with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere can preserve capacity and generalizability, implying that direction suffices to capture the most important information.

The paper illustrates this intuition with a toy autoencoder experiment. Although trained with standard inner products, at test time the authors compare reconstructions produced by using only magnitude information versus only angular information in the inner products. Angular information nearly perfectly recovers inputs, whereas magnitude fails to capture useful information. While this experiment is qualitative, it supports the Fourier-phase analogy: angles, like phase, preserve semantics, and therefore finetuning neuron directions is the most direct means to semantically modify generated images without destroying the pretrained model’s semantic abilities.

# Hyperspherical Geometry and Hyperspherical Energy
To leverage angular information, the paper adopts a hyperspherical viewpoint. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix
\[
\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
where each neuron is a column vector \(\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\). Let \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\) be the normalized neuron, which lies on the unit hypersphere in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). The pairwise angular relationships among the \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i\) define a relational structure on this hypersphere. To quantify this structure, the paper uses hyperspherical energy:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This sum of inverse distances between all pairs of normalized neurons characterizes the uniformity and arrangement of neurons on the unit hypersphere. The central hypothesis is that a good finetuned model should preserve the pretrained model’s hyperspherical energy, because preserving the pairwise angular relationships preserves the semantic structure that enables high-fidelity and diverse generation.

A naive approach to preserve \(\text{HE}(\bm{W})\) would add a regularizer penalizing deviations in this energy during finetuning. However, this would not guarantee exact preservation, and small deviations could accumulate or interact unfavorably with optimization dynamics. The key observation exploited by OFT is an invariance: if all neurons are transformed by the same orthogonal transformation, then their pairwise angles—and thus the hyperspherical energy—are preserved exactly. This invariance yields a simple and principled finetuning strategy that restricts updates to orthogonal transformations, preserving the hyperspherical relational structure while allowing controlled semantic adjustments.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Preserving Pairwise Angles
Orthogonal Finetuning leverages the invariance of hyperspherical energy under orthogonal transformations to produce a stable and semantically faithful finetuning mechanism. Let \(\bm{W}^0\) be the pretrained weight matrix of a layer, and let \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix satisfying \(\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}\). OFT replaces the standard forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
with
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, the normalized neurons \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i\) are rotated or reflected to \(\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}\), preserving all pairwise angles. Consequently, \(\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\) is preserved exactly, and the minimal hyperspherical energy gap objective
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|
\]
is achieved with equality to zero by setting \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\).

The Fourier-phase analogy explains why this should work: if neuron directions play the role analogous to phase in preserving semantics, then finetuning should primarily act on directions while preserving their relational structure. Orthogonal transformation does exactly that by jointly rotating or reflecting all neurons in a layer, which changes directions consistently but preserves pairwise angular relations. The paper further argues that orthogonal transformation strikes a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. While more general angular transformations could rotate different neurons by different angles, such flexibility risks destroying pretrained semantic structure. Orthogonality introduces a strong and effective regularization that preserves this structure.

To start from the pretrained model exactly, OFT initializes \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), so that the initial finetuned model equals \(\bm{W}^0\). During training, the orthogonality constraint must be enforced in a differentiable and efficient way, which motivates the parameterizations described next.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization
Direct orthogonalization procedures, such as Gram–Schmidt, are differentiable but computationally expensive in practice. The paper adopts Cayley parameterization to construct \(\bm{R}\) efficiently. One sets
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\bm{Q}\) is skew-symmetric, i.e., \(\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top\). This parameterization is efficient and guarantees orthogonality; its only limitation is that it produces orthogonal matrices with determinant \(1\), i.e., in the special orthogonal group. The paper observes that this restriction does not affect empirical performance.

Even with Cayley parameterization, a dense \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) can be parameter-inefficient for large \(d\). To address this, the paper proposes a block-diagonal orthogonal structure:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block \(\bm{R}_i\in \text{O}(d/r)\) is orthogonal. This reduces the number of parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\). Optionally, blocks can be shared across positions, further reducing complexity to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\). Crucially, regardless of block structure or sharing, \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal, preserving hyperspherical energy.

The paper contrasts OFT’s parameter efficiency with LoRA. If LoRA uses rank \(r'\) and the layer has size \(d\times n\), its parameter count is \(r'(d+n)\). If both \(r\) and \(r'\) scale with \(d\), the complexity of LoRA is \(\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)\), whereas OFT can be reduced to \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) with appropriate block sharing. A concrete example in the paper shows that for a \(128\times 128\) layer, LoRA with \(r'=8\) uses \(2{,}048\) parameters, while OFT with \(r=8\) uses \(960\) parameters without block sharing.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning
While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, further stability can be achieved by constraining how far \(\bm{R}\) deviates from the identity. Constrained OFT (COFT) imposes an additional radius constraint:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\ \ \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon.
\]
This constraint explicitly limits angular deviation from the pretrained model, striking a tunable balance between flexibility and stability. Enforcing the orthogonality constraint follows the Cayley parameterization. To incorporate the deviation constraint, the paper approximates the Cayley transform via the Neumann series:
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
under the assumption that the series converges in operator norm. Therefore, the constraint \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon\) can be approximately enforced by constraining \(\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'\). Because \(\bm{Q}\) is the optimization variable in the Cayley parameterization, this constraint is naturally handled via projected gradient descent. As with OFT, identity initialization is achieved by setting \(\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\).

COFT thus combines two explicit constraints: exact preservation of hyperspherical energy and bounded deviation from the pretrained model. The paper empirically observes that COFT improves finetuning stability further relative to OFT, and the hyperparameter \(\epsilon\) directly controls the flexibility-regularity trade-off. As \(\epsilon\) increases, COFT approaches OFT; as \(\epsilon\) decreases, COFT behaves more like the pretrained model.

# Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning
The Fourier-phase analogy suggests that magnitudes are less critical for semantics, but they can still be adjusted without changing the hyperspherical energy because normalization removes magnitudes. Motivated by this, the paper proposes a re-scaled OFT that learns a per-neuron scaling in addition to the orthogonal transformation:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}, \quad \bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n), \ s_i>0.
\]
Here, \(\bm{D}\) scales neuron magnitudes, which does not affect hyperspherical energy. This extension increases flexibility with a negligible number of additional parameters. While the main experiments focus on the original OFT to isolate the effect of orthogonality, the paper reports that re-scaled OFT is generally better.

# Why Orthogonality: Regularity, Flexibility, and the Phase Analogy
The paper provides experimental and conceptual evidence that orthogonality is the right constraint to realize the Fourier-phase analogy in finetuning. First, orthogonal transformation directly targets neuron directions, which the paper argues are the primary carriers of semantic content, analogous to phase in Fourier analysis. Second, orthogonality ensures exact preservation of pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy, thereby preserving the relational structure learned during pretraining. Third, orthogonality provides strong regularization that stabilizes optimization. Empirically, the paper shows that removing the orthogonality constraint leads to failure in controllable generation: the model collapses and loses realism and control accuracy. With the constraint, OFT converges stably and achieves accurate control.

Orthogonality also yields practical advantages. Because optimization proceeds on a smooth hypersphere manifold, the optimization landscape is improved, which contributes to faster convergence. The paper notes that OFT modifies the most effective direction of change—neuron directions—for semantic adaptation, which further accelerates training. Finally, orthogonality induces no inference overhead: after training, one can multiply \(\bm{R}\) into \(\bm{W}^0\) to obtain \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\), leaving the inference cost unchanged from the pretrained model.

# Connections and Comparisons
The paper contrasts OFT with LoRA. LoRA constrains updates to be low-rank additive perturbations, which prevents large shifts but does not preserve pairwise angles. OFT instead constrains the transform to be orthogonal, which is full-rank but structured, and therefore preserves pretrained information by design. One can rewrite OFT’s forward pass as
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = \left(\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\right)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
When \(\bm{R}-\bm{I}\) is low-rank, OFT yields a low-rank update analogous to LoRA’s formulation; however, OFT’s central constraint operates on geometry rather than rank. The paper also discusses that OFT is agnostic to architecture and can be applied to attention weights in Transformers and to convolutional layers, where block-diagonal structures admit intuitive interpretations.

It is also instructive to contrast OFT with orthogonal over-parameterized training from scratch, which minimizes hyperspherical energy to encourage uniformity and generalization in classification. The paper emphasizes a different objective in finetuning generative models: preserving the pretrained hyperspherical energy, not minimizing it. Minimizing the energy during finetuning could destroy pretrained semantic structures, which would be counterproductive for preserving generative ability.

# Empirical Consequences: Stability, Convergence, and Control
The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on two finetuning regimes: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. The results consistently support the theoretical motivation:

First, finetuning stability and convergence speed are improved. In subject-driven generation, both OFT and COFT converge faster and remain stable over many iterations, whereas baseline methods exhibit collapse or fail to preserve subject identity. This stability reduces the burden of carefully tuning the number of iterations or learning rates, as OFT and COFT are relatively insensitive to these choices, especially COFT with a suitable \(\epsilon\).

Second, controllability is strengthened. In controllable generation tasks such as canny edge to image, segmentation map to image, and landmark to face, the paper reports that OFT and COFT achieve faster convergence and superior control fidelity. A novel control consistency metric evaluates how well generated images adhere to input control signals by estimating control signals from generated outputs and comparing them to inputs. Under these metrics, OFT and COFT outperform adapter-based baselines and LoRA, often with fewer trainable parameters and no added inference overhead.

These empirical findings align with the Fourier-phase analogy and the hyperspherical perspective: by preserving the phase-like structure encoded in neuron directions, OFT enables semantic modifications (control) without sacrificing the pretrained model’s semantic generation capabilities.

# Concluding Perspective
The Fourier-phase analogy identifies neuron directions as the phase-like carriers of semantic content. Building on this insight, OFT constrains finetuning to orthogonal transformations that preserve pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy, thereby preserving the pretrained model’s semantic structure. COFT further constrains deviation from identity, strengthening stability. Efficient parameterizations via the Cayley transform and block-diagonal structures make OFT practical and parameter-efficient, and extensions like re-scaled OFT add flexibility without undermining the core invariance.

By finetuning the phase-like components—neuron directions—while preserving their relational geometry, OFT achieves a principled balance between flexibility and regularity. The result is fast, stable adaptation that maintains semantic generation ability and improves controllability, all without increasing inference cost.
