Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: Radius-Constrained Adaptation of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

# Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models Without Losing Pretrained Ability

Text-to-image diffusion models achieve remarkable performance in generating high-fidelity, semantically rich images from text prompts. When adapting these large pretrained models to downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation, a central challenge arises: how to modify the model to follow new signals while preserving the semantic generation ability learned during pretraining. Traditional finetuning methods often encourage small Euclidean deviation from pretrained weights by using small learning rates or by adding low-rank weight updates, but these strategies lack a principled guarantee that the pretrained semantics are preserved.

The authors address this by introducing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a finetuning method grounded in preserving the relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere. OFT imposes a strong structural constraint: the pairwise angles between neurons within a layer remain unchanged during finetuning, achieved by applying layer-shared orthogonal transformations. This preserves hyperspherical energy, a measure that captures the uniformity and pairwise similarity of neurons when normalized onto the unit hypersphere. Building on OFT, the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which further improves finetuning stability by bounding the deviation of the orthogonal transformation from the identity, thereby limiting how far the model can move from its pretrained state while retaining the angular invariance that safeguards semantics.

This chapter carefully develops the intuition, mathematics, and practical implications of COFT and its radius constraint, explaining how and why it stabilizes finetuning of diffusion models.

# Preserving Neuron Geometry via Hyperspherical Energy

To motivate OFT and COFT, it is useful to understand why neuron angles matter. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a neuron vector. Normalizing each neuron as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ maps all neurons onto the unit hypersphere. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is defined as
$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$,
which aggregates pairwise hyperspherical similarity (e.g., cosine similarity) and reflects how uniformly neurons are distributed on the hypersphere.

The authors hypothesize that preserving the pretrained hyperspherical energy during finetuning is crucial to preserving semantic generation ability. If the neuron directions encode the semantic structure learned during pretraining, then maintaining their pairwise angular relationships during adaptation should help retain the original semantics while permitting controlled modifications. In other words, finetuning should preserve the geometry of neurons on the hypersphere.

OFT enforces this principle exactly: by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the pretrained weights, it preserves all pairwise angles among neurons and thereby keeps $\text{HE}$ unchanged. Concretely, if $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ are pretrained weights and $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal (i.e., $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$), then setting $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ yields
$\min_{\bm{W}}\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|=0$,
because orthogonal transformations preserve angles and hence hyperspherical energy. This directly operationalizes the goal: adapt the model by rotating or reflecting neurons together, rather than moving individual neurons arbitrarily in ways that could distort their collective geometry and harm semantics.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Finetuning by Rotation and Reflection

In OFT, the forward pass of the finetuned layer is changed from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$,
with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. The orthogonality constraint ensures that angles among neurons are preserved. To initialize the finetuning from the pretrained model, the authors set $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of training, so that the initial forward pass matches the original network.

This approach targets the most effective means of modifying semantics: changing neuron directions while maintaining their relational structure. The authors show that angular information plays the major role in encoding semantics and that modifying directions in a controlled manner can alter generation output while keeping the learned structure intact. OFT finds a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity: it allows the network to move on the hypersphere manifold of directions (a smooth manifold conducive to stable optimization), but disallows arbitrary distortions of pairwise angles, avoiding the collapses that can arise in unconstrained finetuning.

To make OFT parameter-efficient, the authors introduce a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$. Specifically, they set
$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$,
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}$ is orthogonal. This reduces trainable parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (for a dense $\bm{R}$) to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and even to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ if blocks are shared, while preserving orthogonality and hence hyperspherical energy.

# Efficient Orthogonality via the Cayley Transform

A practical challenge in OFT is maintaining orthogonality during training. Standard orthogonalization procedures (e.g., Gram-Schmidt) are differentiable but computationally expensive. The authors therefore adopt the Cayley parameterization to efficiently generate orthogonal matrices. Let $\bm{Q}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley transform constructs
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$,
which is guaranteed to be orthogonal with determinant $1$ (i.e., a special orthogonal matrix). Although this excludes reflections (determinant $-1$), the authors find this limitation does not affect performance in practice.

The Cayley transform enables end-to-end differentiable training with an implicit orthogonality guarantee. By parameterizing $\bm{Q}$ (e.g., with block-diagonal structure to reduce parameters) and mapping it through the Cayley transform, the network learns orthogonal $\bm{R}$ efficiently without expensive projections or re-orthogonalization steps.

# From OFT to COFT: Bounding Deviation with a Radius Constraint

While OFT preserves hyperspherical energy and thereby stabilizes finetuning, large rotations can still move the model far from its pretrained configuration. To further improve stability, the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds a radius constraint that explicitly bounds the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from the identity:
$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$.
The finetuned forward pass becomes
$\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$,
subject to $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$.

This constraint limits the flexibility of finetuning to a neighborhood around the pretrained model, explicitly encoding the common finetuning intuition that smaller deviation tends to preserve pretrained ability. Crucially, COFT maintains OFT’s preservation of pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy while restricting how far the rotation can stray. In effect, COFT combines two explicit constraints: preserving angular structure (via orthogonality) and preserving proximity to the pretrained weights (via the radius constraint).

# Translating the Radius Constraint into Cayley Space

Imposing $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ directly on the Cayley-parameterized $\bm{R}$ is nontrivial due to the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ in the transform. To handle this, the authors apply the Neumann series to approximate the Cayley transform:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$,
under the assumption that the series converges in the operator norm. This approximation shows that, for small $\bm{Q}$, the deviation $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is approximately linear in $\bm{Q}$. Therefore, the original constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ can be moved inside the Cayley transform and expressed as a constraint on $\bm{Q}$:
$\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon'$,
for a suitably chosen hyperparameter $\epsilon'$. In COFT, the authors enforce this constraint on $\bm{Q}$ using projected gradient descent, which is straightforward because the constraint is a simple norm bound around zero. Identity initialization for $\bm{R}$ is achieved by initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of training.

This translation is the key step that makes the radius constraint practical: rather than constraining $\bm{R}$ directly, COFT constrains $\bm{Q}$ to remain within a small ball centered at $\bm{0}$. As training proceeds, updates to $\bm{Q}$ are projected back to satisfy $\|\bm{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$, keeping $\bm{R}$ close to $\bm{I}$ and stabilizing finetuning.

# Why the Radius Constraint Improves Stability

The radius constraint in COFT systematically limits the degree of rotation that can be applied to pretrained weights. Because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy, it already avoids destroying the relational structure among neurons. However, without a bound on how far $\bm{R}$ can move, finetuning may still explore distant regions on the hypersphere, potentially yielding undesirable behavior or training instability. The constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ reduces this risk by confining $\bm{R}$ to a neighborhood around $\bm{I}$.

This stabilization manifests in several ways:

First, it makes the training dynamics smoother and more predictable. By restricting the step sizes in the space of rotations (via bounding $\bm{Q}$), COFT prevents abrupt changes that might lead to model collapse or erratic behavior. The authors empirically observe that COFT yields stable convergence across iterations and is less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning steps.

Second, it eases hyperparameter selection. With a proper choice of $\epsilon$, COFT finetuning becomes robust to learning rate and iteration count, allowing practitioners to set relatively large iteration budgets without overfitting or losing pretrained semantics. The authors note that smaller $\epsilon$ makes the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained diffusion model, while larger $\epsilon$ increases flexibility and makes COFT resemble OFT. This provides an intuitive control knob: adjust $\epsilon$ to balance semantic preservation against task-specific adaptation.

Third, it maintains the benefits of OFT’s angular invariance. Even under the radius constraint, $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, and pairwise neuron angles are preserved. Thus, COFT retains the core mechanism that protects pretrained semantic structures, while additionally managing the magnitude of change.

# Optimization Mechanics: Projected Gradient in Cayley Space

COFT’s optimization is carried out over the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ that defines $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley transform. The training loop proceeds by computing gradients with respect to $\bm{Q}$, updating $\bm{Q}$, and then projecting $\bm{Q}$ back onto the feasible set $\{\bm{Q}:\|\bm{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'\}$ to enforce the radius constraint. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, updates and projections respect this structure. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed by the Cayley transform at every step.

This projected gradient scheme is efficient and compatible with the block-diagonal parameterization that reduces trainable parameters. Importantly, orthogonality is maintained without expensive re-orthogonalization, and the radius constraint is enforced in a simple norm ball around $\bm{0}$, avoiding complicated constraints on $\bm{R}$ itself.

# Practical Behavior and the Role of the Radius Hyperparameter

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ (and its Cayley-space counterpart $\epsilon'$) directly controls finetuning flexibility:

When $\epsilon$ is small, $\bm{R}$ stays close to $\bm{I}$, and COFT behaves more like the pretrained model. This setting is appropriate when strong semantic preservation is desired and the downstream task requires only subtle adjustments.

When $\epsilon$ is large, COFT allows larger rotations and increasingly resembles unconstrained OFT. This setting offers greater expressive power to adapt to challenging control signals, still protected by angular invariance.

The authors observe that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to the explicit deviation constraint. In practice, this translates to faster and smoother convergence, reduced sensitivity to training schedules, and improved robustness across tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Crucially, COFT retains OFT’s parameter efficiency and introduces no additional inference overhead: once training is finished, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ to form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and inference proceeds with the same runtime as the original pretrained model.

# COFT in Context: Flexibility, Regularity, and Efficiency

COFT embodies a principled balance between flexibility and regularity. Standard finetuning is highly flexible but can be unstable and prone to collapsing pretrained capability. OFT imposes a strong regularity by preserving pairwise neuron angles, which stabilizes training and maintains semantics. COFT adds a second layer of regularity via the radius constraint, explicitly limiting deviation from the pretrained model while preserving all OFT benefits. Together, these constraints guide optimization on a smooth hypersphere manifold and encourage rapid, stable convergence.

COFT also leverages the block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization to achieve parameter efficiency without sacrificing orthogonality or hyperspherical energy preservation. Unlike additive low-rank approaches, COFT updates weights multiplicatively via orthogonal transforms, preserving the intrinsic geometric structure of the pretrained model. Because orthogonal transforms can be composed, COFT naturally integrates into finetuning pipelines and maintains efficiency at inference time.

# Concluding Remarks

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) improves the stability of adapting text-to-image diffusion models by combining two explicit constraints: preserving neuron angles through orthogonal transformations (keeping hyperspherical energy unchanged), and bounding the deviation from identity via $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$. Implemented efficiently with the Cayley parameterization and enforced through projected gradient updates on the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$, COFT yields smooth, predictable training dynamics, strong semantic preservation, and rapid convergence across downstream tasks. The radius constraint serves as a practical and intuitive control over finetuning flexibility, making COFT a principled and effective method for guiding powerful diffusion models without sacrificing their pretrained generative strengths.
