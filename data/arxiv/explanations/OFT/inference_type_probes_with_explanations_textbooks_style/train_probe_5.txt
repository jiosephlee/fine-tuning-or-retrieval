Chapter Title: Hyperspherical Energy, Orthogonal Finetuning, and the Preservation of Pretrained Semantics

# Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models: The Core Challenge

Large text-to-image diffusion models exhibit remarkable generative capabilities, yet adapting them to downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation requires finetuning. The central challenge in finetuning is to modify the model to meet new objectives while preserving the rich, hard-won semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. Conventional finetuning strategies, such as gradient descent with small learning rates or low-rank adaptations, implicitly assume that staying close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean distance suffices to preserve semantic performance. However, empirical evidence and structural considerations suggest that this assumption is incomplete. Semantic preservation is not governed purely by Euclidean proximity of weights; rather, it is tightly connected to the angular geometry among neurons.

This chapter develops the intuition and theory behind an angular perspective on finetuning via hyperspherical energy, and explains why minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would likely destroy pretrained semantic structures. Instead, preserving hyperspherical energy—achieved by orthogonal transformations of neurons—proves crucial for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. This perspective underlies Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT), which adapt models while preserving the angular relational structure encoded during pretraining.

# Hyperspherical Energy and the Angular Structure of Neurons

The semantic behavior of a neural network layer can be fruitfully analyzed on the unit hypersphere by focusing on neuron directions rather than magnitudes. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, each neuron $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ can be normalized to $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ so that all neurons lie on the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The pairwise angular relations among these normalized neurons encode the layer’s internal relational structure. 

Hyperspherical energy provides a quantitative measure of this structure. One concrete form of hyperspherical energy used to characterize pairwise neuron relations is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This expression aggregates a notion of hyperspherical similarity across all neuron pairs in a layer. Intuitively, $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ captures how uniformly or non-uniformly the neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere. When neurons are uniformly spaced, redundancy is minimized, and the energy tends toward a small value; when neurons cluster or align, redundancy increases, and the energy increases accordingly.

Crucially, empirical observations indicate that angular information (i.e., neuron directions) is highly predictive of semantics. In particular, training networks with normalized neurons or focusing on angular differences can capture semantic content comparably or even better than magnitude-based representations. A toy autoencoder experiment demonstrates that, even when trained with standard inner products, test-time reconstructions that rely only on angular information can nearly perfectly recover the input images, whereas reconstructions based solely on magnitude carry little useful information. This finding supports the view that the semantic essence of the learned representation is largely stored in neuron directions, and hence in the angular relationships measured by hyperspherical energy.

# Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy

Orthogonal transformations constitute a natural mechanism to manipulate neuron directions while preserving their pairwise angular relationships. Let $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Consider transforming a pretrained layer $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Since $\bm{R}$ rotates or reflects all neurons uniformly in the ambient space, the normalized neurons satisfy
\[
\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|},
\]
and the pairwise angles among $\{\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}\}$ are identical to those among $\{\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\}$. Consequently, the hyperspherical energy is invariant under the shared orthogonal transform:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
This invariance is central to orthogonal finetuning. If the pretrained model’s semantic aptitude is encoded in the angular arrangement of neurons, then orthogonally transforming all neurons in a layer preserves that arrangement and thus preserves the semantic structure. Orthogonal finetuning can therefore make controlled semantic adjustments without destroying the pretrained relationships that make the diffusion model generative and coherent.

# Why Preserving, Not Minimizing, Hyperspherical Energy Matters

Preservation of hyperspherical energy during finetuning stands in sharp contrast to minimizing it. To see why these two objectives have profoundly different consequences, it helps to recall what low hyperspherical energy represents. When hyperspherical energy is minimized, neurons tend to spread uniformly over the unit hypersphere, thereby reducing redundancy. This objective has been linked to improved generalization in classification when training from scratch, where constructing a new representation with minimally redundant neurons is desirable. In that setting, minimizing the energy drives the model to reorganize its representation around a uniform angular configuration.

Finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model is fundamentally different. Pretraining has already formed a highly structured, semantically meaningful configuration of neuron directions, capturing nuanced, compositional knowledge necessary for high-fidelity image synthesis. This configuration manifests as a particular angular arrangement with a corresponding hyperspherical energy value $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ that reflects the pretrained semantic structure. If, during finetuning, one were to minimize hyperspherical energy, the training dynamics would explicitly push the neurons away from their pretrained angular configuration toward a more uniform distribution. In doing so, the model would progressively erase the pretrained relational structure among neurons that encodes semantic knowledge.

Formally, consider the finetuning objective that penalizes deviations in hyperspherical energy:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|.
\]
The optimum of this problem is achieved by any $\bm{W}$ differing from $\bm{W}^0$ by a shared orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$, i.e., $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This keeps the energy exactly preserved and thus preserves the pairwise angular geometry. By contrast, replacing this preservation with minimization of $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ would drive the model’s neuron configuration toward uniformity, irrespective of $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. As articulated in the paper, such a shift “could destroy the original semantic structures,” because it discards the very angular regularities that pretraining established to encode semantics.

Intuitively, pretrained text-to-image models have specialized angular relationships that entangle content, style, structure, and compositional cues necessary for aligning with text prompts and producing coherent scenes. Forcing these neurons into a uniform configuration breaks this specialization. The result is a representation that, while less redundant in a purely geometric sense, is misaligned with the pretrained generative manifold and thus degrades semantic generation capability.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Learning on the Hypersphere without Destroying Semantics

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is designed precisely to preserve hyperspherical energy while allowing controlled semantic adaptation. For a pretrained layer $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, OFT replaces the standard forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ by
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{where } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]
The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as $\bm{I}$ to ensure the finetuning process starts exactly from the pretrained model. Because $\bm{R}$ is shared across all neurons in the layer, the pairwise angles among neurons—and hence the hyperspherical energy—remain invariant.

This formulation reveals OFT as an explicit solution to the preservation objective
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|,
\]
which attains its minimum at $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal $\bm{R}$. In effect, OFT performs finetuning on the hypersphere by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms, which change neuron directions coherently while maintaining their relational structure. The result is an approach that is both flexible enough to adapt semantics and regularized enough to avoid collapsing the pretrained representation.

The intuition is further supported by an analogy to the phase spectrum in 2D Fourier transforms: semantic content is closely tied to angular information. Changing directions in a controlled way adjusts semantics, but changing them arbitrarily or forcing them into a uniform configuration destroys the semantic structure. OFT strikes the balance by constraining changes to shared orthogonal transforms.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization and Stability

To implement OFT efficiently, the orthogonal constraint is enforced via Cayley parameterization, which represents $\bm{R}$ as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}, \quad \text{with } \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}.
\]
This parameterization yields orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ and is practically efficient when coupled with a block-diagonal structure. Specifically, one may set
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}}$ is orthogonal. This reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, or even to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ if blocks are shared, while preserving the orthogonality—and thus preserving hyperspherical energy exactly.

An important practical observation is that the orthogonality constraint itself stabilizes finetuning. Removing this constraint and allowing unconstrained angular transformations can lead to failure modes, including the inability to generate realistic images or to respect control signals. Orthogonality regularizes the transformation space, ensuring that the angular relationships among neurons remain coherent rather than drifting chaotically.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning: Explicitly Limiting Deviation

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) strengthens the preservation principle by explicitly limiting the deviation from the pretrained model:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \ \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon.
\]
The orthogonality constraint preserves hyperspherical energy, while the radius constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$ caps the extent of angular deviation. Using the Neumann series approximation for the Cayley transform,
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
the radius constraint can be approximately imposed on $\bm{Q}$ via $\|\bm{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$, and enforced through projected gradient descent. COFT thus combines two explicit constraints: exact preservation of hyperspherical energy and bounded deviation from the pretrained model. Empirically, this yields even greater stability and insensitivity to hyperparameters such as the number of training iterations.

# What Minimizing Hyperspherical Energy Would Do in Finetuning

It is now clear why minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would be destructive. Minimization pushes the neurons toward a uniform angular configuration that ignores the pretrained arrangement. This is appropriate when training from scratch for classification where the goal is to construct a new, generalizable representation with low redundancy. However, in the finetuning setting for text-to-image diffusion models, pretraining has already encoded an intricate angular structure necessary for semantic generation. Minimization would explicitly override this structure.

Concretely, if one were to optimize
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \ \text{HE}(\bm{W}),
\]
starting from $\bm{W}^0$, then the resulting trajectory would reduce the energy away from $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, altering pairwise neuron angles to increase uniformity. Because semantic relations are carried by these pairwise angles, the process would erode the model’s capacity to preserve subjects, follow fine-grained control signals, or maintain diversity and fidelity. In short, minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning likely destroys the pretrained semantic structures that make the diffusion model effective.

By comparison, OFT and COFT do not minimize hyperspherical energy; they preserve it exactly while adjusting the global orientation of neurons through shared orthogonal transforms. This choice respects the pretrained semantic relationships and enables stable, efficient, and high-quality adaptation to new tasks.

# Extensions that Respect Hyperspherical Structure

An extension of OFT allows additional per-neuron magnitude scaling without altering hyperspherical energy:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}, \quad \bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n), \ s_i > 0.
\]
Since hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, scaling columns by positive factors $s_i$ does not change the pairwise angles among normalized neurons. This re-scaled variant adds flexibility while preserving the angular structure central to semantic retention. In practice, even the baseline OFT, without this scaling, already demonstrates strong stability and controllability by virtue of preserving hyperspherical energy.

# Conclusion

Finetuning text-to-image diffusion models demands modifications that adapt to new tasks while safeguarding pretrained semantics. The angular viewpoint—quantified by hyperspherical energy—reveals why neuron directions encode the essence of semantics and why preserving their pairwise relationships is crucial. Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained variant) achieves this by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms, exactly preserving hyperspherical energy and thus the pretrained semantic structure, while enabling targeted semantic adjustments.

In contrast, minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would reshape the neuron configuration toward uniformity, thereby discarding the pretrained angular organization that underpins semantic generation. As a result, such minimization would likely destroy pretrained semantic structures. Preserving—not minimizing—hyperspherical energy is therefore the correct principle for stable and effective finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models.
