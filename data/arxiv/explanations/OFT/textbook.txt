# Introduction and Motivation

In recent years, the field of generative modeling has witnessed remarkable advances, particularly in the domain of text-to-image synthesis. Large-scale text-to-image diffusion models have emerged as powerful tools capable of generating photorealistic images conditioned on natural language prompts. These models, such as Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and GLIDE, leverage the synergy of diffusion processes and large-scale vision-language pretraining to produce high-fidelity images that align well with textual descriptions. Their success has opened up exciting possibilities for creative applications, content generation, and human-computer interaction.

## The Rise of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Diffusion models have become a dominant paradigm in generative modeling due to their ability to model complex data distributions with high sample quality and diversity. In text-to-image generation, diffusion models are trained to iteratively denoise a noisy latent or pixel representation, guided by a text embedding that encodes the semantic content of the prompt. The combination of powerful text encoders and diffusion-based image synthesis has led to models that can generate diverse and semantically rich images from a wide range of textual inputs.

These models have demonstrated impressive zero-shot capabilities, meaning they can generate images for prompts never seen during training. This generalization is largely attributed to the massive scale of pretraining on diverse datasets. However, despite their versatility, these pretrained models have inherent limitations when it comes to fine-grained control or adapting to specific downstream tasks.

## Limits of Prompt-Only Guidance and Need for Finetuning

While text prompts provide a flexible and intuitive interface for guiding image generation, they are often ambiguous or insufficient for precise control. For example, a prompt like "a photo of a dog in a park" leaves many details unspecified: the breed of the dog, its pose, the style of the photo, and so forth. This ambiguity can lead to outputs that do not meet user expectations or fail to capture specific desired attributes.

Moreover, pretrained diffusion models are typically trained on broad datasets and may not faithfully reproduce rare or personalized subjects, such as a particular person's face or a unique object. Similarly, controlling the generation process with additional modalities—such as edge maps, segmentation masks, or landmarks—is challenging without explicit model adaptation.

To overcome these limitations, finetuning pretrained diffusion models on downstream tasks has become a crucial approach. Finetuning allows the model to specialize or incorporate new control signals, enabling more accurate and personalized image generation. However, finetuning large diffusion models is nontrivial: naive finetuning can lead to catastrophic forgetting, loss of generalization, or unstable training dynamics.

## Two Downstream Tasks: Subject-Driven and Controllable Generation

This chapter focuses on two important downstream tasks that exemplify the need for effective finetuning strategies:

1. **Subject-Driven Generation:** The goal here is to generate images of a specific subject—such as a person, pet, or object—given only a few reference images and a text prompt describing the desired context. For instance, given several photos of a particular dog, the model should generate new images of that dog in different scenes or styles. This task requires the model to internalize the subject's identity and faithfully reproduce it while adapting to new contexts.

2. **Controllable Generation:** In this task, the model is conditioned not only on a text prompt but also on an additional control signal, such as a Canny edge map, segmentation mask, or facial landmarks. The objective is to generate images that adhere closely to the control input while respecting the semantic content of the prompt. This enables fine-grained control over the generated image's structure and composition.

Both tasks highlight the challenge of adapting large pretrained diffusion models to new conditions without sacrificing their pretrained generative capabilities.

## Desiderata for Finetuning Pretrained Generative Models

Effective finetuning methods for text-to-image diffusion models must balance several competing objectives:

- **Training Efficiency:** Large diffusion models have millions or billions of parameters, making full finetuning computationally expensive and time-consuming. Efficient finetuning methods should minimize the number of trainable parameters and require fewer training epochs to converge.

- **Preservation of Pretrained Generative Ability:** The pretrained model encodes rich semantic knowledge and generalization capabilities. Finetuning should preserve this knowledge to maintain high-fidelity, diverse, and photorealistic generation, avoiding overfitting or catastrophic forgetting.

- **Stability and Robustness:** Finetuning should be stable across different tasks and hyperparameter settings, avoiding mode collapse or degenerate outputs. It should also be robust to variations in training data size and quality.

- **Flexibility and Control:** The finetuned model should effectively incorporate new control signals or subject-specific information, enabling precise and semantically meaningful modifications to the generated images.

Existing finetuning approaches often struggle to satisfy all these desiderata simultaneously. For example, naive finetuning with small learning rates can be slow and unstable, while adding small parameter-efficient modules may not fully preserve pretrained knowledge.

## High-Level Idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

To address these challenges, the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework introduces a principled approach to finetuning text-to-image diffusion models by leveraging the geometry of neuron representations. The key insight is that the semantic information encoded by neurons in a neural network is closely related to the angular relationships between their weight vectors, rather than their absolute magnitudes.

OFT proposes to adapt pretrained model weights by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons. Orthogonal transformations—rotations and reflections—preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, thereby maintaining the hyperspherical energy, a measure of neuron uniformity and semantic structure on the unit hypersphere. By constraining finetuning updates to be orthogonal transformations, OFT preserves the intrinsic semantic relationships learned during pretraining while allowing flexible adaptation.

This approach offers several advantages:

- **Semantic Preservation:** By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT maintains the pretrained model's semantic generation ability.

- **Training Stability:** The orthogonality constraint regularizes the finetuning process, preventing drastic and unstable weight changes.

- **Parameter Efficiency:** OFT learns a relatively small orthogonal matrix per layer, reducing the number of trainable parameters.

- **No Inference Overhead:** The orthogonal transformation can be merged with pretrained weights at inference time, incurring no additional computational cost.

An extension called Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) further improves stability by limiting the deviation of the orthogonal transformation from the identity matrix, effectively controlling the radius of the transformation on the hypersphere.

## Fundamentals of Diffusion-Based Generative Models

Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models capable of producing high-fidelity images. The core idea behind diffusion models is to learn a reverse diffusion process that gradually denoises a noisy image to generate a realistic sample. This process is typically formulated as a Markov chain of latent variables, where the forward diffusion gradually adds Gaussian noise to an image, and the model learns to reverse this process.

Formally, let $\mathbf{x}_0$ denote a clean image sample from the data distribution. The forward diffusion process produces a sequence of noisy images $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T$ by progressively adding noise according to a fixed schedule. The reverse process is parameterized by a neural network $\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ that predicts the noise component at each timestep $t$, enabling the model to denoise $\mathbf{x}_t$ to $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$. Training minimizes the difference between the predicted noise and the true noise added at each step.

Diffusion models have several appealing properties: they can generate diverse samples, are stable to train, and can be conditioned on various modalities such as text. Conditioning is typically achieved by incorporating embeddings from a text encoder into the denoising network, allowing the model to generate images aligned with a given text prompt.

---

## Latent vs Pixel-Space Diffusion Models

Diffusion models can operate either directly in pixel space or in a learned latent space. This distinction has important implications for computational efficiency and image quality.

- **Pixel-Space Diffusion Models:** These models, such as GLIDE and Imagen, perform the diffusion process directly on high-dimensional pixel images. For example, Imagen~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} uses a frozen pretrained text encoder to condition the diffusion model, which operates on pixel images. GLIDE~\cite{nichol2022glide} jointly trains a text encoder and a diffusion prior in pixel space. Pixel-space diffusion models often achieve very high image fidelity but can be computationally expensive due to the high dimensionality of images.

- **Latent-Space Diffusion Models:** Models like Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} and DALL-E 2~\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} perform diffusion in a lower-dimensional latent space learned by an autoencoder or a vector quantized generative model. For instance, Stable Diffusion uses a VQ-GAN to encode images into a latent space where the diffusion process is applied. This approach significantly reduces computational cost and memory usage while maintaining high image quality. DALL-E 2 uses CLIP embeddings to represent images and text in a joint latent space.

Both approaches have their merits, and OFT is designed to be model-agnostic, applicable to diffusion models operating in either space.

---

## Model Components: UNet, Text Encoders, and Attention Layers

Modern text-to-image diffusion models typically consist of several key components:

- **UNet Backbone:** The core denoising network is often a UNet architecture, which is a convolutional neural network with skip connections that allow multi-scale feature fusion. The UNet takes as input the noisy image (or latent) at timestep $t$ and outputs a denoised estimate. The UNet is responsible for learning the complex mapping from noisy inputs to clean images.

- **Text Encoder:** To condition image generation on text prompts, a pretrained text encoder (e.g., CLIP text encoder or a transformer-based language model) converts the input text into a fixed-length embedding vector. This embedding is injected into the UNet via cross-attention layers or conditioning mechanisms, guiding the generation process.

- **Attention Layers:** Attention mechanisms, especially cross-attention, play a crucial role in aligning textual and visual features. Within the UNet, attention layers allow the model to selectively focus on relevant parts of the text embedding when generating different image regions. These layers are often the primary targets for finetuning or adaptation, as they mediate the interaction between text and image modalities.

Understanding these components is essential because finetuning methods like OFT, LoRA, and adapters typically modify or augment the weights of attention layers or other parts of the UNet to adapt the model to new tasks.

---

## Subject-Driven Generation: DreamBooth and Inversion-Based Approaches

Subject-driven generation aims to personalize a pretrained text-to-image model to generate images of a specific subject given only a few reference images. The goal is to produce images that preserve the subject's identity while allowing variations in context or style guided by text prompts.

Several approaches have been proposed:

- **Inversion Methods:** Techniques like ILVR~\cite{choi2021ilvr} and image inversion~\cite{dhariwal2021diffusion} attempt to invert an image into the latent space of the diffusion model, enabling editing or context modification without altering the subject. However, these methods often struggle to preserve fine-grained identity details.

- **DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}:** DreamBooth finetunes the diffusion model by introducing a unique token representing the subject and optimizing the model to reconstruct the few subject images while maintaining a class-specific prior. This approach uses a reconstruction loss and a prior preservation loss to prevent overfitting and maintain generalization. DreamBooth finetunes the entire model or parts of it with a small learning rate, which can be computationally expensive and sensitive to hyperparameters.

- **Other Methods:** Pivotal Tuning~\cite{roich2022pivotal} and MyStyle~\cite{nitzan2022mystyle} also finetune generative models to capture subject-specific details but may require more data or complex regularization.

While effective, these methods can suffer from instability during finetuning, slow convergence, and potential degradation of the pretrained model's generalization ability. OFT aims to address these issues by preserving the intrinsic geometric structure of neurons during finetuning.

---

## Controllable Generation: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and Image-to-Image Translation

Controllable generation extends text-to-image synthesis by incorporating additional control signals beyond text prompts, such as edge maps, segmentation masks, or landmarks. The goal is to generate images that not only align with the text but also respect the spatial or structural constraints provided by these signals.

Key approaches include:

- **Image-to-Image Translation:** Earlier methods used conditional GANs~\cite{isola2017image,zhu2017toward} to translate input images (e.g., sketches or segmentation maps) into photorealistic images. These methods are limited by the quality and diversity of training data.

- **Diffusion-Based Methods:** Recent works leverage diffusion models for image-to-image translation, benefiting from their strong generative capabilities. Palette~\cite{saharia2022palette} and other diffusion-based translators have shown promising results.

- **ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}:** ControlNet finetunes a pretrained diffusion model by adding trainable control layers that process the additional control signals. This approach achieves strong controllability but introduces extra parameters and inference overhead.

- **T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}:** Similar to ControlNet, T2I-Adapter adds lightweight adapters to the diffusion model to incorporate control signals. It also requires additional parameters and computational cost during inference.

These methods improve controllability but often at the cost of increased model complexity and slower convergence. OFT proposes a more parameter-efficient and stable finetuning approach that does not add inference overhead.

---

## Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Methods: LoRA, Adapters, and Low-Rank Updates

Finetuning large pretrained models can be computationally expensive and prone to overfitting, especially when downstream data is limited. Parameter-efficient finetuning methods aim to adapt models by updating only a small subset of parameters or introducing low-dimensional modifications.

- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)~\cite{hulora2022}:** LoRA assumes that the weight update during finetuning can be decomposed into a low-rank matrix product. Instead of updating the full weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, LoRA learns two smaller matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ such that the update is $\Delta \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top$. This reduces the number of trainable parameters and stabilizes training. LoRA is widely used in adapting large language and vision models.

- **Adapters~\cite{houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}:** Adapters insert small bottleneck layers into the pretrained model, which are trained while freezing the original weights. This approach also reduces trainable parameters and can be combined with other finetuning strategies.

- **Low-Rank Updates:** More generally, constraining the update to be low-rank or structured helps prevent overfitting and preserves pretrained knowledge.

While effective, these additive update methods do not explicitly preserve geometric properties such as neuron angles or hyperspherical energy, which OFT targets through multiplicative orthogonal transformations.

---

## Common Evaluation Practices and Datasets for Text-to-Image Tasks

Evaluating text-to-image generation models involves assessing image quality, semantic alignment with text, subject fidelity, and controllability. Common practices include:

- **Perceptual Metrics:** Metrics like LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable} measure perceptual similarity and diversity among generated images.

- **Semantic Alignment:** CLIP-based metrics~\cite{radford2021learning} compute cosine similarity between text embeddings and generated image embeddings to assess prompt fidelity.

- **Subject Fidelity:** For subject-driven generation, metrics like DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging} and CLIP image embeddings evaluate how well the generated images preserve the subject's identity.

- **Control Consistency:** For controllable generation, novel metrics estimate the control signal from generated images and compare it to the input control (e.g., IoU for segmentation, landmark distance for face pose).

- **Datasets:** Popular datasets include COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoft} for general image generation, ADE20K~\cite{zhou2017scene} for segmentation-to-image tasks, and CelebA-HQ~\cite{karras2018progressive} for face-related tasks.

These evaluation protocols provide a comprehensive framework to compare finetuning methods like OFT against baselines.

# Mathematical and Conceptual Preliminaries

In this chapter, we introduce the fundamental mathematical concepts and tools that underpin the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework for controlling text-to-image diffusion models. Understanding these preliminaries is essential for grasping the intuition, formulation, and implementation of OFT. We begin with basic linear algebra notions such as vectors, inner products, and norms, then explore geometric concepts on the unit hypersphere, which play a central role in characterizing neuron relationships. We proceed to study orthogonal matrices and their special subgroups, followed by skew-symmetric matrices and key matrix identities used in parameterizing orthogonal transformations. We also provide a brief primer on manifold-constrained optimization, which offers a geometric perspective on optimization under orthogonality constraints. Finally, we discuss spectral decomposition and the phase/magnitude intuition from Fourier analysis, which motivates the focus on angular information in neurons.

---

## Vectors, Inner Products, and Norms

At the heart of neural networks and their finetuning lie vectors and matrices, which represent data, parameters, and transformations. A vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is an ordered collection of $d$ real numbers, often interpreted as a point or direction in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space.

The **inner product** (or dot product) between two vectors $\bm{x}, \bm{y} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is defined as

\[
\langle \bm{x}, \bm{y} \rangle = \bm{x}^\top \bm{y} = \sum_{i=1}^d x_i y_i,
\]

where $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the components of $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{y}$ respectively. The inner product is a measure of similarity between vectors and satisfies linearity, symmetry, and positive-definiteness.

The **Euclidean norm** (or $\ell_2$ norm) of a vector $\bm{x}$ is derived from the inner product:

\[
\|\bm{x}\| = \sqrt{\langle \bm{x}, \bm{x} \rangle} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2}.
\]

This norm measures the length or magnitude of the vector in Euclidean space.

Vectors can be **normalized** to unit length by dividing by their norm:

\[
\hat{\bm{x}} = \frac{\bm{x}}{\|\bm{x}\|}.
\]

Normalized vectors lie on the unit sphere, which we discuss next.

---

## Cosine Similarity and Angular Distance

The **cosine similarity** between two vectors $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{y}$ is defined as the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between them:

\[
\cos \theta = \frac{\langle \bm{x}, \bm{y} \rangle}{\|\bm{x}\| \|\bm{y}\|}.
\]

This measure captures the directional similarity between vectors, independent of their magnitudes. It ranges from $-1$ (opposite directions) to $1$ (same direction), with $0$ indicating orthogonality.

The **angular distance** between two vectors is the angle $\theta$ itself, given by

\[
\theta = \arccos \left( \frac{\langle \bm{x}, \bm{y} \rangle}{\|\bm{x}\| \|\bm{y}\|} \right).
\]

Angular distance is a natural metric on the unit sphere, reflecting how "far apart" two directions are.

In the context of neural networks, cosine similarity and angular distance are often more meaningful than Euclidean distance for comparing neuron weight vectors or feature embeddings, as they focus on direction rather than magnitude. This insight motivates the use of angular measures in OFT.

---

## Unit Hypersphere and Geometry on \( S^{d-1} \)

The **unit hypersphere** in $d$ dimensions, denoted \( S^{d-1} \), is the set of all vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with unit norm:

\[
S^{d-1} = \{ \bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d : \|\bm{x}\| = 1 \}.
\]

This is a $(d-1)$-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^d$. For example, \( S^1 \) is the unit circle in 2D, and \( S^2 \) is the surface of a unit sphere in 3D.

The geometry of \( S^{d-1} \) is non-Euclidean; distances and angles are measured along the curved surface. The natural distance metric on the sphere is the **geodesic distance**, which corresponds to the angular distance between two points:

\[
d_{S^{d-1}}(\bm{x}, \bm{y}) = \arccos(\langle \bm{x}, \bm{y} \rangle).
\]

This metric respects the spherical geometry and is invariant under orthogonal transformations (rotations and reflections).

In neural networks, neurons (weight vectors) are often normalized to lie on \( S^{d-1} \), and their pairwise relationships can be studied via angular distances or cosine similarities. The **hyperspherical energy** (introduced later) quantifies the uniformity and distribution of neurons on this sphere.

---

## Orthogonal Matrices and Special Orthogonal Group \( SO(d) \)

An **orthogonal matrix** \(\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is a square matrix whose columns (and rows) form an orthonormal basis. Formally, \(\bm{R}\) satisfies

\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]

where \(\bm{I}\) is the \(d \times d\) identity matrix.

Orthogonal matrices represent **isometries** in Euclidean space: they preserve lengths and angles. Applying \(\bm{R}\) to a vector \(\bm{x}\) rotates and/or reflects it without changing its norm or the angles between vectors.

The set of all orthogonal matrices forms the **orthogonal group** \( O(d) \), which has two connected components distinguished by the determinant:

- Matrices with \(\det(\bm{R}) = +1\) form the **special orthogonal group** \( SO(d) \), representing pure rotations.
- Matrices with \(\det(\bm{R}) = -1\) include reflections.

The special orthogonal group \( SO(d) \) is a Lie group of dimension \( \frac{d(d-1)}{2} \), meaning it is a smooth manifold with group structure.

In OFT, orthogonal matrices are used to transform neuron weight vectors while preserving their pairwise angles and thus the hyperspherical energy. This ensures that the semantic structure encoded by neuron directions remains intact during finetuning.

---

## Skew-Symmetric Matrices and Basic Matrix Identities

A **skew-symmetric matrix** \(\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) satisfies

\[
\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}.
\]

Such matrices have zeros on the diagonal and antisymmetric off-diagonal entries. Skew-symmetric matrices are closely related to the Lie algebra of the orthogonal group \( O(d) \).

Key properties include:

- The exponential map of a skew-symmetric matrix yields an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1:

\[
\exp(\bm{Q}) \in SO(d).
\]

- The Cayley transform provides an alternative parameterization of orthogonal matrices (except those with eigenvalue -1):

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

where \(\bm{Q}\) is skew-symmetric.

These properties allow efficient parameterization and optimization of orthogonal matrices by optimizing over skew-symmetric matrices \(\bm{Q}\), which have fewer degrees of freedom and simpler constraints.

In OFT, the Cayley transform is used to ensure the orthogonality of the transformation matrix \(\bm{R}\) during finetuning.

---

## Manifold Constrained Optimization: A Brief Riemannian View

Optimization over orthogonal matrices is an example of **manifold-constrained optimization**, where the parameter space is a smooth manifold rather than a Euclidean space.

The orthogonal group \( O(d) \) is a Riemannian manifold with a natural metric induced by the Frobenius norm. Optimization on \( O(d) \) involves:

- **Retraction**: mapping a point in the tangent space back to the manifold.
- **Projection**: projecting gradients onto the tangent space.
- **Geodesics**: shortest paths on the manifold.

Standard gradient descent steps do not preserve orthogonality, so specialized algorithms are used, such as:

- **Projected gradient descent**: after a gradient step, project back onto \( O(d) \) via orthogonalization (e.g., QR decomposition).
- **Cayley transform updates**: parameterize \(\bm{R}\) via \(\bm{Q}\) and optimize \(\bm{Q}\) in Euclidean space, ensuring \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal.

This geometric perspective explains why OFT can maintain the hyperspherical energy by restricting updates to orthogonal transformations, which lie on the manifold \( O(d) \).

---

## Spectral Decomposition and Phase/Magnitude Intuition from Fourier Analysis

Spectral decomposition and Fourier analysis provide intuition for separating information into magnitude and phase components, which is analogous to the role of neuron directions and magnitudes.

In the 2D Fourier transform of an image, the complex coefficients can be expressed in polar form:

\[
F(u,v) = A(u,v) e^{i \phi(u,v)},
\]

where \(A(u,v)\) is the magnitude spectrum and \(\phi(u,v)\) is the phase spectrum.

Empirically, the phase spectrum encodes most of the structural and semantic information of the image, while the magnitude spectrum contributes to contrast and intensity.

This analogy motivates the focus on neuron directions (angles) rather than magnitudes in OFT. By preserving the angular relationships (phase-like information) among neurons via orthogonal transformations, OFT maintains the semantic content learned during pretraining.

Moreover, re-scaling neuron magnitudes (analogous to magnitude spectrum) can be added as a separate degree of freedom without affecting the hyperspherical energy, leading to the re-scaled OFT variant.

# Hyperspherical Energy and Neuron Geometry

In this chapter, we delve into the concept of hyperspherical energy (HE) and its critical role in understanding the geometry of neurons within neural networks, particularly in the context of finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models. We will explore how HE captures the pairwise relationships among neurons on the unit hypersphere, why these angular relationships are essential for preserving semantic information, and how this insight motivates the design of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). To build intuition, we will also examine illustrative toy experiments that highlight the importance of neuron directions, drawing analogies to phase and magnitude information in signal processing.

---

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy and Motivations

Hyperspherical energy is a geometric measure that quantifies the uniformity and relational structure of a set of vectors constrained to lie on the unit hypersphere. Consider a set of neurons represented by weight vectors in a neural network layer. Each neuron corresponds to a vector $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$. To analyze their geometric relationships independent of scale, we normalize each neuron vector to lie on the unit hypersphere:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}, \quad \text{for } i=1,2,\ldots,n,
\]

where $n$ is the number of neurons in the layer.

The hyperspherical energy (HE) of this set of normalized neurons is defined as the sum of inverse pairwise Euclidean distances between all distinct pairs of neurons on the hypersphere:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

Intuitively, this energy measures how "clustered" or "spread out" the neurons are on the hypersphere. A lower HE corresponds to neurons that are more uniformly distributed, maximizing angular separation, while a higher HE indicates neurons that are more clustered or aligned.

The motivation for considering HE arises from the observation that the angular relationships between neurons encode important semantic information. By focusing on the directions of neurons rather than their magnitudes, HE captures the intrinsic geometry of the neural representation space, which is crucial for understanding and preserving the learned knowledge during finetuning.

---

## Relation Between Hyperspherical Energy and Pairwise Cosine Similarities

To better understand HE, it is helpful to relate it to pairwise cosine similarities, a common measure of angular similarity between vectors. Recall that the cosine similarity between two normalized vectors $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ is given by:

\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j,
\]

where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between the two vectors.

The Euclidean distance between two points on the unit hypersphere can be expressed in terms of their cosine similarity:

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2 = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\|^2 + \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2 - 2 \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j = 2 - 2 \cos \theta_{ij}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\| = \sqrt{2 - 2 \cos \theta_{ij}} = 2 \sin \left( \frac{\theta_{ij}}{2} \right).
\]

Substituting this into the definition of HE, we get:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|} = \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{1}{2 \sin \left( \frac{\theta_{ij}}{2} \right)}.
\]

This expression shows that HE is inversely related to the sine of half the angle between neuron vectors. When neurons are close together (small $\theta_{ij}$), the sine term is small, and the contribution to HE is large, indicating high energy due to clustering. Conversely, when neurons are well separated (large $\theta_{ij}$), the contribution to HE is smaller.

Thus, HE effectively captures the angular distribution of neurons on the hypersphere, emphasizing the importance of pairwise angles in the neural representation.

---

## Why Neuron Directions Matter: Empirical Evidence and Literature

The importance of neuron directions (angles) over magnitudes has been highlighted in several empirical studies and theoretical works. Notably:

- **SphereNet (Liu et al., 2017)** demonstrated that training neural networks with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere can achieve comparable or even better generalization performance. This suggests that the direction of neurons encodes the most critical information for the network's function.

- **Angular feature difference as semantic gap (Liu et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020)**: These works showed that angular differences between feature vectors correlate well with semantic differences, indicating that angular geometry is a meaningful representation of semantic content.

- **Hyperspherical energy and generalization (Liu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021)**: Lower hyperspherical energy, corresponding to more uniform neuron distributions, has been linked to better generalization in classification tasks.

These findings collectively motivate the hypothesis that preserving the angular relationships among neurons during finetuning is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model's semantic generation ability. Changes in neuron directions correspond to semantic modifications, while preserving pairwise angles helps retain the original knowledge.

---

## Toy Experiments: Autoencoder Study and Phase/Magnitude Analogy

To build intuition about the role of neuron directions, consider a toy experiment involving a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. The autoencoder learns to reconstruct input images through a bottleneck representation.

- **Training stage**: The feature map is computed using the standard inner product between convolutional kernels (neurons) and input patches.

- **Testing stage**: Three different methods are used to generate the feature map:

  1. **Inner product (baseline)**: The same as training, using the full inner product.

  2. **Magnitude only**: Using only the magnitude information of the neuron weights, ignoring direction.

  3. **Angular information only**: Using only the direction (angle) of the neuron weights, ignoring magnitude.

The results show that the angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the input images, while the magnitude information contains little useful information. This implies that the directions of neurons carry the bulk of semantic information.

This phenomenon is analogous to the **phase and magnitude decomposition in Fourier transforms**. In signal processing, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum encodes the spatial structure and semantics of the image, while the magnitude spectrum encodes intensity information. Remarkably, reconstructing an image using the original phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum still preserves the image's semantic content.

By analogy, neuron directions correspond to phase information, and magnitudes correspond to magnitude information. This analogy supports the idea that finetuning should focus on modifying neuron directions to effect semantic changes, while preserving the overall angular structure to maintain pretrained knowledge.

---

## Interpretation of Hyperspherical Energy in Pretrained Networks

In pretrained text-to-image diffusion models, neurons in each layer form a complex geometric structure on the unit hypersphere. The hyperspherical energy captures the pairwise angular relationships that encode semantic knowledge learned during pretraining.

Preserving HE during finetuning means maintaining the relative angles between neurons, which in turn preserves the semantic structure of the model. This is crucial because:

- **Semantic preservation**: The pretrained model's ability to generate photorealistic and semantically coherent images depends on the learned neuron geometry.

- **Stability**: Large deviations in neuron angles can disrupt the learned representations, leading to degraded generation quality or mode collapse.

- **Generalizability**: Maintaining HE helps retain the model's capacity to generalize to diverse prompts and contexts.

Therefore, HE serves as a principled measure to quantify how much the finetuned model deviates from the pretrained semantic structure.

---

## Why Euclidean Distance is Insufficient as a Preservation Metric

A common implicit assumption in finetuning is that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\mathbf{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\mathbf{M}^0$ preserves the pretrained model's capabilities. Formally, this corresponds to minimizing:

\[
\|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^0\|.
\]

While this constraint encourages small parameter updates, it does not explicitly account for the geometric or semantic structure encoded in the neuron directions. Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm but have very different angular relationships among neurons, leading to significant semantic changes.

Moreover, Euclidean distance does not capture invariances such as rotations or reflections of the neuron space, which can preserve semantic information but result in large Euclidean differences.

In contrast, hyperspherical energy focuses on preserving pairwise angles, which are more directly related to semantic content. Thus, HE is a more meaningful and robust metric for guiding finetuning.

---

## Implications of Hyperspherical Energy for Finetuning Design

The insights from hyperspherical energy and neuron geometry have several important implications for designing finetuning methods:

1. **Preserve pairwise neuron angles**: Finetuning should maintain the angular relationships among neurons to preserve semantic knowledge.

2. **Use transformations that preserve HE**: Orthogonal transformations (rotations and reflections) preserve pairwise angles and thus HE, making them ideal candidates for finetuning updates.

3. **Balance flexibility and stability**: While some modification of neuron directions is necessary to adapt to downstream tasks, excessive changes can disrupt HE and degrade performance. Methods should constrain updates to preserve HE as much as possible.

4. **Move beyond Euclidean constraints**: Finetuning strategies should incorporate geometric constraints reflecting neuron direction preservation rather than relying solely on Euclidean proximity.

5. **Inspiration for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: These principles motivate OFT, which learns layer-shared orthogonal transformations to adapt pretrained weights while exactly preserving HE, leading to stable and semantically consistent finetuning.

---

In summary, hyperspherical energy provides a powerful lens to understand the geometry of neurons in neural networks and their role in encoding semantic information. By focusing on preserving HE during finetuning, we can design principled methods that maintain the pretrained model's generative capabilities while enabling effective adaptation to new tasks. The following chapters will build on these concepts to formalize Orthogonal Finetuning and explore its practical implementation and empirical benefits.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Concept and Formalism

In this chapter, we delve into the core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled method for adapting large pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks. OFT is designed to preserve the intrinsic semantic structure learned during pretraining by maintaining the geometric relationships among neurons. We will explore the intuition behind OFT, formalize its mathematical framework, and discuss its practical implications.

---

## Intuition: Rotation and Reflection Preserve Angles but Can Change Semantics

When finetuning a large pretrained model, a key challenge is to modify the model’s behavior to suit a new task without destroying the valuable knowledge it has already acquired. Traditional finetuning methods often update weights directly, which can inadvertently distort the internal representations and lead to loss of semantic fidelity or unstable training.

OFT is motivated by the insight that the *angles* between neurons—i.e., the directions of weight vectors in parameter space—encode crucial semantic information. This is supported by empirical observations in the literature showing that angular similarity (e.g., cosine similarity) between neurons correlates strongly with semantic similarity in learned representations.

Orthogonal transformations, such as rotations and reflections, have a fundamental geometric property: they preserve angles between vectors. Applying an orthogonal transformation to a set of neurons changes their directions collectively but maintains the pairwise angular relationships intact. This means that the *relative geometry* of the neurons on the unit hypersphere remains unchanged.

Why is this important? Because preserving these angles helps maintain the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained model. At the same time, applying an orthogonal transformation allows the model to adapt by changing the *coordinate system* in which neurons are expressed, enabling controlled semantic modifications without destroying the pretrained knowledge.

In summary, orthogonal transformations strike a balance: they allow meaningful adaptation by changing neuron directions, but constrain the update so that the semantic relationships encoded by neuron angles are preserved.

---

## Formal OFT Forward Pass for Fully Connected Layers

To formalize OFT, consider a fully connected layer in a neural network. Let the pretrained weight matrix be

\[
\mathbf{W}^0 = [\mathbf{w}_1^0, \mathbf{w}_2^0, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_n^0] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n},
\]

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_i^0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) represents a neuron (weight vector) in the layer.

Given an input vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the output of this layer before finetuning is

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n.
\]

In OFT, instead of directly updating \(\mathbf{W}^0\), we introduce a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) that transforms the pretrained weights:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0,
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\) satisfies the orthogonality constraint

\[
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I},
\]

with \(\mathbf{I}\) being the \(d \times d\) identity matrix.

The finetuned forward pass becomes

\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]

This formulation means that the finetuning process learns an orthogonal transformation \(\mathbf{R}\) that rotates or reflects the pretrained neurons collectively, adapting the model while preserving the geometric structure of the neuron set.

---

## Derivation: Hyperspherical Energy Preservation and Orthogonality

To understand why OFT preserves semantic information, we introduce the concept of *hyperspherical energy* (HE), which measures the uniformity and pairwise relationships of neurons on the unit hypersphere.

First, normalize each neuron vector to lie on the unit sphere:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

The hyperspherical energy of the weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) is defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

This quantity aggregates the inverse Euclidean distances between all pairs of normalized neurons, effectively capturing how neurons are distributed on the hypersphere. A low HE corresponds to neurons being well-separated and uniformly distributed, which is linked to better generalization and semantic encoding.

The key property is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and distances on the unit sphere. Formally, for an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\),

\[
\|\widehat{\mathbf{R} \mathbf{w}_i} - \widehat{\mathbf{R} \mathbf{w}_j}\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0).
\]

This invariance means that by restricting finetuning updates to orthogonal transformations, OFT *provably preserves* the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained neurons, maintaining the intrinsic semantic structure encoded by their angular relationships.

In contrast, unconstrained finetuning can arbitrarily distort these angles, potentially destroying semantic information and leading to unstable or degraded performance.

---

## Initialization and Why Start from Identity

Since OFT learns an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to transform pretrained weights, it is natural to initialize \(\mathbf{R}\) as the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}\). This initialization ensures that at the start of finetuning,

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{I} \mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{W}^0,
\]

meaning the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly.

Starting from identity has several benefits:

1. **Stable Initialization:** The model begins in a known, stable state with preserved pretrained performance.

2. **Smooth Adaptation:** The orthogonal transformation \(\mathbf{R}\) can gradually deviate from identity during training, allowing controlled adaptation.

3. **Preservation of Hyperspherical Energy:** Since \(\mathbf{I}\) is orthogonal, the initial hyperspherical energy is exactly that of the pretrained model.

This initialization strategy aligns with the goal of finetuning: to adapt the model with minimal disruption to its pretrained knowledge.

---

## Interpretation: Changing Canonical Coordinates vs Changing Neurons

OFT can be interpreted from two complementary perspectives:

1. **Changing Neuron Directions:** Applying \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates or reflects the neurons themselves, changing their directions in the input space. This modifies the semantic features the neurons represent, enabling the model to adapt to new tasks.

2. **Changing Canonical Coordinates:** Alternatively, OFT can be seen as changing the coordinate system or basis in which the neurons are expressed. The pretrained neurons \(\mathbf{W}^0\) remain fixed, but the input \(\mathbf{x}\) is effectively transformed by \(\mathbf{R}^\top\). This viewpoint highlights that OFT adjusts the representation space without altering the intrinsic relationships among neurons.

Both interpretations emphasize that OFT preserves the *relative geometry* of neurons, which is crucial for maintaining semantic consistency.

---

## Phase-Spectrum Analogy Revisited: Why Angle Changes Affect Semantics

An intuitive analogy for OFT comes from the 2D Fourier transform of images, which decomposes an image into magnitude and phase spectra:

- The **magnitude spectrum** encodes the strength of frequency components.

- The **phase spectrum** encodes the angular relationships between input signals and basis functions.

Empirically, the phase spectrum preserves most of the semantic content of an image. For example, reconstructing an image using the original phase spectrum but random magnitudes still yields a recognizable image.

By analogy, the directions (angles) of neurons in a neural network encode semantic information, while their magnitudes are less critical. OFT focuses on modifying neuron directions via orthogonal transformations, which correspond to phase-like changes, enabling semantic adaptation.

This analogy supports the hypothesis that controlling neuron angles is key to effective finetuning without losing pretrained semantics.

---

## Practical Considerations for Applying OFT to Layers in Networks

While the formalism above focuses on fully connected layers, OFT is broadly applicable to various layer types in text-to-image diffusion models:

- **Attention Layers:** OFT can be applied to the weight matrices in self-attention and cross-attention modules, which are critical for conditioning on text prompts.

- **Feedforward Networks:** The multilayer perceptron (MLP) blocks within Transformer architectures can be finetuned with OFT by applying orthogonal transformations to their weight matrices.

- **Convolutional Layers:** OFT can be adapted to convolutional kernels by interpreting them as collections of neurons and applying block-diagonal orthogonal transformations to preserve computational efficiency.

In practice, OFT is implemented by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) per finetuned layer, initialized as identity and optimized with appropriate parameterizations (e.g., Cayley transform) to maintain orthogonality.

This approach yields several practical benefits:

- **Parameter Efficiency:** OFT introduces relatively few additional parameters compared to full finetuning.

- **Stability:** By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT stabilizes training and prevents catastrophic forgetting.

- **No Inference Overhead:** The orthogonal transformation can be merged into the pretrained weights after finetuning, incurring no additional cost during inference.

---

# Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled finetuning method that adapts pretrained text-to-image diffusion models by learning layer-wise orthogonal transformations of neuron weights. This approach preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, as measured by hyperspherical energy, thereby maintaining the semantic structure learned during pretraining. OFT balances flexibility and stability by allowing meaningful semantic adaptation through rotations/reflections of neuron directions while constraining updates to preserve pretrained knowledge. Initialized at identity, OFT smoothly adapts models with minimal disruption and can be efficiently applied to various network layers without inference overhead. The phase-spectrum analogy further supports the focus on neuron directions as the key to semantic control in finetuning.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterizations

In the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework, a key technical challenge is how to efficiently represent and optimize orthogonal matrices that transform pretrained neuron weights while preserving their pairwise angular relationships. This chapter delves into the mathematical and computational techniques used to parameterize these orthogonal transformations in a way that is both computationally feasible and effective for large-scale neural networks, such as those used in text-to-image diffusion models.

We begin by discussing why naive orthogonalization methods are computationally expensive and impractical for large models. Then, we introduce the Cayley parameterization, a differentiable and efficient approach to generate orthogonal matrices, along with its properties and limitations. Next, we explore the distinction between the special orthogonal group and the full orthogonal group, which has implications for the types of transformations achievable. To further improve parameter efficiency, we describe the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix design and the idea of block sharing. Finally, we analyze the parameter complexity of OFT compared to other finetuning methods like LoRA and discuss practical considerations such as numerical stability and computational cost.

---

## Why Naive Orthogonalization is Expensive

Orthogonal matrices are square matrices \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) satisfying the condition

\[
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I},
\]

where \( \mathbf{I} \) is the \( d \times d \) identity matrix. This condition ensures that \( \mathbf{R} \) represents a rotation or reflection in \( d \)-dimensional space, preserving lengths and angles.

A straightforward way to obtain an orthogonal matrix from an arbitrary matrix is to apply orthogonalization procedures such as the Gram–Schmidt process or QR decomposition. For example, given a matrix \( \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \), one can compute its QR decomposition:

\[
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R},
\]

where \( \mathbf{Q} \) is orthogonal and \( \mathbf{R} \) is upper-triangular. The matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \) can then be used as the orthogonal matrix.

However, these methods have several drawbacks when used in the context of neural network finetuning:

1. **Computational Cost:** QR decomposition or Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization requires \( \mathcal{O}(d^3) \) operations, which becomes prohibitive for large \( d \) (e.g., hundreds or thousands of neurons per layer).

2. **Differentiability and Stability:** While QR decomposition is differentiable, backpropagating through it can be numerically unstable and computationally expensive, complicating gradient-based optimization.

3. **Parameterization Complexity:** These methods do not provide a direct parameterization of the orthogonal matrix in terms of unconstrained parameters, making it difficult to optimize the orthogonal matrix directly.

Because of these limitations, naive orthogonalization is not practical for finetuning large models, motivating the need for more efficient and differentiable parameterizations of orthogonal matrices.

---

## Cayley Parameterization: Formula, Properties, and Limitations

To efficiently parameterize orthogonal matrices for optimization, OFT adopts the **Cayley transform**, a classical construction from linear algebra that maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices.

### The Cayley Transform

Given a skew-symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) satisfying

\[
\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q},
\]

the Cayley transform defines an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) as

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\]

Here, \( \mathbf{I} \) is the identity matrix, and \( (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}) \) is invertible as long as \( \mathbf{Q} \) does not have eigenvalue 1.

### Properties

1. **Orthogonality:** The matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) constructed via the Cayley transform is orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
\]

2. **Differentiability:** The Cayley transform is differentiable with respect to \( \mathbf{Q} \), enabling gradient-based optimization of \( \mathbf{Q} \) to indirectly optimize \( \mathbf{R} \).

3. **Parameterization via Skew-Symmetric Matrices:** Since \( \mathbf{Q} \) is skew-symmetric, it has \( d(d-1)/2 \) independent parameters, which matches the degrees of freedom of the orthogonal group \( O(d) \).

4. **Initialization:** Setting \( \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0} \) yields \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \), which corresponds to starting finetuning from the pretrained weights without any transformation.

### Limitations

- **Determinant Constraint:** The Cayley transform only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant \( +1 \), i.e., it parameterizes the **special orthogonal group** \( SO(d) \), which consists of pure rotations without reflections. Orthogonal matrices with determinant \( -1 \) (reflections) are not reachable via the Cayley transform.

- **Matrix Inversion:** Computing \( (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) requires a matrix inversion at each forward pass, which can be computationally expensive for large \( d \).

- **Domain Restrictions:** The matrix \( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} \) must be invertible, which restricts the allowable values of \( \mathbf{Q} \) during optimization.

Despite these limitations, the Cayley transform offers a practical and differentiable way to parameterize orthogonal matrices for OFT, balancing expressivity and computational feasibility.

---

## Special Orthogonal Group vs Full Orthogonal Group

The set of all orthogonal matrices of size \( d \times d \) forms the **orthogonal group** \( O(d) \), which has two connected components distinguished by the determinant:

- **Special Orthogonal Group \( SO(d) \):** Orthogonal matrices with determinant \( +1 \). These represent pure rotations in \( d \)-dimensional space.

- **Reflection Component:** Orthogonal matrices with determinant \( -1 \), representing reflections combined with rotations.

The Cayley transform parameterizes only \( SO(d) \), excluding reflections. In the context of OFT, this restriction is acceptable because:

- Rotations alone are sufficient to preserve pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy.

- Empirically, the inability to represent reflections does not degrade finetuning performance.

- The determinant \( -1 \) component is a measure-zero subset and often unnecessary for practical finetuning.

Thus, OFT focuses on learning orthogonal transformations within \( SO(d) \), simplifying parameterization and optimization.

---

## Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Motivation and Design

While the Cayley transform reduces the problem of parameterizing orthogonal matrices to optimizing skew-symmetric matrices, the number of parameters remains quadratic in \( d \):

\[
\text{Number of parameters} = \frac{d(d-1)}{2} = \mathcal{O}(d^2).
\]

For large layers (e.g., \( d = 1024 \)), this is still computationally expensive and memory-intensive.

To address this, OFT introduces a **block-diagonal structure** for the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \):

\[
\mathbf{R} = \text{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{R}_r),
\]

where each block \( \mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}} \) is an orthogonal matrix parameterized independently.

### Benefits of Block-Diagonal Structure

1. **Parameter Efficiency:** The total number of parameters reduces to

\[
r \times \frac{\frac{d}{r} \left( \frac{d}{r} - 1 \right)}{2} = \frac{d \left( \frac{d}{r} - 1 \right)}{2} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right),
\]

which is a factor of \( r \) smaller than the full matrix.

2. **Computational Efficiency:** Matrix multiplications and inversions are performed on smaller blocks, reducing computational cost.

3. **Regularization:** The block structure imposes an inductive bias that limits the complexity of the transformation, potentially improving finetuning stability.

4. **Interpretability:** In convolutional layers, blocks can correspond to groups of channels, allowing channel-wise orthogonal transformations.

### Design Choices

- The number of blocks \( r \) is a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between flexibility and efficiency.

- When \( r = 1 \), the block-diagonal matrix reduces to a full orthogonal matrix.

- When \( r = d \), each block is \( 1 \times 1 \), and \( \mathbf{R} \) becomes a diagonal matrix with entries \( \pm 1 \), which is too restrictive.

- Intermediate values of \( r \) (e.g., 8, 16, 32) balance parameter count and expressivity.

---

## Block Sharing to Further Reduce Parameter Counts

To further reduce the number of trainable parameters, OFT optionally employs **block sharing**, where all blocks \( \mathbf{R}_i \) are constrained to be identical:

\[
\mathbf{R}_1 = \mathbf{R}_2 = \cdots = \mathbf{R}_r.
\]

This reduces the parameter count to that of a single block:

\[
\frac{\frac{d}{r} \left( \frac{d}{r} - 1 \right)}{2} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right).
\]

### Advantages

- **Parameter Reduction:** Sharing blocks reduces parameters quadratically with respect to \( r \).

- **Regularization:** Sharing enforces uniform transformations across blocks, which can improve generalization and stability.

- **Simplified Optimization:** Fewer parameters mean faster convergence and less risk of overfitting.

### Trade-offs

- **Reduced Flexibility:** Sharing blocks limits the diversity of transformations across neuron groups.

- **Potential Bias:** The uniformity assumption may not hold for all layers or tasks.

In practice, block sharing is a useful tool to tailor OFT to resource constraints and task requirements.

---

## Parameter Count and Complexity Comparisons vs LoRA

To understand the efficiency of OFT, it is instructive to compare its parameter complexity with that of LoRA, a popular low-rank adaptation method.

### LoRA Parameterization

LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) by adding a low-rank matrix:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^0 + \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}^\top,
\]

where \( \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r'} \) and \( \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r'} \), with \( r' \ll \min(d, n) \).

- Number of trainable parameters in LoRA:

\[
r'(d + n) = \mathcal{O}(r' (d + n)).
\]

### OFT Parameterization

OFT parameterizes the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) via block-diagonal Cayley transforms with \( r \) blocks:

- Number of parameters per block:

\[
\frac{\frac{d}{r} \left( \frac{d}{r} - 1 \right)}{2} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right).
\]

- Total parameters without block sharing:

\[
r \times \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right).
\]

- With block sharing:

\[
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right).
\]

### Complexity Comparison

Assuming \( r = r' = \alpha d \) for some \( 0 < \alpha \leq 1 \), and \( d \approx n \), we have:

- LoRA parameters:

\[
r'(d + n) \approx \alpha d (2d) = 2 \alpha d^2 = \mathcal{O}(d^2).
\]

- OFT parameters without block sharing:

\[
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{\alpha d}\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d}{\alpha}\right) = \mathcal{O}(d).
\]

- OFT parameters with block sharing:

\[
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^2}{\alpha^2 d^2}\right) = \mathcal{O}(1).
\]

This shows that OFT can be significantly more parameter-efficient than LoRA, especially with block sharing, scaling linearly or even constant with respect to \( d \), compared to LoRA's quadratic scaling.

### Concrete Example

For a weight matrix of size \( 128 \times 128 \):

- LoRA with \( r' = 8 \) has:

\[
8 \times (128 + 128) = 8 \times 256 = 2048
\]

trainable parameters.

- OFT with \( r = 8 \) (no block sharing) has:

\[
8 \times \frac{16 \times 15}{2} = 8 \times 120 = 960
\]

trainable parameters.

Thus, OFT achieves fewer trainable parameters while preserving orthogonality constraints.

---

## Numerical Stability, Matrix Inverse Cost, and Practical Tricks

While the Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal design improve efficiency, practical implementation requires attention to numerical stability and computational cost.

### Matrix Inversion Cost

- The Cayley transform requires computing \( (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) at each forward pass.

- For full \( d \times d \) matrices, this inversion costs \( \mathcal{O}(d^3) \), which is expensive.

- Block-diagonal structure reduces inversion to smaller blocks of size \( \frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r} \), lowering cost to \( r \times \mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)^3\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^3}{r^2}\right) \).

- Choosing a larger \( r \) (more blocks) reduces inversion cost but also reduces transformation flexibility.

### Numerical Stability

- The matrix \( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} \) must be invertible. During optimization, \( \mathbf{Q} \) should be constrained to avoid eigenvalues close to 1.

- Initialization at \( \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0} \) ensures invertibility at the start.

- Gradient clipping or norm constraints on \( \mathbf{Q} \) can prevent instability.

- Projected gradient descent can enforce constraints on \( \mathbf{Q} \) (e.g., norm bounds) to maintain stability.

### Practical Tricks

- **Initialization:** Initialize \( \mathbf{Q} \) as zero matrices to start from identity orthogonal matrices.

- **Block Size Selection:** Choose block sizes balancing parameter efficiency and computational cost.

- **Regularization:** Apply weight decay or norm constraints on \( \mathbf{Q} \) to prevent large deviations.

- **Numerical Precision:** Use 32-bit floating point or higher precision to reduce inversion errors.

- **Caching:** Cache inverses if \( \mathbf{Q} \) changes slowly during training.

- **Parallelization:** Exploit block structure for parallel computation of inverses and matrix multiplications.

---

# Summary

Efficient orthogonal parameterization is crucial for the practical success of Orthogonal Finetuning. The Cayley transform provides a differentiable and theoretically sound way to parameterize orthogonal matrices via skew-symmetric matrices. To handle large neuron dimensions, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices reduce parameter and computational complexity, with optional block sharing further improving efficiency. Compared to low-rank additive methods like LoRA, OFT offers a multiplicative, angle-preserving update with competitive or better parameter efficiency. Careful attention to numerical stability and matrix inversion cost ensures that OFT can be effectively applied to large-scale text-to-image diffusion models, enabling stable and efficient finetuning with preserved semantic structure.

# Constrained and Extended Variants: COFT and Re-scaled OFT

In the previous chapter, we introduced the core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which adapts pretrained text-to-image diffusion models by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons in each layer. This approach preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, thereby maintaining the hyperspherical energy and the semantic structure learned during pretraining. While OFT provides a principled and effective finetuning method, there are practical considerations and potential improvements that motivate the development of variants. In this chapter, we explore two important extensions: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) and Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (Re-scaled OFT). These variants introduce additional constraints or flexibility to improve finetuning stability, control, and expressiveness.

---

## COFT Formulation: Orthogonality Plus Epsilon-Deviation Constraint

The original OFT framework enforces that the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ in a given layer is obtained by applying an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0, \quad \text{with} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]

This orthogonality constraint ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved, maintaining the hyperspherical energy and thus the semantic structure of the pretrained model. However, while orthogonality restricts the transformation to rotations and reflections, it still allows for potentially large deviations from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. In other words, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be any rotation or reflection, which might cause the finetuned model to deviate significantly from the pretrained model in terms of Euclidean distance, potentially affecting stability and generalizability.

To address this, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) introduces an additional radius or deviation constraint that explicitly limits how far $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity matrix. Formally, COFT solves the following constrained optimization problem:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0, \quad \text{subject to} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \quad \text{and} \quad \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon,
\]

where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a suitable matrix norm (e.g., spectral norm or Frobenius norm), and $\epsilon > 0$ is a hyperparameter controlling the maximum allowed deviation from the identity. This constraint ensures that the orthogonal transformation remains close to the identity, effectively restricting the finetuning to a neighborhood around the pretrained weights.

The intuition behind COFT is that by explicitly bounding the deviation, the finetuning process becomes more stable and less prone to overfitting or catastrophic forgetting. It also makes the hyperparameter tuning easier, as the radius $\epsilon$ directly controls the flexibility of the finetuning.

---

## Neumann Series Approximation and Mapping Constraint to $Q$

Recall from the efficient orthogonal parameterization chapter that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized via the Cayley transform:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

where $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. This parameterization guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal with determinant $+1$, i.e., $\bm{R} \in SO(d)$, the special orthogonal group.

The radius constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is nontrivial to enforce directly on $\bm{R}$ because it involves a matrix inverse and a nonlinear mapping from $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{R}$. To simplify this, we use the Neumann series expansion to approximate the inverse:

\[
(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \bm{Q}^3 + \cdots,
\]

assuming the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ is less than 1 for convergence. Using this, we approximate $\bm{R}$ as:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]

Neglecting higher-order terms, we have:

\[
\bm{R} - \bm{I} \approx 2 \bm{Q}.
\]

Therefore, the radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ approximately translates to a constraint on $\bm{Q}$:

\[
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon \quad \Rightarrow \quad \|2 \bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon \quad \Rightarrow \quad \|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon' := \frac{\epsilon}{2}.
\]

This approximation allows us to enforce the radius constraint by directly constraining the skew-symmetric parameter matrix $\bm{Q}$, which is much easier to handle during optimization.

---

## Projected Gradient Descent for Enforcing Radius Constraint

With the radius constraint mapped to $\bm{Q}$, we can enforce $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ during training using projected gradient descent (PGD). The procedure is as follows:

1. **Gradient Step:** Update $\bm{Q}$ by taking a gradient descent step with respect to the finetuning loss.

2. **Projection:** After the gradient step, project $\bm{Q}$ back onto the feasible set defined by the radius constraint:

\[
\bm{Q} \leftarrow \arg\min_{\bm{Q}': \|\bm{Q}'\| \leq \epsilon'} \|\bm{Q}' - \bm{Q}\|.
\]

This projection is straightforward if we use the Frobenius norm: if $\|\bm{Q}\|_F \leq \epsilon'$, do nothing; otherwise, scale $\bm{Q}$ to have norm $\epsilon'$:

\[
\bm{Q} \leftarrow \epsilon' \frac{\bm{Q}}{\|\bm{Q}\|_F}.
\]

3. **Skew-Symmetry Maintenance:** Since $\bm{Q}$ must remain skew-symmetric, the gradient update and projection should preserve this property. In practice, this is ensured by parameterizing $\bm{Q}$ as $\bm{Q} = \bm{A} - \bm{A}^\top$ for some unconstrained matrix $\bm{A}$, or by explicitly symmetrizing after each update.

4. **Initialization:** Initialize $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix, so that initially $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, meaning the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights.

This PGD approach ensures that the orthogonal transformation remains within a controlled neighborhood of the identity, improving finetuning stability and preventing large, potentially harmful deviations.

---

## Re-scaled OFT: Forward Pass with Diagonal Scaling Matrix

While OFT and COFT preserve the pairwise angles between neurons by applying orthogonal transformations, they do not modify the magnitudes (norms) of the neurons. However, neuron magnitudes can also carry useful information and provide additional flexibility in finetuning.

To incorporate this, Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning extends OFT by introducing a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, where $n$ is the number of neurons in the layer. The diagonal entries $s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n$ are positive scaling coefficients applied to each neuron individually.

The forward pass in Re-scaled OFT is:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0 \bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]

where:

- $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the orthogonal matrix parameterized as before,
- $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is the pretrained weight matrix,
- $\bm{D} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n)$ is the diagonal scaling matrix,
- $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector,
- $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output vector.

Because the scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ is diagonal and positive, it rescales each neuron independently without affecting the angles between neurons after normalization. This means the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged, preserving the semantic structure while allowing magnitude adjustments.

The number of additional parameters introduced by $\bm{D}$ is only $n$, which is negligible compared to the size of $\bm{R}$ or the full weight matrix. This extension improves the flexibility of OFT, enabling it to better adapt to downstream tasks without sacrificing the core benefits of angle preservation.

---

## When to Use COFT vs OFT vs Re-scaled OFT

Choosing among OFT, COFT, and Re-scaled OFT depends on the specific requirements of the finetuning task, including stability, flexibility, and computational budget.

- **OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning):**  
  Use OFT when you want to preserve the hyperspherical energy exactly and allow the model to explore the full space of orthogonal transformations. OFT is suitable when you have moderate data and want a principled finetuning method that balances flexibility and stability without explicit radius constraints.

- **COFT (Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning):**  
  Use COFT when finetuning stability is paramount, especially in low-data regimes or when you want to avoid catastrophic forgetting. The explicit radius constraint $\epsilon$ controls the deviation from the pretrained model, making hyperparameter tuning easier and finetuning more robust. COFT is recommended when you want to limit the finetuning flexibility to a neighborhood around the pretrained weights.

- **Re-scaled OFT:**  
  Use Re-scaled OFT when you want to add a small amount of additional flexibility by allowing neuron magnitudes to adapt, in addition to directions. This variant is useful when the downstream task requires subtle magnitude adjustments that pure orthogonal transformations cannot provide. It is a lightweight extension that can improve performance with minimal parameter overhead.

In practice, COFT and Re-scaled OFT can be combined to gain both stability and flexibility, though this may require careful tuning of hyperparameters.

---

## Hyperparameters: Epsilon, Block Size, Learning Rates

The performance and behavior of COFT and Re-scaled OFT depend on several hyperparameters:

- **Radius Constraint $\epsilon$ (COFT):**  
  Controls the maximum allowed deviation of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from the identity. Smaller $\epsilon$ values enforce stricter constraints, leading to more stable but less flexible finetuning. Larger $\epsilon$ values relax the constraint, allowing more freedom but potentially risking instability. Empirically, moderate values of $\epsilon$ strike a good balance.

- **Block Size $r$ (OFT and COFT):**  
  To reduce parameter complexity, $\bm{R}$ is often parameterized as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks. Smaller block sizes (larger $r$) reduce parameters and impose stronger regularization but limit flexibility. Larger block sizes increase flexibility but also parameter count and computational cost. Choosing $r$ depends on the layer dimension and available resources.

- **Learning Rates:**  
  The learning rate for optimizing $\bm{Q}$ (and $\bm{D}$ in Re-scaled OFT) should be chosen carefully. Too large learning rates can cause instability or violate constraints; too small learning rates slow convergence. COFT’s radius constraint helps stabilize training, allowing for more forgiving learning rates.

- **Initialization:**  
  Initialize $\bm{Q}$ as zero to start from the identity matrix $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Initialize scaling coefficients $s_i$ in $\bm{D}$ to 1 to start with no magnitude change.

Hyperparameter tuning should consider the trade-off between finetuning flexibility and stability, guided by validation performance and convergence behavior.

---

## Empirical Effects of Constraints on Stability and Flexibility

Empirical studies in the original paper demonstrate the following effects of COFT and Re-scaled OFT:

- **COFT Improves Stability:**  
  By explicitly constraining the deviation from the pretrained model, COFT reduces the risk of model collapse or overfitting during finetuning. It yields smoother convergence curves and less sensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations or learning rate choices.

- **COFT Controls Flexibility:**  
  The radius parameter $\epsilon$ acts as a knob to tune the flexibility of finetuning. Smaller $\epsilon$ values make the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained model, preserving generalization but limiting adaptation. Larger $\epsilon$ values allow more adaptation but risk losing pretrained knowledge.

- **Re-scaled OFT Enhances Expressiveness:**  
  Adding per-neuron scaling allows the model to adjust neuron magnitudes, which can improve downstream task performance without sacrificing the angle preservation property. This extension often leads to better final accuracy or image quality with minimal additional parameters.

- **Trade-offs:**  
  While COFT improves stability, it may slightly reduce the ultimate flexibility of the model. Re-scaled OFT adds flexibility but introduces a small number of extra parameters. The choice depends on the task complexity and data availability.

In summary, COFT and Re-scaled OFT provide practical tools to tailor the OFT framework to different finetuning scenarios, balancing stability, flexibility, and parameter efficiency.

# Connections to Other Adaptation Methods

In this chapter, we explore how Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its variants relate to and differ from other popular finetuning and adaptation methods used in text-to-image diffusion models. Understanding these connections is crucial for appreciating the conceptual novelty and practical advantages of OFT, as well as recognizing scenarios where alternative methods might be preferable. We analyze OFT in relation to additive low-rank updates like LoRA, adapter-based tuning mechanisms, and task-specific finetuning approaches such as DreamBooth, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter. We also discuss the implications of these differences for convergence speed, parameter efficiency, stability, and inference overhead.

## Rewriting OFT as a Multiplicative Update and Relation to Additive Updates

At its core, OFT adapts a pretrained weight matrix by applying a learned orthogonal transformation. Consider a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each column $\bm{w}_i^0$ represents a neuron. OFT modifies this layer by introducing an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, constrained such that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, and defines the finetuned weights as

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0.
\]

The forward pass changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to

\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]

This multiplicative update can be rewritten as an additive update by expanding $\bm{R}$ around the identity matrix $\bm{I}$:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = (\bm{I} + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})) \bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0.
\]

Here, $(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$ acts as an additive update to the pretrained weights. However, unlike typical additive updates, the matrix $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$ is constrained by the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$, which imposes a strong structural regularization on the update.

This perspective reveals that OFT can be viewed as a structured additive update, where the update is the product of a constrained matrix $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. This contrasts with other methods that directly add unconstrained or low-rank matrices to $\bm{W}^0$.

## LoRA vs OFT: Low-Rank Additive Update vs Block-Orthogonal Multiplicative Update

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a widely used parameter-efficient finetuning method that models the weight update as a low-rank additive matrix. Specifically, LoRA assumes the finetuned weights take the form

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A} \bm{B},
\]

where $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r'}$ and $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{r' \times n}$ are learnable low-rank matrices with $r' \ll \min(d, n)$. This low-rank structure drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters and constrains the update to a low-dimensional subspace.

In contrast, OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to $\bm{W}^0$, with $\bm{R}$ often parameterized as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix to improve parameter efficiency. The block-diagonal structure partitions the $d$-dimensional neuron space into $r$ blocks, each transformed by a smaller orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}}$. This design balances flexibility and regularity, preserving pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) while limiting the number of parameters.

The key differences between LoRA and OFT are:

- **Update Type:** LoRA uses an additive low-rank update, while OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal update.

- **Parameter Efficiency:** LoRA reduces parameters by low-rank factorization; OFT reduces parameters by block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization and sharing.

- **Regularization:** OFT's orthogonality constraint preserves neuron angular relationships, promoting stability and semantic preservation. LoRA's low-rank constraint limits the update's expressivity but does not explicitly preserve neuron geometry.

- **Expressivity:** OFT's multiplicative update can represent a richer class of transformations within the orthogonal group, potentially capturing more nuanced semantic changes.

- **Initialization:** OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix, ensuring the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights. LoRA typically initializes $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ to zero matrices for the same effect.

In terms of parameter counts, for a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, LoRA's trainable parameters scale as $r'(d + n)$, while OFT's parameters scale roughly as $d^2 / r$ (or less with block sharing). For large $d$, OFT can be more parameter-efficient if $r$ is chosen appropriately.

## Adapters and Tuning Mechanisms in Transformers

Adapter-based methods insert small trainable modules into pretrained Transformer architectures, enabling efficient finetuning with minimal parameter updates. These adapters typically consist of bottleneck feedforward layers or attention modifications and are trained while freezing the original model weights.

ControlNet and T2I-Adapter extend this idea to text-to-image diffusion models by adding trainable modules that condition the model on additional control signals (e.g., edges, segmentation maps). These methods finetune the model by learning new parameters that modulate the pretrained network's behavior without altering its core weights.

OFT differs from adapter-based methods in that it directly modifies the pretrained weights via orthogonal transformations rather than adding new modules. This approach has several implications:

- **Parameter Efficiency:** OFT updates existing weights with a structured transformation, often requiring fewer parameters than adapters that add new layers.

- **Inference Overhead:** Adapters increase model size and computational cost during inference, as additional modules are evaluated. OFT introduces no extra inference overhead because the orthogonal transformation can be merged into the pretrained weights before deployment.

- **Stability and Preservation:** By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT maintains pretrained semantic structures more explicitly than adapters, which may alter the model's behavior more freely.

- **Flexibility:** Adapters can introduce more flexible conditioning mechanisms, potentially capturing complex control signals beyond what orthogonal transformations can represent.

## Why OFT Can Converge Faster: Manifold View and Search Space Reduction

OFT constrains finetuning to the manifold of orthogonal transformations, which is a smooth Riemannian manifold embedded in Euclidean space. This constraint reduces the effective search space during optimization, focusing updates on transformations that preserve neuron angular relationships and thus pretrained semantic structures.

This manifold-constrained optimization has several benefits:

- **Improved Stability:** By preserving pairwise neuron angles, OFT avoids drastic semantic shifts that can destabilize training.

- **Faster Convergence:** The reduced search space and smooth manifold structure lead to more predictable and efficient optimization trajectories.

- **Better Generalization:** Preserving hyperspherical energy helps maintain the pretrained model's generalization capabilities, reducing overfitting to downstream data.

In contrast, unconstrained additive updates (e.g., standard finetuning or LoRA) explore a larger parameter space, which can lead to slower convergence and instability, especially with limited data.

## When Additive Updates May Be Preferable

Despite OFT's advantages, additive updates like LoRA or adapters may be preferable in certain scenarios:

- **Expressivity Beyond Orthogonal Transformations:** Some downstream tasks may require transformations that cannot be well approximated by orthogonal matrices, such as scaling or shearing neuron directions.

- **Nonlinear Modulation:** Adapters can introduce nonlinear transformations and conditioning mechanisms that OFT's linear orthogonal updates cannot capture.

- **Simplicity and Implementation:** Additive updates are often simpler to implement and integrate into existing frameworks without the need for orthogonality constraints or matrix inverses.

- **Parameter Budget:** For very large models, low-rank additive updates may offer better parameter efficiency than block-orthogonal matrices, depending on the chosen ranks and block sizes.

- **Task-Specific Requirements:** Some tasks may benefit from the flexibility of additive updates to capture subtle or complex changes in model behavior.

Therefore, the choice between OFT and additive methods depends on the task, model architecture, and resource constraints.

## No Additional Inference Overhead: Practical Implications

A significant practical advantage of OFT is that it introduces no additional computational overhead during inference. Since the finetuned weights are obtained by multiplying the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix, the product $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ can be precomputed and stored as a single weight matrix before deployment.

In contrast, adapter-based methods and ControlNet introduce extra modules or networks that must be evaluated alongside the base model, increasing inference time and memory usage.

This property makes OFT particularly attractive for deployment scenarios where inference efficiency and model size are critical, such as on-device generation or real-time applications.

## Selecting Layers for OFT in UNet / Transformer Architectures

Text-to-image diffusion models, such as Stable Diffusion, typically consist of a UNet backbone with Transformer-based components, including attention layers and feed-forward networks (FFNs). When applying OFT, a key practical question is: **which layers should be finetuned with orthogonal transformations?**

### Attention Layers

Attention layers are central to the model’s ability to fuse text and image information. They contain weight matrices for query, key, value, and output projections. These matrices are often large and have a significant impact on the model’s semantic representation.

- **Why finetune attention weights?**  
  Attention weights directly influence how textual prompts guide image generation. Finetuning these weights with OFT can effectively adapt the model’s semantic alignment without disrupting pretrained knowledge.

- **Which attention weights?**  
  Typically, OFT is applied to the query and value projection matrices, as these are most sensitive to input changes. The key and output projections can also be finetuned, but this increases parameter count and complexity.

### Feed-Forward Networks (FFNs)

FFNs consist of fully connected layers that process intermediate representations. While important, finetuning FFNs with OFT is less common in initial experiments due to the larger parameter size and potentially less direct impact on semantic control.

- **When to finetune FFNs?**  
  If additional flexibility is needed or if attention finetuning alone is insufficient, OFT can be extended to FFN layers. However, this increases computational cost and may require more careful hyperparameter tuning.

### Convolutional Layers

UNet architectures often include convolutional layers, especially in early and late stages of the network.

- **Applying OFT to convolutional kernels:**  
  Convolutional kernels can be viewed as collections of neurons arranged spatially and across channels. OFT can be applied by treating each kernel as a weight matrix and learning orthogonal transformations per channel or per group of channels.

- **Block-diagonal interpretation:**  
  The block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in OFT naturally aligns with channel-wise transformations in convolutional layers, allowing efficient and interpretable finetuning.

### Practical Recommendation

- Start by applying OFT to the attention projection matrices, especially query and value weights, as these provide a good balance between control and parameter efficiency.
- Optionally extend to FFNs or convolutional layers if needed, but monitor computational overhead.
- For fair comparison with other methods like LoRA, apply OFT to the same layers where LoRA is used.

---

## Applying OFT to Attention Weight Matrices and Convolutional Kernels

### Attention Weight Matrices

Attention layers typically have weight matrices of shape $(d_{\text{model}}, d_{\text{head}} \times n_{\text{heads}})$, where $d_{\text{model}}$ is the model dimension, $d_{\text{head}}$ is the dimension per attention head, and $n_{\text{heads}}$ is the number of heads.

- **Reshaping for OFT:**  
  OFT requires applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. For attention weights, reshape or view the weight matrix so that the neuron dimension aligns with $d$.

- **Block-diagonal structure:**  
  To reduce parameters, partition $\bm{R}$ into blocks corresponding to attention heads or groups of neurons. This respects the architecture’s modularity and reduces computational cost.

- **Forward pass modification:**  
  Replace the original weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ with the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ during the forward pass.

### Convolutional Kernels

Convolutional layers have weight tensors of shape $(C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k_h, k_w)$, where $C_{\text{out}}$ and $C_{\text{in}}$ are output and input channels, and $k_h$, $k_w$ are kernel height and width.

- **Flattening kernels:**  
  Flatten each convolutional kernel into a matrix of shape $(C_{\text{in}} \times k_h \times k_w, C_{\text{out}})$ or vice versa, depending on the framework.

- **Applying OFT:**  
  Learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ acting on the flattened dimension corresponding to neurons. The block-diagonal structure can be aligned with input or output channels.

- **Interpretation:**  
  Each block in $\bm{R}$ can be seen as a depth-wise orthogonal transformation, similar to depth-wise convolutions, preserving the hyperspherical energy within each channel group.

- **Reconstruction:**  
  After applying $\bm{R}$, reshape the weight matrix back to the original convolutional kernel shape for the forward pass.

## Parameter Initialization: Identity $\bm{R}$ and Zero $\bm{Q}$

OFT relies on learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ parameterized via the Cayley transform:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
\]

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$).

### Initialization Strategy

- **Initialize $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix:**  
  This yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning, meaning the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly.

- **Why identity initialization?**  
  Starting from the identity ensures that the finetuning process begins with no deviation, preserving pretrained knowledge and enabling stable optimization.

- **Block-diagonal $\bm{Q}$:**  
  If using block-diagonal parameterization, initialize each block $\bm{Q}_i$ as zero matrices of appropriate size.

### Practical Tips

- Ensure that the skew-symmetric property of $\bm{Q}$ is maintained during optimization by parameterizing only the upper (or lower) triangular part and reflecting it.

- Use a parameter vector representing the independent elements of $\bm{Q}$ to reduce redundancy.

---

## Optimization: Learning Rate Schedules and Regularization

### Learning Rate

- **Start with a moderate learning rate:**  
  OFT typically converges faster than standard finetuning or LoRA, so a slightly higher learning rate (e.g., $1 \times 10^{-3}$) can be used initially.

- **Learning rate decay:**  
  Employ learning rate decay schedules (e.g., cosine decay or step decay) to stabilize training as it progresses.

- **Separate learning rates:**  
  If finetuning multiple layers or components, consider using different learning rates for $\bm{R}$ parameters and other model parameters.

### Regularization

- **Weight decay:**  
  Apply weight decay to the parameters of $\bm{Q}$ to prevent overfitting and encourage smooth transformations.

- **Radius constraint (COFT):**  
  For Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), enforce the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ by projecting $\bm{Q}$ after each gradient step (see next section).

- **Early stopping:**  
  Monitor validation metrics and stop finetuning when performance plateaus or degrades to avoid overfitting.

### Optimization Algorithm

- Use standard optimizers such as Adam or AdamW, which handle sparse gradients and adaptive learning rates well.

- For projected gradient steps (in COFT), perform the projection after the optimizer update.

---

## Implementing Cayley Transform and Projected Gradients Efficiently

### Cayley Transform Computation

Recall the Cayley transform formula:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
\]

where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric.

- **Matrix inverse:**  
  Computing $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ requires a matrix inversion, which can be expensive for large $d$.

- **Block-diagonal parameterization:**  
  To reduce cost, partition $\bm{Q}$ into smaller blocks and compute inverses block-wise, which is much cheaper.

- **Numerical stability:**  
  Ensure that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ is invertible by keeping $\bm{Q}$ small in norm, which is naturally encouraged by initialization and regularization.

- **Differentiability:**  
  Matrix inversion is differentiable and supported by modern deep learning frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, TensorFlow).

### Projected Gradient Descent for COFT

For COFT, we enforce the constraint:

\[
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon
\]

Using the Neumann series approximation:

\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
\]

we approximate the constraint as:

\[
\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'
\]

where $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$.

- **Projection step:**  
  After each gradient update on $\bm{Q}$, project $\bm{Q}$ onto the ball of radius $\epsilon'$:

  \[
  \bm{Q} \leftarrow \begin{cases}
  \bm{Q}, & \text{if } \|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon' \\
  \epsilon' \cdot \frac{\bm{Q}}{\|\bm{Q}\|}, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \]

- **Implementation:**  
  This projection is straightforward to implement and computationally cheap.

- **Effect:**  
  The projection keeps the orthogonal transformation close to identity, improving finetuning stability.

---

## Memory and Runtime Profiling: Block Sizes and Trade-offs

### Parameter Efficiency

- **Full orthogonal matrix:**  
  For dimension $d$, a full orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which can be large for high-dimensional layers.

- **Block-diagonal structure:**  
  Dividing $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks reduces parameters to approximately $d(d/r - 1)/2$, a factor of $r$ reduction.

- **Block sharing:**  
  Sharing blocks further reduces parameters to $d(d/r^2 - 1)/2$.

### Computational Cost

- **Matrix inversion:**  
  Inversion cost scales cubically with block size, so smaller blocks reduce runtime significantly.

- **Forward pass:**  
  Multiplying $\bm{R}$ with $\bm{W}^0$ adds negligible overhead compared to the overall model forward pass.

- **Memory footprint:**  
  Storing $\bm{Q}$ and intermediate matrices requires additional memory, but block partitioning keeps this manageable.

### Choosing Block Size

- **Trade-off:**  
  Larger blocks provide more flexibility but increase parameters and computation.

- **Empirical guidance:**  
  Typical block sizes range from 8 to 64, depending on layer dimension and available resources.

- **Experimentation:**  
  Tune block size based on validation performance and resource constraints.

---

## Common Pitfalls, Debugging, and Reproducibility Tips

### Common Pitfalls

- **Orthogonality violation:**  
  Failing to enforce skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ or incorrect Cayley transform implementation can break orthogonality.

- **Numerical instability:**  
  Large $\bm{Q}$ norms can cause $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ to be near-singular, leading to unstable inverses.

- **Initialization errors:**  
  Not initializing $\bm{Q}$ as zero or $\bm{R}$ as identity can cause large initial deviations and unstable training.

- **Mismatch in dimensions:**  
  Incorrect reshaping of weight matrices or orthogonal matrices can cause shape errors or unintended behavior.

### Debugging Tips

- **Verify orthogonality:**  
  Periodically compute $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$ and check closeness to identity matrix.

- **Check skew-symmetry:**  
  Ensure $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$ holds after each update.

- **Monitor norms:**  
  Track $\|\bm{Q}\|$ and $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\|$ to detect divergence.

- **Unit tests:**  
  Implement small tests for Cayley transform correctness on known skew-symmetric matrices.

- **Gradient checks:**  
  Use numerical gradient checking to verify backpropagation through Cayley transform.

### Reproducibility Tips

- **Seed setting:**  
  Fix random seeds for weight initialization, data loading, and optimizer states.

- **Deterministic operations:**  
  Use deterministic algorithms where possible to reduce variability.

- **Logging:**  
  Record hyperparameters, training curves, and model checkpoints systematically.

- **Version control:**  
  Track code and dependency versions to ensure consistent environments.

---

By following these practical guidelines, you can effectively implement OFT in large text-to-image diffusion models, achieving stable and efficient finetuning with strong preservation of pretrained generative capabilities. The orthogonal parameterization and block-diagonal strategies provide a flexible yet regularized approach, balancing expressivity and stability in downstream adaptation tasks.

# Experimental Protocols and Evaluation Metrics

In this chapter, we provide a comprehensive overview of the experimental setups and evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in controlling text-to-image diffusion models. We focus on two primary downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. For each task, we describe the datasets employed, the training paradigms followed, the baseline methods used for comparison, and the evaluation metrics designed to quantify various aspects of generation quality and controllability. We also discuss the protocols for convergence and stability experiments, as well as statistical considerations to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of results.

---

## Datasets for Subject-Driven and Controllable Generation

The choice of datasets is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of finetuning methods like OFT, as they must provide diverse and challenging scenarios that reflect real-world applications. We summarize the datasets used for the two main tasks below.

### Subject-Driven Generation Datasets

Subject-driven generation aims to generate images of a specific subject in novel contexts, given only a few reference images of that subject. The datasets used for this task typically contain images of individual subjects with sufficient variation in pose, lighting, and background to enable meaningful finetuning.

- **DreamBooth Dataset Setup:** The experiments generally follow the setup introduced by DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}. This involves collecting a small set (usually 3 to 5) of images of a subject, such as a person, animal, or object, which serve as the reference for finetuning. The subject images are paired with text prompts that describe the subject and the desired context for generation.

- **Additional Subject Image Collections:** In some cases, datasets with multiple images of the same subject under different conditions are used to evaluate the robustness of finetuning methods. These datasets allow for testing the ability to preserve subject identity while varying other attributes.

### Controllable Generation Datasets

Controllable generation involves guiding the image synthesis process using additional control signals beyond text prompts, such as edge maps, segmentation masks, or facial landmarks. The datasets for this task provide paired control signals and images.

- **COCO Dataset (Canny Edge to Image - C2I):** The COCO dataset~\cite{lin2014microsoft} is a large-scale dataset containing diverse images with rich annotations. For the C2I task, Canny edge maps are extracted from COCO images to serve as control signals. The goal is to generate photorealistic images consistent with both the edge map and a text prompt.

- **ADE20K Dataset (Segmentation Map to Image - S2I):** ADE20K~\cite{zhou2017scene} provides densely annotated semantic segmentation maps for scenes. The S2I task uses these segmentation maps as control signals to guide image generation, testing the model's ability to respect semantic layouts.

- **CelebA-HQ Dataset (Landmark to Face - L2F):** CelebA-HQ~\cite{karras2018progressive,xia2021tedigan} contains high-quality face images with annotated facial landmarks. The L2F task uses these landmarks as control signals to generate faces with specific poses and expressions.

These datasets collectively cover a broad spectrum of control modalities and semantic complexities, providing a rigorous testbed for evaluating OFT and competing methods.

---

## Training Regimens: DreamBooth-like and ControlNet-like Pipelines

The training procedures for subject-driven and controllable generation tasks differ in their objectives and data modalities. We describe the general training pipelines used to finetune the pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 model~\cite{rombach2022high} with OFT and baseline methods.

### Subject-Driven Generation Training

- **Framework:** The training follows the DreamBooth framework~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, which finetunes the diffusion model to learn a new subject token representing the specific subject.

- **Input:** A small set of subject images and corresponding text prompts containing a unique identifier token for the subject.

- **Loss Functions:** The training optimizes a reconstruction loss to ensure the model can generate images faithful to the subject, along with a class-specific prior preservation loss to prevent overfitting and maintain generalization.

- **Finetuning Details:** OFT is applied to the same layers as LoRA for fair comparison, with orthogonal transformations learned per layer. The orthogonal matrices are initialized as identity to start from the pretrained weights.

- **Epochs and Iterations:** Typically, finetuning is performed for a few thousand iterations, with OFT demonstrating faster convergence and greater stability.

### Controllable Generation Training

- **Framework:** The training follows the ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i} paradigms, which finetune the diffusion model to incorporate additional control signals.

- **Input:** Paired control signals (e.g., edge maps, segmentation masks, landmarks) and text prompts.

- **Loss Functions:** The model is trained to generate images consistent with both the control signal and the text prompt, typically using a combination of reconstruction and perceptual losses.

- **Finetuning Details:** OFT is applied to the attention weights of the diffusion model, matching the layers where LoRA is applied for fair comparison.

- **Epochs:** Training is conducted for a fixed number of epochs (e.g., 20), with OFT showing faster convergence and better control consistency.

---

## Baselines and Fair-Comparison Principles

To rigorously evaluate OFT, it is compared against several state-of-the-art finetuning and adaptation methods under controlled and fair conditions.

- **DreamBooth:** The original subject-driven finetuning method, which updates the entire model weights with a small learning rate.

- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):** A parameter-efficient finetuning method that adds low-rank updates to pretrained weights, applied to the same layers as OFT.

- **ControlNet:** A controllable generation method that finetunes additional network components to incorporate control signals.

- **T2I-Adapter:** Another adapter-based method for controllable generation.

**Fairness Considerations:**

- All methods use the same pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone.

- The same training data, loss functions, and hyperparameter tuning efforts are applied to all methods.

- OFT and LoRA are applied to the same layers to ensure comparable parameter counts.

- Random seeds and sampling procedures are controlled to minimize variance.

This rigorous setup ensures that observed performance differences are attributable to the finetuning method rather than confounding factors.

---

## Perceptual and Semantic Metrics

Evaluating text-to-image generation quality and fidelity requires metrics that capture both visual realism and semantic alignment with the input prompts or control signals. We summarize the key metrics used.

### CLIP-based Metrics

- **CLIP-I (Image-Image Similarity):** Measures the average cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of generated images and real images of the same subject. Higher values indicate better subject fidelity.

- **CLIP-T (Text-Image Similarity):** Measures the cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of the text prompt and generated images, assessing prompt fidelity.

CLIP embeddings~\cite{radford2021learning} are widely used due to their strong alignment with human perception of semantic similarity.

### DINO

- **DINO (Self-Supervised ViT Embeddings):** Uses ViT S/16 DINO embeddings~\cite{caron2021emerging} to compute similarity between generated and real images, providing an alternative measure of subject fidelity.

### LPIPS

- **LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity):** Measures diversity among generated images by computing perceptual distances between pairs of generated samples with the same subject and prompt~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}. Higher LPIPS indicates greater diversity.

These metrics collectively assess how well the finetuned model preserves subject identity, respects the text prompt, and generates diverse outputs.

---

## Control Consistency Metric: Motivation and Computation

A novel contribution in evaluating controllable generation is the introduction of the **control consistency metric**, designed to quantitatively measure how well the generated images adhere to the input control signals.

### Motivation

Traditional metrics focus on image quality or semantic similarity but do not directly measure the fidelity of control adherence. The control consistency metric addresses this gap by:

- Estimating the control signal from the generated image using an off-the-shelf model.

- Comparing the estimated control signal to the original input control signal.

This approach provides a direct and interpretable measure of controllability.

### Computation per Task

- **Canny Edge to Image (C2I):** The control signal is a Canny edge map. After generating an image, Canny edges are extracted from the output and compared to the input edges using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score.

- **Segmentation Map to Image (S2I):** The control signal is a semantic segmentation map. A pretrained segmentation model is applied to the generated image to produce a predicted segmentation map. Metrics such as mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy are computed against the input segmentation.

- **Landmark to Face (L2F):** The control signal is a set of facial landmarks. A facial landmark detector is run on the generated image to extract predicted landmarks. The mean $\ell_2$ distance between predicted and input landmarks quantifies control accuracy.

This metric suite enables a rigorous and task-specific evaluation of controllability, complementing perceptual and semantic metrics.

---

## Protocol for Convergence and Stability Experiments

Understanding the training dynamics of finetuning methods is essential for practical deployment. The following protocols are used to evaluate convergence speed and stability.

- **Convergence Speed:** Models are trained for varying numbers of epochs or iterations. Performance metrics (e.g., control consistency, CLIP scores) are recorded at checkpoints to plot learning curves.

- **Stability:** The variance of performance metrics across training runs with different random seeds is measured. Stability is also assessed qualitatively by inspecting generated images for artifacts or collapse.

- **Early Stopping Sensitivity:** The sensitivity of model performance to the number of finetuning steps is evaluated to determine robustness to hyperparameter choices.

- **Data Efficiency:** Experiments with varying amounts of training data assess how quickly models learn with limited samples.

These protocols provide insights into the practical usability of OFT compared to baselines.

---

## Statistical Considerations: Seeds, Sampling, and Qualitative Selection

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of experimental results, the following statistical practices are adopted:

- **Multiple Random Seeds:** Each finetuning experiment is repeated with multiple random seeds (e.g., 30) to account for stochasticity in training and sampling.

- **Random Sampling of Generated Images:** For qualitative comparisons, images are randomly sampled from the model outputs without cherry-picking to avoid bias.

- **Reporting Means and Variances:** Quantitative metrics are reported as means with standard deviations or confidence intervals.

- **Human Evaluation:** Where applicable, human raters assess image quality and fidelity to complement automated metrics.

- **Ablation Studies:** Systematic variation of hyperparameters and model components helps isolate the effects of OFT design choices.

These practices ensure that conclusions drawn from experiments are statistically sound and generalizable.

---

# Summary

This chapter has detailed the experimental protocols and evaluation metrics essential for assessing Orthogonal Finetuning in text-to-image diffusion models. By carefully selecting datasets, adhering to fair training regimens, employing comprehensive perceptual and control metrics, and following rigorous statistical practices, researchers can robustly evaluate the benefits and limitations of OFT. These foundations enable meaningful comparisons to existing methods and guide future improvements in controllable image generation.

# Empirical Results: Subject-Driven Generation

In this chapter, we delve into the empirical evaluation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on the task of subject-driven generation. This task involves adapting a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to generate images of a specific subject, given only a few images of that subject and a text prompt describing the desired context. We compare OFT and COFT against established baselines such as DreamBooth and LoRA, focusing on convergence speed, finetuning stability, quantitative metrics, qualitative results, human evaluation, and ablation studies. We also discuss observed failure cases and limitations to provide a comprehensive understanding of OFT’s performance in this setting.

---

## Experiment Setup Specific to DreamBooth Comparisons

Subject-driven generation is a challenging task that requires the model to faithfully preserve the identity and appearance of a subject while allowing flexible generation in new contexts. DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} is a widely used baseline finetuning method for this task, which finetunes the entire diffusion model with a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} is another popular parameter-efficient finetuning method that updates the model weights via low-rank additive matrices.

To evaluate OFT and COFT in this context, we adopt the DreamBooth experimental framework and datasets, ensuring a fair comparison. Specifically, we use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. The finetuning loss functions and training protocols are kept consistent across all methods, including DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT. This setup isolates the effect of the finetuning strategy itself.

For OFT and COFT, we apply the orthogonal transformation only to the same layers where LoRA is applied, typically the attention layers of the UNet backbone. The orthogonal matrices are initialized as identity matrices, ensuring that the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained weights. For COFT, we additionally impose a radius constraint parameterized by $\epsilon$ to limit the deviation from the identity matrix, enhancing stability.

We conduct multiple finetuning runs with different random seeds to account for stochasticity in training and sampling. The number of finetuning iterations is varied to study convergence behavior. Evaluation is performed on both quantitative metrics and qualitative visual inspection.

---

## Convergence Curves and Stability Analysis

A critical aspect of finetuning large diffusion models is the stability and speed of convergence. Unstable finetuning can lead to model collapse or loss of subject identity, while slow convergence increases computational cost and complicates hyperparameter tuning.

Figure~\ref{fig:db_convergence} (referenced from the paper) illustrates the convergence behavior of DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT over finetuning iterations. The key observations are:

- **Fast and stable convergence of OFT and COFT:** Both OFT and COFT achieve good control over the subject identity and prompt fidelity within 400 iterations. This is significantly faster than DreamBooth and LoRA, which require more iterations to reach comparable performance.

- **Stability over long finetuning:** After 2000 iterations, DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images, indicating overfitting or instability. LoRA struggles to preserve subject identity, sometimes generating images with incorrect attributes (e.g., yellow fur instead of a yellow shirt). In contrast, OFT and COFT maintain stable and consistent generation quality even after extended finetuning.

- **Insensitivity to iteration count:** OFT and COFT are less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning iterations, reducing the need for careful tuning of training duration. This robustness is particularly valuable in practice.

These results validate the hypothesis that preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal transformations leads to more stable and efficient finetuning dynamics.

## Quantitative Results: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS

To quantitatively assess the quality of subject-driven generation, we use several established metrics:

- **DINO similarity:** Measures the cosine similarity between DINO ViT S/16 embeddings of generated and real subject images, reflecting subject identity preservation.

- **CLIP-I (Image) similarity:** Measures the cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings of generated and real subject images, another proxy for identity fidelity.

- **CLIP-T (Text) similarity:** Measures the cosine similarity between CLIP text embeddings of the prompt and CLIP image embeddings of generated images, indicating prompt adherence.

- **LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity):** Measures diversity among generated images of the same subject and prompt; higher LPIPS indicates more diverse outputs.

- **Comparable or better prompt fidelity:** OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable CLIP-T scores, showing that they maintain or improve adherence to the text prompt.

- **Maintained diversity:** LPIPS scores are similar across methods, indicating that OFT and COFT do not sacrifice diversity for fidelity.

- **Consistency across runs:** The standard deviations are lower for OFT and COFT, reflecting more consistent finetuning outcomes.

These quantitative results confirm that OFT and COFT not only converge faster and more stably but also yield higher-quality subject-driven generation.

## Ablations: Effect of Block Size, COFT Epsilon, Number of Finetuning Steps

To understand the influence of key hyperparameters and design choices in OFT and COFT, several ablation studies are conducted.

### Block Size in Orthogonal Matrix

The orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as block-diagonal with $r$ blocks to improve parameter efficiency. Ablations vary $r$ to study the trade-off between flexibility and regularization:

- **Smaller block size (larger $r$):** More blocks mean smaller blocks, leading to stronger regularization and fewer parameters. This tends to improve stability but may limit expressivity.

- **Larger block size (smaller $r$):** Fewer blocks with larger size increase flexibility but may reduce stability.

Empirically, moderate block sizes strike a good balance, yielding strong performance without excessive parameters. Very small blocks can underfit, while very large blocks may cause instability.

### COFT Radius Constraint $\epsilon$

COFT introduces a radius constraint $\epsilon$ controlling the maximum deviation of $\bm{R}$ from the identity matrix:

- **Small $\epsilon$:** Strong constraint, finetuning remains close to pretrained weights, enhancing stability but limiting adaptation.

- **Large $\epsilon$:** Looser constraint, more flexibility but potential instability.

Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft} shows that moderate $\epsilon$ values yield the best trade-off, improving stability without sacrificing performance. Very small $\epsilon$ restricts finetuning too much, while very large $\epsilon$ behaves like unconstrained OFT.

### Number of Finetuning Steps

Unlike DreamBooth and LoRA, OFT and COFT are less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations:

- **Short finetuning:** OFT and COFT achieve good results quickly.

- **Long finetuning:** OFT and COFT maintain stable performance without collapse.

This robustness reduces the need for careful tuning of training duration, simplifying practical deployment.

## Failure Cases and Limitations Observed

While OFT and COFT demonstrate strong performance, some limitations and failure modes are noted:

- **Extreme subject complexity:** For subjects with highly complex or unusual appearances, finetuning may require more iterations or parameters to fully capture identity.

- **Limited flexibility in COFT:** The radius constraint in COFT can sometimes overly restrict adaptation, leading to suboptimal prompt adherence if $\epsilon$ is set too low.

- **Block-diagonal bias:** The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ introduces inductive bias that may limit expressivity in some layers.

- **Computational overhead during training:** Although OFT introduces no inference overhead, the Cayley parameterization involves matrix inverses that can be computationally expensive for very large layers.

- **Generalization to other architectures:** While OFT is architecture-agnostic in principle, empirical validation beyond attention layers and fully connected layers is limited.

These limitations suggest directions for future research, such as exploring more flexible orthogonal parameterizations, adaptive radius constraints, and efficient implementations.

In summary, the empirical evaluation of OFT and COFT on subject-driven generation tasks demonstrates that:

- They preserve subject identity and prompt fidelity better, as confirmed by quantitative metrics and human evaluation.

- The orthogonal transformation preserves hyperspherical energy, which is crucial for maintaining pretrained semantic knowledge.

- COFT’s radius constraint further improves stability by limiting deviation from pretrained weights.

- OFT and COFT are robust to hyperparameter choices such as finetuning iterations and block size.

- Some limitations remain, motivating future work.

These results establish OFT as a powerful and principled finetuning method for controlling text-to-image diffusion models in subject-driven generation.

# Empirical Results: Controllable Generation

In this chapter, we delve into the empirical evaluation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on controllable generation tasks. Controllable generation refers to the ability of a text-to-image diffusion model to generate images not only guided by a text prompt but also conditioned on additional control signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. These tasks require the model to faithfully incorporate the control input into the generated image, ensuring that the output respects the structural or semantic constraints imposed by the control.

We focus on three representative and challenging controllable generation tasks: Canny edge to image (C2I), segmentation map to image (S2I), and landmark to face (L2F). These tasks cover a range of control modalities and datasets, providing a comprehensive testbed for evaluating the effectiveness of OFT in enhancing controllability while preserving image quality and generation fidelity.

---

## Task Descriptions and Dataset-Specific Settings

### Canny Edge to Image (C2I)

The C2I task involves generating photorealistic images conditioned on Canny edge maps extracted from natural images. The goal is to produce images that align closely with the edge structure while maintaining semantic consistency with the accompanying text prompt. This task is evaluated on the COCO dataset, a large-scale collection of diverse images with rich annotations.

- **Control signal:** Canny edge maps derived from COCO images.
- **Text prompt:** Descriptions corresponding to the images.
- **Dataset:** COCO dataset, which contains complex scenes with multiple objects and diverse backgrounds.

### Segmentation Map to Image (S2I)

In the S2I task, the model generates images conditioned on semantic segmentation maps, which label each pixel with a semantic category (e.g., sky, building, person). The challenge is to produce images that respect the semantic layout and boundaries defined by the segmentation map, while also adhering to the text prompt.

- **Control signal:** Semantic segmentation maps from the ADE20K dataset.
- **Text prompt:** Captions or descriptions associated with the images.
- **Dataset:** ADE20K, a dataset with detailed semantic annotations covering indoor and outdoor scenes.

### Landmark to Face (L2F)

The L2F task requires generating face images conditioned on facial landmark points, which specify key facial features such as eyes, nose, and mouth positions. The model must generate faces that accurately reflect the pose and expression indicated by the landmarks, while also matching the textual description.

- **Control signal:** Facial landmarks extracted from CelebA-HQ images.
- **Text prompt:** Descriptions of the face or scene.
- **Dataset:** CelebA-HQ, a high-quality face image dataset with annotated landmarks.

---

## Convergence Speed Comparisons and Plots

A critical aspect of finetuning methods is how quickly they converge to a stable and high-quality solution. We compare OFT and COFT against strong baselines including ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, focusing on the L2F task as a representative example.

- **Metric:** Mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and landmarks predicted from the generated images.
- **Setup:** All methods finetune Stable Diffusion v1.5 for 20 epochs on the CelebA-HQ dataset.

### Observations

- Both OFT and COFT achieve significantly faster convergence than the baselines. For instance, OFT reaches the performance level of LoRA at the 8th epoch within only 8 epochs, whereas LoRA requires the full 20 epochs.
- ControlNet and T2I-Adapter converge more slowly and exhibit less stable training dynamics.
- Qualitative inspection of generated images during training shows that OFT and COFT produce faces with gradually improving pose alignment, while ControlNet’s controllability emerges abruptly after several epochs, indicating less smooth optimization.

### Implications

The faster and more stable convergence of OFT and COFT reduces the computational cost and hyperparameter tuning effort, making them more practical for real-world applications. The smooth training dynamics also facilitate easier monitoring and early stopping.

---

## Control Consistency Metrics: IoU/F1, Mean IoU/Accuracy, Landmark $\ell_2$

To quantitatively evaluate how well the generated images adhere to the control signals, we introduce a control consistency metric tailored to each task. The core idea is to extract the control signal from the generated image using off-the-shelf models and compare it to the original input control.

### C2I Task

- **Metric:** Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score between the input Canny edges and edges detected from the generated image.
- **Purpose:** Measures how accurately the generated image preserves the edge structure.

### S2I Task

- **Metric:** Mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy between the input segmentation map and the segmentation predicted from the generated image.
- **Purpose:** Evaluates semantic consistency and boundary adherence.

### L2F Task

- **Metric:** Mean $\ell_2$ distance between the input facial landmarks and landmarks detected from the generated image.
- **Purpose:** Assesses pose and expression fidelity.

### Results Summary

- OFT and COFT outperform all baselines across all metrics and tasks, demonstrating stronger and more accurate control.
- Adapter-based methods like T2I-Adapter and ControlNet show slower convergence and lower final control consistency.
- LoRA performs variably, doing better on S2I but worse on C2I and L2F, indicating limited flexibility.
- OFT’s performance continues to improve with increased parameter budget, suggesting room for further gains.

## Proof: Orthogonal Transforms Preserve Pairwise Angles and Hyperspherical Energy

A central theoretical pillar of OFT is the invariance of pairwise neuron angles under orthogonal transformations. This invariance directly implies the preservation of hyperspherical energy, a key measure of the relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere.

### Setup and Definitions

Consider a weight matrix of a neural network layer, denoted by

\[
\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1, \bm{w}_2, \ldots, \bm{w}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n},
\]

where each column vector \(\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) represents a neuron. We define the normalized neurons as

\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|},
\]

which lie on the unit hypersphere \(S^{d-1}\).

The **hyperspherical energy (HE)** of the layer is defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

This quantity captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons because the Euclidean distance between two unit vectors relates to their cosine similarity:

\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2 \cos \theta_{ij},
\]

where \(\theta_{ij}\) is the angle between \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i\) and \(\hat{\bm{w}}_j\).

### Orthogonal Transformation

An orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) satisfies

\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]

where \(\bm{I}\) is the identity matrix. Orthogonal matrices represent rotations and reflections in \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

Applying \(\bm{R}\) to the neurons yields transformed neurons

\[
\bm{W}' = \bm{R} \bm{W} = [\bm{R} \bm{w}_1, \bm{R} \bm{w}_2, \ldots, \bm{R} \bm{w}_n].
\]

Since \(\bm{R}\) is an isometry, it preserves norms:

\[
\|\bm{R} \bm{w}_i\| = \|\bm{w}_i\|.
\]

Thus, the normalized transformed neurons are

\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i' = \frac{\bm{R} \bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{R} \bm{w}_i\|} = \bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_i.
\]

### Preservation of Pairwise Angles

The cosine similarity between two transformed neurons is

\[
\cos \theta_{ij}' = \langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i', \hat{\bm{w}}_j' \rangle = \langle \bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_i, \bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_j \rangle = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_j = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j = \cos \theta_{ij}.
\]

Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, it preserves inner products and hence angles between vectors.

### Preservation of Hyperspherical Energy

Since the pairwise distances \(\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|\) depend only on the angles \(\theta_{ij}\), and these angles are preserved, the hyperspherical energy remains invariant:

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}') = \text{HE}(\bm{R} \bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]

### Implications

This invariance means that by restricting finetuning updates to orthogonal transformations, OFT guarantees that the relational geometry of neurons on the hypersphere is preserved exactly. This is a stronger and more semantically meaningful constraint than simply minimizing Euclidean distance between weight matrices.

## Manifold Perspective: Optimization on the Hypersphere vs Euclidean Updates

The space of normalized neurons lies on the unit hypersphere \(S^{d-1}\), a smooth Riemannian manifold embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). OFT can be interpreted as performing finetuning constrained to the manifold of orthogonal transformations, which form the Lie group \(O(d)\).

### Hypersphere Geometry and Optimization

When neurons are normalized, their directions encode semantic information. Optimizing neuron directions corresponds to moving points on the hypersphere. Standard finetuning unconstrained in Euclidean space can cause neurons to drift off the hypersphere or distort their relative angles, potentially damaging semantic structure.

OFT restricts updates to orthogonal transformations, which correspond to isometries on the hypersphere. This means the optimization trajectory remains on the manifold of rotations/reflections, preserving the intrinsic geometry of neuron directions.

### Benefits of Manifold-Constrained Optimization

- **Preservation of semantic structure:** By staying on the manifold, OFT maintains the pairwise angular relationships critical for semantic encoding.
- **Improved optimization landscape:** The search space is reduced to a compact manifold, which can lead to smoother loss surfaces and more stable convergence.
- **Regularization effect:** The orthogonality constraint acts as a strong regularizer, preventing overfitting and catastrophic forgetting.

This manifold view explains why OFT converges faster and more stably than unconstrained finetuning methods.

## Trade-off: Flexibility (Expressivity) vs Regularity (Stability)

A fundamental challenge in finetuning large pretrained models is balancing the flexibility to adapt to new tasks with the regularity needed to preserve pretrained knowledge and ensure stable training.

### Flexibility

- **Standard finetuning** allows arbitrary updates to weights, offering maximal expressivity.
- This flexibility enables the model to fit downstream data but risks overfitting, catastrophic forgetting, and unstable training dynamics.
- Large deviations from pretrained weights can destroy the semantic structure learned during pretraining.

### Regularity

- **OFT imposes orthogonality constraints**, limiting updates to rotations/reflections.
- This constraint preserves neuron angular relationships and hyperspherical energy, maintaining pretrained semantic structure.
- The block-diagonal parameterization further restricts the degrees of freedom, acting as an inductive bias that encourages stable and meaningful updates.

### OFT as a Sweet Spot

OFT strikes a balance by:

- Allowing meaningful semantic changes via neuron direction rotation.
- Preventing destructive changes that alter pairwise neuron relationships.
- Reducing the effective search space to a structured manifold, improving optimization stability.

The constrained variant COFT further tightens this balance by limiting the deviation radius from the identity transformation, enhancing stability at the cost of some flexibility.

## Why Small Euclidean Deviation Is Insufficient to Capture Semantic Preservation

Many existing finetuning methods implicitly assume that minimizing the Euclidean distance between finetuned and pretrained weights preserves pretrained knowledge. However, this assumption is limited.

### Limitations of Euclidean Distance

- Euclidean distance measures absolute differences in weight values but ignores geometric relationships among neurons.
- Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm but have drastically different neuron directions, leading to semantic shifts.
- Conversely, weight matrices differing by an orthogonal transformation can have large Euclidean distance but identical semantic structure.

### Hyperspherical Energy as a Superior Metric

- HE captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, which better reflect semantic encoding.
- Preserving HE ensures that the relative geometry of neurons remains intact, maintaining the pretrained model's generative capabilities.
- OFT explicitly enforces HE preservation via orthogonal transformations, going beyond Euclidean proximity.

This insight motivates the design of OFT and explains its superior performance in preserving pretrained generative ability.

## The Role of Block Structure as Inductive Bias

To make OFT scalable and parameter-efficient, the orthogonal transformation \(\bm{R}\) is parameterized as a block-diagonal matrix:

\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]

where each \(\bm{R}_i \in O(d/r)\) is an orthogonal block.

### Benefits of Block-Diagonal Parameterization

- **Parameter efficiency:** Reduces the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)\).
- **Computational efficiency:** Smaller blocks allow faster matrix operations and easier enforcement of orthogonality.
- **Regularization:** The block structure restricts the transformation to act independently on neuron subgroups, preventing overfitting and encouraging structured updates.
- **Interpretability:** In convolutional layers, blocks can correspond to channels, enabling channel-wise orthogonal transformations akin to depth-wise convolutions.

### Trade-offs

- The block structure introduces bias by limiting the expressivity of \(\bm{R}\).
- Larger block sizes increase flexibility but also parameter and computational costs.
- Smaller blocks improve efficiency but may limit adaptation capacity.

Choosing block size is thus a hyperparameter balancing flexibility, efficiency, and stability.

## Connections to Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training Literature

OFT is conceptually related to orthogonal over-parameterized training methods, which have been studied in classification and other domains.

### Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training

- Introduced by Liu et al. (2021), this approach trains neural networks by applying orthogonal transformations to randomly initialized weights.
- The goal is to maintain low hyperspherical energy during training, which correlates with better generalization.
- Orthogonal transformations serve as a structured parameterization that preserves neuron geometry.

### Differences and Similarities with OFT

| Aspect                         | Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training | Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)                |
|-------------------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Objective                     | Minimize hyperspherical energy         | Preserve pretrained hyperspherical energy |
| Initialization                | Random weights                         | Pretrained weights                          |
| Task                         | Training from scratch                   | Finetuning pretrained models                |
| Constraint                   | Orthogonal transformation              | Orthogonal transformation + optional radius constraint (COFT) |
| Flexibility vs Stability     | Emphasizes generalization via low HE  | Emphasizes semantic preservation and stable adaptation |
| Parameterization             | Full orthogonal matrices               | Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices          |

OFT extends the orthogonal training paradigm to finetuning large diffusion models, focusing on preserving pretrained semantic structure rather than minimizing HE.

   - Are there other classes of transformations that preserve semantic structure but allow more flexibility?
   - How to incorporate magnitude scaling (as in re-scaled OFT) into the theoretical framework?

   - Can more advanced Riemannian optimization techniques improve convergence and stability over Cayley parameterization and projected gradient descent?

Addressing these questions will deepen our understanding of OFT and guide the development of more powerful and efficient finetuning methods.

# Limitations, Open Problems and Future Directions

In this chapter, we critically examine the limitations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as introduced for controlling text-to-image diffusion models, and we outline several open problems and promising directions for future research. While OFT offers a principled and effective approach to finetuning by preserving hyperspherical energy through orthogonal transformations, it also introduces new challenges and opportunities that merit further investigation. We organize the discussion into key thematic areas, including scalability, parameter efficiency, compositionality, potential extensions, hybrid methods, theoretical understanding, and research projects.

## Scalability Challenges: Matrix Inverse, Large Dimension, and Block Sizing

A fundamental technical component of OFT is the parameterization of orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform. Recall that the Cayley transform expresses an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). This parameterization guarantees orthogonality and is differentiable, making it suitable for gradient-based optimization. However, it requires computing a matrix inverse $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ at every optimization step, which can be computationally expensive and memory-intensive for large $d$.

In practice, the dimension $d$ corresponds to the neuron dimension in a layer, which can be very large in modern diffusion models, especially in attention layers or large fully connected layers. The cubic complexity of matrix inversion ($\mathcal{O}(d^3)$) can become a bottleneck, limiting the scalability of OFT to very large models or layers.

To mitigate this, the original work proposes a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$, where $\bm{R}$ is composed of $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}}$ along the diagonal:

\[
\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r).
\]

This reduces the size of each matrix inverse to $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$, lowering the computational cost to approximately $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^3}{r^2}\right)$. While this is a practical solution, it introduces a trade-off: smaller blocks reduce computational cost but also restrict the expressivity of the orthogonal transformation, potentially limiting finetuning flexibility.

Open problems in this area include:

- **Efficient Differentiable Matrix Inversion:** Developing faster and numerically stable algorithms or approximations for the matrix inverse in the Cayley transform that scale well with large $d$.

- **Alternative Orthogonal Parameterizations:** Exploring other parameterizations of orthogonal matrices that avoid explicit matrix inversion, such as Householder reflections, Givens rotations, or Lie algebra exponential maps, and assessing their suitability for OFT.

- **Adaptive Block Sizing:** Designing methods to adaptively choose block sizes or hierarchical block structures to balance computational efficiency and model flexibility.

- **Hardware Acceleration:** Leveraging specialized hardware or parallelization techniques to speed up matrix inversions during training.

Addressing these scalability challenges is crucial for applying OFT to very large-scale diffusion models or for finetuning multiple layers simultaneously.

## Bias Introduced by Block-Diagonal Parametrization and Its Consequences

While block-diagonal parameterization improves computational efficiency, it imposes structural constraints on the orthogonal transformation. Specifically, neurons are only rotated or reflected within their respective blocks, and no cross-block mixing occurs. This introduces an inductive bias that may limit the capacity of OFT to fully adapt the pretrained model to downstream tasks.

The consequences of this bias include:

- **Reduced Flexibility:** The inability to perform global rotations across all neurons in a layer may prevent OFT from capturing certain complex semantic modifications that require coordinated changes across neuron groups.

- **Potential Suboptimality:** The constrained search space may lead to suboptimal finetuning solutions compared to full orthogonal transformations.

- **Dependence on Block Design:** The choice of block size and whether blocks are shared or independent can significantly affect performance, requiring careful tuning.

Understanding and quantifying the impact of this bias is an open research question. Potential directions include:

- **Empirical Studies:** Systematic experiments varying block sizes and sharing schemes to characterize the trade-offs between parameter efficiency and finetuning performance.

- **Theoretical Analysis:** Investigating how block-diagonal constraints affect the representational capacity of orthogonal transformations and their ability to preserve hyperspherical energy while enabling semantic adaptation.

- **Relaxed Constraints:** Exploring parameterizations that allow limited cross-block interactions or low-rank perturbations on top of block-diagonal orthogonal matrices to increase flexibility without sacrificing efficiency.

- **Learned Block Structures:** Instead of fixed blocks, learning the block partitioning or using soft block assignments to dynamically balance bias and flexibility.

Addressing these issues will help improve the practical effectiveness of OFT and guide principled design choices.

## Compositionality: Multiplying Orthogonal Matrices for Multi-Tasking

One intriguing property of orthogonal matrices is that their product is also orthogonal. This algebraic closure suggests a natural compositionality in OFT: if we have multiple finetuning tasks, each represented by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i$, then composing these transformations by multiplication

\[
\bm{R}_{\text{composed}} = \bm{R}_1 \bm{R}_2 \cdots \bm{R}_k
\]

yields another orthogonal matrix that could, in principle, encode the combined effect of all tasks.

This compositionality opens exciting possibilities:

- **Multi-Task Finetuning:** Instead of training a separate finetuned model for each task, one could compose orthogonal transformations corresponding to different tasks to obtain a single model capable of handling multiple downstream objectives.

- **Modular Adaptation:** Orthogonal matrices could serve as modular adapters that can be combined or swapped to control different aspects of generation.

- **Continual Learning:** Sequentially applying orthogonal transformations may enable continual finetuning without catastrophic forgetting, as each transformation preserves hyperspherical energy.

However, several open questions arise:

- **Preservation of Knowledge:** Does the product of orthogonal matrices trained independently on different tasks preserve the semantic knowledge of all tasks simultaneously? Or do interactions between transformations cause interference?

- **Optimization and Stability:** How to effectively optimize and regularize compositions of orthogonal transformations to avoid degradation?

- **Parameter Efficiency:** How does the parameter count scale with multiple composed transformations, and can parameter sharing or compression be applied?

- **Empirical Validation:** Systematic experiments are needed to validate the compositionality hypothesis in practice, including multi-task generation and continual learning scenarios.

Exploring the compositionality of OFT transformations is a promising research direction with potential impact on scalable and flexible model adaptation.

## Potential Extensions: Approximate Orthogonal Parameterizations, Sparse and Structured Transforms

Beyond the Cayley transform and block-diagonal parameterization, there is a rich space of possible extensions to the orthogonal parameterization in OFT that could improve scalability, flexibility, or interpretability.

Some promising avenues include:

- **Approximate Orthogonality:** Instead of enforcing strict orthogonality, allowing approximate orthogonal transformations with soft constraints or penalties could reduce computational cost and increase flexibility.

- **Sparse Orthogonal Matrices:** Designing sparse orthogonal matrices that maintain angle preservation but with fewer parameters and faster computation.

- **Structured Orthogonal Transforms:** Leveraging structured transforms such as fast Fourier transform (FFT)-like matrices, Hadamard transforms, or butterfly matrices to parameterize orthogonal transformations efficiently.

- **Low-Rank Plus Orthogonal Decomposition:** Combining low-rank additive updates (as in LoRA) with orthogonal multiplicative transformations to capture complementary adaptation modes.

- **Learned Manifold Constraints:** Using manifold optimization techniques to learn orthogonal transformations with additional geometric or task-specific constraints.

- **Stochastic Orthogonal Updates:** Introducing stochasticity or noise in orthogonal transformations to improve generalization or exploration during finetuning.

Each of these extensions requires careful theoretical and empirical study to assess their impact on hyperspherical energy preservation, finetuning stability, and downstream task performance.

## Combining OFT with Other Adaptation Techniques (e.g., LoRA Hybrids)

OFT represents a fundamentally different approach to finetuning compared to additive low-rank methods like LoRA. While LoRA updates weights additively with low-rank matrices, OFT applies multiplicative orthogonal transformations. These complementary mechanisms suggest that hybrid methods could leverage the strengths of both.

Potential hybrid approaches include:

- **Sequential Application:** Applying OFT followed by LoRA (or vice versa) to capture both angular transformations and additive fine-grained adjustments.

- **Joint Optimization:** Simultaneously optimizing orthogonal transformations and low-rank additive updates within a unified framework.

- **Task-Specific Allocation:** Using OFT for tasks requiring strong semantic preservation and LoRA for tasks needing more flexible adaptation.

- **Parameter Sharing:** Sharing parameters or constraints between OFT and LoRA components to improve efficiency.

Exploring such hybrids could yield more powerful and flexible finetuning methods that balance stability, expressivity, and parameter efficiency.

## Better Theoretical Understanding of Hyperspherical Energy and Downstream Generalization

While hyperspherical energy (HE) is a central concept motivating OFT, many theoretical aspects remain open:

- **HE and Semantic Preservation:** Formalizing the relationship between HE preservation and the preservation of semantic generation ability in diffusion models.

- **HE and Generalization:** Understanding how HE relates to generalization performance on downstream tasks, including robustness and diversity.

- **HE Dynamics During Finetuning:** Characterizing how HE evolves during standard finetuning and how OFT constrains this evolution.

- **Alternative Metrics:** Investigating other geometric or spectral metrics that may better capture semantic preservation or finetuning quality.

- **Manifold Geometry:** Developing a rigorous manifold optimization framework for OFT, including convergence guarantees and landscape analysis.

Advancing the theoretical foundations will deepen our understanding of why OFT works and guide the design of improved finetuning strategies.

# Proofs and Derivations Omitted from the Main Chapters

## Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Pairwise Angles and Hyperspherical Energy

Recall that the hyperspherical energy (HE) of a weight matrix $\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1, \bm{w}_2, \ldots, \bm{w}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\]

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}$ is the normalized neuron vector on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.

### Theorem

Let $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be an orthogonal matrix, i.e., $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. Then for any weight matrix $\bm{W}$,

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R} \bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]

### Proof

1. **Orthogonal matrices preserve norms:**

For any vector $\bm{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d$,

\[
\|\bm{R} \bm{v}\| = \sqrt{(\bm{R} \bm{v})^\top (\bm{R} \bm{v})} = \sqrt{\bm{v}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{v}} = \|\bm{v}\|.
\]

2. **Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products:**

\[
(\bm{R} \bm{u})^\top (\bm{R} \bm{v}) = \bm{u}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{v} = \bm{u}^\top \bm{v}.
\]

3. **Preservation of cosine similarity:**

The cosine similarity between two normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ is

\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j.
\]

Since $\bm{R}$ preserves inner products and norms,

\[
(\bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_i)^\top (\bm{R} \hat{\bm{w}}_j) = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j = \cos \theta_{ij}.
\]

4. **Preservation of pairwise distances on the unit sphere:**

The Euclidean distance between two normalized vectors is related to the angle by

\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2 \cos \theta_{ij}.
\]

Since $\cos \theta_{ij}$ is preserved, so is $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$.

5. **Therefore,**

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R} \bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\widehat{\bm{R} \bm{w}}_i - \widehat{\bm{R} \bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} = \text{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]

This completes the proof.

## Neumann Series Approximation of the Cayley Transform

The Cayley transform parameterizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
\]

where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$).

Assuming $\|\bm{Q}\| < 1$ (operator norm), the inverse can be expanded as a Neumann series:

\[
(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots
\]

Therefore,

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]

This approximation justifies the constraint on $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\|$ being approximately equivalent to a constraint on $\|\bm{Q}\|$ in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT).

# Algorithm Pseudocode for OFT, COFT, and Re-scaled OFT

Below we provide high-level pseudocode for the three variants of Orthogonal Finetuning.

---

## Algorithm 1: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

```python
# Inputs:
#   pretrained_weights: dict of pretrained weight matrices {layer_name: W0}
#   training_data: dataset for finetuning
#   num_epochs: number of finetuning epochs
#   learning_rate: optimizer learning rate
#   block_size: size of blocks for block-diagonal orthogonal matrices

# Initialize orthogonal matrices R for each finetuned layer as identity
for layer in finetuned_layers:
    R[layer] = IdentityMatrix(d)  # d = input dimension of layer
    # Parameterize R[layer] via Cayley transform with skew-symmetric Q[layer] initialized to zero

optimizer = Adam(params=Q_parameters, lr=learning_rate)

for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for batch in training_data:
        # Forward pass with OFT:
        # For each finetuned layer:
        #   W = R[layer] @ pretrained_weights[layer]
        #   Use W in forward computation
        
        loss = compute_loss(batch, model_with_OFT_weights)
        
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        
        # Gradient step on Q parameters
        optimizer.step()
        
        # Re-orthogonalize R via Cayley transform from updated Q
        for layer in finetuned_layers:
            R[layer] = cayley_transform(Q[layer])
```

---

## Algorithm 2: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

```python
# Same as OFT, with additional constraint on Q norm

epsilon_prime = hyperparameter  # radius constraint on Q

for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for batch in training_data:
        # Forward pass same as OFT
        
        loss = compute_loss(batch, model_with_COFT_weights)
        
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # Project Q to ball of radius epsilon_prime
        for layer in finetuned_layers:
            if norm(Q[layer]) > epsilon_prime:
                Q[layer] = Q[layer] * (epsilon_prime / norm(Q[layer]))
        
        # Update R via Cayley transform
        for layer in finetuned_layers:
            R[layer] = cayley_transform(Q[layer])
```

---

## Algorithm 3: Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning

```python
# Same as OFT, with additional learnable diagonal scaling matrix D per layer

for layer in finetuned_layers:
    D[layer] = IdentityDiagonalMatrix(n)  # n = number of neurons in layer
    # D[layer] diagonal elements initialized to 1 and constrained to be positive

optimizer = Adam(params=[Q_parameters, D_parameters], lr=learning_rate)

for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for batch in training_data:
        # Forward pass:
        # W = R[layer] @ pretrained_weights[layer] @ D[layer]
        
        loss = compute_loss(batch, model_with_rescaled_OFT_weights)
        
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        
        # Project Q and enforce positivity on D diagonal elements
        for layer in finetuned_layers:
            if norm(Q[layer]) > epsilon_prime:
                Q[layer] = Q[layer] * (epsilon_prime / norm(Q[layer]))
            D[layer] = clamp(D[layer], min=small_positive_value)
        
        # Update R via Cayley transform
        for layer in finetuned_layers:
            R[layer] = cayley_transform(Q[layer])
```

# Detailed Descriptions of Datasets and Preprocessing Steps

## Subject-Driven Generation Dataset

- **Source:** Custom subject images collected for DreamBooth-style finetuning.
- **Number of Images:** Typically 3-5 images per subject.
- **Preprocessing:** Images resized to $512 \times 512$ pixels, normalized to $[0,1]$.
- **Augmentation:** Random horizontal flips and slight color jittering to improve generalization.

## Controllable Generation Datasets

1. **COCO Dataset (Canny Edge to Image - C2I)**
   - Images resized to $512 \times 512$.
   - Canny edge maps extracted using standard OpenCV implementation with fixed thresholds.
   - Paired edge-image data used for training.

2. **ADE20K Dataset (Segmentation Map to Image - S2I)**
   - Images resized to $512 \times 512$.
   - Semantic segmentation maps provided by ADE20K annotations.
   - Segmentation maps encoded as one-hot or color-coded masks.

3. **CelebA-HQ Dataset (Landmark to Face - L2F)**
   - Images resized to $512 \times 512$.
   - Facial landmarks extracted using dlib or similar landmark detectors.
   - Landmarks encoded as heatmaps or coordinate maps as control signals.

# Practical Tips for Numerical Stability and Implementation

- **Initialization:**
  - Initialize skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ to zero to start from identity orthogonal matrix.
  - Initialize diagonal scaling matrices $\bm{D}$ to ones for re-scaled OFT.

- **Orthogonality Enforcement:**
  - Use Cayley transform for differentiable orthogonal parameterization.
  - Regularly re-orthogonalize if numerical drift occurs.

- **Projection in COFT:**
  - Use projected gradient descent to enforce $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$.
  - Choose $\epsilon'$ small enough to ensure stability but large enough for flexibility.

- **Block-Diagonal Parameterization:**
  - Choose block size $r$ to balance parameter efficiency and expressivity.
  - Sharing blocks across layers or channels can reduce parameters further.

- **Optimization:**
  - Use Adam optimizer with learning rate warmup and decay.
  - Monitor training loss and control consistency metrics to detect collapse early.

- **Memory and Speed:**
  - Block-diagonal structure reduces memory footprint.
  - Matrix inverses in Cayley transform can be accelerated with GPU-optimized linear algebra libraries.
