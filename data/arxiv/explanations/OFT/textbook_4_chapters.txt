\title{A Textbook about the Paper: OFT (4 chapters)}

Foundations: Diffusion Models, Fine-tuning Challenges, and Angular Semantics

# Diffusion model basics: denoising diffusion, score-based modeling, latent vs. pixel-space training

Diffusion models are a class of generative models that synthesize data by reversing a gradual noising process. The central idea is to define a forward process that progressively corrupts data (for images, by adding Gaussian noise across a sequence of timesteps) and then to train a neural network to approximate the reverse process that denoises step-by-step. Practically, the denoising network is trained to predict either the score (gradient of the log-density) or the original clean signal from noisy inputs; running the learned reverse dynamics yields samples from the learned distribution. The literature frames this family of approaches under names such as denoising diffusion probabilistic models and score-based modeling, but the shared intuition is the same: learn to undo noise gradually, and sampling is accomplished by iteratively applying that learned denoiser or score estimator.

Text-to-image diffusion systems use this denoising machinery conditioned on language (text) so that the reverse sampling process is guided by the prompt. Architectures differ in whether the diffusion model operates in pixel space or a lower-dimensional latent space. Pixel-space training (used by models like GLIDE and Imagen in the referenced work) trains the diffusion model to produce images directly in pixel coordinates; this keeps the diffusion target high-fidelity but carries a higher computational cost. Latent-space training (used by Stable Diffusion in the paper) first maps images into a learned latent code (for example via a VQ-GAN or an autoencoder) and then trains the diffusion model to operate in that latent space. Latent-space diffusion significantly reduces computation and storage because the diffusion process handles smaller-dimensional representations, while a separate decoder (the VQ-GAN or similar) maps latents back to pixels at inference.

# Text-to-image systems overview: Stable Diffusion, GLIDE, Imagen

The paper situates its contributions in the context of contemporary text-to-image systems. GLIDE and Imagen are examples of pixel-space diffusion approaches: they model images directly in pixel coordinates and condition the denoiser on text embeddings produced by some text encoder. Stable Diffusion, by contrast, is a latent-space text-to-image model: it uses a learned latent representation (a visual codebook or encoder/decoder) so the diffusion model operates on compact latents rather than full-resolution pixels. Each design choice trades off computational cost, fidelity, and the complexity of conditioning on text; the important point for downstream adaptation is that all these models are large pretrained diffusion models with impressive generative capability, and they share a need to be adapted (finetuned) for tasks requiring more precise control.

# Downstream tasks targeted by the paper: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

Two downstream tasks drive the paper's investigation into finetuning strategies.

Subject-driven generation asks: given only a few images of a particular subject (for example, a specific person or object), how can a pretrained text-to-image model be adapted so that, when provided a textual prompt that includes the subject, the model generates images of that same subject in new contexts? This is the setting of DreamBooth in the referenced literature: the generator must learn to preserve the identity-specific visual details while enabling new arrangements, poses, or environments specified by text.

Controllable generation asks: given an additional control signal beyond text — for example, Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, landmark coordinates, or other structured guidance — how can a pretrained model be adapted so that generated images faithfully follow that control input while still respecting text prompts and maintaining photorealism? Recent methods aim to make a pretrained diffusion model accept such auxiliary signals to achieve spatial or structural control over outputs.

Both tasks share the same core technical need: adapt a powerful pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to new conditioning or subject-specific distributions while avoiding degradation of the model’s pretrained generative abilities (photorealism, diversity, and faithful text conditioning).

# Fine-tuning desiderata: training efficiency, parameter efficiency, and generalizability preservation

When adapting large pretrained diffusion models for downstream control, the paper emphasizes three desiderata:

- Training efficiency: the finetuning process should require relatively few iterations and modest amounts of data to reach good performance. This is particularly important for subject-driven generation, where only a few subject images are available, and for practical deployment where retraining resources are limited.

- Parameter efficiency: finetuning should introduce and adjust as few parameters as necessary. Large models make full-weight finetuning expensive in storage, compute, and risk of overfitting; so adapter-style updates or other compact parameterizations are appealing.

- Generalizability preservation: crucially, the finetuned model should retain the pretrained model’s broad generative capabilities — high-fidelity realism, diverse sampling, and accurate text conditioning. A finetuned model that sacrifices these properties for strict adherence to the new task is not useful; a good finetuning method finds a balance between adapting to the downstream requirement and preserving the pretrained inductive structure.

The paper treats these desiderata as design goals and evaluates finetuning strategies according to them.

# Limitations of standard fine-tuning and adapter-style methods (e.g., LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter)

Conventional finetuning typically proceeds by applying gradient-based updates to weight matrices (or subsets of weights) using small learning rates to avoid large deviations from the pretrained parameters. This approach implicitly enforces a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights, which can provide some safety against catastrophic changes but lacks a principled structural guarantee about what aspects of the pretrained model are preserved.

Adapter-style methods, such as LoRA, take a different route: instead of changing the main weights directly, they add a small reparameterized module (for example, a low-rank additive update) and only train those added parameters. LoRA assumes that the fine-grained adjustments needed for a downstream task lie in a low-rank subspace, and it uses that structure to remain parameter-efficient.

ControlNet and T2I-Adapter belong to the family of approaches that adapt pretrained diffusion models to accept new control signals. ControlNet inserts additional trainable modules to condition on control inputs; T2I-Adapter plays a similar role by adding small auxiliary networks that guide generation. These methods typically add parameters and training complexity, and some introduce inference-time overhead because the extra modules remain active at sampling time.

The paper points out limitations common to these approaches. Small Euclidean deviations (standard finetuning) do not guarantee preservation of the pretrained model’s semantic behavior. LoRA’s low-rank additive updates and adapter modules are pragmatic and efficient but lack a structural guarantee that the relational geometry among neurons (which may encode semantic knowledge) is preserved. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter can succeed but may require more parameters, longer training, and introduce runtime cost relative to methods that avoid extra inference-time modules.

# Why Euclidean closeness to a pretrained model is insufficient for semantic preservation

A common implicit assumption behind small learning rates or additive regularizers is that a smaller Euclidean norm difference between finetuned weights and pretrained weights implies better preservation of pretrained behavior. The paper argues this assumption is incomplete.

Euclidean closeness measures pointwise proximity in parameter space, but neural networks represent information in structured ways: relationships between neurons (for example, the pairwise angles or relative orientations of weight vectors) capture semantic structure that a scalar Euclidean norm does not fully describe. Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm yet have substantially different angular relationships among their columns (neurons), and those angular changes can produce large semantic differences in the network's function.

Because the key behaviors of pretrained diffusion models — such as which directions in feature space correspond to which semantic attributes — can be encoded in relational, angular patterns among neurons, preserving Euclidean distance alone is insufficient to guarantee preservation of these semantic generative properties.

# Angular semantics: why neuron directions matter more than magnitudes (empirical and intuitive evidence)

The paper motivates the primacy of neuron directions (angles) with empirical and conceptual observations. Prior empirical work cited in the paper has found that angular similarity (e.g., cosine similarity) captures semantic relationships effectively: when neurons or features are normalized onto a unit hypersphere, the directional component often contains most of the task-relevant information. SphereNet and related works show that constraining neurons to lie on a unit hypersphere retains model capacity and can even improve generalization, which suggests that the direction of weight vectors (rather than their scale) is a primary carrier of information.

The paper supplements this with a toy experiment using a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During testing, the authors compared three ways to form feature maps at convolutional layers: (a) using the original inner-product activations used in training, (b) using only the magnitude information of weights, and (c) using only the angular (directional) information via cosine activations. The result was striking: angular information was able to almost perfectly reconstruct the input images, while magnitude information alone carried little useful semantic content. Importantly, the network was trained without explicit cosine activations; yet at test time, the angular component retained semantic structure. This experiment supports the interpretation that changing neuron directions is the lever by which semantic modification occurs, whereas magnitudes often contribute less to the represented semantics.

# Fourier phase analogy: phase retains semantics, magnitude contributes less

To build intuition, the paper draws an analogy to the Fourier decomposition of images. In a 2D Fourier transform, an image decomposes into magnitude (amplitude) and phase spectra. The phase spectrum encodes angular relationships between spatial patterns and largely preserves the perceptual structure and semantics of the image. In fact, reconstructing an image from its phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum often produces an image whose semantics and recognizable structure are preserved. This observation suggests a useful analogy for neural representations: angles (directional information) among neurons are akin to the phase spectrum that encodes structural and semantic relationships, while magnitudes play a secondary or modulatory role.

From this perspective, to effect controlled semantic changes (for subject-driven or controllable generation), one should target directional changes of neurons rather than arbitrary changes in magnitude. However, unconstrained directional changes risk destroying pretrained structure; therefore, the paper seeks transformations that change directions in a constrained and structured way.

# Hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy: intuition and definition

To formalize angular relationships among neurons, the paper uses the concepts of hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy. Consider a layer represented by a weight matrix \(\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), where each column \(\bm{w}_i\) is a neuron (weight vector). Normalize the neuron vectors to the unit sphere by defining \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|\). Hyperspherical similarity refers to measures of pairwise angular closeness between these normalized neurons — cosine similarity is a typical choice. Hyperspherical energy (HE) aggregates these pairwise similarities into a single scalar that reflects the uniformity or clustering of neurons on the unit hypersphere.

Concretely, the paper uses the following definition of hyperspherical energy for a layer \(\bm{W}\):

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

This expression sums the reciprocals of Euclidean distances between normalized neurons, so configurations where neurons are close on the sphere (small angular separation) contribute large terms to HE. HE therefore captures the aggregate pairwise closeness of neuron directions: lower HE corresponds (in a certain sense) to a more uniformly spread set of directions, while higher HE indicates clusters or concentrated angular structure. Importantly, HE depends only on normalized directions, not on magnitudes.

# Why preserving pairwise angles among neurons can preserve pretrained knowledge

Because hyperspherical energy and other angular statistics capture the relational geometry of neurons — the set of pairwise angles that collectively encode directions associated with semantic features — preserving those relations during finetuning is a plausible route to maintaining pretrained generative capabilities. The paper hypothesizes that a finetuned model whose HE closely matches the pretrained model's HE will retain the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model: text conditioning, photorealism, and the variety of learned concepts should be preserved because the angular relationships that encode those concepts are unchanged.

One way to attempt this preservation would be to add a regularization term that penalizes deviations in HE between \(\bm{W}\) and \(\bm{W}^0\). However, directly minimizing an HE difference is neither straightforward nor guaranteed to be effective. Instead, the paper leverages an important invariance: applying the same orthogonal transformation \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) to all neurons in a layer — replacing \(\bm{W}^0\) with \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) — preserves pairwise angles among the column vectors. Normalized vectors transform as \(\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i\) when \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, and because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and Euclidean distances, all pairwise angular relationships (and hence HE) remain exactly unchanged.

This invariance gives a practical path forward: instead of attempting to directly keep an unwieldy scalar HE difference small via regularization, one can parameterize finetuning updates as layer-shared orthogonal transformations that leave HE invariant by construction. Such orthogonal updates change directions in a globally consistent way — effectively rotating the coordinate system of the neurons — which provides the flexibility needed to encode task-specific alterations (for instance, encoding a small subject-specific canonical change or incorporating control inputs) while maintaining the essential angular geometry that underlies pretrained semantics.

In summary, the foundational insight is that neuron directions (angles) carry much of the semantic information, hyperspherical metrics formalize these relationships, and orthogonal transformations give a constructive, principled means to adapt neurons while preserving pairwise angular structure and, by hypothesis, the pretrained model’s generative competence. This motivates designing finetuning strategies that operate multiplicatively through orthogonal transforms rather than purely additive Euclidean perturbations, with the aim of better balancing flexibility, parameter efficiency, and preservation of learned generalization.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Theory, Algorithms, and Variants

# Core idea: preserve pairwise neuron angles by applying a shared orthogonal transform per layer

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is built on a single geometric intuition: the directions of neurons (their angles on the unit hypersphere) encode the crucial semantic structure learned during pretraining, and a finetuning procedure that preserves pairwise angles among neurons will therefore be less likely to destroy the pretrained model's generative capabilities. Concretely, for a weight matrix representing a layer, consider each column (or channel) as a neuron vector and normalize it to the unit hypersphere. The pairwise relationships between these normalized neuron vectors — their cosine similarities or angular separations — form a compact description of the layer's internal geometry. If finetuning changes those angles arbitrarily it can break the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained network.

OFT enforces a structured form of finetuning: instead of independently changing each neuron, OFT multiplies the whole pretrained neuron set in a layer by a shared orthogonal matrix. This layer-shared orthogonal transformation rotates (or reflects) the neuron set as a rigid body on the hypersphere. Because orthogonal maps preserve inner products and norms, this single multiplicative update preserves all pairwise angles among neurons. This constraint reduces the degrees of freedom compared to unconstrained finetuning, while still allowing rich directional changes that are often the most semantically relevant.

# Mathematical formulation for linear layers: W = R W^0 with R orthogonal

Let a pretrained fully connected layer be represented by the matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\{\bm{w}_i^0\}_{i=1}^n$ are neuron vectors. A standard forward pass for input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}.
\]
Orthogonal Finetuning parameterizes the finetuned weight matrix as
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix satisfying
\[
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}.
\]
With this parameterization, the layer output becomes
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix, the finetuned model begins exactly at the pretrained weights. During finetuning we optimize the downstream objective (for example, a reconstruction or conditional diffusion loss), but the only trainable parameters for that layer are the (structured) parameters that define $\bm{R}$.

This multiplicative update contrasts with additive updates used by many adapter methods. Algebraically,
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0,
\]
so OFT can be viewed as an implicit additive update $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ where the update matrix is constrained to arise from an orthogonal transform.

# Hyperspherical energy invariance under orthogonal transformations (why it holds and why it matters)

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar measure of how uniformly neuron vectors are distributed on the unit hypersphere. For normalized column vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, the hyperspherical energy of a layer $\bm{W}$ is defined in the paper as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This objective depends only on pairwise distances (or equivalently pairwise inner products / angles) among normalized neurons. Because an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms and inner products,
\[
\langle \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i,\,\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle = \langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\,\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle,
\]
and therefore
\[
\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|.
\]
From this identity it immediately follows that
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W})
\]
for any orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Thus orthogonal transforms are exact symmetries of hyperspherical energy.

Why this invariance matters: the paper argues that hyperspherical relationships encode semantic structure used by the pretrained generator. A finetuning method that preserves HE is thereby less likely to distort that semantic structure. Instead of trying to directly regularize HE (which would be a soft constraint and may be hard to optimize to zero), OFT enforces a structural transformation that provably leaves HE unchanged. The result is a finetuning family with built-in guarantees about pairwise neuron relations, which empirically leads to better preservation of pretrained generative abilities and more stable finetuning.

# Optimization objective and identity initialization to start from pretrained weights

In principle OFT seeks a finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ satisfying
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|.
\]
Because HE is invariant under a layer-shared orthogonal transform, any $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$ achieves zero hyperspherical-energy difference. In practice, the training objective is the downstream loss (e.g., the diffusion model finetuning loss), and we restrict the parameterization of $\bm{W}$ to the family $\{\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 : \bm{R}\in O(d)\}$ so that HE is preserved by construction.

Identity initialization is crucial for stable finetuning: initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so that at the start of optimization the finetuned weights coincide exactly with the pretrained ones. From that starting point we update the parameters that define $\bm{R}$ to reduce the downstream loss while retaining orthogonality. In implementation this is done by parametrizing $\bm{R}$ in a differentiable way (see next section), initializing the parameterization to represent the identity, and optimizing via standard gradient-based methods applied to the downstream objective while preserving the orthogonality constraint.

# Efficient orthogonal parameterization via the Cayley transform with a skew-symmetric matrix Q

A naively represented orthogonal matrix has many parameters and maintaining orthogonality during training is nontrivial. OFT adopts an efficient and differentiable parameterization based on the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix (i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$). The Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix via
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and (for finite $\bm{Q}$ with $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ invertible) has determinant $+1$, placing it in the special orthogonal group. The Cayley transform is differentiable with respect to the entries of $\bm{Q}$, so we can backpropagate through the matrix inverse and train the entries of $\bm{Q}$.

The Cayley parameterization has two practical characteristics to note. First, the restriction to determinant $+1$ (no reflections with $\det=-1$) is not observed to harm finetuning performance in the experiments reported. Second, computing the inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ can be expensive for large $d$; the next section explains how a block structure mitigates this cost.

To start from identity, initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which gives $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Gradients with respect to $\bm{Q}$ then move $\bm{R}$ away from identity in a manner that remains orthogonal by construction.

# Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices for parameter efficiency; optional block sharing across channels

Directly parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom and becomes expensive for large hidden dimensions. OFT reduces parameter cost by restricting $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal:
\[
\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r\big),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)}$ is orthogonal. With $r$ blocks, the total number of orthogonal parameters reduces to
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r \frac{(d/r)\big(d/r-1\big)}{2} = \frac{d\big(d/r-1\big)}{2},
\]
i.e., a factor of approximately $r$ savings over the full matrix. In addition, the Cayley transform and inverse only need to be applied to each small block, greatly reducing computational cost.

An additional economy comes from block sharing: one can set $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all blocks, so that the same orthogonal transform is used repeatedly across multiple subspaces. With complete sharing, parameter cost reduces further (in the limit described by the paper it becomes $O(d^2/r^2)$ under particular choices). The choice of $r$ trades capacity for parameter efficiency; small $r$ gives richer transforms, large $r$ yields stronger regularization and lower cost.

Block-diagonal structure also has an interpretation in convolutional and channelized layers. If blocks are matched to channel groups, each block acts like a channel-local orthogonal transform; if the same block is shared across channels, the transform becomes channel-global. This connection motivates how OFT can be applied to convolutional layers with a depthwise-style interpretation.

# Parameter complexity and comparison with LoRA (multiplicative orthogonal vs additive low-rank updates)

LoRA uses an additive low-rank decomposition to limit trainable parameters: it assumes the weight update $\Delta \bm{W} := \bm{W}-\bm{W}^0$ factors as $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ with small inner rank $r'$, producing a parameter count of $r'(d+n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight matrix. OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal update $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with structured parameterization for $\bm{R}$; with block-diagonalization and sharing the parameter cost can be much smaller.

To make the comparison concrete, consider $d=n=128$ and a low-rank LoRA with $r'=8$. LoRA learns $r'(d+n)=8(128+128)=2048$ parameters. If OFT uses $r=8$ blocks (no block-sharing), the number of parameters for the orthogonal transform is $128(128/8-1)/2=960$. Thus with similar design choices OFT can be more parameter-efficient. The two methods impose different structural priors: LoRA constrains the update to be low-rank and additive, while OFT constrains the update to be an orthogonally generated multiplicative perturbation, which preserves hyperspherical geometry. These are complementary biases — low-rank additive vs structured multiplicative — and each has different implications for flexibility and preservation of pretrained structure.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): restricting deviation from identity; translating the epsilon constraint to Q via Neumann approximation; projected gradient enforcement

While OFT already preserves hyperspherical energy, the paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) to further control deviation from the pretrained model. COFT augments the orthogonality constraint with an explicit radius constraint that bounds how far the orthogonal matrix can move from identity:
\[
\text{COFT:}\quad
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\qquad
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon,
\]
where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a chosen matrix norm and $\epsilon$ is a user-specified radius that controls finetuning flexibility. COFT thereby enforces both (i) exact hyperspherical-energy preservation and (ii) that the orthogonal change is small.

Directly constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ is awkward when $\bm{R}$ is Cayley-parameterized. The paper uses a local linearization via the Neumann series to translate this constraint to the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$. Observe that
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
so for sufficiently small $\bm{Q}$ we have
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\|.
\]
Therefore the COFT constraint can be approximately enforced by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ for an appropriately chosen $\epsilon'$. In practice, the paper enforces this constraint on $\bm{Q}$ via projected gradient descent: after a gradient step on $\bm{Q}$, project $\bm{Q}$ back onto the ball $\{\bm{Q} : \|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'\}$.

COFT thus combines two explicit constraints: hyperspherical-energy invariance (via orthogonality) and bounded deviation from identity. Empirically, COFT exhibits improved finetuning stability and makes hyperparameter choices (such as number of iterations) less sensitive, because the $\epsilon$ radius directly controls how far the model may drift from the pretrained weights.

# Re-scaled OFT: adding a learnable diagonal magnitude matrix without breaking energy preservation

Hyperspherical energy depends only on neuron directions, not their magnitudes. Exploiting this, the paper proposes a simple extension called re-scaled OFT that adds a learnable per-neuron magnitude scaling without altering HE invariance. The re-scaled forward pass is
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries. The diagonal $\bm{D}$ modulates neuron magnitudes; because HE is computed from normalized neuron directions, adjusting $\bm{D}$ does not change HE. This extra multiplicative degree of freedom increases finetuning flexibility with negligible parameter cost (only $n$ extra parameters per layer). The paper notes an erratum in the printed forward pass in one version but emphasizes the implementation uses the correct $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ ordering. Empirically, re-scaled OFT improves flexibility and often performs better than the base OFT; however, the paper also shows that base OFT (without rescaling) already provides significant benefits.

# Applying OFT to Transformers (attention weights) and CNNs (depthwise-style interpretation)

OFT is architecture-agnostic in principle, because the core operation — applying a layer-shared orthogonal transform to groups of neurons — can be applied to any linear map in a network. In practice, for fair comparison to LoRA-style adapters, the experiments apply OFT primarily to attention weight matrices in Transformer-based architectures: the same multiplicative orthogonal parameterization replaces the pretrained attention projection weights during finetuning.

For convolutional layers, OFT's block-diagonal structure admits an intuitive channelwise interpretation. If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block maps one input channel's neuron subspace independently — analogous to a per-channel rotation that resembles depthwise convolutions. If all blocks are shared across channels, OFT applies the same orthogonal transform to every channel. Thus OFT can be applied to convolution kernels either by grouping channels into blocks or by using channelwise blocks, giving a flexible set of inductive biases that align with standard convolution designs.

# Training dynamics: finetuning on a smooth hypersphere manifold and implications for convergence

By constraining updates to orthogonal transforms, OFT effectively restricts optimization to a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group or a blockwise product of orthogonal groups). Optimizing parameters that live on this manifold yields a smoother geometry than unconstrained Euclidean updates, which can improve optimization stability. The paper reports empirical observations consistent with this reasoning: OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than unconstrained finetuning, LoRA, and other adapters in both subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks. Intuitively, OFT focuses the optimization on angular degrees of freedom, which the paper's toy experiments suggest are the most effective for changing semantic content. The combined effect — meaningful parameter directions plus a smooth constrained manifold — produces faster and more predictable convergence.

# No inference overhead: folding learned R into weights for deployment

A practical advantage of OFT is that the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weight matrix for inference. After finetuning, compute
\[
\bm{W}_{\text{deploy}} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
and replace the original layer weights with $\bm{W}_{\text{deploy}}$. Because $\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0$ produces the same forward computation as the run-time multiplication-by-$\bm{R}$ followed by $\bm{W}^0$, there is no additional computational or memory overhead at inference time. This is in contrast to some adapter approaches that introduce extra modules or concatenated structures at test time.

# Practical summary and implementation notes

A minimal recipe to implement OFT for a layer:

1. Choose a blockization parameter $r$ (often tuned to trade off parameter cost and flexibility).
2. Parameterize each orthogonal block via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}_i$ and the Cayley transform $\bm{R}_i=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}_i)(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}$. Initialize all $\bm{Q}_i=\bm{0}$ so each $\bm{R}_i=\bm{I}$.
3. Replace the layer's forward pass by $(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ where $\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$.
4. Optionally add a diagonal rescaling matrix $\bm{D}$ and learn it with positive diagonal entries.
5. For COFT, enforce a norm constraint on $\bm{Q}$ each update (project onto $\{\bm{Q}:\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\}$) to approximate a bound $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$.
6. Optimize the downstream finetuning loss with respect to the parameters that define $\bm{Q}$ (and $\bm{D}$ if used). After training, fold $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ for deployment.

These steps implement a finetuning routine that preserves hyperspherical energy provably, keeps a controlled deviation from the pretrained model if desired, and adds only a modest parameter budget depending on block choices. Empirically, OFT and COFT give improved finetuning stability, faster convergence, and stronger preservation of pretrained generative capabilities across subject-driven and controllable generation tasks, while introducing no inference overhead.

Applications and Empirical Evaluation: Subject-Driven and Controllable Generation

# Experimental backbone and protocols
All experiments reported in the paper use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image diffusion backbone. To ensure fair comparisons with other parameter-efficient finetuning methods (notably LoRA), OFT and COFT are applied to the same model layers where LoRA is used in each experiment. This parity guarantees that differences in performance reflect the finetuning strategy rather than different choices of which weights are adapted.

Training recipes follow the same high-level protocols used by the corresponding baseline methods. For subject-driven generation we adopt the DreamBooth training scheme (same loss functions and optimization objectives), and for controllable generation we follow the dataset splits, conditioning, and task setups used by ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. When comparing to baselines, every method is given careful hyperparameter tuning, and the paper reports averaged statistics over multiple runs where indicated (for example, subject-driven experiments used repeated finetuning with 30 different random seeds to reduce randomness in reported results).

In implementation, OFT initializes each layer's orthogonal transform to the identity so the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained weights. The Cayley parameterization is used to maintain orthogonality efficiently, and block-diagonal parameterizations (with optional block sharing) reduce parameter cost while preserving the theoretical property that pairwise neuron angles are preserved. At inference time the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to produce an equivalent dense weight matrix, so OFT introduces no additional runtime or memory overhead during generation.

# Subject-driven generation setup
The subject-driven experiments use the DreamBooth formulation: given a small set of images of a specific subject (often just a few images), the goal is to finetune the diffusion model so that later text prompts containing a learned special token (or identifier) will generate new images that preserve the subject identity while placing the subject in novel contexts specified by the text prompt.

Training objective and losses follow DreamBooth: a reconstruction-style diffusion loss on subject images together with a class-specific prior-preservation regularizer that prevents the model from overfitting to the few subject images and losing generalization. Concretely, the finetuning optimizes the same diffusion denoising objective used in the pretrained model, with additional examples drawn from a broader class prior (so the model retains general class characteristics). OFT replaces the naively finetuned weight updates in DreamBooth with layer-shared orthogonal transforms applied multiplicatively to pretrained neuron weights:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{with }\ \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I},
\]
and COFT further constrains the orthogonal transform to remain close to identity:
\[
\| \bm{R} - \bm{I} \| \le \epsilon.
\]
Both methods start from \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), so training only moves weights within this orthogonal (and optionally radius-constrained) family.

The paper keeps the DreamBooth loss and training schedule intact so that the only significant change is how model parameters are adapted (orthogonally instead of additive small-weight updates). This isolates the impact of OFT and COFT on subject fidelity, prompt adherence, convergence speed and stability.

# Controllable generation setups
For controllable generation the paper follows the standard setting used by ControlNet and T2I-Adapter: a pretrained text-to-image model is adapted so it can accept an additional conditional signal (for example, Canny edges, semantic segmentation maps, or facial landmarks) together with the text prompt, and then generate images that both respect the control and match the prompt.

Three representative control tasks are used to probe the capability of OFT/COFT:

- Canny edges to image (C2I) on COCO: input is a set of edge maps derived from COCO images; the model should produce images that conform to the provided edges and the text prompt.
- Segmentation map to image (S2I) on ADE20K: input is a semantic segmentation map; the model must generate an image whose semantic regions correspond to the map while also following the text prompt.
- Landmark to face (L2F) on CelebA-HQ: input is a sparse set of face landmarks; the model must produce faces whose pose and layout match the landmark cues.

All methods (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, OFT, COFT) are used to finetune Stable Diffusion v1.5 on these datasets, with the paper reporting runs for 20 epochs in the main controllable generation experiments. As with subject-driven experiments, OFT is applied to the same weight matrices targeted by LoRA to keep the comparisons fair. For evaluation, the paper introduces a control consistency methodology (described below) in which the control signal is re-estimated from the generated image via off-the-shelf detectors/segmenters, and compared to the original control input as an objective measure of how well the model obeys control.

# Metrics for subject-driven generation
The paper evaluates subject-driven finetuning along three dimensions: how well the generated images preserve the specific subject identity, how well they match the text prompt, and how diverse the generated samples are for a fixed subject-and-prompt combination. The metrics used are:

- DINO: average pairwise cosine similarity between DINO (ViT-S/16) embeddings of generated images and real subject images. This measures subject fidelity in a way that emphasizes perceptual and identity-preserving cues captured by self-supervised ViT features.
- CLIP-I (image-side CLIP): average cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings of generated images and the real subject images; another measure of subject fidelity using vision-language embeddings.
- CLIP-T (text-side CLIP): average cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of generated images and the text prompt; this measures how faithfully the generation matches the prompt.
- LPIPS: Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (often used as an image diversity metric when computed among multiple generated images with same prompt), here measuring intra-prompt pairwise diversity: lower LPIPS indicates less diversity; higher LPIPS indicates more diverse outputs.

These metrics together capture the trade-offs a finetuning method faces: retaining subject identity (DINO, CLIP-I), obeying the textual prompt (CLIP-T), and providing varied outputs (LPIPS). In the paper, OFT and COFT improve subject-fidelity metrics substantially compared to DreamBooth and LoRA while maintaining comparable or slightly improved prompt fidelity and diversity.

# Control consistency metrics
To quantify controllability the paper introduces a control consistency evaluation procedure: estimate the control signal (edges, segmentation, or landmarks) from each generated image using a standard, off-the-shelf detector or segmentation model, then compare this estimated signal to the original control input. The specific metrics per task are:

- C2I (Canny-to-Image): Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and F1 score between the binary edge maps extracted from generated images and the original input Canny edges. These measure how well the generated image contains the requested edge structure.
- S2I (Segmentation-to-Image): mean IoU across semantic classes, mean accuracy and overall pixel accuracy between segmentation maps computed from generated images and the input segmentation map. These capture whether semantic regions and class assignments in the generated image align with the requested layout.
- L2F (Landmark-to-Face): mean Euclidean (L2) distance between landmarks extracted from the generated face and the input control landmarks. This directly measures pose and facial-structure adherence.

This re-extraction approach yields an interpretable, task-specific numerical measure of how well the finetuned model obeys the control, subject to the accuracy of the off-the-shelf estimator used to reconstruct the control from generated pixels.

# Convergence and stability: OFT/COFT vs DreamBooth/LoRA/ControlNet
A central empirical theme of the paper is that OFT and its constrained variant COFT converge faster and more stably than competing adaptation methods. Several consistent behaviors are highlighted.

For subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT show rapid and stable acquisition of the subject control signal. In qualitative and training-curve comparisons, both OFT and COFT produce reasonable subject-conditional generations after only a few hundred iterations (the paper notes good control at about 400 iterations), whereas LoRA often fails to preserve identity under the same settings. DreamBooth (standard fine-tuning) initially achieves good control but can deteriorate with too many iterations (the paper observes model collapse-style degradations around 2000 iterations in some cases). By contrast, OFT/COFT remain stable even with larger numbers of training iterations, which reduces the burden of carefully tuning the stopping time.

For controllable generation, OFT and COFT converge substantially faster than the baselines. The paper reports that in the L2F task, LoRA required 20 epochs to reach performance similar to OFT at 8 epochs; COFT and OFT consistently reach competitive control-consistency metrics in fewer epochs than ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. Qualitatively, OFT/COFT produce a steady and smooth improvement in control fidelity over epochs. ControlNet's learning dynamics are characterized as more abrupt: the model often shows a sudden emergence of controllability after a certain epoch (reported around epoch 8 in the experiments), rather than the gradual, stable improvement seen with OFT/COFT.

COFT in particular improves training stability: by explicitly constraining the orthogonal transform to remain close to identity, COFT reduces the variance in finetuning trajectories and sensitivity to learning rates and iteration counts. The paper presents an analysis of the \(\epsilon\) radius hyperparameter showing the intuitive trade-off: a smaller \(\epsilon\) anchors the model closer to pretrained behavior (more stability, less flexibility), while a larger \(\epsilon\) approaches OFT behavior (more flexibility, potentially less stability). This makes COFT a practical option when users want a method that is robust to iteration counts and learning-rate choices.

# Quantitative outcomes: improved subject fidelity and control accuracy; faster convergence and better sample efficiency
Quantitatively, OFT and COFT improve subject-fidelity metrics compared to DreamBooth and LoRA. The paper reports consistent gains in DINO and CLIP-I (subject fidelity) across randomized runs, while maintaining similar CLIP-T (prompt fidelity) and LPIPS (diversity). These results indicate that preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal transforms is effective at keeping pretrained semantic generation ability intact while allowing the model to specialize to a subject.

On control tasks the control consistency metrics show that OFT and COFT yield better adherence to input control signals than LoRA, T2I-Adapter and ControlNet for the tasks evaluated, often with fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs. The L2F results explicitly show faster convergence: LoRA at 20 epochs matches OFT's performance around epoch 8. The COCO (C2I) and ADE20K (S2I) experiments similarly demonstrate higher IoU/F1 and mean IoU/accuracy for OFT/COFT versus baselines.

Sample efficiency is another practical advantage: in the S2I example the paper demonstrates that OFT achieves strong control even when trained with only a small fraction of the dataset (an illustrative case uses about 5% of ADE20K), while ControlNet and LoRA degrade more substantially under the same low-data regimes. These findings are consistent with the intuition that OFT updates effective degrees of freedom (neuron directions) that are directly relevant for semantic editing and control.

# Qualitative outcomes: identity preservation, adherence to prompts, geometric realism and vivid details
Across randomly selected qualitative examples, OFT and COFT show consistent strengths:

- Identity preservation: for subject-driven tasks OFT/COFT retain fine-grained subject details (facial features, specific clothing patterns, object shapes) better than LoRA and often better than vanilla DreamBooth under comparable iteration budgets. This matches the improvements seen in DINO and CLIP-I metrics.
- Prompt adherence: OFT/COFT also produce images that respect textual instructions. In some examples DreamBooth preserved identity but failed to follow the prompt as closely as OFT/COFT; LoRA sometimes produced images that lost subject identity or produced implausible substitutions (e.g., turning clothing into fur) while attempting to satisfy prompts.
- Geometric realism and details: in controllable generation OFT/COFT produce images that better respect structural constraints (segmentation layouts, landmark poses) while also producing vivid textures and plausible geometry. Compared to ControlNet, OFT/COFT often yield richer details and fewer odd artifacts despite using fewer finetuned parameters.

The paper includes human-evaluation experiments (in an appendix) corroborating these qualitative impressions, with raters preferring OFT/COFT outputs in many comparative judgements.

# Ablations and insights
The paper presents several ablations that illuminate why OFT/COFT work and how to tune them:

- Necessity of orthogonality: an ablation removes the orthogonality constraint and allows arbitrary linear transforms of the same parameter count. The result is an unstable finetuning that fails to produce realistic images and loses control (the paper reports that OFT without the orthogonality constraint produced no realistic images in a controllable generation trial). This shows the orthogonality constraint is an essential regularizer: it preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) and prevents destructive parameter drift.
- Effect of \(\epsilon\) in COFT: varying \(\epsilon\) controls the flexibility-stability trade-off. Small \(\epsilon\) enforces strong fidelity to the pretrained weights and yields very stable but conservative behavior; larger \(\epsilon\) approaches OFT and allows more adaptation. The paper provides examples and training curves showing how larger \(\epsilon\) increases finetuning flexibility at the cost of some stability, while moderate choices give a strong practical balance.
- Block count \(r\) and block sharing: block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization governs parameter efficiency. Increasing \(r\) (more blocks) reduces per-block matrix size and therefore reduces the number of orthogonal parameters roughly as \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\). Sharing blocks across channels further reduces parameter cost (to roughly \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\) in the shared-block extreme). The experiments trade off parameter count versus finetuning expressivity: smaller block sizes with sharing are more parameter-efficient but more constrained; larger blocks give more expressivity and further improvements in final performance. The paper shows that with moderate \(r\) and optional sharing, OFT can achieve similar trainable-parameter counts to LoRA while delivering better convergence and final metrics.
- Re-scaled OFT: as an extension, learning a nonnegative diagonal rescaling \(\bm{D}\) per neuron (so \(\bm{z}= (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}\)) increases expressivity without changing hyperspherical energy. The main paper sticks to the original OFT for clarity, but the appendix reports that re-scaled OFT generally helps further — an empirical detail to consider if pursuing the method.

# Parameter count and training speed comparisons
A recurring practical theme is parameter efficiency. OFT/COFT typically finetune a number of parameters comparable to LoRA and much smaller than heavier adapters such as ControlNet. The paper gives a concrete illustrative example: for a \(128\times 128\) weight matrix, LoRA with rank \(r'=8\) has 2048 trainable parameters while OFT with \(r=8\) blocks has 960 trainable parameters (no block sharing). More generally, LoRA's parameter cost scales like \(r'(d+n)\) for a \(d\times n\) matrix, while OFT with block-diagonal orthogonal transforms scales like \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) (and further reductions are possible with block sharing). Importantly, despite this compactness OFT converges faster: in the L2F task LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach performance that OFT achieves in about 8 epochs. ControlNet typically needs yet more parameters and epochs to reach similar control quality.

Finally, OFT and COFT introduce no inference-time computational overhead. The learned orthogonal transform can be multiplied into the existing pretrained weight matrix once after training, yielding a single dense weight matrix for generation. Thus deployed models run at the same speed and memory footprint as the original pretrained model.

# Practical recommendations and takeaways
From the empirical results and ablations the paper derives a set of practical recommendations:

- If you need rapid adaptation that preserves pretrained generative abilities, prefer OFT or COFT over naive fine-tuning and low-rank additive methods like LoRA, especially when finetuning budgets (data, epochs) are small.
- Use COFT (nonzero but small \(\epsilon\)) when training stability and insensitivity to iteration count are important: COFT explicitly constrains deviation from identity and yields predictable behavior.
- Choose block size \(r\) and whether to share blocks to balance parameter cost and expressivity. Start with moderate block granularity that gives a parameter footprint comparable to LoRA and tune upward if more expressivity is needed.
- Initialize orthogonal matrices to identity (initialize Cayley skew-symmetric parameter \(\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\)) so finetuning begins exactly at pretrained weights; this is key to avoid initial disruption of pretrained capabilities.
- Because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy and requires no extra inference modules, it is suitable for deployment scenarios where resource constraints or architectural simplicity matter.

In summary, the experiments in the paper demonstrate that orthogonal finetuning (and its constrained variant) are practical, parameter-efficient, and robust approaches to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models for subject-driven personalization and a variety of controllable generation tasks, yielding faster convergence, improved control fidelity, and better preservation of pretrained generative semantics than several popular baselines.

Practical Guidance, Implementation Details, and Open Problems

# Implementation steps: inserting orthogonal modules, initializing $R=I$ via $Q=0$, forward and backward passes

Implementing OFT/COFT requires adding a small, layer-shared orthogonal transformation into the forward pass of the existing pretrained layer weights and ensuring that transformation is learnable and differentiable during training while initialized to identity. The paper's canonical formulation for a fully connected layer uses a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ so the OFT forward pass becomes
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. For re-scaled OFT the forward pass is
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$ with $\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ learnable and $s_i>0$.

A practical, efficient way to parametrize $\bm{R}$ is via the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). The Cayley parameterization sets
$\displaystyle \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$,
which is an orthogonal matrix with $\det(\bm{R})=1$. To obtain $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at initialization, initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}=\bm{I}$. If you use the re-scaled variant, initialize $\bm{D}=\bm{I}$.

Insertion steps, in order:
1. Identify the target weight matrices to adapt (see next section for guidelines).
2. Replace the forward computation of that layer from $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, or to $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$ for re-scaled OFT. Realize this by computing $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{Q}$ using Cayley and then multiplying.
3. Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ (so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$) and, if used, initialize $\bm{D}=\bm{I}$.
4. Train by optimizing only the added parameters ($\bm{Q}$ blocks and optional $\bm{D}$ diagonal). During backward passes, gradients flow through the Cayley mapping into $\bm{Q}$ (and into $\bm{D}$ when present).

For COFT, in addition to the orthogonality constraint enforced by the Cayley parameterization, enforce a radius constraint around identity. The paper approximates $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ by a Neumann series; thus the COFT deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ is implemented approximately by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ (see the COFT section below). This is enforced with projected gradient steps on $\bm{Q}$ after each parameter update.

# Choosing where to finetune: attention projections in Transformers; extending to convolutional layers

Which layers to adapt determines both effectiveness and parameter cost. The paper follows the common practice of applying adapter-style finetuning to attention projection matrices in Transformer-based architectures when comparing to LoRA, because LoRA typically adapts attention weights and that creates a fair comparison. Thus a practical default is to apply OFT to the key/query/value and output projection weight matrices in the attention blocks of a diffusion model's Transformer components.

OFT is not restricted to fully connected or Transformer layers. For convolutional layers, the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ naturally maps to channel-wise transformations: using one block per input channel makes each block act on a single channel (akin to depthwise transforms), while sharing blocks across channels applies the same orthogonal transform to each channel. This means you can insert OFT modules for convolutional kernels by treating the flattened spatial kernel vector per channel as the neuron vector the orthogonal matrix multiplies.

Practical guidance:
- For Transformer-based diffusion U-Nets or text encoders, start with attention projection matrices (the same places LoRA is commonly applied). This provides good leverage with few added parameters.
- If you need more expressivity, extend OFT to feed-forward / MLP weight matrices or to selected convolutional layers using the block interpretation described above.
- If computational budget is limited, prefer adapting a small set of layers (e.g., attention projections) with block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ rather than full dense $\bm{R}$ everywhere.

# Parameterization choices: number of blocks $r$, block sharing, re-scaled OFT vs base OFT

The block-diagonal parameterization trades expressivity for parameter and compute efficiency. Let $d$ be the input neuron dimension for a layer and choose $r$ blocks so each block is size $d/r$. Then
$\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with $\bm{R}_i\in\mathrm{O}(d/r)$ and each block is orthogonal. The paper highlights these effects:

- Increasing $r$ (more blocks) makes blocks smaller, reducing both the number of parameters and the cost of the necessary matrix inverse in the Cayley transform. Extreme $r$ (one block per channel) biases the transform toward channel-wise rotations.
- When $r=1$ you have a single full-size orthogonal matrix (maximal flexibility but highest cost).
- Block sharing (setting $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i,j$) reduces parameters further and enforces a shared transform across groups of neurons; this yields parameter complexity reductions to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ under shared-block assumptions reported in the paper.

Choosing $r$ is thus a design knob:
- Use moderate $r$ (e.g., in the paper they report examples with $r=8$) to balance flexibility and efficiency.
- If you know the layer structure (channels, embedding dimension), aligning block boundaries with channels can give interpretable transforms and efficient implementations.

Re-scaled OFT (learning $\bm{D}$) adds a small number of extra parameters (one positive scalar per output neuron) and increases flexibility because magnitude rescaling does not change hyperspherical energy. The paper notes re-scaled OFT generally improves performance with negligible parameter cost, though experiments in the main text focus on unscaled OFT to isolate the effect of orthogonal transformations. If you need extra capacity, enable re-scaling.

# COFT hyperparameters: setting $\epsilon$; projected gradient on $Q$; when to use COFT for extra stability

COFT adds an explicit deviation constraint to keep the learned orthogonal transform close to identity:
$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ in addition to $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Directly constraining $\bm{R}$ inside Cayley is awkward, so the paper uses the Neumann-series approximation
$\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ and replaces the constraint by a simpler bound on $\bm{Q}$:
$\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$, which can be enforced with projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$: after each gradient step, project $\bm{Q}$ back into the ball of radius $\epsilon'$ (under the chosen matrix norm).

Practical rules from the paper's observations:
- Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so the finetuning starts at identity.
- Use the COFT radius to control flexibility: smaller $\epsilon$ (and hence smaller $\epsilon'$) makes finetuning behave closer to the pretrained model and improves stability; larger $\epsilon$ approaches unconstrained OFT.
- COFT is especially helpful when finetuning is unstable (subject-driven examples where DreamBooth or LoRA may collapse with many iterations). If you find sensitivity to the number of iterations or learning rate, switch to COFT and tune $\epsilon$ conservatively.
- The appropriate $\epsilon$ scales with your layer parameterization and block size; the paper shows that $\epsilon$ controls the trade-off between stability and flexibility (see their Figure demonstrating behavior across $\epsilon$). Start with a small $\epsilon$ that still allows the model to change direction meaningfully, then increase if required.

# Training tips: learning rates, iteration/epoch counts, batch sizes, monitoring stability and convergence

The paper emphasizes that OFT/COFT converges faster and is more stable than baseline finetuning strategies. Use the empirical findings to guide training:

- Iteration/epoch guidance: in controllable generation experiments the paper finetuned for 20 epochs on datasets like ADE20K / CelebA-HQ and found OFT often reached good performance substantially earlier (for one face task, OFT achieved later LoRA performance at 8 epochs vs LoRA at 20). For subject-driven tasks the paper reports that OFT and COFT could achieve good control in a few hundred iterations (e.g., ~400) and remain stable even at much larger iteration counts where other methods collapse. Use OFT/COFT to allow larger numbers of iterations without catastrophic collapse.
- Learning rates: the paper does not prescribe a universal scalar, but its empirical point is that OFT can tolerate larger effective updates than naïve finetuning because it preserves hyperspherical energy. If you previously used tiny learning rates to avoid divergence, you can often increase the learning rate with OFT/COFT, but monitor validation metrics carefully.
- Batch sizes: follow the usual constraints of your hardware and dataset. OFT adds relatively few parameters compared to the full model, so memory impact is modest compared to approaches that add whole sub-networks.
- Monitoring: track control-specific metrics (e.g., the paper's control consistency metrics: IoU/F1 for edge-to-image, mean IoU and accuracy for segmentation-to-image, mean $\ell_2$ landmark error for face-landmark tasks) and general generation metrics (CLIP similarity, DINO, LPIPS for diversity in subject-driven tasks). Watch for sudden degradation in image quality; OFT/COFT are designed to avoid sudden collapse but you should still monitor sample outputs during training.
- Iteration insensitivity: because COFT constrains deviation explicitly, it reduces sensitivity to the number of iterations and learning rate, making hyperparameter selection easier in practice.

# Computational considerations: efficient Cayley inversion, batched block inverses, memory and runtime trade-offs

The key computational cost introduced by OFT is constructing $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley transform, which requires inverting $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ (or inverses per block when using block-diagonal $\bm{R}$). The paper acknowledges that the matrix inverse slightly limits scalability but proposes practical mitigations:

- Block-diagonal $\bm{R}$: dividing $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks reduces the dimension of matrices you need to invert to $d/r$, and enables inverting small matrices in parallel. This both reduces compute and memory and often maps better to GPU kernels.
- Block sharing: sharing the same block across multiple groups reduces parameter count and further reduces the number of distinct inverses you need to compute.
- Batched inverses: implement the inverse per-block as a batched small-matrix inverse so that modern linear-algebra libraries can parallelize these on GPU efficiently.
- Numerical stability: ensure $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is well-conditioned. Because we initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ and (in COFT) constrain $\|\bm{Q}\|$, the inversion should remain numerically safe in practice. Still, use numerically stable inversion routines and consider adding a tiny diagonal damping (if necessary) when solving linear systems.
- Trade-offs: smaller blocks reduce per-step compute but may reduce expressivity; larger blocks increase expressivity but cost more. The paper reports practical parameter counts (e.g., for a $128\times128$ weight matrix: LoRA with $r'=8$ has 2048 trainable params, OFT with $r=8$ has 960 trainable params) illustrating how OFT can be parameter-efficient.

The paper notes that speeding up the Cayley inverse in a differentiable way remains an open problem; block-diagonalization is the primary practical workaround.

# Combining OFT with other adapters (e.g., LoRA) and multi-task composition via multiplying orthogonal matrices

The paper frames OFT and LoRA as complementary parameter-efficiency strategies: LoRA adds a low-rank additive update $\Delta\bm{W}$, whereas OFT multiplies the pretrained weights by a structured orthogonal transform. Formally,
$\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$,
so the OFT update is additive in that view but constrained by $\bm{R}$'s properties.

Direct combination is not experimentally explored in the main paper, so if you want to combine OFT and LoRA in practice you should treat it as an engineering experiment: you can allow both multiplicative orthogonal transforms and additive low-rank updates simultaneously, but you must then tune the relative capacities to avoid redundancy or overfitting.

The paper highlights an interesting property for multi-task composition: orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication, so orthogonal matrices learned for different finetuning tasks can be multiplied to yield another orthogonal matrix. Whether multiplying task-specific $\bm{R}$ matrices preserves each task's knowledge (i.e., compositional multi-task deployment) is an open research question the paper explicitly calls out. Practically, you can store each task's $\bm{R}$ and apply them in sequence, but the preservation of task performance when composed is not guaranteed and requires empirical validation.

# Deployment: folding $R$ (and $D$) into pretrained weights; zero overhead inference

A key practical advantage reported in the paper is that OFT/COFT introduce no extra inference-time overhead. Before deployment, you can fold the learned transform into the pretrained weight:
$\bm{W} \leftarrow \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ (and, if re-scaled OFT is used, $\bm{W}\leftarrow \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$). After folding, the model uses a standard weight matrix with identical runtime and memory profile to the original pretrained model. This makes OFT attractive for production: you pay additional computation only during finetuning, not at inference.

# Limitations: Cayley inverse scalability, bias from block-diagonal structure, dependency on pretrained rank/structure

The paper clearly acknowledges practical limitations you should consider:

- Cayley inverse scalability: the Cayley parametrization requires matrix inverses and can be a bottleneck for very high-dimensional layers. Block-diagonalization alleviates this but does not eliminate it. Finding faster differentiable alternatives is an open problem.
- Block-diagonal bias: block-diagonal parameterization introduces an inductive bias and can limit flexibility. Small blocks are efficient but might not capture global transforms; large blocks are expressive but expensive. This trade-off must be tuned per-layer and per-task.
- Dependency on pretrained structure: OFT works by preserving pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy). If pretrained neurons already have structure that depends heavily on magnitudes or specific Euclidean offsets, OFT's angular-only transforms may be less effective unless re-scaling ($\bm{D}$) is used. The paper emphasizes that preserving hyperspherical energy is desirable for maintaining pretraining generative abilities, but this design choice could be less ideal for some modifications where magnitude changes matter more.

# Ethical and safety considerations for personalized and controllable generation

While the paper's technical contributions focus on stable and efficient finetuning for subject-driven and controllable generation, practitioners should be mindful of the broader implications of deploying improved personalization and control. The two target tasks—subject-driven generation (personalized images from a few photos) and controllable generation (image production guided by dense signals)—both enable finer control over generated content. That capability increases utility but also raises familiar concerns in the domain of image synthesis:

- Privacy and consent: subject-driven finetuning uses a few images of a person to produce novel images of that subject. Ensure that people whose images are used have given informed consent for model personalization, and apply appropriate data governance and storage protections.
- Misuse potential: stronger controllability and subject preservation can make it easier to produce convincing impersonations or deepfakes. Practitioners should apply standard mitigations such as usage policies, watermarking, provenance tracking, and making model access contingent on compliance with ethical use guidelines.
- Safety of generated content: stronger control may enable the generation of harmful or misleading imagery more reliably. Standard content moderation processes and guardrails should remain in place.

The paper does not itself propose mitigation strategies, but when adopting OFT/COFT in production contexts, incorporate the usual safeguards relevant to image-generation technologies and consider the above risks in your deployment planning.

# Open problems: faster differentiable orthogonalization, compositionality across tasks, improved parameter efficiency beyond block structure

The paper ends by highlighting several concrete open research directions that are directly relevant for implementers and researchers:

1. Faster differentiable orthogonalization: computing $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ is the practical bottleneck in Cayley parameterization. Designing a faster, numerically robust, and differentiable orthogonal parameterization (or approximations that preserve the theoretical invariance to hyperspherical energy) remains an important open problem.

2. Compositionality of task transforms: because orthogonal matrices multiply to an orthogonal matrix, task-specific $\bm{R}$ matrices can in principle be composed. Whether a sequence or product of task-specific orthogonal transforms preserves the knowledge of multiple downstream tasks simultaneously is an intriguing question for multi-task personalization and efficient multi-skill deployment.

3. Improved parameter-efficiency beyond block structure: block-diagonalization is an effective practical compromise, but it introduces structural bias. Discovering parameterizations that reduce cost without the same bias (for example, structure that exploits low-dimensional manifolds of useful rotations) is an open direction.

4. Empirical limits of hyperspherical preservation: the paper motivates preserving hyperspherical energy as crucial to maintain pretrained generative ability. Further theoretical and empirical work could clarify when angular preservation is necessary versus when magnitude changes should be allowed (and how to balance them adaptively).

5. Combining multiplicative and additive adapters: exploring principled ways to combine orthogonal (multiplicative) transforms with additive low-rank updates (LoRA-style) — including how to allocate capacity between them — is an empirical design space to explore.

These open problems map directly to practical implementation challenges you will encounter: scaling the Cayley transform, choosing block structures, composing multiple task adapters, and deciding when to allow magnitude changes (re-scaled OFT) versus preserving angles strictly.

Concluding note

OFT and COFT are practical, parameter-efficient ways to finetune large text-to-image diffusion models while preserving a structural measure (hyperspherical energy) believed to encode semantic generation ability. The implementation recipe is straightforward: insert a Cayley-parametrized orthogonal transform per chosen weight, initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, optionally add a diagonal rescaling $\bm{D}$, and (for COFT) project $\bm{Q}$ to enforce a small radius. Use block-diagonalization and batched inverses to control compute and memory, prefer adapting attention projections first, and monitor task-specific control and fidelity metrics during training. The paper documents faster convergence, improved stability, and zero inference overhead when you fold learned transforms into pretrained weights; the remaining engineering questions concern scaling and composition, which are promising directions for future work.
