\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Can someone clarify the FLOP accounting for Cross-Attention vs Self-Attention in BLT?
Question:
The paper claims in Section 5.3 that 'Patches Scale Better Than Tokens' because one can increase both model size and patch size while keeping inference flops constant. However, looking at Figure 4 (Fixed Inference Scaling), it seems that simply increasing patch size without adjusting model parameters actually hurts performance initially (the crossover point). Why does increasing patch size alone fail? Is it because reducing the number of global transformer steps reduces effective context length or information flow unless compensated by increasing hidden dimensions or depth? How does this relate to the claim that 'larger patch sizes appear to perform closer to BPE models at larger model scales'?
Answer:
The confusion regarding the initial performance dip when increasing patch size stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of how FLOPs are distributed and consumed within the {\textsc BLT} architecture versus standard token-based models. When you increase the patch size without adjusting the model parameters, you are essentially compressing the sequence length (reducing the number of patches $n_p$) while keeping the total byte count constant. According to Equation~\ref{eq:5} in the paper, the dominant computational cost in {\textsc BLT} resides in the global latent transformer, which operates once per patch. Therefore, increasing the patch size directly reduces the number of times this expensive global model is invoked. If you keep the global model's hidden dimensions and depth fixed while simply increasing the patch size, you are effectively shrinking the model's "working memory" and reducing its exposure to sequential dependencies because it processes fewer discrete steps.

The paper explicitly notes that simply increasing patch size without compensatory architectural changes hurts performance initially because it limits the effective context length available to the global transformer at each step. The global model relies on attending to previous patches to build a coherent representation; by skipping intermediate byte-level nuances (which are handled by lighter local models), you risk losing fine-grained information flow unless the local encoder-decoder components are sufficiently powerful to encapsulate that information into a single latent representation. The authors argue that "longer patch sizes... save compute," but this saved compute must be *reallocated* to grow the global transformer (increasing $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ or $h_{\mathcal{G}}$) to compensate for the reduced number of steps. Without this reallocation—specifically, without increasing the model size—the system lacks the capacity to process information as densely as it did with smaller patches, leading to an initial performance drop until sufficient scale is reached.

Regarding your intuition about FLOP accounting, it is crucial to distinguish between self-attention and cross-attention costs here. In {\textsc BLT}, self-attention occurs in three places: locally within bytes (in encoder/decoder transformers), globally among patches (in the latent transformer), and via cross-attention mechanisms linking bytes to patches. The self-attention in the global latent transformer is quadratic with respect to the number of patches ($O(n_p^2)$), whereas cross-attention in the encoder scales with $n_p \times k$ (where $k$ is patch size). When you increase patch size ($k$) while holding parameters constant, $n_p$ decreases proportionally ($n_p = n_{\text{ctx}}/k$). This drastically cuts down on global self-attention steps, which is good for efficiency but bad for capacity if not balanced by larger internal dimensions. The initial failure occurs because you have removed "steps" (contextual transitions) without adding "capacity" (hidden states or layers) to understand those skipped transitions in one go.

The statement that "larger patch sizes appear to perform closer to BPE models at larger model scales" refers precisely to this balancing act described in Section~5.3 ("Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"). As you scale up from 400M to 8B parameters, you can afford to grow the hidden dimensions of that global latent transformer significantly while simultaneously using larger patches (e.g., 6 or 8 bytes). The paper observes that at these larger scales, the relative contribution of byte-level modules (encoder/decoder) diminishes compared to the massive global transformer. Consequently, even though larger patches mean fewer steps, a sufficiently large global transformer can absorb this reduction because its increased width and depth allow it to capture complex patterns within each sparse step more effectively than a smaller model processing denser steps could. Essentially, at small scales, reducing steps hurts because you haven't built enough capacity; at large scales, reducing steps frees up resources that can be poured into a super-capacious global model that handles longer-range dependencies more efficiently than a token-based model constrained by fixed vocabulary sizes. This dynamic allows {\textsc BLT} to eventually surpass token-based models by leveraging a new degree of freedom: trading sequence length for model width/depth more aggressively than BPE architectures allow.
