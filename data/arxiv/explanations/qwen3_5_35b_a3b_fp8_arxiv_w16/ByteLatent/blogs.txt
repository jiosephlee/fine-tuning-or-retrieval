# Why Your LLM Should Stop Using Tokens: Introducing Byte Latent Transformers

Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized artificial intelligence, yet they all share a fundamental bottleneck: tokenization. Before an LLM can process your text, it must be chopped into discrete chunks called tokens using a fixed vocabulary, typically via algorithms like Byte Pair Encoding (BPE). This heuristic pre-processing step forces the model to compress raw data into a limited set of symbols, often leading to inefficiencies in handling noise, rare languages, and complex patterns. Enter the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), a new architecture that discards this rigid tokenization entirely. By learning directly from raw bytes and dynamically grouping them into "patches," BLT matches the performance of industry giants like Llama 3 while offering significant gains in inference efficiency and robustness.

## The Problem with Fixed Vocabulary Tokenization

Current state-of-the-art models rely heavily on tokenizers that map strings of bytes to a finite set of tokens determined before training begins. While this speeds up computation by reducing sequence length, it introduces several critical limitations. First, the compression heuristics used to create tokens are arbitrary and not necessarily aligned with the actual complexity of the data. A model might waste computational effort on predictable sequences or fail to allocate enough capacity for complex regions simply because the tokenizer decided they belonged to the same token unit.

Furthermore, fixed vocabularies create biases that hinder performance across different domains and modalities. Tokenizers struggle with out-of-vocabulary words, exhibit sensitivity to input noise (like typos or random casing), and often suffer from multilingual inequity where low-resource languages are poorly represented in the vocabulary. Most importantly, once a model is trained on tokens, it loses direct access to the underlying byte-level features, making it difficult to understand orthography, phonology, or subtle character-level manipulations that human readers grasp effortlessly.

## Dynamic Patching: Allocating Compute Where It Matters

The core innovation of BLT lies in its replacement of static tokens with dynamic "patches." Instead of forcing every word or sub-word into a fixed slot, BLT groups raw bytes into variable-sized patches based on the entropy of the next byte prediction. Entropy here serves as a proxy for uncertainty; high entropy indicates a complex region requiring more attention, while low entropy suggests predictable text where less compute is needed.

This approach mimics how humans read: we don't need deep cognitive processing to finish reading "the quick brown fox jumps o..." because the pattern is clear; however, we pay close attention when encountering new proper nouns or complex mathematical expressions. BLT implements this by using a small auxiliary language model to estimate next-byte entropies during data loading. Based on these estimates, it decides where to cut the byte stream into patches. If a sequence is predictable, it forms long patches containing many bytes; if it is chaotic or novel, it creates shorter patches with finer granularity. This dynamic allocation ensures that the computationally expensive global transformer is invoked only when necessary, drastically improving efficiency compared to models that treat every token equally regardless of difficulty.

## Architectural Efficiency: The Three-Block Design

To support this dynamic patching strategy without exploding computational costs at scale, BLT employs a unique three-part architecture consisting of two lightweight local models flanking one massive global model. The flow begins with a **Local Encoder**, which takes individual bytes and aggregates them into latent patch representations using cross-attention mechanisms and hash-based n-gram embeddings. These embeddings allow the model to capture local context and byte-level patterns without needing a massive vocabulary table.

Once encoded, these patches are fed into the **Latent Global Transformer**. This is the heavy lifter—a standard autoregressive transformer that operates on patch representations rather than individual bytes or fixed tokens. Because it runs fewer steps (one per patch rather than one per byte), it consumes significantly fewer floating-point operations (flops) for predictable data streams compared to token-based models processing thousands of tiny tokens per sentence. Finally, a **Local Decoder** reverses this process, taking the global patch outputs and decoding them back into raw bytes sequentially. This separation allows BLT to scale effectively: as data grows more complex globally, we can expand the global transformer's size while maintaining efficiency through smarter patch sizing.

## Breaking Scaling Laws: Training Flops vs Inference Cost

One of the most compelling findings from BLT's research is its ability to decouple training efficiency from inference efficiency in ways token-based models cannot achieve easily. In traditional architectures like Llama 3 increasing model size usually requires proportionally increasing inference cost unless you accept performance drops or reduce context length artificially via larger vocabularies (which eventually hit limits). BLT breaks this trade-off by allowing researchers to simultaneously increase both model size *and* average patch size while keeping inference flops constant longer patches mean fewer steps for the global transformer free up compute budget which can then be reinvested into expanding the global transformer itself or adding more layers locally).

In their scaling studies involving up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes BLT achieved parity with Llama 3 on compute-optimal training regimes but offered up to 50% fewer flops during inference when configured for larger patch sizes (e.g., averaging 8 bytes per patch versus ~4 for BPE). This means developers can train massive models that are cheaper and faster at runtime without sacrificing accuracy—a game-changer for deploying large-scale AI applications in production environments where latency matters most).

# Smart Compute Allocation: How Dynamic 'Patching' Makes AI Faster and Smarter

Large Language Models (LLMs) have traditionally relied on tokenization, a process that chops text into fixed chunks called tokens before feeding them to the model. While effective, this approach forces the model to spend the same amount of computational effort on every single unit, regardless of whether that unit is a simple letter like "e" or a complex concept requiring deep reasoning. This inefficiency stems from the rigid nature of static tokenizers, which cannot adapt to the varying complexity of the data they process. Enter the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), a new architecture that abandons fixed vocabularies in favor of dynamic "patching," allowing models to intelligently allocate compute where it is actually needed.

At the heart of BLT is a fundamental shift in how data is grouped for processing. Instead of treating every token equally, BLT segments raw bytes into variable-sized "patches." These patches are not arbitrary; they are determined by the entropy, or uncertainty, of the next byte in the sequence. If the model encounters a predictable section of text—like common words or repetitive patterns—it groups many bytes into a single long patch, processing them with minimal overhead. Conversely, when it hits a complex section with high uncertainty, such as the beginning of a new sentence or a rare mathematical formula, it breaks the sequence into shorter patches to allow for more granular computation. This dynamic allocation means that predictable data flows through quickly, while complex data receives the heavy lifting required to resolve ambiguity.

The architecture supporting this logic consists of three distinct components working in harmony: two lightweight local models and one powerful global transformer. The local encoder acts as a gateway, rapidly ingesting sequences of raw bytes and compressing them into patch representations using cross-attention mechanisms and hash-based n-gram embeddings. Once these patches are formed based on entropy thresholds, they are fed into the global latent transformer, which performs the bulk of the reasoning work. Because long patches require fewer steps through this computationally expensive global layer, the system naturally saves floating-point operations (FLOPS) on easy tasks. A local decoder then reverses this process, expanding the patch representations back into individual bytes for output generation. This structure ensures that compute is reserved specifically for moments where prediction difficulty spikes.

The impact of this approach extends beyond mere speed; it fundamentally changes how models scale and generalize. In extensive scaling studies involving up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, BLT demonstrated that it can match or exceed the performance of state-of-the-art token-based models like Llama 3 while using significantly fewer resources during inference. Specifically, by adjusting patch sizes to capture more predictable data in fewer steps, researchers achieved up to 50% savings in inference flops without sacrificing accuracy. More impressively, BLT unlocked a new dimension for scaling: because larger patches reduce inference costs, developers can simultaneously increase both the model size and the average patch length within a fixed hardware budget. This dual scaling strategy allows for more capable models that run just as fast as their smaller predecessors.

Furthermore, by operating directly on raw bytes rather than pre-defined tokens, BLT exhibits superior robustness and understanding of sub-word structures. Traditional tokenizers often struggle with noisy inputs or languages outside their training distribution because they rely on finite vocabularies that may not cover rare characters or novel combinations. BLT bypasses these limitations entirely. Experiments showed that BLT models are significantly more resilient to input noise, such as random character deletions or case changes, outperforming token-based baselines by an average margin in noisy environments. Additionally, they excel at tasks requiring fine-grained character manipulation and phonological understanding—areas where BPE-tokenized models frequently fail due to their inability to see individual letters clearly during training.

Ultimately, BLT proves that breaking free from fixed tokenization opens new pathways for efficiency and capability in artificial intelligence. By treating text as a fluid stream where compute is dynamically distributed based on information density rather than rigid rules, we move closer to ideal resource utilization. The results suggest that future LLMs need not be bound by heuristics invented decades ago; instead, they can learn to organize themselves around the intrinsic complexity of their input data. As we continue to push toward larger scales and more demanding applications, architectures like BLT offer a compelling blueprint for building systems that are not only smarter but also leaner and more adaptable than ever before.



# Beyond the Vocabulary: Why Raw Bytes Make AI More Robust Against Noise and Errors

Large language models (LLMs) have traditionally relied on a rigid preprocessing step called tokenization, where raw text is converted into a finite set of subword units. While this approach has enabled massive scale, it introduces inherent biases and fragilities. Recent advancements in the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenge this status quo by demonstrating that models trained directly on raw bytes can match the performance of industry giants like Llama 3 while offering superior resilience to noise and errors. By eliminating the fixed vocabulary bottleneck, BLT unlocks a new level of robustness that allows AI systems to understand phonology, spelling, and low-resource languages with unprecedented clarity.

## The Fragility of Fixed Tokenization

Standard LLMs process text through a tokenizer that maps sequences of bytes to tokens from a predefined dictionary. This heuristic compression creates a disconnect between the model and the fundamental structure of language. Because tokens are arbitrary groupings, models often struggle with inputs that deviate from their training distribution, such as typos, mixed casing, or rare characters. This limitation manifests in several ways: sensitivity to input noise, a lack of true orthographic knowledge (understanding how letters form words), and inequities when handling diverse writing systems or low-resource languages. Essentially, if a word contains characters outside the model's expected patterns or is formatted unexpectedly, the tokenization process can fragment meaning before the model even sees it.

BLT addresses these issues by operating directly on byte sequences without a fixed vocabulary. Instead of forcing data into static chunks, BLT employs a dynamic patching mechanism that groups bytes based on the entropy (uncertainty) of the next byte prediction. This allows the model to allocate computational resources intelligently: it uses larger patches for predictable text and finer-grained processing for complex regions. This flexibility not only improves efficiency but fundamentally changes how the model perceives data, granting it direct access to character-level information that token-based models inherently lose during compression.

## Superpowers in Noisy Environments

One of the most striking findings from recent experiments is BLT's remarkable robustness against corrupted inputs. Traditional tokenizers often fail catastrophically when faced with simple perturbations like random character deletions or case changes because these alterations break established token boundaries. In contrast, BLT maintains high performance even when inputs are heavily distorted.

Researchers tested this by applying five distinct noising strategies to benchmark tasks: converting text to uppercase separated characters ("AntSpeak"), randomly dropping 10% of characters ("Drop"), mixing random upper and lower case ("RandomCase"), repeating characters ("Repeat"), and forcing all caps ("UpperCase"). The results were conclusive; BLT outperformed tokenizer-based models across all noise types by an average margin of 8 percentage points on tasks like HellaSwag. Remarkably, even when compared against Llama 3.1—a model trained on vastly more data—BLT showed superior resistance to corruption. This suggests that by learning directly from bytes rather than tokens, the model develops an internal representation that is invariant to superficial formatting errors, making it far more reliable in real-world scenarios where user input is rarely pristine.

## Mastering Orthography and Phonology

Beyond mere error tolerance, raw byte modeling grants models a deep understanding of sub-word structures that token-based approaches struggle to grasp effectively. Two specific areas highlight this advantage: orthography (spelling) and phonology (sound-to-text relationships).

In orthographic tasks evaluated by the CUTE benchmark, which tests a model's ability to manipulate text sequences (like deleting or inserting letters), BLT achieved near-perfect scores (99.9%) on spelling-related tasks. Token-based models frequently falter here because they treat words as atomic units; they know what "apple" looks like as a whole but often fail when asked to delete the letter 'p'. BLT, seeing individual bytes, manipulates text with surgical precision because it never loses track of constituent characters during training.

Similarly, in grapheme-to-phoneme conversion tasks—mapping written characters to their spoken sounds—BLT outperformed Llama 3 significantly. Phonetic rules rely heavily on consistent character mappings rather than word-level semantics. Since tokenizers often split words inconsistently based on frequency statistics rather than linguistic logic (e.g., splitting "playing" into "play" and "ing", but treating "played" as "play" plus "-ed"), they obscure these phonetic patterns for the model. By processing raw bytes, BLT naturally learns these universal sound-letter relationships without needing explicit instruction or specialized data augmentation.

## Breaking Barriers in Low-Resource Languages

The impact of bypassing tokenization extends globally to languages with limited digital presence or non-Latin scripts where standard tokenizers often perform poorly due to sparse vocabulary coverage for rare symbols or scripts. In machine translation tasks involving 21 low-resource languages from diverse families using the FLORES-101 benchmark, BLT demonstrated clear advantages over Llama 3 tokenizer-based baselines.

While performance gaps narrowed for major language pairs where abundant training data exists for both architectures, BLT consistently outperformed competitors on lower-resource languages and those with complex scripts where BPE tokenizers tend to fragment text inefficiently. The ability to generalize from raw byte sequences allowed BLT to handle long-tail distributions more effectively than models constrained by fixed vocabularies that must compress rare characters into infrequent tokens or unknown symbols (`[UNK]`). This suggests that for global applications requiring support for diverse languages and dialects, moving away from static tokenization is not just an efficiency gain but a necessity for equitable performance across linguistic communities.

## Conclusion: A New Paradigm for Scaling AI

The introduction of Byte Latent Transformer marks a pivotal shift in how we approach large-scale language modeling challenges associated with rigidity and brittleness found in traditional pipelines removed barriers posed by fixed vocabularies allow models to engage directly with raw data complexity enabling significant gains in inference efficiency alongside robustness improvements By simultaneously growing patch sizes and model capacity within fixed flop budgets researchers have shown that byte-level architectures can match state-of-the-art performance while trading minor metric losses for substantial operational savings Ultimately as AI moves toward more open-ended interactions where input quality varies wildly adopting byte-native designs offers a path toward systems that are not only smarter but also safer and more resilient in unpredictable environments



# The New Scaling Law: Growing Models Without Breaking Your Budget

For years, the trajectory of Large Language Model (LLM) development has been defined by a rigid trade-off. To get bigger and smarter, models had to accept slower inference speeds or consume exponentially more compute. This bottleneck stems from the industry's reliance on tokenization—the process of grouping bytes into fixed vocabulary items. While effective, this approach forces a model to allocate the same amount of computational effort to every token, regardless of whether that token represents a complex concept or a trivial repetition. Enter the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), a new architecture that shatters this constraint by introducing a dynamic scaling law where you can simultaneously increase model size and patch size without exceeding your inference budget.

Traditional LLMs treat every token equally, meaning the computational cost is driven by the number of tokens rather than the actual complexity of the data. If a sentence contains mostly predictable words, the model still spends significant resources processing them as individual tokens. BLT flips this script by operating directly on raw bytes and dynamically grouping them into "patches." These patches are not fixed units like tokens; instead, they are formed based on the entropy, or uncertainty, of the next byte. In regions where the data is predictable—like common suffixes in English words—the model groups many bytes into a single patch. Conversely, in complex areas requiring careful prediction, it breaks data down into smaller chunks. This allows BLT to allocate its "brainpower" exactly where it is needed most.

The architectural innovation lies in how BLT manages these patches through a hybrid system of local and global models. Instead of running one massive transformer on every unit of input, BLT utilizes two lightweight "local" models flanking a powerful "global" latent transformer. The local encoder compresses sequences of raw bytes into compact patch representations, while the global transformer performs heavy lifting on these higher-level patches. Once the global model processes the information, a local decoder expands it back into raw bytes for output. Crucially, because dynamic patching reduces the number of steps required for predictable text, the expensive global transformer is invoked less frequently for easy sections of text. This efficiency gain creates a unique opportunity: we can reallocate the saved compute to grow the size of that global transformer itself.

This leads to what we call "flop-controlled scaling," where performance improves not just by adding parameters, but by intelligently managing how those parameters are accessed. In our scaling studies involving models up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, we observed that BLT can maintain a fixed inference budget while increasing both model capacity and average patch size. For instance, by doubling the average patch size from 4 to 8 bytes, we effectively cut inference costs by nearly half without sacrificing performance on standard benchmarks like MMLU or HumanEval compared to Llama 3. In fact, when holding inference costs constant, BLT consistently outperforms tokenization-based architectures as training data grows beyond optimal ratios. This means developers can invest more in pre-training—a one-time cost—to build significantly more capable models that remain fast during deployment.

Beyond raw efficiency and scaling trends, working directly with raw bytes unlocks capabilities that token-based models often miss due to their reliance on finite vocabularies. Tokenizers inherently lose orthographic knowledge because they cannot distinguish between letters that belong to different words if those letter combinations aren't explicitly in their dictionary. BLT retains direct access to byte-level features through specialized hash n-gram embeddings and cross-attention mechanisms within its local encoder and decoder layers. This results in remarkable robustness against input noise; whether text is scrambled with random casing or missing characters, BLT maintains high accuracy where other models fail spectacularly. Furthermore, it demonstrates superior performance in character-level tasks such as grapheme-to-phoneme conversion and low-resource machine translation for languages with scarce training data or unique scripts where tokenizers struggle with long-tail distributions.

The implications for future AI development are profound. By decoupling model growth from inference latency constraints via dynamic patching, we open a new dimension for scaling laws that was previously inaccessible to byte-level approaches without prohibitive costs. While current infrastructure is optimized for token-based workflows, techniques like FlexAttention allow us to bridge this gap efficiently today. As we move forward, this architecture suggests that the path to superintelligence may not lie solely in larger vocabularies or denser attention heads alone, but in smarter ways of organizing computation based on information density itself. For practitioners looking to maximize performance per dollar spent during inference while pushing the boundaries of model intelligence on limited hardware budgets, BLT offers a compelling blueprint for what comes next in generative AI evolution.</p>
