\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How exactly does the 'two-step reasoning' in the prompt prevent spurious correlations compared to standard CoT?
Question:
I am trying to understand the core innovation of the prompt design in FeatLLM. The paper mentions dividing the inference into two steps: first inferring causal relationships based on general knowledge, and second deriving rules based on few-shot examples. Why is this separation necessary? Does this explicitly force the model to ignore features that don't align with its priors before looking at the data, or is it just a heuristic? How does this differ fundamentally from a standard Chain-of-Thought prompt that asks for reasoning in one go?
Answer:
The 'two-step reasoning' mechanism in \textsf{FeatLLM} acts as a structural intervention that separates the activation of the model's parametric prior knowledge from the induction of patterns from the limited empirical data, thereby mitigating spurious correlations that plague standard Chain-of-Thought (CoT) in few-shot regimes. In a standard CoT prompt, the model is typically asked to reason and derive rules simultaneously while viewing the few-shot examples. This creates a cognitive conflict where the model must rely heavily on surface-level statistical associations present in the small dataset to satisfy the immediate request for a solution. Since few-shot data contains noise and coincidental alignments (spurious correlations), a standard CoT approach often latches onto these irrelevant features simply because they appear correlated in the provided examples, especially when the model lacks sufficient evidence to override these superficial patterns with deeper logic.

The innovation in \textsf{FeatLLM} lies in forcing a temporal decoupling of these processes. In Step 1, the prompt explicitly instructs the LLM to infer causal relationships or tendencies based $*$only$*$ on general world knowledge and feature definitions, strictly forbidding reference to the few-shot examples at this stage. This step leverages the vast pre-trained priors of the LLM to establish a "ground truth" regarding what features are logically relevant to the task (e.g., knowing that age is medically relevant to heart failure). By isolating this step, the model filters out features that lack semantic justification in its internal knowledge base before it ever sees the sparse data. Only in Step 2 does the model incorporate the few-shot examples to refine these initial hypotheses into concrete decision rules.

This separation functions as an explicit regularization mechanism rather than a mere heuristic. It prevents the "contamination" of prior knowledge by noisy data signals early in the reasoning chain. If a feature is irrelevant according to general knowledge but happens to correlate perfectly with labels in just five training examples, a standard CoT might treat this correlation as definitive proof of causality due to its reliance on pattern matching within the prompt context. Conversely, \textsf{FeatLLM}'s two-step process requires that any rule derived from data must also be consistent with or at least not contradicted by established domain priors derived in Step 1. The paper validates this empirically: when spurious columns were added to datasets, \textsf{FeatLLM} exhibited significantly less performance degradation compared to baselines because it rarely generated rules based on those irrelevant columns---their lack of alignment with general knowledge caused them to be discarded during Step 1 filtering. Thus, unlike standard CoT which treats all input tokens equally during reasoning, \textsf{FeatLLM}'s architecture imposes an "inductive bias" where valid rules must survive both semantic plausibility checks (Step 1) and empirical verification (Step 2), effectively reducing variance and overfitting inherent in low-data scenarios.

### Clarification on Equation 1: What happens if a rule evaluates to false for all samples?
Question:
Looking at Section 3.2, specifically Equation 1 and the surrounding text, I see that new binary features are generated where $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is 1 if a sample satisfies a rule for class $k$, and 0 otherwise. If an LLM generates a rule that is logically impossible (e.g., 'Age > 150') or simply never true for any sample in the dataset, that column will be all zeros. Since the model learns weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ via cross-entropy loss, will such a useless feature naturally get assigned a weight of zero, or does it cause numerical instability in the optimization? The paper mentions filtering rules with weights below 0.1, but doesn't discuss handling constant-zero features explicitly.
Answer:
The short answer is that while the paper does not explicitly detail a special-case routine for handling columns of all zeros, such features are naturally handled by the optimization dynamics of the linear model combined with the explicit post-processing step mentioned in the "Quality of features" section. According to Equation~1 in Section~3.2, the logit for a class is computed as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$. Here, $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ represents the binary feature vector derived from the LLM-generated rules. If a rule is logically impossible or never satisfied by any sample in the dataset, the corresponding entry in $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ will be zero for every row $i$. Mathematically, this creates a column of zeros in the design matrix. During training via cross-entropy loss, a feature that contributes nothing to distinguishing between classes (because its value is constant across all samples) provides no gradient signal regarding its contribution to reducing error. Consequently, the optimizer (Adam, as noted in Implementation Details) will drive the corresponding weight $w_{k,j}$ toward zero or keep it near zero because changing it offers no improvement to the loss function.

Furthermore, the paper explicitly addresses this scenario through its feature filtering mechanism described in Section~4.2 ("Quality of features generated by FeatLLM"). The authors state that rules with little relevance to the data are "automatically filtered out" after tuning if their learned weights fall below a specific threshold. They specify that this threshold was set at $0.1$. Therefore, even if a constant-zero feature manages to retain a tiny non-zero weight due to numerical noise or regularization effects during training, it will be pruned from the final model during inference because its weight will inevitably remain below this cutoff. This dual mechanism—optimization driving irrelevant weights down and an explicit pruning threshold removing them—ensures that useless features do not cause numerical instability nor degrade performance. The architecture relies on this sparsity induction rather than pre-computation checks for constant columns, which aligns with their goal of keeping the downstream model simple and computationally efficient while leveraging the LLM's generative flexibility.

### Is 'Feature Bagging' here equivalent to Random Forest's bootstrap sampling of rows?
Question:
The paper describes using 'feature bagging' and selecting subsets of features/instances for each trial to mitigate prompt size limits and improve robustness. I am confused about the exact mechanism: Are they resampling rows (instances) like standard bagging in Random Forests, or are they randomly dropping columns (features) from the input prompt sent to the LLM? Or perhaps both? If they drop columns, how does this help if the LLM needs to see all columns to understand context? I need clarification on what constitutes a 'trial' in their ensemble method versus a standard bootstrapping procedure.
Answer:
In the \textsf{FeatLLM} paper, the term "feature bagging" refers specifically to the random selection of a **subset of features (columns)** and/or **instances (rows)** for each trial, rather than strictly adhering to the row-only bootstrap sampling characteristic of standard Random Forests. While standard Random Forests rely exclusively on bagging rows to introduce variance among trees, \textsf{FeatLLM} explicitly adapts this concept to solve the unique constraint of Large Language Model (LLM) context windows. The authors state that when tabular data contains a large number of features—exceeding the allowable input prompt size—bagging is essential to reduce the prompt length by selecting only a subset of features for each specific LLM query. This mechanism allows the system to handle datasets with hundreds of attributes (like `Communities` or `Myocardial`) that would otherwise be impossible to serialize into a single prompt.

The distinction lies in *why* and *how* this sampling occurs. In a standard Random Forest, row bagging creates diversity so that no single tree sees every data point, reducing correlation between trees. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, the primary driver for feature bagging is **mitigating prompt size limits**. By randomly dropping columns (features) in each trial, the LLM receives a manageable input containing only a fraction of the total features, forcing it to reason about relationships within that specific subset. The authors clarify that while an LLM generally benefits from seeing all context, the physical token limit makes seeing everything impossible for high-dimensional tables; thus, feature bagging is a necessary workaround to ensure feasibility. Furthermore, the method also involves sampling subsets of instances (few-shot demonstrations), which introduces another layer of randomness similar to traditional bagging but serves primarily to diversify the few-shot examples seen by the model in each iteration.

This approach differs from standard bootstrapping because it combines two forms of subsampling: selecting a random subset of features and selecting a random subset of training samples for each trial. The ensemble aggregates results from these varied perspectives; even if a specific trial misses critical interactions because those features were dropped, other trials covering different feature combinations can compensate. This aligns with the principle of self-consistency mentioned in the paper: rules inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate. Therefore, "feature bagging" in this context is not just about statistical variance reduction via row resampling but is fundamentally an architectural strategy to bypass token limits while maintaining robustness through ensemble diversity derived from both column and row subsampling.

### Why use a linear model with ReLU-like projection ($\max(\mathbf{w}, 0)$) instead of just counting satisfied rules?
Question:
In Section 3.2, after generating binary features from rules, they fit a linear model without bias but project weights into a positive subspace ($\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$). Why not just sum the number of satisfied rules per class (which seems like what they describe as an initial idea)? Does forcing non-negative weights imply that satisfying *more* rules must always increase probability? What theoretical justification exists for this constraint over allowing negative weights (which would mean satisfying a rule decreases confidence)? Also, why exclude the bias term entirely?
Answer:
The decision to employ a linear model with a ReLU-like projection, $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$, rather than simply summing the number of satisfied rules, stems from the necessity to differentiate the relative importance of distinct rules derived by the LLM. While the authors acknowledge that summing binary features (effectively assuming equal weights of $1$) serves as a valid baseline, they argue that not all generated rules carry the same predictive power. In the context of few-shot learning, where data is scarce, blindly treating every rule equally risks diluting the signal from highly discriminative rules with noise from weaker or less reliable ones. By introducing trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, the framework allows the model to learn a weighted contribution for each feature during optimization, effectively filtering out uninformative rules that receive near-zero weights after training.

The constraint enforcing non-negative weights via $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ carries specific theoretical and practical implications aligned with the generative nature of these features. Since the binary features $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ indicate whether a sample satisfies a condition *specifically* hypothesized to support class $k$, satisfying such a rule intuitively acts as evidence *for* that class rather than against it. Allowing negative weights would imply that satisfying a rule explicitly generated for class $k$ decreases the probability of belonging to class $k$, which contradicts the premise that these rules are "decisive conditions" identified to distinguish classes positively. The projection ensures monotonicity: satisfying more rules associated with a class can only maintain or increase its logit score, never decrease it. This aligns with the interpretation of these features as additive evidence supporting a hypothesis, simplifying the decision boundary in a way that matches the intended logic of rule-based reasoning.

Furthermore, excluding the bias term entirely is a deliberate design choice driven by the structure of the multi-class formulation and the normalization inherent in Softmax. The logit for each class is computed solely based on the interaction between its specific set of positive weights and its corresponding binary features. In this architecture, if no rules are satisfied for any class, all logits become zero before Softmax activation, resulting in uniform probabilities across classes—a neutral starting point appropriate when no evidence supports any specific category. Introducing an explicit bias term might disrupt this symmetry or require careful regularization to prevent dominance by background priors in extremely sparse few-shot settings where most samples satisfy few or none of the generated rules. The authors emphasize that this simple linear projection, coupled with weight tuning on positive subspaces, proves sufficient to capture necessary nuances without overcomplicating the downstream model, especially given that high-quality features are already supplied by the LLM's prior knowledge.

### How does FeatLLM handle conflicting rules generated by different ensemble members?
Question:
Since FeatLLM relies on an ensemble of LLM queries with randomized temperatures and demonstrations, it is inevitable that different trials will generate contradictory rules (e.g., Trial A says 'Rule X implies Class Y', while Trial B says 'Rule X implies NOT Class Y'). How does the final aggregation step resolve these conflicts mathematically? Does it rely solely on the weighted voting scheme derived from training data weights $\mathbf{w}$, or is there an explicit conflict resolution logic before fitting the linear model?
Answer:
The resolution of conflicting rules in \textsf{FeatLLM} is handled implicitly through the linear weighting mechanism of the downstream model rather than through an explicit pre-processing conflict resolution logic or a hard voting scheme among the ensemble members. While the framework employs an ensemble of $T$ trials with randomized temperatures and demonstrations to generate diverse rules, it does not mathematically reconcile contradictions (such as Rule X implying Class Y versus Rule X implying $\neg$Class Y) before feature construction. Instead, each trial independently generates its own set of binary features based on the rules produced in that specific iteration, and these features are treated as distinct inputs to a separate linear model for that trial.

Mathematically, for a specific trial $t$, the system constructs a binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i[t] \in \{0, 1\}^R$ for each class $k$, where each element indicates whether a sample satisfies one of the $R$ rules generated in that trial. If Trial A produces a rule stating "Condition C implies Class Y" and Trial B produces "Condition C implies $\neg$Class Y," these are simply two different binary features in their respective contexts. The model learns a weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k[t]$ for each trial to quantify the importance of these features. Crucially, the paper specifies that the logits are computed using only positive weights via $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$. This projection onto a non-negative subspace ensures that any feature satisfying a rule can only increase (or leave unchanged) the predicted likelihood of its associated class; it never actively suppresses it. Therefore, if conflicting rules exist across different trials, they manifest as competing signals where one trial boosts the score for Class Y while another trial (if it generated a rule for Class Z triggered by Condition C) might boost Class Z.

The actual aggregation occurs at the probability level after individual models have been trained on their respective feature sets. The final prediction is obtained by averaging the class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i[t]$ derived from each of the $T$ independent trials: $\bar{\mathbf{p}}^i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_{1}^i[t], ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i[t]])$. This averaging process acts as a soft consensus mechanism. Rather than forcing a single global truth upon conflicting rules immediately, the ensemble allows the model to weigh evidence probabilistically. If a rule appears consistently across many trials (high self-consistency), its associated features will likely receive high weights during training, reinforcing its signal in the final average probability. Conversely, if contradictory rules appear sporadically or lead to poor performance on training data during the optimization phase, their corresponding weights $\mathbf{w}$ will naturally converge toward lower values or remain negligible because they fail to minimize cross-entropy loss effectively against ground truth labels. Thus, the "conflict resolution" is emergent: it is driven by which rules prove predictive on the few-shot training data when optimized individually per trial and then averaged globally, rather than by any syntactic or logical parser resolving contradictions beforehand.
