\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why use a linear model with ReLU-like projection ($\max(\mathbf{w}, 0)$) instead of just counting satisfied rules?
Question:
In Section 3.2, after generating binary features from rules, they fit a linear model without bias but project weights into a positive subspace ($\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$). Why not just sum the number of satisfied rules per class (which seems like what they describe as an initial idea)? Does forcing non-negative weights imply that satisfying *more* rules must always increase probability? What theoretical justification exists for this constraint over allowing negative weights (which would mean satisfying a rule decreases confidence)? Also, why exclude the bias term entirely?
Answer:
The decision to employ a linear model with a ReLU-like projection, $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$, rather than simply summing the number of satisfied rules, stems from the necessity to differentiate the relative importance of distinct rules derived by the LLM. While the authors acknowledge that summing binary features (effectively assuming equal weights of $1$) serves as a valid baseline, they argue that not all generated rules carry the same predictive power. In the context of few-shot learning, where data is scarce, blindly treating every rule equally risks diluting the signal from highly discriminative rules with noise from weaker or less reliable ones. By introducing trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, the framework allows the model to learn a weighted contribution for each feature during optimization, effectively filtering out uninformative rules that receive near-zero weights after training.

The constraint enforcing non-negative weights via $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ carries specific theoretical and practical implications aligned with the generative nature of these features. Since the binary features $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ indicate whether a sample satisfies a condition *specifically* hypothesized to support class $k$, satisfying such a rule intuitively acts as evidence *for* that class rather than against it. Allowing negative weights would imply that satisfying a rule explicitly generated for class $k$ decreases the probability of belonging to class $k$, which contradicts the premise that these rules are "decisive conditions" identified to distinguish classes positively. The projection ensures monotonicity: satisfying more rules associated with a class can only maintain or increase its logit score, never decrease it. This aligns with the interpretation of these features as additive evidence supporting a hypothesis, simplifying the decision boundary in a way that matches the intended logic of rule-based reasoning.

Furthermore, excluding the bias term entirely is a deliberate design choice driven by the structure of the multi-class formulation and the normalization inherent in Softmax. The logit for each class is computed solely based on the interaction between its specific set of positive weights and its corresponding binary features. In this architecture, if no rules are satisfied for any class, all logits become zero before Softmax activation, resulting in uniform probabilities across classes—a neutral starting point appropriate when no evidence supports any specific category. Introducing an explicit bias term might disrupt this symmetry or require careful regularization to prevent dominance by background priors in extremely sparse few-shot settings where most samples satisfy few or none of the generated rules. The authors emphasize that this simple linear projection, coupled with weight tuning on positive subspaces, proves sufficient to capture necessary nuances without overcomplicating the downstream model, especially given that high-quality features are already supplied by the LLM's prior knowledge.
