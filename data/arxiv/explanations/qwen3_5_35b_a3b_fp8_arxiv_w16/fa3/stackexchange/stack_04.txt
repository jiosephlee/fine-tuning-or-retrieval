\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Does 'block quantization' imply that we lose information about correlations between blocks?
Question:
The paper suggests using block quantization (keeping one scalar per block) instead of per-tensor scaling to reduce numerical error in FP8. Intuitively, per-tensor scaling assumes all elements share a global range, which fails with outliers. Block quantization seems safer locally. However, if I scale each block independently, doesn't this implicitly assume that there are no significant correlations or gradients spanning across block boundaries that need to be preserved accurately during backpropagation? Does this independence assumption hurt convergence in practice?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the potential loss of cross-block correlation information is astute, but in the context of \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, block quantization does not imply a loss of gradient information across boundaries because the quantization occurs strictly on the operands ($\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$) before they enter the attention mechanism, rather than on the resulting attention scores or gradients themselves. The paper clarifies that block quantization involves splitting the input tensors into blocks (e.g., $B_r \times d$ for $\mathbf{Q}$ and $B_c \times d$ for $\mathbf{K}$) and assigning a unique scaling scalar to each block to handle local outliers, contrasting this with per-tensor scaling which forces a single global range for the entire matrix.

Crucially, this independence assumption does not hurt convergence or ignore correlations spanning block boundaries because the fundamental linear algebra operation remains unchanged. When computing the attention score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, if $\mathbf{Q}$ is composed of blocks $\mathbf{Q}_i$ scaled by scalars $s_{q,i}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ consists of blocks $\mathbf{K}_j$ scaled by $s_{k,j}$, the dot product between a specific pair of blocks becomes $(\mathbf{Q}_i s_{q,i}) (\mathbf{K}_j s_{k,j})^\top = s_{q,i} s_{k,j} (\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top)$. The paper notes that these scaling factors can be fused directly into the computation or accounted for at no extra cost, meaning the final value of every element in $\mathbf{S}$ is mathematically equivalent to what it would be if no quantization had occurred (assuming infinite precision in the accumulation). Consequently, any statistical correlation between values residing in adjacent blocks is preserved perfectly in the unquantized domain of the accumulator; the "independence" only applies to how we approximate the *magnitude* of individual blocks to fit them into FP8 format, not to how they interact.

Furthermore, to specifically address your concern about outliers disrupting this process across boundaries, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} employs a complementary technique called **incoherent processing**. Before quantization, both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$. As stated in Section~\ref{sec:algofp8}, since $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$, this transformation preserves the dot products exactly ($( \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$) while effectively mixing all entries together. This ensures that extreme outlier values are "spread out" across many different blocks rather than being concentrated in one specific location. By combining this preprocessing step with block quantization, the method mitigates the risk that a massive outlier confined to a single block would distort that block's scale so severely that it loses fidelity relative to its neighbors. Empirically, this combination reduces numerical error by $2.6\times$ compared to standard per-tensor FP8 baselines without introducing bias or breaking gradient flow during backpropagation. Therefore, while each block is treated independently regarding its scaling factor for storage efficiency, the mathematical integrity of their interactions—and thus their correlations—is rigorously maintained throughout both forward and backward passes.
