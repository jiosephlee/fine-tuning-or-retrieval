# Why Your AI Model is Too Big to Train: Enter Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

The era of massive foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion has revolutionized artificial intelligence, offering capabilities that were once science fiction. However, this progress comes with a steep price tag: the sheer scale of these models. With architectures boasting hundreds of billions of parameters, training them from scratch is now prohibitively expensive, requiring resources available only to a handful of tech giants. Consequently, the industry has shifted toward efficient adaptation strategies, allowing us to repurpose these pre-trained behemoths for specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire system. While methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) have made strides in this area, they often sacrifice the geometric integrity of the model's learned representations. This is where Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) steps in, offering a principled way to adapt large models efficiently by preserving the delicate angular relationships between neurons while drastically cutting down on trainable parameters.

At the heart of traditional fine-tuning lies a fundamental challenge: balancing parameter efficiency with model expressivity. Standard approaches often rely on adding small amounts of noise or low-rank updates to existing weights. While computationally cheap, these methods can distort the internal geometry of the neural network. Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT) was proposed as a more robust alternative. Instead of additive updates, OFT multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix. Mathematically, an orthogonal matrix preserves lengths and angles; in practical terms, this ensures that the relative positions and semantic relationships between neurons remain intact during adaptation. This preservation of pairwise angles helps maintain the knowledge acquired during pre-training while allowing the model to specialize for new tasks. However, standard OFT faces a bottleneck: generating a dense orthogonal matrix for high-dimensional layers requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which negates much of the efficiency gains sought in modern machine learning.

To solve this paradox, researchers reframed the problem through an "information transmission" lens. Imagine trying to send information from one side of a grid to another; if you want every node on one side to communicate with every node on the other (creating a dense connection), you typically need a massive web of wires ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ connections). The breakthrough came by realizing that we don't need direct connections everywhere if we allow information to hop through intermediate stages efficiently. Drawing inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, which uses a recursive "butterfly" structure to mix data efficiently across frequencies, BOFT applies this logic to weight matrices. By decomposing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly topology, BOFT can achieve full connectivity using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This is a significant reduction compared to the quadratic growth required by dense matrices or even the linear-but-blocked approach of earlier methods like block-diagonal OFT.

The mechanics of BOFT involve constructing an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a sequence of sparse transformations rather than a single monolithic block. In this framework, each step acts as a layer in an information highway where data flows between nodes via sparse connections before moving to the next stage. The resulting composite matrix is dense enough to capture complex interactions across all dimensions but parameterized so sparsely that it remains lightweight for training. Unlike LoRA's low-rank approximations which assume data lies on lower-dimensional manifolds, BOFT operates within the full orthogonal group but restricts itself to highly structured subsets defined by butterfly topologies. This structure acts as an implicit prior or inductive bias; just as Fourier transforms naturally handle periodic signals, butterfly structures are adept at handling general linear transformations common in deep networks without overfitting to noisy task-specific data. Furthermore, BOFT maintains spectral stability—the largest singular value of the weight matrix remains unchanged—which contributes to more stable training dynamics and better generalization compared to additive methods that might inadvertently amplify gradients or destabilize activations.

Empirical validation confirms that BOFT is not just theoretically sound but practically superior across diverse domains including Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and Text-to-Image diffusion models. By replacing heavy block-diagonal constraints with flexible butterfly factorizations, BOFT allows for smooth interpolations between identity operations and full orthogonal transformations tailored to specific tasks. Experiments show that BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods while utilizing fewer trainable parameters than standard OFT implementations. Crucially, unlike many optimization tricks that add latency during deployment, BOFT involves no inference overhead because its learned parameters can be fused directly into the original weights after training finishes. This makes it an ideal candidate for deploying powerful foundation models on resource-constrained devices or integrating them into production pipelines where speed and cost are paramount considerations alongside accuracy and reliability.



# From FFT to Finetuning: How Math's Fastest Algorithm Saves Parameters

The era of massive foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion has revolutionized artificial intelligence, but it has also created a bottleneck: training these models from scratch is prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward efficiently adapting these pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks. While methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) have become popular by adding small, low-rank matrices to weights, a newer approach called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers superior stability by preserving the geometric relationships between neurons. However, OFT traditionally struggles with parameter efficiency because maintaining a dense orthogonal matrix requires a quadratic number of parameters relative to the layer size. In this post, we explore how researchers solved this dilemma by borrowing a trick from signal processing: the butterfly structure found in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm.

## The Challenge of Preserving Geometry

To understand why standard finetuning can be tricky, imagine that a pre-trained model contains valuable knowledge encoded in the angles between its neurons. If we simply tweak the weights arbitrarily during fine-tuning, we risk destroying this delicate geometry. Orthogonal Finetuning addresses this by applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the weights. Because orthogonal transformations rotate vectors without changing their lengths or the angles between them, they act as a safeguard against catastrophic forgetting.

Mathematically, if we have a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT modifies the forward pass to compute outputs using $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. Ideally, $\bm{R}$ would be a dense orthogonal matrix capable of mixing information across all dimensions freely. However, a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. For modern layers where $d$ can be thousands or tens of thousands, storing and updating such a matrix defeats the purpose of parameter-efficient finetuning. Previous attempts tried to solve this by forcing $\bm{R}$ into a block-diagonal shape, splitting dimensions into independent groups. While this saved parameters, it imposed an unnatural restriction that prevented information from flowing between those groups, limiting the model's expressiveness.

## Reframing Matrix Construction as Information Transmission

The breakthrough in creating Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) came from viewing the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix not just as algebraic manipulation, but as an **information transmission problem**. Imagine a grid where nodes on one side represent input dimensions and nodes on the other represent output dimensions. A non-zero entry in our matrix represents an edge allowing information to flow between two nodes. To create a dense matrix, every input node must eventually reach every output node through a chain of connections.

If we try to connect everyone directly with one giant step (a single dense matrix), we need too many edges ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). Instead, we can break this journey into multiple steps using intermediate layers of sparse matrices. If we multiply several sparse matrices together—say $\bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$—we can achieve full connectivity even if each individual step only connects neighbors locally. This perspective allows us to ask: what is the most efficient graph topology that connects all inputs to all outputs using the fewest possible edges?

## Borrowing from Signal Processing: The Butterfly Structure

The answer lies in computer science history, specifically in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for computing Discrete Fourier Transforms (DFT). This famous algorithm decomposes an $N$-point DFT into smaller sub-problems recursively. Visually, this process creates a "butterfly" pattern where data pairs interact at each stage before moving to the next level of abstraction. Crucially, this structure ensures that information propagates globally across all $N$ points using only $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ operations rather than $N^2$.

Researchers realized that this same topological efficiency applies to constructing orthogonal matrices. By arranging sparse orthogonal matrices in a butterfly configuration, they could generate a dense orthogonal transformation with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters—a massive reduction compared to the quadratic requirement for dense matrices or the limited expressiveness of block-diagonal ones. In BOFT, each layer consists of small $2 \times 2$ rotations arranged in blocks and permuted according to butterfly logic. When multiplied together over $\log d$ stages, these local interactions mix information thoroughly across all dimensions while keeping the parameter count manageable.

## Why BOFT Matters for Generalization

One might wonder if restricting ourselves to such highly structured matrices hurts performance compared to fully flexible approaches like LoRA or full finetuning. Surprisingly, BOFT often outperforms them on diverse tasks ranging from vision transformers to large language models and diffusion models. The reason lies in **inductive bias**. Just as humans rely on heuristics to navigate complex problems efficiently, neural networks benefit from constraints that guide them toward solutions likely to generalize well rather than memorize noise.

Because butterfly structures underpin many classic linear transforms (like Fourier and Hadamard transforms), BOFT implicitly encodes prior knowledge about how signals should mix across scales and frequencies. This structural prior acts as a regularizer, preventing overfitting while maintaining sufficient flexibility for adaptation experiments show that BOFT maintains excellent spectral properties—the singular values of weights remain stable—which contributes significantly to training stability and convergence speed compared to additive methods like LoRA. Furthermore, unlike LoRA which adds residual updates prone to instability if ranks are mismatched across layers, BOFT performs pure multiplicative rotations that preserve the intrinsic scale of the original model perfectly at initialization and throughout training when properly scaled via dropout techniques adapted for multiplicative updates.

In summary, Orthogonal Butterfly demonstrates that deep learning optimization isn't just about throwing more compute at problems; sometimes it's about finding elegant mathematical shortcuts hidden in decades-old algorithms from signal processing and networking theory!



# Preserving Knowledge: Why Keeping Neurons at Right Angles Matters

When we talk about Large Language Models like ChatGPT or image generators like Stable Diffusion, the sheer scale of their parameters is staggering. Retraining these massive architectures from scratch is simply not feasible for most researchers or organizations. Instead, the industry has shifted toward efficient adaptation techniques—methods that allow us to tweak a pre-trained model for a specific task without touching the vast majority of its weights. While many approaches exist, such as adding small adapter modules or tweaking prompt tokens, they often struggle to balance parameter efficiency with the ability to retain the model's original "knowledge." Enter Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a method that takes a fundamentally different approach by focusing on geometry rather than just magnitude.

The core philosophy behind OFT is rooted in preserving the internal structure of the model. In deep learning, neurons in a layer are essentially vectors, and their relationship to one another—the angle between them—encodes crucial semantic information. Standard fine-tuning methods often update weights freely, which can inadvertently distort these angles and cause the model to "forget" valuable patterns learned during pre-training. OFT addresses this by enforcing a strict rule: the transformation applied to the weights must be an orthogonal matrix. Mathematically, this means that if you imagine the neurons as points in space, rotating them via an orthogonal matrix ensures that the distances and angles between every pair of neurons remain exactly the same. This guarantees that the relational information encoded in the weight matrix is preserved, leading to more stable training and better generalization on downstream tasks.

However, early versions of OFT faced a significant hurdle: parameter efficiency. To enforce orthogonality across a high-dimensional layer with $d$ neurons, one might naively think they need an $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, which contains roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—a prohibitive cost for large models. To mitigate this, initial implementations adopted a block-diagonal structure, breaking the large matrix into smaller independent chunks. While this reduced the parameter count, it came with drawbacks. By forcing independence between blocks, it artificially restricted how information could flow between different parts of a neuron's representation. It was akin to trying to solve a puzzle by cutting it into separate boxes where pieces couldn't interact across boundaries; it saved resources but limited expressivity and introduced unwanted biases regarding which dimensions could influence others.

This brings us to Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a novel advancement proposed in recent research that solves this dilemma through clever mathematics inspired by signal processing algorithms like the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The authors realized that generating a dense orthogonal matrix doesn't strictly require storing all $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ numbers simultaneously if we instead describe it as a sequence of simpler operations. Imagine transmitting information across a grid: if you want every node on one side to eventually reach every node on the other side (creating density), you don't need direct lines everywhere; you just need enough intermediate hops arranged correctly. This is where "butterfly structures," famous for their role in FFT algorithms, shine. By decomposing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices arranged in a butterfly topology, BOFT can achieve full connectivity using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters.

This shift from block-diagonal constraints to butterfly factorization offers profound advantages for modern AI adaptation strategies:

*   **Dense Connectivity:** Unlike block-diagonal methods which isolate groups of dimensions, BOFT allows every part of a neuron's feature vector to interact with every other part through multiple stages of sparse transformations.
*   **Parameter Efficiency:** The reduction from quadratic complexity ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$) to nearly linear-logarithmic complexity ($\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$) makes applying orthogonal constraints viable even for massive models like Vision Transformers and LLMs without bloating memory usage excessively.
*   **Structural Inductive Bias:** The butterfly structure isn't arbitrary; it mirrors patterns found in classic linear transforms like Fourier and Hadamard transforms. This inherent structure acts as an implicit regularizer, guiding the optimization toward solutions that generalize well while preventing overfitting on small datasets.

Beyond just saving parameters, BOFT maintains all the stability benefits of traditional OFT while offering greater flexibility. Because it constructs dense matrices from sparse components, it can approximate any orthogonal transformation more accurately than its block-diagonal predecessor within the same parameter budget. Furthermore, since orthogonality preserves spectral norms (the maximum singular value), BOFT inherits excellent training stability properties compared to additive methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), which can inadvertently amplify gradients and destabilize training dynamics if not carefully tuned.

Empirical studies have validated BOFT across diverse domains, including computer vision tasks involving Vision Transformers and generative modeling with text-to-image diffusion systems. In these tests, BOFT consistently outperformed state-of-the-art parameter-efficient methods by finding better trade-offs between fitting new data and retaining pre-trained knowledge. Whether adapting a language model for translation or refining an image generator for specific styles, BOFT demonstrates that respecting the geometric integrity of neural representations is not just theoretically sound but practically superior for scaling foundation models responsibly.



# The Information Highway: Visualizing Matrix Factorization as Data Transmission

When we talk about adapting massive foundation models like LLMs or image generators to new tasks, the usual suspects are LoRA or adapter layers. But what if we looked at the problem not just as updating numbers, but as moving data across a network? In our recent work, *Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization*, we introduce a fresh perspective: treating the construction of dense orthogonal matrices as an information transmission problem. By visualizing this process through a grid-structured graph, we uncover how "butterfly" topologies can achieve global connectivity with minimal cost, leading to a new method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT).

## From Weight Updates to Network Flows

Traditional fine-tuning often involves tweaking individual weights or adding low-rank perturbations. Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT) takes a different approach. Instead of adding noise to the weights, it applies an orthogonal transformation—a rotation that preserves the angles between neurons—to the entire layer. This ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pre-training remain intact while allowing the model to adapt. However, there is a catch: standard orthogonal matrices are dense and require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a dimension $d$. Even with block-diagonal tricks used in OFT, maintaining expressivity while staying parameter-efficient remains difficult.

To solve this, we stepped back and asked: What does it mean for a matrix to be "dense"? Mathematically, a dense matrix implies that every input dimension influences every output dimension. We can visualize this as an information transmission network with $d$ source nodes and $d$ destination nodes. If an entry in the matrix is zero, information cannot flow between those specific nodes; if it is non-zero, a connection exists. The challenge of generating a dense orthogonal matrix then becomes a network design problem: how do we arrange sparse connections so that every node on the left can eventually reach every node on the right?

By framing this as an information transmission problem on a grid graph, we shift our focus from raw parameter counts to connectivity paths. We need two things: **dense connectivity**, meaning every source must have at least one path to every destination, and **minimum free edges**, meaning we want to use as few trainable connections as possible. This graphical view reveals why simple block-diagonal structures fail—they simply cannot route information from all sources to all destinations simultaneously without losing density.

## The Butterfly Solution

If you've ever seen how data moves in parallel computing or fast algorithms like Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT), you might recognize the solution immediately: the butterfly structure. Originally developed by Cooley and Tukey for efficient signal processing, butterfly networks are famous for their ability to mix local information into global signals efficiently using logarithmic depth.

In our framework, we leverage this topology to factorize a large orthogonal matrix into a product of many small, sparse orthogonal matrices. Instead of storing one giant $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ matrix, BOFT constructs the transformation using roughly $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. Visually, imagine stacking several layers of these sparse "butterfly" gates between your inputs and outputs. Each gate performs a localized mixing operation (like swapping or rotating pairs of values), but when stacked together in the correct recursive order, they create a pathway where information from any single input bit can influence any output bit after traversing just $\log d$ layers.

This isn't just about saving memory; it changes how the model learns. By restricting our search space to these structured butterfly matrices rather than arbitrary dense ones or rigid block diagonals, we introduce an implicit inductive bias toward classic linear transforms like Fourier or Hadamard transforms. This bias acts as a regularizer, helping prevent overfitting while still providing enough flexibility to approximate almost any required transformation for downstream tasks.

## Why BOFT Matters for Foundation Models

The implications of this approach extend far beyond theory. We tested BOFT across diverse domains including Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and Text-to-Image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion. The results were compelling: BOFT consistently outperformed existing state-of-the-art parameter-efficient methods while maintaining lower computational costs during training compared to full fine-tuning approaches that rely on dense matrices.

One unique feature of BOFT is its smooth interpolation capability between regularization and expressivity. Unlike LoRA's additive updates or OFT's block-diagonal constraints which force hard splits in dimensions, BOFT allows for continuous adjustments through its layered factorization. Furthermore, because our method relies on multiplicative updates ($\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$), it naturally preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weights—a property known to stabilize training dynamics significantly better than additive methods like LoRA.

While there is a slight increase in training runtime due to the repeated matrix multiplications inherent in factorization (similar to how FFT takes more operations than direct DFT), this cost vanishes once training is complete since we can merge the learned factors back into the main model weights before deployment. Crucially, inference latency remains identical to standard fine-tuning because no extra tokens or complex architectures are needed at test time.

In summary, by viewing matrix factorization through the lens of information transmission networks, we discovered that butterfly structures offer an elegant middle ground between efficiency and power. They allow us to build dense orthogonal transformations with remarkably few parameters, proving that sometimes the best way forward is not just adding more capacity but designing smarter highways for our data to travel through.
