\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How exactly does the 'information transmission' framework map matrix multiplication to a graph, and why does a zero in the resulting matrix mean 'no edge'? 
Question:
I am trying to grasp the core analogy in Section 3 where the authors treat generating a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. They state that if $R_{ij}$ is zero, information from node $j$ cannot flow to node $i$. However, I am confused about the directionality here.

In standard matrix multiplication $m{R} = m{B}_m m{B}_{m-1} 	imes m{B}_1$, if we view this as layers in a graph, does a non-zero entry in $m{B}_k$ represent an edge from layer $k$ to $k+1$? If so, how does the existence of *multiple* paths through intermediate layers (which might involve zeros) result in a non-zero entry in the final product $m{R}$? Specifically, if I have a path where one link is zero, does that kill the whole path? I need clarification on how the sparsity patterns of individual sparse matrices combine to determine the density of the final matrix.
Answer:
The confusion regarding the mapping of matrix multiplication to a graph stems from viewing the matrices as static objects rather than dynamic channels of information flow. In the "Information Transmission" framework proposed in Section 3, the authors interpret the product of sparse orthogonal matrices $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ as a multi-stage communication process across a grid-structured graph. Here, each matrix $\bm{B}_k$ acts as a connectivity layer connecting nodes at level $k$ to nodes at level $k+1$. Specifically, if we visualize this as a graph with $m+1$ vertical columns (levels) and $d$ horizontal rows (dimensions), the entry $(i, j)$ in matrix $\bm{B}_k$ represents a directed edge from node $j$ in level $k$ to node $i$ in level $k+1$. Consequently, a zero value at position $(i, j)$ strictly signifies that there is **no direct edge** allowing information to flow immediately from node $j$ to node $i$ during that specific step; the path is blocked locally.

However, this local blockage does not necessarily mean that information from node $j$ can never reach node $i$ in the final output. The crucial mechanism lies in how these local constraints accumulate over multiple stages. Even if a specific path contains a zero (a broken link) at one layer, information can still propagate from the initial source to the final destination via alternative routes through intermediate nodes. Mathematically, an entry $(u, v)$ in the final product matrix $\bm{R}$ is computed as the sum of products along all possible paths from source node $v$ to target node $u$:
$$ R_{uv} = \sum_{p_1} B_m(u, p_1) B_{m-1}(p_1, p_2) \cdots B_1(p_{m-1}, v) $$
For $R_{uv}$ to be non-zero (meaning information successfully transmits from input dimension $v$ to output dimension $u$), there must exist **at least one** sequence of indices $(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_{m-1})$ such that every term in that specific chain product is non-zero. If even one link in this specific chain is zero, that particular path contributes nothing to the sum. Yet, if other chains exist where all links are non-zero—perhaps taking a detour through different intermediate nodes—the summation will yield a non-zero result. Thus, a single zero kills only one specific trajectory of information but leaves others intact unless *all* possible trajectories are severed by zeros.

This explains why multiplying several sparse matrices can yield a dense final matrix $\bm{R}$. While each individual matrix $\bm{B}_k$ might have many zeros (representing limited immediate connectivity), their sequential combination creates complex routing possibilities. The authors emphasize that for $\bm{R}$ to be fully dense (allowing every input dimension to influence every output dimension), the collection of sparse matrices must collectively form a connected graph where every starting node has at least one valid path reaching every ending node across all layers. The "Butterfly" structure mentioned later in the paper is essentially an optimal topology designed precisely for this purpose: it ensures dense connectivity between inputs and outputs using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges (trainable parameters) by cleverly arranging these sparse layers so that short-range interactions compound into long-range dependencies without requiring every single entry in every layer to be non-zero. Therefore, sparsity in individual layers serves as an efficiency mechanism (reducing parameters), while the depth of the multiplication ensures global expressivity (dense connectivity).

### Is BOFT($1,b$) strictly equivalent to Block-Diagonal OFT, or are there subtle differences in parameterization? 
Question:
The paper states that BOFT($1,b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. However, I noticed that OFT uses Cayley parameterization ($m{R}=(m{I}+m{Q})(m{I}-m{Q})^{-1}$) on skew-symmetric matrices. Does BOFT apply this same Cayley transform directly to each $b 	imes b$ block within the diagonal structure?

If BOFT is just a product of sparse matrices where $m=1$, isn't it just one big sparse matrix? Why do we need the recursive definition of butterfly components even for $m=1$? Are there any constraints on how the blocks are arranged or initialized differently compared to standard OFT that I missed?
Answer:
The confusion regarding the equivalence of $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ and block-diagonal OFT stems from conflating the *sparsity pattern* of the resulting matrix with the *mechanism* used to enforce orthogonality on that pattern. According to the paper, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ is indeed structurally identical to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, meaning they produce the exact same set of orthogonal matrices and possess the same number of trainable parameters. However, they differ fundamentally in their internal parameterization logic, which explains why the recursive butterfly definition is maintained even when $m=1$.

In standard block-diagonal OFT, as described in the introduction and related work sections, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is explicitly constructed as $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal matrix. To ensure these blocks remain orthogonal during optimization, one applies a direct Cayley transform (or similar parameterization) to each independent $b \times b$ skew-symmetric block. In contrast, BOFT adopts a unified framework based on butterfly factorization. Even when $m=1$, the construction follows the recursive definition of a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. Specifically, for $m=1$, the parameterization involves a single butterfly component where the "factors" are essentially block-diagonal matrices formed by arranging $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks. The paper notes that while the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k>2$ is a permutation of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, for $m=1$, we are looking at $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,b)$ (depending on indexing conventions in Eq~4), which simplifies to a block-diagonal structure. Crucially, BOFT ensures orthogonality by parameterizing these constituent blocks (which happen to be isolated due to sparsity) using Cayley transforms on skew-symmetric matrices just as OFT does. The distinction lies in consistency: BOFT treats every layer's update as a product of these specific butterfly components regardless of depth ($m$), allowing for seamless transitions between sparse ($m=1$) and dense ($m > 1$) regimes without changing the underlying codebase or mathematical formalism.

Regarding your question about why we need a recursive definition if $m=1$ results in one big sparse matrix: this is essential for scalability and generalization within the proposed framework. If one were to hard-code separate logic for "block-diagonal mode," it would break the theoretical continuity provided by the information transmission view presented in Section~3. The recursive structure allows BOFT to interpolate smoothly between a highly constrained subspace (identity-like behavior when parameters are near zero) and rich expressivity (dense connectivity when multiple layers are stacked). Furthermore, initializing all components as identities relies on this uniform structure; initializing a custom "block-diagonal" module might inadvertently violate the symmetry required for smooth interpolation toward dense matrices later in training or hyperparameter search.

Finally, there are no hidden constraints on how blocks are arranged differently compared to standard OFT; rather, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ simply inherits the arrangement imposed by OFT's design choice—grouping dimensions into contiguous blocks—to satisfy the "minimum free edges" criterion for that specific sparsity pattern. The paper emphasizes that while block-diagonal structures introduce an inductive bias by assuming dimensions can be grouped arbitrarily (which may be suboptimal), maintaining this structure as a special case ($m=1$) allows researchers to benchmark against it directly before unlocking the denser connectivity offered by higher values of $m$. Thus, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ is not merely "similar" but mathematically equivalent in outcome to block-diagonal OFT, differing only in that it adheres to a generalized family of transformations designed to eventually yield dense orthogonal matrices through deeper compositions.

### What is the precise mechanism behind 'Multiplicative Dropout' for BOFT compared to standard dropout in LoRA? 
Question:
The paper mentions that standard dropout doesn't work well with BOFT because it uses multiplicative weight updates ($m{z} = (m{R}m{W}^0)^T m{x}$). Instead, they propose 'multiplicative dropout' which randomly picks percentages of butterfly components and diagonal blocks to replace with identity matrices.

My confusion lies in two areas:
1. If we replace a butterfly component with an Identity matrix during training, does this break the orthogonality constraint of the remaining active components locally, or is orthogonality maintained globally across the stochastic mask?
2. Mathematically, if $m{R}$ is supposed to be orthogonal ($m{R}^Tm{R}=m{I}$), replacing one factor $	ilde{m{B}}_i$ with $m{I}$ essentially removes it from the chain. Does this change the effective depth of the network during training steps? How does this differ fundamentally from dropping ranks in LoRA?
Answer:
The core distinction between standard dropout and the proposed multiplicative dropout in BOFT stems from the fundamental difference in how the weight matrix is updated: LOFA relies on additive low-rank updates, whereas BOFT employs a multiplicative orthogonal transformation. Standard dropout functions by scaling activations or setting individual weights to zero probabilistically, which aligns naturally with the additive structure of LoRA where $\Delta W = BA$. If one were to apply standard dropout directly to the multiplicative update in BOFT, such as randomly zeroing out elements within the learned orthogonal factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}$, it would violate the orthogonality constraint required for the transformation to remain valid. Specifically, forcing a continuous orthogonal matrix to contain zero entries arbitrarily would destroy its unitary property, breaking the geometric guarantees that preserve pairwise angles between neurons.

To resolve this while maintaining regularization, BOFT adopts a "structural" masking approach rather than element-wise masking. As described in the paper, multiplicative dropout here operates by stochastically selecting specific butterfly components or diagonal blocks within those components and replacing them entirely with identity matrices during training. This mechanism answers your concern regarding orthogonality: because each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is designed to be an orthogonal matrix, and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is trivially orthogonal, substituting a component with an identity matrix ensures that every term in the product remains orthogonal. Consequently, the resulting masked matrix is simply a product of fewer orthogonal terms (since identity acts as a neutral element in multiplication), meaning the orthogonality constraint is strictly maintained globally across the stochastic mask without requiring complex re-projection or normalization steps after sampling. The "local" orthogonality of active components is never compromised because inactive components are not modified; they are simply bypassed via multiplication by identity.

Regarding the effective depth of the network and comparisons to LoRA, replacing a factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ with $\bm{I}$ does indeed alter the computational graph for that specific training step, effectively removing that stage from the information transmission chain. In the context of BOFT's information transmission view, where layers represent stages of signal propagation through a grid-like graph, skipping a layer means information flows directly from level $i$ to level $i+2$ without intermediate mixing at level $i+1$. This creates a dynamic variation in network depth during training, acting as an ensemble method where different subsets of transformations are active at different times. This differs fundamentally from dropping ranks in LoRA because LoRA's rank drop reduces the capacity of a static low-rank subspace (effectively shrinking a vector space), whereas BOFT's dropout changes the *sequence* of orthogonal rotations applied. In BoFT, you aren't just reducing dimensionality; you are altering the topology of how information propagates through successive sparse permutations and rotations. While LoRA drops dimensions within an additive correction term, BOFT drops entire stages of a multiplicative cascade, preserving the strict orthogonality of every active stage while varying the complexity of the overall transformation path from sample to sample.

### Does Theorem 1 imply that BOFT can theoretically represent ANY orthogonal matrix if we allow sufficient depth and width? 
Question:
In Section 4, Theorem 1 discusses the expressivity of BOFT. It states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Later, it mentions that by composing multiple butterfly matrices (specifically mentioning a kaleidoscope hierarchy structure like $m{B}_{d-1,1}oldsymbol{

This seems contradictory or at least confusing regarding parameter efficiency. If we need roughly $d-1$ stages (where each stage involves logarithmic factors) to cover *all* orthogonal matrices, doesn't that push us back toward $oldsymbol{

Answer:
The short answer is yes, Theorem 1 implies that with sufficient depth, BOFT can theoretically represent the entire orthogonal group, but this universality comes at the cost of returning to a parameter count comparable to the full dense matrix, which contradicts the primary goal of parameter efficiency. The apparent contradiction in your question stems from conflating the conditions required for *universal expressivity* with those required for *practical parameter efficiency*.

According to the paper, specifically in Section 4 under "Expressivity of BOFT," Theorem 1 establishes that a standard BOFT configuration (with a fixed number of stages $m = \log d$ and block size $b=2$) yields a matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. However, the theorem explicitly states that to approximate *all* orthogonal matrices—meaning to cover the entire orthogonal group—one must employ a "kaleidoscope hierarchy." This hierarchy involves composing butterfly matrices in a specific alternating sequence: $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}$. Crucially, the paper notes that achieving this full coverage requires setting $l = d-1$ stages in this generalized composition. Since each stage contributes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters, having $d-1$ stages results in a total parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. Therefore, while deepening the network allows BOFT to eventually span the entire space of orthogonal matrices (matching the expressivity of a dense matrix), it simultaneously erases the parameter savings that made BOFT attractive in the first place.

This clarifies why there is no paradox regarding parameter efficiency: Theorem 1 does not claim that a shallow or moderately deep BOFT (which uses $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters) can represent *every* arbitrary orthogonal matrix exactly. Instead, it claims that such configurations represent a highly expressive *subset* of the orthogonal group that is strictly larger and more flexible than what block-diagonal OFT can achieve with the same block size. The authors emphasize in Section 4 that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning." In fact, they argue that full finetuning (which is universally expressive) often performs poorly due to overfitting. Consequently, practical usage relies entirely on finding an optimal balance within this structured subset rather than attempting to reach universality.

Regarding your question about limits on shallow/middle-depth approximations: Yes, there is a hard limit imposed by the finite depth $m$. A BOFT configuration with $m < \log d$ (or even $m = \log d$ with limited block sizes) cannot represent all possible rotations; it is constrained by its specific butterfly sparsity pattern and permutation structure. However, as shown in their simulations (Figure 4), these structured subsets are surprisingly effective at approximating random dense orthogonal matrices relative to their parameter count. The "limit" here is not necessarily a failure to approximate well for typical downstream tasks, but rather an inability to capture *every* conceivable orthogonal transformation without sacrificing efficiency. The power of BOFT lies precisely in this limitation: by restricting the hypothesis class to structured butterfly products, it introduces an implicit inductive bias that favors generalizable transformations (similar to FFT or DCT) over arbitrary noise-like rotations, thereby preventing overfitting while maintaining sufficient flexibility for adaptation. Thus, Theorem 1 serves as an upper bound proof of capability ("we *can* do anything if we pay enough"), whereas practical application leverages the lower-cost regime where we can do "enough" with far fewer resources.

### Why does preserving spectral norm via Orthogonal Finetuning help stability more than Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)? 
Question:
The paper argues that OFT/BOFT preserves spectral norms better than LoRA because they multiply by an orthogonal matrix rather than adding a low-rank perturbation. I understand mathematically why $oldsymbol{
m ||RU||_2 = ||U||_2}$ for orthogonal R.

However, practically speaking: In LoRA, if we initialize low-rank weights near zero and train them aggressively, don't we risk changing singular values significantly anyway? Conversely, if OFT keeps singular values fixed but rotates directions (preserving pairwise angles), isn't there a risk that we lose capacity to learn features requiring scaling changes (e.g., amplifying weak signals)? Could someone clarify why fixing singular values is considered strictly superior for stability/generalization in large foundation models compared to allowing flexible scaling?
Answer:
The core advantage of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its efficient variant BOFT over Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) lies in the fundamental geometric operation performed on the weight matrix: multiplicative rotation versus additive perturbation. While LoRA updates weights by adding a low-rank term ($\Delta W = BA$), this addition inherently alters the singular values of the resulting matrix unless carefully constrained, whereas OFT applies a rotation via an orthogonal matrix $R$ such that the updated weights become $RW^0$. As noted in the paper's section on spectral properties, if we decompose the pretrained weight matrix as $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$, multiplying by an orthogonal $R$ yields $RW^0 = (RU)\Sigma V^\top$. Crucially, because $RU$ remains an orthogonal matrix, the singular values $\Sigma$ are preserved exactly. This guarantees that the spectral norm—the largest singular value—remains identical to that of the original pretrained model.

Your concern regarding LoRA aggressive training leading to unstable singular values is precisely why OFT offers superior stability. In LoRA, even with small learning rates, the additive nature allows the magnitude of eigenvalues to drift during optimization. If the learning rate is high or training is prolonged, this can inflate or deflate the spectral radius of the layer, potentially causing exploding gradients or vanishing activations deep within large foundation models. The paper highlights that preserving these spectral norms is a proven mechanism for stabilizing training and improving generalization, citing works on hyperspherical energy and spectral normalization. By enforcing orthogonality, OFT acts as a strict regularizer that confines the search space to rotations of the feature space rather than arbitrary deformations of scale. This prevents the model from "forgetting" pre-trained scales while adapting to new tasks.

Regarding your second concern about losing capacity for scaling changes (e.g., amplifying weak signals), it is important to distinguish between *global* signal amplification and *feature-specific* weighting. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons, meaning they rotate directions without stretching or compressing distances uniformly across all inputs. However, this does not render them incapable of learning complex representations; rather, it shifts how they learn. Instead of scaling individual features arbitrarily, OFT learns optimal coordinate systems where existing features are aligned more effectively for the downstream task. The paper argues that foundation models already possess rich representations with appropriate scales learned during pre-training; thus, fine-tuning often requires refining these directions rather than re-calibrating magnitudes globally. Furthermore, BOFT maintains this property while introducing structural priors via butterfly factorization, which allows it to approximate classic linear transforms (like FFT) and interpolate smoothly between identity and dense orthogonal matrices. This structured bias helps prevent overfitting by restricting the hypothesis class to a subset of rotations that share properties with efficient linear algebra operations, thereby enhancing generalization even without explicit scaling adjustments at every step. Ultimately, while LoRA offers flexibility in magnitude modification at the cost of potential instability, OFT/BOFT trades some raw scaling freedom for robustness and consistency in gradient propagation, which empirically leads to better convergence in large-scale settings where stability is paramount.
