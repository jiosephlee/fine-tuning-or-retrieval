\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Is BOFT($1,b$) strictly equivalent to Block-Diagonal OFT, or are there subtle differences in parameterization? 
Question:
The paper states that BOFT($1,b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. However, I noticed that OFT uses Cayley parameterization ($m{R}=(m{I}+m{Q})(m{I}-m{Q})^{-1}$) on skew-symmetric matrices. Does BOFT apply this same Cayley transform directly to each $b 	imes b$ block within the diagonal structure?

If BOFT is just a product of sparse matrices where $m=1$, isn't it just one big sparse matrix? Why do we need the recursive definition of butterfly components even for $m=1$? Are there any constraints on how the blocks are arranged or initialized differently compared to standard OFT that I missed?
Answer:
The confusion regarding the equivalence of $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ and block-diagonal OFT stems from conflating the *sparsity pattern* of the resulting matrix with the *mechanism* used to enforce orthogonality on that pattern. According to the paper, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ is indeed structurally identical to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, meaning they produce the exact same set of orthogonal matrices and possess the same number of trainable parameters. However, they differ fundamentally in their internal parameterization logic, which explains why the recursive butterfly definition is maintained even when $m=1$.

In standard block-diagonal OFT, as described in the introduction and related work sections, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is explicitly constructed as $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal matrix. To ensure these blocks remain orthogonal during optimization, one applies a direct Cayley transform (or similar parameterization) to each independent $b \times b$ skew-symmetric block. In contrast, BOFT adopts a unified framework based on butterfly factorization. Even when $m=1$, the construction follows the recursive definition of a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. Specifically, for $m=1$, the parameterization involves a single butterfly component where the "factors" are essentially block-diagonal matrices formed by arranging $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks. The paper notes that while the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k>2$ is a permutation of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, for $m=1$, we are looking at $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,b)$ (depending on indexing conventions in Eq~4), which simplifies to a block-diagonal structure. Crucially, BOFT ensures orthogonality by parameterizing these constituent blocks (which happen to be isolated due to sparsity) using Cayley transforms on skew-symmetric matrices just as OFT does. The distinction lies in consistency: BOFT treats every layer's update as a product of these specific butterfly components regardless of depth ($m$), allowing for seamless transitions between sparse ($m=1$) and dense ($m > 1$) regimes without changing the underlying codebase or mathematical formalism.

Regarding your question about why we need a recursive definition if $m=1$ results in one big sparse matrix: this is essential for scalability and generalization within the proposed framework. If one were to hard-code separate logic for "block-diagonal mode," it would break the theoretical continuity provided by the information transmission view presented in Section~3. The recursive structure allows BOFT to interpolate smoothly between a highly constrained subspace (identity-like behavior when parameters are near zero) and rich expressivity (dense connectivity when multiple layers are stacked). Furthermore, initializing all components as identities relies on this uniform structure; initializing a custom "block-diagonal" module might inadvertently violate the symmetry required for smooth interpolation toward dense matrices later in training or hyperparameter search.

Finally, there are no hidden constraints on how blocks are arranged differently compared to standard OFT; rather, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ simply inherits the arrangement imposed by OFT's design choice—grouping dimensions into contiguous blocks—to satisfy the "minimum free edges" criterion for that specific sparsity pattern. The paper emphasizes that while block-diagonal structures introduce an inductive bias by assuming dimensions can be grouped arbitrarily (which may be suboptimal), maintaining this structure as a special case ($m=1$) allows researchers to benchmark against it directly before unlocking the denser connectivity offered by higher values of $m$. Thus, $\text{BOFT}(1,b)$ is not merely "similar" but mathematically equivalent in outcome to block-diagonal OFT, differing only in that it adheres to a generalized family of transformations designed to eventually yield dense orthogonal matrices through deeper compositions.
