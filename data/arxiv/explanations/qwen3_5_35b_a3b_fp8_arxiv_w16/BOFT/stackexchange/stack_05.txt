\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does preserving spectral norm via Orthogonal Finetuning help stability more than Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)? 
Question:
The paper argues that OFT/BOFT preserves spectral norms better than LoRA because they multiply by an orthogonal matrix rather than adding a low-rank perturbation. I understand mathematically why $oldsymbol{m ||RU||_2 = ||U||_2}$ for orthogonal R.

However, practically speaking: In LoRA, if we initialize low-rank weights near zero and train them aggressively, don't we risk changing singular values significantly anyway? Conversely, if OFT keeps singular values fixed but rotates directions (preserving pairwise angles), isn't there a risk that we lose capacity to learn features requiring scaling changes (e.g., amplifying weak signals)? Could someone clarify why fixing singular values is considered strictly superior for stability/generalization in large foundation models compared to allowing flexible scaling?
Answer:
The core advantage of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its efficient variant BOFT over Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) lies in the fundamental geometric operation performed on the weight matrix: multiplicative rotation versus additive perturbation. While LoRA updates weights by adding a low-rank term ($\Delta W = BA$), this addition inherently alters the singular values of the resulting matrix unless carefully constrained, whereas OFT applies a rotation via an orthogonal matrix $R$ such that the updated weights become $RW^0$. As noted in the paper's section on spectral properties, if we decompose the pretrained weight matrix as $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$, multiplying by an orthogonal $R$ yields $RW^0 = (RU)\Sigma V^\top$. Crucially, because $RU$ remains an orthogonal matrix, the singular values $\Sigma$ are preserved exactly. This guarantees that the spectral norm—the largest singular value—remains identical to that of the original pretrained model.

Your concern regarding LoRA aggressive training leading to unstable singular values is precisely why OFT offers superior stability. In LoRA, even with small learning rates, the additive nature allows the magnitude of eigenvalues to drift during optimization. If the learning rate is high or training is prolonged, this can inflate or deflate the spectral radius of the layer, potentially causing exploding gradients or vanishing activations deep within large foundation models. The paper highlights that preserving these spectral norms is a proven mechanism for stabilizing training and improving generalization, citing works on hyperspherical energy and spectral normalization. By enforcing orthogonality, OFT acts as a strict regularizer that confines the search space to rotations of the feature space rather than arbitrary deformations of scale. This prevents the model from "forgetting" pre-trained scales while adapting to new tasks.

Regarding your second concern about losing capacity for scaling changes (e.g., amplifying weak signals), it is important to distinguish between *global* signal amplification and *feature-specific* weighting. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons, meaning they rotate directions without stretching or compressing distances uniformly across all inputs. However, this does not render them incapable of learning complex representations; rather, it shifts how they learn. Instead of scaling individual features arbitrarily, OFT learns optimal coordinate systems where existing features are aligned more effectively for the downstream task. The paper argues that foundation models already possess rich representations with appropriate scales learned during pre-training; thus, fine-tuning often requires refining these directions rather than re-calibrating magnitudes globally. Furthermore, BOFT maintains this property while introducing structural priors via butterfly factorization, which allows it to approximate classic linear transforms (like FFT) and interpolate smoothly between identity and dense orthogonal matrices. This structured bias helps prevent overfitting by restricting the hypothesis class to a subset of rotations that share properties with efficient linear algebra operations, thereby enhancing generalization even without explicit scaling adjustments at every step. Ultimately, while LoRA offers flexibility in magnitude modification at the cost of potential instability, OFT/BOFT trades some raw scaling freedom for robustness and consistency in gradient propagation, which empirically leads to better convergence in large-scale settings where stability is paramount.
