\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the precise mechanism behind 'Multiplicative Dropout' for BOFT compared to standard dropout in LoRA? 
Question:
The paper mentions that standard dropout doesn't work well with BOFT because it uses multiplicative weight updates ($m{z} = (m{R}m{W}^0)^T m{x}$). Instead, they propose 'multiplicative dropout' which randomly picks percentages of butterfly components and diagonal blocks to replace with identity matrices.

My confusion lies in two areas:
1. If we replace a butterfly component with an Identity matrix during training, does this break the orthogonality constraint of the remaining active components locally, or is orthogonality maintained globally across the stochastic mask?
2. Mathematically, if $m{R}$ is supposed to be orthogonal ($m{R}^Tm{R}=m{I}$), replacing one factor $	ilde{m{B}}_i$ with $m{I}$ essentially removes it from the chain. Does this change the effective depth of the network during training steps? How does this differ fundamentally from dropping ranks in LoRA?
Answer:
The core distinction between standard dropout and the proposed multiplicative dropout in BOFT stems from the fundamental difference in how the weight matrix is updated: LOFA relies on additive low-rank updates, whereas BOFT employs a multiplicative orthogonal transformation. Standard dropout functions by scaling activations or setting individual weights to zero probabilistically, which aligns naturally with the additive structure of LoRA where $\Delta W = BA$. If one were to apply standard dropout directly to the multiplicative update in BOFT, such as randomly zeroing out elements within the learned orthogonal factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}$, it would violate the orthogonality constraint required for the transformation to remain valid. Specifically, forcing a continuous orthogonal matrix to contain zero entries arbitrarily would destroy its unitary property, breaking the geometric guarantees that preserve pairwise angles between neurons.

To resolve this while maintaining regularization, BOFT adopts a "structural" masking approach rather than element-wise masking. As described in the paper, multiplicative dropout here operates by stochastically selecting specific butterfly components or diagonal blocks within those components and replacing them entirely with identity matrices during training. This mechanism answers your concern regarding orthogonality: because each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is designed to be an orthogonal matrix, and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is trivially orthogonal, substituting a component with an identity matrix ensures that every term in the product remains orthogonal. Consequently, the resulting masked matrix is simply a product of fewer orthogonal terms (since identity acts as a neutral element in multiplication), meaning the orthogonality constraint is strictly maintained globally across the stochastic mask without requiring complex re-projection or normalization steps after sampling. The "local" orthogonality of active components is never compromised because inactive components are not modified; they are simply bypassed via multiplication by identity.

Regarding the effective depth of the network and comparisons to LoRA, replacing a factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ with $\bm{I}$ does indeed alter the computational graph for that specific training step, effectively removing that stage from the information transmission chain. In the context of BOFT's information transmission view, where layers represent stages of signal propagation through a grid-like graph, skipping a layer means information flows directly from level $i$ to level $i+2$ without intermediate mixing at level $i+1$. This creates a dynamic variation in network depth during training, acting as an ensemble method where different subsets of transformations are active at different times. This differs fundamentally from dropping ranks in LoRA because LoRA's rank drop reduces the capacity of a static low-rank subspace (effectively shrinking a vector space), whereas BOFT's dropout changes the *sequence* of orthogonal rotations applied. In BoFT, you aren't just reducing dimensionality; you are altering the topology of how information propagates through successive sparse permutations and rotations. While LoRA drops dimensions within an additive correction term, BOFT drops entire stages of a multiplicative cascade, preserving the strict orthogonality of every active stage while varying the complexity of the overall transformation path from sample to sample.
