# Chapter 1: The Challenge of Adapting Foundation Models

## The Era of Large Foundation Models

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a seismic shift in recent years, driven by the emergence of what are commonly referred to as large foundation models. These architectures, encompassing vast language models like ChatGPT and image generation systems such as Stable Diffusion, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generalization across diverse domains. Their success is not merely incremental; it represents a qualitative leap in performance that was previously thought to be unattainable by smaller models. This surge in capability is inextricably linked to the sheer scale of these models, which now contain billions, and in some cases hundreds of billions, of parameters. For instance, the widely cited GPT-3 model possesses approximately 175 billion parameters. This exponential growth in model size has enabled them to capture complex patterns, linguistic nuances, and visual representations with unprecedented fidelity. Consequently, these foundation models have become ubiquitous tools in research and industry alike, serving as the backbone for a wide array of downstream applications ranging from automated reasoning to creative content generation.

However, this dominance comes with a fundamental characteristic: the immense complexity required to build them. The training process involves feeding massive datasets through deep neural networks optimized over vast computational resources. As noted in the literature surrounding these advancements, the performance gains achieved by scaling up model sizes are substantial but come at a steep price. The community is currently witnessing a transition where the primary bottleneck shifts from algorithmic innovation to resource availability. Researchers and organizations are no longer solely competing on architectural novelty but are constrained by the physical limits of hardware and energy consumption required to train these behemoths from scratch. This reality forces a re-evaluation of how we develop machine learning solutions for specific tasks. Instead of viewing every application as an opportunity to train a new model from initialization, the focus has shifted toward leveraging these pre-existing giants as static knowledge bases that can be adapted with minimal modification.

## Cost Barriers to Training from Scratch

The prohibitive cost associated with training foundation models from scratch presents a formidable barrier to entry for all but the most well-resourced entities. Training a model like GPT-3 or similar large-scale architectures requires not only access to clusters containing thousands of specialized GPUs or TPUs but also weeks or even months of continuous compute time. Beyond the direct financial costs of electricity and cloud infrastructure rental, there are significant environmental implications tied to carbon emissions generated during such intensive training runs. Furthermore, the data requirements are equally staggering; preparing terabytes or petabytes of cleaned text or images adds another layer of logistical complexity and expense. For individual researchers working on niche downstream tasks—such as medical diagnosis support using language models or specialized object detection for autonomous vehicles—the investment required to train a foundational model anew is simply unjustifiable when compared to the marginal gains expected over existing solutions.

Even if one were willing to absorb these costs, the iterative nature of research demands agility that full retraining cannot provide. Scientific discovery often relies on rapid experimentation cycles where hypotheses must be tested against specific datasets quickly. Waiting months for a training run creates a bottleneck that stifles innovation and slows down the pace at which new ideas can be validated against real-world problems. Therefore, the field faces an urgent imperative: we must decouple the development phase from the deployment phase for downstream tasks. The ability to adapt these powerful pre-trained weights efficiently allows researchers to bypass the initial heavy lifting while still benefiting from the rich representations learned during pre-training. This necessity drives the entire field toward developing methodologies that allow for task-specific customization without touching the bulk of the model's parameters or requiring massive computational budgets for optimization loops that span days or weeks.

## Overview of Efficient Adaptation Paradigms

To navigate these constraints without sacrificing performance, researchers have developed three primary paradigms for efficient task adaptation: model finetuning (specifically partial parameter updates), adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each approach offers a distinct mechanism for integrating prior knowledge into new tasks while minimizing trainable parameters and computational overhead. Model finetuning traditionally refers to updating all parameters of a pretrained model using gradient descent on downstream data; however, in its efficient incarnation discussed here, it involves freezing most parameters and only updating a carefully selected subset or applying transformations that maintain structural integrity without expanding memory footprint excessively. Adapter tuning introduces entirely new modules into the network architecture—typically small feed-forward networks inserted between transformer layers—that act as side branches processing information before passing it back into the main stream; these adapters contain trainable weights while leaving the original backbone frozen intact. Prompt tuning takes a different route entirely by appending learnable token embeddings (prefixes) directly onto the input sequence before they enter the transformer encoder or decoder layers; here, only these soft prompts are updated while every parameter within the core model remains static during inference on new tasks.

These approaches share a common goal: preserving as much knowledge as possible from pre-training while allowing sufficient flexibility for specialization on downstream objectives without inflating storage requirements significantly when deploying multiple specialized versions of a single base model across different clients or environments. The choice among them depends heavily on factors such as available GPU memory during training versus inference constraints, desired latency characteristics (since adding adapters increases depth slightly whereas prompts add negligible overhead), and theoretical assumptions about how best to modify internal representations versus external inputs or intermediate activations depending on whether one believes task specificity lies better encoded in latent features (adapters/finetuning) or input contexts (prompts). # Chapter 2: Principles of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

As foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion continue to dominate the landscape of artificial intelligence, the sheer scale of these architectures presents a formidable barrier to entry. Models such as GPT-3 contain approximately 175 billion parameters, rendering the cost of training such systems from scratch prohibitive for almost all researchers and practitioners. Consequently, the field has shifted its focus toward efficiently adapting these massive pre-trained models to specific downstream tasks without the luxury of full retraining. Within this realm of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT), three primary paradigms have emerged: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. While adapters and prompts offer flexibility, model finetuning stands out for its simplicity and lack of inference latency, as it leaves the underlying architecture untouched while modifying a subset of the existing parameters. However, simply updating weights risks eroding the vast amount of knowledge encoded during pre-training. To mitigate this risk, modern approaches prioritize maintaining similarity between the pre-trained and finetuned models. The core philosophy driving Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to preserve not just the magnitude of weights, but more critically, the relational information embedded within the weight matrices—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons.

The fundamental challenge in adapting large models lies in balancing two competing objectives: retaining the general capabilities acquired during pre-training while acquiring sufficient specificity for a new task. Standard fine-tuning strategies often rely on heuristics such as using a small learning rate to ensure minimal deviation from the original weights. While effective in practice, this approach lacks a rigorous theoretical grounding regarding what exactly is being preserved. In contrast, OFT offers a principled framework rooted in geometry. By transforming neurons through an orthogonal matrix, OFT guarantees that the pairwise angles between any two neurons within a layer remain invariant throughout the adaptation process. This geometric preservation ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pre-training are structurally intact, allowing the model to leverage its prior knowledge while shifting its decision boundaries to accommodate new data distributions. This chapter explores these principles in depth, dissecting how OFT achieves parameter efficiency through matrix factorization and identifying its inherent limitations that motivate more advanced structures like butterfly factorization.

## Geometric Preservation in Neural Networks

The efficacy of deep neural networks relies heavily on their ability to represent complex functions through hierarchical feature extraction. At each layer of a transformer or convolutional network, neurons operate as units that project inputs onto specific directions in high-dimensional space. The collective behavior of these neurons defines how information flows through the network and ultimately determines its predictive capability. When a model undergoes fine-tuning on a downstream task, there is an inherent danger that aggressive optimization might distort these internal representations in ways that degrade performance on unseen data or destroy previously acquired skills—a phenomenon often referred to as catastrophic forgetting or representation collapse. Traditional fine-tuning methods treat weight updates as perturbations in Euclidean space, aiming to minimize loss without explicitly considering the geometric integrity of the weight matrix itself.

Orthogonal Finetuning posits that maintaining the relative orientation of neurons is crucial for preserving semantic knowledge. In linear algebra terms, if we view each column vector of a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ as representing a neuron's direction in feature space, then the dot product between any two columns captures their alignment or angle relationship. If we alter these angles arbitrarily during fine-tuning, we disrupt the intrinsic geometry that allowed the model to distinguish between different classes or concepts during pre-training. For instance, if two features were highly correlated (small angle) before fine-tuning due to co-occurrence patterns in natural language images or text changes drastically post-finetuning even if they are unrelated semantically), it implies that the model has lost some structural coherence essential for generalization. Therefore, any robust adaptation strategy should ideally constrain updates to transformations that respect this angular structure.

Orthogonal transformations possess a unique mathematical property: they are isometries with respect to Euclidean norms and inner products. Mathematically speaking, an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, meaning it preserves lengths and angles perfectly under linear mapping ($(\bm{R}\mathbf{u})^\top (\bm{R}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}$). By applying such a transformation to the weight matrix during fine-tuning, OFT ensures that whatever geometric configuration existed between neurons before adaptation remains unchanged afterward. ## Chapter Description

This chapter delves into Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled approach that preserves the geometric relationships between neurons during adaptation. It explains the mathematical motivation behind preserving pairwise angles between neurons to maintain semantic knowledge. The chapter details the mechanism of OFT using multiplicative orthogonal matrices rather than additive updates, covering Cayley parameterization and the initial implementation using block-diagonal structures.</text>

# Chapter 3: An Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

In the realm of modern deep learning, particularly when dealing with massive foundation models like large language models and vision transformers, the challenge of adapting these systems to downstream tasks without prohibitive computational costs has become paramount. While traditional parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods often rely on low-rank approximations or sparse additive updates, a more geometrically principled approach involves preserving the relational structure of the model's internal representations. One such method, Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT), posits that transforming neurons via orthogonal matrices can preserve pairwise angles between features, thereby retaining pre-trained knowledge. However, a critical bottleneck remains: constructing a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ traditionally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, rendering it inefficient for high-dimensional layers. To overcome this limitation, this chapter introduces a transformative perspective: viewing the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix not merely as an algebraic operation, but as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. By reframing matrix factorization through this lens, we uncover the structural requirements necessary to build dense matrices from sparse components efficiently. This conceptual shift serves as the foundation for Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a novel method that leverages butterfly structures to achieve parameter efficiency while maintaining full expressivity.

## Matrix Multiplication as Graph Connectivity

To truly grasp the mechanics of efficient matrix factorization, one must first abandon the purely numerical interpretation of matrix multiplication and adopt a topological viewpoint. Consider a linear layer in a neural network defined by a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In standard operations, an input vector $\bm{x}$ is transformed into an output vector $\bm{z}$ via the relation $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Here, every element $W_{ji}$ in the weight matrix represents a direct channel through which information from the $i$-th feature of the input flows to influence the $j$-th feature of the output. If an entry $W_{ji}$ is zero, it signifies that there is no direct communication pathway between these two specific features; they are disconnected in that specific layer. Conversely, a non-zero entry implies a functional link where information can be transmitted and scaled.

When we seek to fine-tune this layer using an orthogonal transformation, we introduce a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ such that the updated weight becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The forward pass then becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. In this context, $\bm{R}$ acts as a mixer or router that redistributes information among the $d$ dimensions before they interact with the original weights. The fundamental challenge lies in designing $\bm{R}$ such that it is dense—meaning every input dimension eventually influences every output dimension—but parameter-efficient. Algebraically constructing such a matrix directly is expensive because density implies full connectivity ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters).

However, if we decompose $\bm{R}$ into a product of $m$ sparse matrices, say $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm_{B_{m-1}} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, we transform the problem from one of static storage to one of dynamic flow. Each intermediate matrix $\bm{B}_i$ can be interpreted as an adjacency matrix describing connections between two consecutive layers in a multi-stage system. Specifically, if we visualize this process as passing signals through $m+1$ stages (or levels) labeled from $0$ to $m$, each containing $d$ nodes representing the dimensions at that stage, then each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ defines the edges connecting level $i-1$ to level $i$. An element $(\bm{B}_i)_{jk}$ being non-zero indicates that information from node $k$ at level $i-1$ is allowed to propagate to node $j$ at level $i$. Consequently, computing the product $\bm{B}_m \cdots \bm{B}_1$ is equivalent to tracing paths through this layered graph. The resulting composite matrix $\bm{R}$ describes whether there exists *any* valid path from any starting node at level 0 to any ending node at level $m$. If such paths exist for all pairs of nodes $(j,k)$ where $(\bm{R})_{jk} \neq 0$, then information has successfully flowed from input to output across all dimensions. This graphical abstraction allows us to treat sparsity not as a loss of capability but as a controlled routing mechanism where multiple hops replace direct long-range connections.

## Visualizing Factorization as Information Flow

Once we establish that matrix multiplication corresponds to path traversal on a graph, we can visualize complex factorizations by "unrolling" them into explicit grid structures. Imagine a rectangular grid with width equal to the dimension size $d$ and height equal to the number of factor matrices plus one ($m+1$). Each row represents a stage in our information pipeline. At stage 0 (the input), we have our initial signal distribution across $d$ nodes. At stage 1, these signals have passed through filter $\_bm_1$, potentially mixing with neighbors according to its sparsity pattern. This continues until stage $m$, where signals emerge after passing through filter $\_bm_m$. The crucial insight derived from this visualization is that even if individual filters $\_bm_i$ are extremely sparse—perhaps having only one non-zero entry per column—the cumulative effect over multiple stages can create complete connectivity between all inputs and outputs in stage 0 and stage $m$.

Consider a concrete example involving five sparse matrices multiplied together: $\ bm_5\ bm_4\ bm_3\ bm_2\ bm_1 $. In this scenario, we have six levels of nodes (from 0 to 5). If we were to look only at the partial product involving four matrices ($\ bm_4\ bm_3\ bm_2\ bm_1 ), we might observe gaps in connectivity; perhaps node 1 at level 0 cannot reach node 3 at level 4 because no sequence of edges links them directly through those specific intermediate steps. However, introducing just one additional layer ($\ bm_5 ) might bridge these gaps entirely. By carefully arranging which nodes connect across adjacent levels in each step—a process dictated by our choice of sparsity pattern—we can ensure that every node at level 0 finds at least one unique path leading to every single node at level 5. This phenomenon illustrates how depth compensates for sparsity: what looks like isolated islands in early stages becomes fully connected archipelagoes once propagated through sufficient depth.

This visualization also clarifies why simply stacking random sparse matrices is insufficient; their arrangement matters immensely for global connectivity versus local isolation. For instance, if every sparse matrix follows an identical block-diagonal pattern where groups of nodes never mix with outside groups across all layers, then no amount of depth will ever allow information from Group A to reach Group B; they remain forever segregated regardless of how many times you multiply them together (as seen in standard block-diagonal OFT). In contrast, strategies like butterfly structures deliberately permute connections at each step so that neighboring groups swap roles periodically (mixing locally), allowing distant parts of the graph to communicate globally over logarithmic depths relative to dimension size $d$. Thus, visualizing factorization reveals that successful dense construction relies on orchestrating local interactions such that their composition yields global reachability without requiring explicit direct links everywhere simultaneously.

## Desiderata for Efficient Dense Matrices

Having established our framework where dense orthogonal matrices are constructed via products of sparse factors acting as routers on a grid-graph, we must now formalize what constitutes an "optimal" design within this paradigm. Our primary objective remains twofold: achieving **dense connectivity** while minimizing **trainable parameters**. These goals translate directly into topological constraints on our graph representation and drive our search for efficient architectures like butterfly networks or variations thereof discussed later regarding orthogonality constraints themselves impose subtle dependencies among edges beyond mere count metrics alone—they dictate allowable configurations rather than arbitrary choices made freely during optimization processes unless explicitly constrained otherwise via mathematical transformations such as Cayley transforms etcetera...
