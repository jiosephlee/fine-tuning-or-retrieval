\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How exactly does the '1.58' bit count arise from ternary {-1, 0, 1} weights?
Question:
The abstract states that BitNet b1.58 has ternary weights taking values of {-1, 0, 1}, yet calls it a '1.58-bit' model. Intuitively, I know that representing three distinct states requires $	ext{log}_2(3)$ bits per weight, which is approximately 1.5849... However, the paper mentions adding a '0' to the original 1-bit BitNet. Does this mean the original BitNet was strictly binary {-1, 1}? If so, why does introducing the zero state increase the theoretical information density to ~1.58 bits rather than just keeping it at a lower effective bit rate due to sparsity? I want to confirm if this terminology refers purely to the entropy of the distribution or if there's a specific encoding scheme implied.
Answer:
The term "$1.58$-bit" arises directly from the information-theoretic definition of entropy applied to the uniform distribution of the ternary weight set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ described in the paper. When a variable can take on $N$ distinct equiprobable states, the minimum number of bits required to encode it is given by the base-2 logarithm of that count, expressed as $\log_2(N)$. Since BitNet b1.58 utilizes three discrete states for its weights—specifically $-1$, $0$, and $+1$—the calculation becomes $\log_2(3)$. Evaluating this yields approximately $1.58496$, which rounds to the $1.58$ cited in the title and abstract.

This nomenclature distinguishes the model from its predecessor, referred to in the text as "original 1-bit BitNet," which utilized a strict binary set of $\{-1, 1\}$. For a binary system with two equally likely outcomes, the information content is exactly $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit per parameter. The introduction of the third state ($0$) expands the alphabet of possible values from two to three. Crucially, the authors note that this addition is not merely a reduction in density due to sparsity but an intentional expansion of the representation space that enhances modeling capability through "explicit support for feature filtering." While one might intuitively think adding zeros (which represent null contributions) would dilute information density, in this context, it effectively triples the number of distinguishable configurations available to each parameter slot without increasing storage width beyond what is necessary for three symbols.

It is important to clarify that this "bit count" refers strictly to the theoretical entropy or information content per parameter under an assumption of equal probability distribution across the three states. It does not necessarily imply a specific hardware encoding scheme where every weight physically occupies $1.58$ bits on disk or in memory; rather, it describes the efficiency gain relative to standard floating-point representations (like FP16 or BF16). In practical implementations mentioned in the paper, such as using a "2-bit kernel," engineers must allocate enough physical bits (typically 2 bits per weight) to store any combination of three states (since 2 bits can represent four combinations: $00$, $01$, $10$, $11$), leaving one unused pattern if only three are needed. Thus, while the *physical* storage might consume slightly more than $1.58$ bits per weight due to alignment constraints or unused codes, the *effective* information density that determines how much knowledge each parameter carries is mathematically bounded by $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits. This theoretical metric serves as a powerful shorthand for communicating that these models achieve near-binary efficiency while retaining significantly richer expressivity than true binary systems through their ternary nature.

### Clarification on activation scaling ranges: [-Qb, Qb] vs [0, Qb]
Question:
In the section describing Quantization Functions, the authors mention that unlike previous implementations (like standard BitNet), they scale activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ instead of $[0, Q_b]$ to avoid zero-point quantization. My confusion lies in how this affects the subsequent ReLU-like operations within SwiGLU or other non-linearities. If activations are centered around zero without shifting them to positive integers first, doesn't this change how the 'integer-only' matrix multiplication interacts with the gating mechanisms? Specifically, how does an integer-based GEMM handle inputs that include negative values when passed into a SwiGLU layer designed for standard floating point logic?
Answer:
Your concern touches upon a subtle but crucial distinction between the *representation* of numbers and the *arithmetic logic* applied to them, particularly regarding how integer-based General Matrix Multiplications (GEMM) interact with non-linearities like SwiGLU. The key to resolving this confusion lies in understanding that the shift from $[0, Q_b]$ to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ is primarily an optimization for the quantization scheme itself, not a fundamental alteration of how the neural network's mathematics operates during inference.

The paper explicitly states that while standard implementations might scale activations to $[0, Q_b]$ (which necessitates a "zero-point" offset to represent negative values in unsigned integer formats), $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ scales activations directly to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. This choice eliminates the need for zero-point quantization entirely. In traditional signed integer representations (like INT8), values naturally span from negative to positive without needing an offset adjustment; for instance, an 8-bit signed integer ranges from $-128$ to $127$. By keeping the activation range symmetric around zero, the authors ensure that the data stays within a format that maps directly to standard signed integer types available on modern hardware. This simplifies the implementation because you do not need to add or subtract a bias term (the zero-point) before every operation just to align the data range with unsigned storage constraints.

Regarding your specific worry about SwiGLU and negative values: SwiGLU relies on element-wise operations—specifically a sigmoid gate multiplied by a linear projection ($\text{Swish}(x) \cdot x$, or variations thereof involving ReLU-like behaviors). When these layers operate in an "integer-only" environment as described in the paper, they do not execute floating-point logic gates like `if x > 0`. Instead, the "integer-only" claim refers specifically to the heavy lifting of **matrix multiplication**. The paper notes that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ replaces standard `nn.Linear` layers with `BitLinear`, where weights are ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Consequently, matrix multiplication reduces entirely to additions and subtractions (since multiplying by $-1$ is just subtraction, and by $0$ is skipping), which are extremely fast on digital logic circuits.

However, non-linearities like SwiGLU generally require floating-point precision or very careful fixed-point handling because functions like Sigmoid are transcendental and cannot be represented exactly by simple integer addition/subtraction alone. The paper mentions that activations are kept at **8-bit** precision while weights are 1.58-bit (ternary). This implies that while the matrix multiplication step is optimized for pure integer addition via ternary weights, the intermediate activations flowing through SwiGLU likely remain in a signed integer domain (INT8) or a mixed-precision format where sign extension handles negatives natively. When an INT8 vector containing negative values enters a SwiGLU layer implemented on hardware designed for this purpose, the hardware simply treats those bits as signed numbers. The gating mechanism effectively checks signs or applies approximate lookup tables if strict integer arithmetic is enforced for non-linearity as well, but fundamentally, having negative values present in $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ does not break the logic; it merely means the hardware must interpret those bit patterns as signed quantities rather than unsigned ones shifted by a zero-point.

Therefore, the interaction between GEMM and gating mechanisms remains consistent with standard floating-point behavior because the underlying algebraic structure is preserved: adding negative integers behaves exactly like adding negative floats relative to their magnitude. The removal of zero-point quantization simply streamlines this process by ensuring that the raw bit representation corresponds directly to the intended numerical value without extra arithmetic steps for conversion. As noted in the text, this change introduces "negligible effects" on performance while making system-level optimization easier, confirming that the mathematical integrity of operations like SwiGLU is maintained despite operating on signed integers rather than re-centered unsigned integers.

### Is the 'new scaling law' empirical or derived from first principles?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 defines a 'new scaling law' where efficiency gains (latency/memory/energy) grow disproportionately compared to FP16 models as size increases (e.g., 70B is much more efficient relative to its FP counterpart than smaller models). Is this observed behavior simply a consequence of fixed overheads becoming negligible as model size grows (standard asymptotic analysis), or does the removal of multiplications fundamentally alter the exponent in Chinchilla-style scaling laws? I am trying to determine if this implies we need fewer total training tokens for large-scale BitNets compared to LLaMA-sized counterparts to reach similar perplexity.
Answer:
The claim in the paper regarding a "new scaling law" for BitNet b1.58 is primarily an empirical observation driven by the distinct computational characteristics of 1-bit arithmetic, rather than a theoretical derivation from first principles that alters the fundamental exponents of standard Chinchilla scaling laws (which relate performance to parameter count and data volume). The authors explicitly demonstrate that while BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and task performance of FP16 LLaMA models when trained with identical configurations (model size and training tokens), its *efficiency* gains—specifically in latency, memory, and energy—scale super-linearly as the model size increases. This creates a scenario where a larger BitNet model becomes disproportionately more efficient relative to its FP16 counterpart compared to how smaller models behave.

This phenomenon arises because the dominant operation in Transformers, matrix multiplication ($XW$), shifts from requiring complex floating-point multiplications in FP16 models to simple integer additions in BitNet b1.58. In standard asymptotic analysis, one might argue that fixed overheads become negligible for large $N$, but here the change is structural: the computational complexity class effectively changes from involving heavy multiply-add units to pure adders. The paper notes that as the model size grows, the proportion of time spent on $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ layers increases relative to other components like embeddings or normalization. Since these linear layers constitute the vast majority of compute in large models, removing multiplications yields compounding returns. Consequently, a 70B BitNet is not just slightly faster than a 70B LLaMA; it is 4.1 times faster and consumes significantly less energy because the "cost" of adding integers scales differently than multiplying floats under real-world hardware constraints like power limits and memory bandwidth bottlenecks.

Regarding your specific query about whether this implies fewer training tokens are needed: the paper does not suggest that BitNets require fewer tokens to achieve comparable perplexity to LLaMA-sized counterparts. On the contrary, the authors state they train both architectures on exactly **100 billion tokens** for their primary comparisons and find that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting at 3B parameters using those exact same settings. They further validate this by training a 3B BitNet on **2 trillion tokens**, achieving superior performance over existing benchmarks without claiming a reduction in token requirements for convergence. The "new scaling law" described refers strictly to the trade-off curve between model size and *inference cost*, not the learning efficiency curve defined by Chinchilla's $P \propto D^{0.\dots}$ relationship. Essentially, for a target level of performance (perplexity), you can choose a larger model with far less inference cost using BitNets, but you do not necessarily save on training compute or data volume; instead, you gain massive advantages in deployment efficiency as you scale up the parameter count.

### What is the mechanism behind 'feature filtering' provided by zeros?
Question:
The authors state that including 0 in the weights provides explicit support for 'feature filtering,' which improves performance over strict binary (-1/+1) models. Conceptually, I understand that setting a weight to zero effectively turns off a connection (similar to dropout or sparse attention). However, in dense matrix multiplication where every neuron connects to every other neuron via these ternary weights, how does this differ from simply having very small weights in FP models? Why is an exact zero in an integer domain considered qualitatively different for feature selection compared to near-zero floating point numbers?
Answer:
The qualitative difference between an exact zero in the ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ domain of BitNet b1.58 and a near-zero value in a floating-point model lies in the distinction between **structural sparsity** (an active decision to disconnect) and **numerical attenuation** (a passive reduction in signal strength). In standard floating-point models, a weight close to zero still participates fully in the matrix multiplication operation; the hardware must fetch the value from memory, load it into the Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU), and perform a multiplication, regardless of whether the result is $0.0001 \times x$ or $0 \times x$. This means that computationally, a tiny float behaves exactly like any other float: it incurs the same memory bandwidth cost to transfer from DRAM to SRAM and the same energy cost to execute the multiply-add instruction. Consequently, "near-zero" weights do not actually filter features; they merely contribute negligibly to the final sum while still consuming resources.

In contrast, BitNet b1.58 leverages the discrete nature of integers to implement true structural feature filtering. When a weight is explicitly set to $0$, it acts as a hard switch that physically breaks the connection between specific neurons in that layer's linear transformation. From a computational perspective, this allows for significant optimizations: during matrix multiplication involving ternary weights, operations where one operand is zero can be skipped entirely or replaced by simple additions/subtractions of non-zero paths. This transforms the computation from a dense matrix-vector product requiring multiplications ($y = Wx$) into a sparse operation where only connections with $\pm 1$ weights are processed. As noted in the paper, this explicit support for feature filtering enables the model to learn representations where certain input features are completely ignored for specific outputs, rather than just being down-weighted. This capability is what allows BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision performance despite having fewer effective degrees of freedom per parameter, because those degrees of freedom are allocated more efficiently toward meaningful signal propagation rather than noise management via tiny coefficients.

Furthermore, this mechanism aligns with broader concepts of efficiency in deep learning, such as pruning and sparse attention, but differs fundamentally in *when* and *how* it occurs. Traditional pruning often happens after training or requires complex dynamic masking logic during inference that might still involve conditional branching overheads on standard GPUs. BitNet b1.58 integrates this sparsity natively into its data representation; because $0$ is just another valid integer symbol alongside $-1$ and $+1$, modern hardware accelerators designed for this paradigm can exploit bit-level parallelism or specialized integer adders that inherently skip zero-multiplication steps without needing complex control flow checks for every element. Thus, the "filtering" is not just a statistical property of the learned distribution but an architectural feature that reduces both memory bandwidth (by storing fewer non-zero values relative to dense floats) and arithmetic energy (by eliminating unnecessary multiplications), providing a distinct advantage over floating-point models where near-zeros remain computationally expensive liabilities disguised as insignificant signals.
