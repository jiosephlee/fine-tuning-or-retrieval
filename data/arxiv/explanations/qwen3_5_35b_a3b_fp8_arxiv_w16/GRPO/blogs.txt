\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Can Web Scraping Beat Closed-Source Giants? How DeepSeekMath Cracked the Math Code

When discussing the limits of mathematical reasoning in Large Language Models (LLMs), the conversation often defaults to model size. The prevailing wisdom suggested that solving competition-level math problems required massive architectures like Minerva 540B or proprietary black boxes like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. However, the introduction of DeepSeekMath challenges this assumption. By leveraging a meticulously curated dataset of 120 billion tokens derived entirely from public web data, the researchers achieved a 51.7% accuracy on the rigorous MATH benchmark using only a 7-billion parameter model. This performance approaches that of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 without relying on external toolkits or complex voting mechanisms, suggesting that data engineering might be just as critical as scaling parameters.

At the heart of this achievement is the construction of the "DeepSeekMath Corpus." Rather than relying on expensive, niche academic repositories or pre-packaged textbook collections, the team turned to Common Crawl, a vast archive of web pages. They implemented an iterative data selection pipeline to extract high-quality mathematical content from this sea of noise. Starting with a seed corpus called OpenWebMath, they trained a fastText classifier to identify similar mathematical pages within Common Crawl. The process was cyclical: they mined new candidates, refined them through human annotation to diversify the positive examples, retrained the classifier, and repeated the cycle until convergence. This effort resulted in 35.5 million unique web pages totaling 120 billion tokens—nearly seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.

What makes this approach particularly striking is not just the sheer volume of data, but its quality and diversity. The resulting base model, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, outperformed Minerva 540B despite having roughly one-seventy-seventh of its parameters. This finding underscores a crucial lesson: pre-training on high-quality, diverse web data can compensate for smaller model sizes. Furthermore, because the corpus was not restricted to English-only sources but included high-quality non-English content (particularly Chinese), the model demonstrated superior performance on multilingual benchmarks compared to existing English-centric corpora like Proof-Pile-2 or MathPile. Interestingly, their experiments revealed that training exclusively on arXiv papers did not yield noticeable improvements on standard mathematical benchmarks, whereas code pre-training significantly boosted reasoning capabilities both with and without tool use.

Beyond data collection, DeepSeekMath introduces a novel reinforcement learning technique called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Traditional methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) rely on a separate "critic" model to estimate values during training, which doubles memory requirements since it requires storing two large models simultaneously. GRPO eliminates this bottleneck by removing the critic entirely. Instead of comparing individual outputs against a learned value function, GRPO groups multiple outputs generated for the same question and uses their average reward as a baseline to calculate advantages relative to one another. This design significantly reduces memory usage while maintaining optimization efficacy. By applying GRPO to their instruction-tuned model using only a subset of English instruction tuning data, they pushed their MATH score from 46.8% up to 51.7%, demonstrating that efficient RL can unlock significant gains even when starting from a strong foundation.

The implications extend beyond simple benchmark scores; they reshape our understanding of what drives mathematical proficiency in AI. The authors found that reinforcement learning acts less as a fundamental capability booster and more as an amplifier for robustness in output distributions—a phenomenon observed through increased "Majority@K" accuracy rather than "Pass@K." This suggests that RL helps align the model's internal logic with correct reasoning paths rather than teaching new facts from scratch. Combined with their insights into data sourcing and algorithmic efficiency, DeepSeekMath proves that open-source communities can compete with closed giants not by building bigger models first, but by mining public resources smarter and optimizing training dynamics more effectively. As we look toward future developments in LLMs for science and education, this work highlights that the next leap forward may come from better curation of public knowledge rather than simply throwing more compute at larger networks.



# Say Goodbye to Heavy Critics: Meet GRPO, the Lightweight RL Algorithm Taking Over Math Reasoning

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a transformative force in enhancing the mathematical reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). However, the path to optimization has traditionally been paved with significant resource constraints. Standard approaches like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) rely heavily on an "actor-critic" architecture, necessitating the maintenance of a separate value function model that is roughly the same size as the policy model itself. This dual-model requirement creates a substantial memory and computational burden, often limiting access to high-performance RL fine-tuning to organizations with massive GPU clusters. Enter Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a novel algorithm introduced in the DeepSeekMath paper that dismantles this bottleneck by eliminating the critic entirely.

At its core, GRPO reimagines how we estimate baselines for policy updates. In traditional PPO, the advantage of an action is calculated by comparing its reward against a learned value function $V_\psi$, which predicts expected returns for every state. Training this auxiliary value function requires significant overhead and introduces complexity in ensuring accuracy at every token generation step. GRPO simplifies this landscape by adopting a group-relative approach. Instead of learning a global value function, GRPO samples a group of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the current policy for a single prompt $q$. It then calculates the advantage for each token by normalizing the rewards within that specific group—subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. By using the average reward of peers as the baseline, GRPO effectively removes the need for a dedicated critic model while maintaining stable training dynamics.

This architectural shift yields immediate practical benefits without sacrificing performance. The elimination of the value function drastically reduces memory consumption and computational costs, making sophisticated RL fine-tuning feasible on smaller hardware setups. Despite this streamlined design, GRPO delivers exceptional results in mathematical reasoning tasks. When applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which was initially trained via supervised fine-tuning on chain-of-thought data, GRPO propelled performance from 46.8% to 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without requiring any external toolkits or voting techniques. Furthermore, self-consistency checks over 64 samples from the final model push accuracy to 60.9%. These gains were achieved using only a subset of English instruction tuning data, demonstrating that GRPO can extract significant value even from limited datasets while simultaneously optimizing out-of-domain performance on tasks like GSM8K and CMATH.

Beyond mere efficiency, GRPO introduces flexibility in how we structure reinforcement learning objectives. The algorithm supports both outcome supervision and process supervision seamlessly within its unified framework. In outcome supervision mode, rewards are assigned at the end of an output sequence based on correctness; GRPO simply propagates this normalized reward back to all tokens in that trajectory as their advantage. Conversely, for process supervision—which is critical for complex reasoning chains—a process reward model can score individual steps of reasoning. GRPO aggregates these step-wise rewards to compute advantages for each token based on future outcomes in that specific branch of thought. This granularity allows models to learn not just *what* answer is correct, but *how* to arrive there correctly through intermediate logical steps.

The efficacy of GRPO also invites deeper theoretical reflection on why RL improves LLMs so dramatically in this domain. Experiments suggest that RL does not necessarily enhance fundamental capabilities measured by pass rates ($Pass@K$); rather, it makes the output distribution more robust by boosting success rates among top-$K$ candidates ($Maj@K$). Essentially, RL acts as an alignment strategy that resolves misalignments found in standard supervised fine-tuning models, pushing correct reasoning paths toward higher probability mass while suppressing hallucinated or flawed logic paths within a group comparison context. This mechanism explains why models trained with GRPO can outperform much larger competitors despite having fewer parameters and less training data diversity during the RL phase itself.

Looking forward, GRPO represents more than just a memory-saving trick; it establishes a new paradigm for accessible AI development in specialized domains like mathematics and science coding. By unifying various methods such as Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and PPO under a single analytical lens where all are viewed as variations of direct or simplified RL techniques researchers can now focus on refining data sources and reward functions rather than wrestling with infrastructure limits. As we move past simple text generation into complex problem-solving territories where precision is paramount, lightweight algorithms like GRIO will likely become standard tools for democratizing high-level reasoning capabilities across open-source communities worldwide.



# The Surprising Link Between Coding and Calculus: Why Code Training Boosts Math Skills

When we think about teaching a machine to solve complex mathematical problems, our minds often jump straight to feeding it thousands of calculus textbooks or reams of geometric proofs. However, the researchers behind DeepSeekMath discovered a counterintuitive truth: the secret weapon wasn't just more math data, but a heavy dose of code. Their work reveals that initializing a model with code pre-training creates a stronger foundation for mathematical reasoning than starting from scratch with general language data. This finding challenges the traditional assumption that specialized knowledge requires purely specialized training data, suggesting instead that the logical structures inherent in programming are surprisingly transferable to mathematics.

The core of the DeepSeekMath architecture lies in its initialization strategy. Instead of building upon a generic large language model, the team started with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a model already proficient in programming logic. They then continued pre-training this coder-first model using 120 billion tokens derived from web-sourced mathematical content. This approach yielded immediate and dramatic results. The resulting base model, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, achieved a score of 36.2% on the competition-level MATH benchmark and 64.2% on GSM8K, outperforming Minerva 540B—a massive closed-source model built on PaLM—despite having only one-seventieth of its parameters. This performance gap highlights that the quality and type of pre-training matter far more than sheer model scale when it comes to reasoning capabilities.

But why does knowing how to write Python help you solve abstract algebra problems? The authors conducted rigorous ablation studies comparing different training sequences to isolate the variable of code exposure. They found that code training significantly enhances a model's ability to perform mathematical reasoning both with and without external tools like Python interpreters. In two-stage training scenarios where models were first exposed to code tokens and then math tokens, performance on program-aided reasoning saw substantial jumps even before the math training began. More impressively, when they attempted one-stage training by mixing code and math tokens together, they observed that this hybrid approach effectively mitigated "catastrophic forgetting." Catastrophic forgetting occurs when a neural network learns new information so thoroughly that it erases previously acquired skills; by interleaving code data with math data, DeepSeekMath maintained its coding proficiency while simultaneously unlocking advanced mathematical reasoning powers.

This synergy extends beyond just solving equations via code execution. Even in tasks requiring pure textual reasoning without tool use, models initialized with code showed faster convergence and higher final accuracy during subsequent math training phases. The hypothesis is that programming forces models to learn precise syntax, strict logical flow control, and modular decomposition—all cognitive habits that mirror the requirements of rigorous mathematical proofs and step-by-step derivations. Consequently, when these models encountered mathematical symbols later in their training cycle, they didn't see them as mere statistical patterns but as structured logical entities they had already practiced manipulating in a different context.

It is worth noting what *didn't* work as well as this surprising success story involving code. Contrary to popular belief in some circles within the field, simply throwing arXiv papers at a model did not yield noticeable improvements on standard mathematical benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH when used in isolation or as part of an arXiv-heavy mix for smaller scales tested in their experiments. While arXiv papers are dense with formal mathematics, they appeared less effective for improving practical reasoning capabilities compared to the dynamic interplay between natural language descriptions found on the web and the structural rigor enforced by code training protocols. This suggests that for current open-source architectures at reasonable scales (like 7B parameters), the practical application logic found in code might be more valuable for developing reasoning muscles than reading static theoretical papers alone.

Ultimately, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that breaking down silos between disciplines can lead to breakthroughs in AI capabilities. By leveraging public web data through a meticulous selection pipeline and prioritizing code-centric initialization followed by targeted math pre-training, they managed to push open-source models closer to frontier closed systems like GPT-4 without needing billions of extra parameters or expensive tool integrations during inference alone (though tools helped). Their journey confirms that if you want your AI to master calculus, perhaps you should start by teaching it how to write clean Python scripts first; after all, both are fundamentally languages describing relationships between variables and operations waiting to be optimized.
