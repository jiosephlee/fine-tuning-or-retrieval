\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What exactly distinguishes 'online' vs 'offline' data sources in the unified RL paradigm described in the Discussion section?
Question:
In the 'Towards a Unified Paradigm' section, the authors categorize methods like RFT, DPO, Online RFT, and GRPO based on their 'Data Source'. They define 'online sampling' as data from the real-time training policy model and 'offline sampling' as data from the initial SFT model. However, I am confused about the boundary conditions here. Does 'initial SFT model' strictly mean frozen weights from before RL starts, or can it include a model that was just updated in a previous step? Furthermore, since GRPO samples $G$ outputs from $	ilde{	heta}_{old}$ (the old policy), isn't this technically 'online' if $	heta_{old}$ changes every iteration? Why is GRPO grouped with Online RFT rather than Offline RFT/DPO?
Answer:
Based on the "Towards a Unified Paradigm" section in the Discussion, the distinction between 'online' and 'offline' data sources is defined strictly by the temporal relationship between the policy model generating the data and the policy model being updated during a specific training step, rather than by whether the weights were frozen at the very beginning of the entire RL process.

The authors explicitly categorize **Offline Sampling** as data generated by the *initial* SFT model ($\pi_{\text{SFT}}$), meaning all training examples $(q, o)$ are fixed before reinforcement learning begins. Methods like standard RFT and DPO fall into this category because they rely on a static dataset sampled once from this initial checkpoint. Conversely, **Online Sampling** is defined as data drawn from the *real-time training policy model*. In this setup, as soon as the policy model updates its parameters (even slightly), it immediately becomes the generator for new training batches. Consequently, Online RFT and GRPO are grouped together because they both utilize this dynamic feedback loop: GRPO samples $G$ outputs from $\tilde{\theta}_{\text{old}}$ (the current policy at that iteration) to calculate relative advantages. Even though $\theta_{\text{old}}$ changes every iteration, it represents the "current" state of the agent exploring the environment at that moment, satisfying the definition of online sampling where data comes from an evolving policy rather than a static historical snapshot.

This classification highlights a crucial boundary condition: if you freeze your generation policy at step 0 (the SFT weights) and never let it generate new data for training again, you are doing offline sampling. If you allow your generation policy to update continuously and feed its own new outputs back into the optimization loop—regardless of how frequent those updates are—you are doing online sampling. The confusion regarding GRPO stems from interpreting $\theta_{\text{old}}$ as "historical," but in the context of this unified paradigm, $\theta_{\text{old}}$ serves as the immediate predecessor in an online trajectory, making GRPO fundamentally distinct from DPO/RFT which cannot adapt their data distribution as learning progresses.

### How does the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) advantage calculation differ fundamentally from PPO's Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)?

Since all tokens in an output receive the *same* normalized reward scalar for outcome supervision, does GRPO implicitly assume that every token contributes equally to the final correctness? How does this compare to PPO/GAE which assigns different values to intermediate states via $V_
u$? Is there a risk of vanishing gradients for early tokens in long chains of thought if they don't directly influence the final binary correctness?
Answer:
The fundamental difference between GRPO and PPO's GAE lies in how the baseline for the advantage function is estimated, which directly alters the mechanism of temporal credit assignment. In PPO, the advantage $A_t$ relies on a learned value function $V_\psi(s_t)$ to approximate the expected return from state $t$ onwards. This allows PPO to perform granular temporal credit assignment by assigning different advantage values to individual tokens based on their position in the sequence and the predicted value of future states. Conversely, GRPO explicitly discards the critic model ($V_\psi$) to save memory, replacing it with a statistical baseline derived entirely from the group of sampled outputs $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$ generated for a single prompt. As described in the paper, GRPO normalizes the rewards within this group by subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation to compute $\tilde{r}_i$, which serves as the sole source of signal for all tokens in that output under outcome supervision.

Regarding your concern about equal contribution to all tokens: yes, under **outcome supervision**, GRPO implicitly assumes that every token in a specific output contributes equally to that output's final correctness score. The paper confirms that for outcome supervision, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde{r}_i$ is set identically for all $t$ within an output $o_i$. This contrasts sharply with PPO/GAE, where earlier tokens might receive lower or even negative advantages if the value function predicts poor future outcomes despite immediate progress. In GRPO's outcome mode, if an entire chain-of-thought is deemed correct relative to its peers (positive $\tilde{r}_i$), every token receives a positive boost; conversely, if it is incorrect relative to peers (negative $\tilde{r}_i$), every token is penalized equally. This design simplifies training but indeed treats the reasoning path as a monolithic unit rather than decomposing credit step-by-step.

However, the paper explicitly addresses this limitation by introducing **process supervision** as a distinct mechanism within GRPO. For process supervision, a separate process reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps rather than just the final answer. Here, GRDO computes the advantage differently: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{j=t}^{\text{end}} \tilde{r}_i^{(j)}$. This formulation aggregates normalized rewards from all steps occurring at or after token $t$, effectively propagating credit backward through time similar to how GAE works in PPO but without requiring a learned value network. This ensures that early tokens contributing to successful intermediate steps receive appropriate positive advantages proportional to those steps' contributions, mitigating the risk of vanishing gradients for early reasoning stages found in pure outcome supervision.

Therefore, while pure outcome-based GRPO does treat tokens uniformly within an episode—relying on the stochasticity of sampling many groups (the paper notes sampling 64 outputs per question) to statistically reinforce paths that lead to success—it avoids vanishing gradients through two main factors mentioned in the text: first, by using process supervision when fine-grained feedback is available; and second, by leveraging relative comparison across a large group ($G=64$) which creates strong signal variance even without per-token value estimates. The authors argue that this "group-relative" approach aligns well with comparative reward models and significantly reduces resource costs while maintaining high performance on mathematical reasoning tasks where step-wise correctness can be verified via process reward models or inferred through high-frequency sampling of correct vs. incorrect trajectories.

### Can anyone explain the intuition behind why training on arXiv papers seemed ineffective compared to Common Crawl math data?
Question:
The paper makes a striking claim that training on arXiv papers (like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama) resulted in 'no notable improvements or even deterioration' on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. Given that arXiv is traditionally considered gold-standard for mathematical reasoning (as seen in Minerva), I am struggling to reconcile this finding with my prior knowledge. The authors suggest it might be due to noise or format issues (LaTeX macros), but I suspect there might be deeper structural reasons. Could it be that arXiv papers are too dense/formal and lack the step-by-step explanatory structure found in Common Crawl forums/stacks? Or perhaps the distribution shift between formal theorem statements and solvable arithmetic problems is too large? What specific characteristics of Common Crawl math data made them more suitable for pre-training than formal academic papers?
Answer:
The observation that training on arXiv papers yielded negligible or even detrimental results compared to Common Crawl data stems from a combination of data density, formatting artifacts, and the specific nature of the reasoning tasks targeted by benchmarks like $\text{GSM8K}$ and $\text{MATH}$. According to the DeepSeekMath paper, while arXiv is traditionally viewed as a gold standard for formal mathematics, the corpus suffers from significant structural issues when processed for pre-training large language models. Specifically, the authors note that arXiv documents are heavily laden with LaTeX macros, preambles, comments, and bibliographic references. Even after aggressive cleaning—such as removing these elements as done in the ArXiv-RedPajama pipeline—the remaining text retains a highly dense, formal style that differs fundamentally from the pedagogical structure required for solving standard math problems.

The core intuition lies in the mismatch between the *presentation* of mathematics in arXiv papers versus the *process* of mathematical reasoning captured in Common Crawl forums. Academic papers prioritize conciseness, rigor, and theorem-proving logic, often omitting the step-by-step explanatory narratives that define successful problem-solving on benchmarks like $\text{GSM8K}$ (grade school math) or $\text{MATH}$ (competition level). In contrast, Common Crawl contains vast amounts of user-generated content from platforms like Stack Exchange and MathOverflow. This data naturally includes "chain-of-thought" explanations where users break down complex problems into manageable steps, discuss alternative approaches, and explicitly articulate their reasoning process. The DeepSeekMath authors hypothesize that this "step-by-step" explanatory structure is critical for teaching a model how to solve novel problems via self-generation. Formal theorem statements in arXiv papers represent static truths rather than dynamic problem-solving trajectories; consequently, training on them may fail to teach the model the procedural fluency needed for benchmarks that test reasoning chains rather than just fact retrieval or formal verification.

Furthermore, there is a distinct distribution shift between formal academic text and solvable arithmetic problems. Benchmarks like $\text{GSM8K}$ involve word problems requiring translation from natural language into mathematical operations. The language style in Common Crawl closely mirrors this requirement, providing abundant examples of translating narrative descriptions into equations. Conversely, arXiv papers often assume a high degree of background knowledge and use specialized notation without defining terms or explaining the motivation behind derivations. This creates a domain gap where the model learns to recognize complex symbols but fails to generalize to solving open-ended word problems. The paper explicitly states that despite trying different processing pipelines on arXiv corpora ($\text{MathPile}$ and $\text{ArXiv-RedPajama}$), models showed no notable improvements or even deteriorated on these benchmarks. This suggests that simply having access to "correct" mathematical facts is insufficient; the data must also encode the *reasoning process*. The success of Common Crawl highlights that quantity coupled with high-quality instructional scaffolding (the explanatory text found in forums) outweighs the sheer density of formal truth found in academic literature for this specific task.

It is important to contextualize these findings within the paper's broader conclusions: this inefficiency applies specifically to *mathematical reasoning* benchmarks tested in this study ($\text{GSM8K}$, $\text{MATH}$). The authors caution that arXiv might still be valuable for other tasks not covered here, such as informalizing formal proofs or handling very large-scale models where capacity might allow absorption of sparse patterns differently. They also acknowledge limitations regarding whether these benefits would manifest at larger scales or when combined with other data types. However, for building a $7\text{B}$ parameter model optimized for step-by-step reasoning via chain-of-thought prompting, the empirical evidence strongly favors the diverse, explanatory richness of filtered Common Crawl over the rigid formality of arXiv preprints.

### Is DeepSeekMath's improvement driven by actual capability gain or simply increased confidence/reliability (Maj@K vs Pass@K)?
Question:
In the discussion about why RL works, the authors state: 'RL enhances Maj@K's performance but not Pass@K... it seems that the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.' This sounds counter-intuitive. If Pass@1 remains unchanged but Maj@64 increases drastically, doesn't that imply we are just getting lucky with sampling more often rather than making each individual generation better? How does this relate to their observation about 'misalignment'? If RL only shifts probability mass towards correct answers without changing Perplexity on those answers significantly, how should we interpret claims of 'improved mathematical reasoning capability'? Is this merely a statistical artifact of ensemble methods disguised as algorithmic progress?
Answer:
The apparent paradox you describe—where Reinforcement Learning (RL) dramatically increases $\text{Maj}@K$ without affecting $\text{Pass}@1$—is actually the central finding of the DeepSeekMath paper regarding the nature of "capability" versus "sampling stability." The authors explicitly attribute this phenomenon to a shift in the model's probability distribution rather than a fundamental increase in its underlying knowledge or reasoning logic. When the paper states that RL enhances $\text{Maj}@K$ but not $\text{Pass}@K$, it implies that the model has not necessarily become better at generating *the* correct solution on its very first try ($\text{Pass}@1$); instead, it has reduced the variance in its outputs, making the correct answer appear more frequently within a top-$K$ sample set.

This distinction resolves your concern about statistical artifacts. The authors suggest that before RL, the model might possess the necessary latent capability to solve a problem correctly, but its internal representation is "misaligned," causing it to assign low probability to the correct reasoning path or get stuck in plausible-sounding but incorrect loops. In this misaligned state, a single sample ($\text{Pass}@1$) is unlikely to hit the correct trajectory, but if you draw enough samples (increasing $K$), you eventually stumble upon the right path by chance. This explains why $\text{Pass}@1$ remains flat while $\text{Maj}@K$ skyrockets: RL does not teach the model entirely new facts or logical steps it didn't know; rather, it acts as a calibration mechanism that pushes probability mass toward those previously under-represented correct paths and suppresses confident errors.

Regarding your question about perplexity and "fundamental capability," the paper notes that RL effectively reweights the output distribution without significantly altering the perplexity on the correct answers themselves. This supports the interpretation that RL is refining *reliability* rather than inventing new *competence*. The improvement is genuine in terms of utility—if you ask for 64 attempts, you are far more likely to get a correct answer—but it stops short of claiming that every single generation is inherently smarter. The "misalignment" mentioned refers precisely to this gap between what the model knows (latent capability) and what it consistently produces (observed behavior). By optimizing for group-relative rewards via GRPO, DeepSeekMath narrows this gap, ensuring that when a correct thought pattern exists in its weights, it becomes statistically dominant enough to be captured reliably even with modest sampling budgets.

Therefore, viewing this purely as a statistical artifact of ensemble methods would miss the nuance of what changed: RL transformed a sparse distribution of correct answers into a dense cluster. While $\text{Pass}@1$ measures raw generative power on a single shot, $\text{Maj}@K$ measures robustness and consistency. The DeepSeekMath authors argue that for practical applications involving complex reasoning, robustness is often more valuable than raw single-shot accuracy because it reduces reliance on brute-force sampling while maintaining high success rates. Consequently, claims of "improved mathematical reasoning" should be interpreted as improved *consistency* in accessing existing knowledge structures, effectively turning sporadic flashes of insight into dependable patterns of thought through reinforcement signals derived from group performance rather than individual token likelihoods alone.

Specifically, does placing it outside prevent it from interfering with variance reduction mechanisms inherent in GAE or group-relative normalization? Answer:
The shift from adding the KL penalty inside the reward function in PPO to adding it directly to the loss objective in GRPO is driven by the fundamental architectural difference between the two algorithms: GRPO eliminates the need for a separate value function (critic) entirely. In standard PPO, the advantage term $A_t$ is computed as $r_t - V(s_t)$, where $V(s_t)$ is a learned baseline. If the KL penalty were kept inside the reward term ($r_t$), it would become part of this advantage calculation. Since GRPO replaces the learned value function with a simple statistical baseline derived from the group of sampled outputs (specifically, normalizing rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation), keeping a token-level KL penalty inside this simplified advantage calculation would introduce significant noise and inconsistency. The group-relative normalization assumes a scalar or sequence-level reward structure; injecting a dense, token-wise variable like $\log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ directly into the numerator of a relative advantage estimator would distort the variance reduction properties intended by GAE or its group-relative equivalent.

By moving the KL regularization term outside of the advantage calculation and appending it directly to the final objective function as seen in Equation 3 of the paper, GRPO ensures that the gradient updates for policy optimization remain decoupled from the mechanics of variance reduction via group normalization. This separation allows $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ to purely reflect the relative standing of an output within its generated group (the "signal"), while the KL term acts as a global constraint on how far the new policy drifts from the reference model (the "regularizer"). This design choice prevents the KL penalty from interfering with the stability mechanisms inherent in calculating advantages relative to a static group baseline. Instead of competing with or corrupting the benefit estimates used to determine which parts of a generation are good or bad, the KL penalty simply pulls all probabilities toward the reference distribution uniformly across all tokens.

Regarding your question on implementation simplicity versus convergence properties, this architectural choice serves both purposes but primarily targets resource efficiency without sacrificing theoretical soundness. Implementing PPO requires maintaining and updating a massive critic network alongside every roll-out, which doubles memory usage and introduces instability if value predictions are inaccurate—a known issue when rewards are sparse (e.g., only at the end of a long math solution). GRPO removes this bottleneck entirely. The decision to place KL outside allows them to use an unbiased estimator involving ratios like $\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi} - \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi} - 1$, which avoids numerical instabilities that might arise if this term were embedded deep within a complex advantage computation loop. Consequently, while PPO relies on accurate value estimation to guide learning through advantage scaling, GRPO relies on accurate relative ranking within groups plus explicit regularization. This alters convergence dynamics slightly by making learning more dependent on immediate group feedback rather than bootstrapped value estimates, but it ultimately leads to faster convergence in terms of wall-clock time and memory usage for large-scale mathematical reasoning tasks where rewards are naturally binary or coarse-grained.
