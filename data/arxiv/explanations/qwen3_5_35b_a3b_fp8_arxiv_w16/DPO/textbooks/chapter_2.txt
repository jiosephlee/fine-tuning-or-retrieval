### Formal Definition of Preference Data: Chosen ($y_w$) vs. Rejected ($y_l$) Outputs

The fundamental premise of aligning large language models with human values rests upon the availability of comparative data rather than absolute scores. In the realm of unsupervised language modeling, a system generates text based solely on statistical patterns learned from vast corpora, lacking inherent mechanisms to distinguish between "good" and "bad" output in the context of specific human goals. To address this, researchers curate datasets consisting of structured comparisons. Formally, let $x$ denote a prompt or input condition provided to the model. The model generates candidate responses, which are then evaluated by human annotators or sophisticated reward proxies. Instead of asking humans to assign a continuous score ranging from 0 to 100 to every possible response—a task fraught with inconsistency and cognitive load—humans are asked to select the superior option between two distinct completions generated by an initial model.

Let us denote a completed response as $y$. When presented with two distinct candidates, $y_1$ and $y_2$, derived from the same prompt $x$, a human evaluator expresses a strict preference for one over the other. If $y_1$ is deemed preferable to $y_2$, we record this observation as $y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x$. Conversely, if $y_2$ is preferred, we observe $y_2 \succ y_1 \mid x$. In the context of algorithmic training pipelines described in recent literature, these observations are standardized into pairs labeled as winning and losing completions. Let $y_w$ represent the "chosen" or "winning" completion—the response favored by the human—and let $y_l$ represent the "rejected" or "losing" completion—the less favorable response among the pair. The triplet $(x, y_w, y_l)$ constitutes a single data point in what is termed a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$. Mathematically, this dataset is constructed as a collection of such triplets indexed by $i$:
$$
\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N
$$
where $N$ is the total number of comparison pairs collected. It is crucial to note that this formulation assumes transitivity in preferences for theoretical consistency, though real-world data may exhibit noise or contradictions which act as regularization during optimization.

The generation process for creating these datasets typically involves an intermediate model acting as a proposer. Often referred to as a Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model and denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, this model is first trained on high-quality demonstration data relevant to the target domain (e.g., dialogue or summarization). Once established, $\pi^\text{SFT}$ serves as a generator that proposes diverse responses for various prompts. By prompting $\pi^\text{SFT}$ repeatedly with inputs drawn from a distribution $\mathcal{D}_x$, we can sample pairs of completions $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These raw generations are then subjected to human annotation protocols where labelers determine which completion better adheres to criteria such as helpfulness, honesty, or harmlessness. The resulting dataset $\mathcal{D}$ captures not merely *what* humans prefer in isolation but *how* they rank alternatives relative to one another given identical contexts. This relational structure is vital because it isolates subtle differences in quality that might be obscured by absolute scoring scales influenced by subjective biases regarding difficulty or style. Furthermore, relying on pairwise comparisons mitigates issues associated with calibration errors in rating scales; humans are generally more reliable at distinguishing which of two options is better than assigning precise numerical values across thousands of instances. Consequently, modern alignment methodologies treat $\mathcal{D}$ not just as training data but as an empirical approximation of an underlying stochastic process governing human judgment distributions.

### The Bradley-Terry Model for Pairwise Comparisons

Having formally defined the observed preference data through winning and losing tokens $(x, y_w, y_l)$, we require a probabilistic framework to link these discrete observations to continuous latent variables representing value or utility. Without such a bridge between observable choices and internal reward signals, optimization becomes impossible because gradients cannot flow back through categorical decisions naturally unless modeled stochastically. The standard approach adopted throughout contemporary research utilizes the Bradley-Terry model (often abbreviated as BT), originally proposed for analyzing sports rankings but now ubiquitous in machine learning for preference modeling due to its mathematical tractability and intuitive interpretation.

Underlying any preference learning algorithm is the hypothesis that there exists an unobserved "true" reward function, denoted here as $r^*(x, y)$, which assigns a scalar value reflecting how much humanity desires outcome $y$ given prompt $x$. Human preferences are assumed not to be deterministic functions of this hidden reward alone but rather noisy manifestations governed by probabilities derived from it. Specifically, the Bradley-Terry model posits that the probability that response $y_a$ is preferred over response $y_b$ given context $x$ depends exclusively on the exponential transformation of their respective rewards divided by their sum against competing outcomes in that specific comparison set—in our case restricted to pairs for simplicity:
$$
p^*(y_a \succ y_b \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r^*(x,y_a))}{\exp(r^*(x,y_a)) + \exp(r^*(x,y_b))}
$$
Applying this formula specifically to our notation where one item wins ($w$) and one loses ($l$), we arrive at the central equation describing human preference dynamics:
$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r^*(x,y_w))}{\exp(r^*(x,y_w)) + \exp(r^*(x,y_l))}
$$
By dividing numerator and denominator by $\exp(r^*(x,y_l))$, this expression simplifies elegantly into a sigmoidal form involving only the difference between rewards:
$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\Big(r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l)\Big) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-[r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l)])}
$$
Here $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function mapping real numbers onto $(0, 1)$. This reformulation reveals profound insights about how preferences behave mathematically: they depend entirely on *relative* advantage rather than absolute magnitude. Adding any constant offset independent of actions (but potentially dependent on context) affects both terms equally inside subtraction operations within exponents; consequently such offsets cancel out completely leaving decision boundaries invariant under additive transformations conditioned solely on input sequences $x$.
