Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: The Alignment Challenge and the Limitations of Current Methods

## The Gap Between Capability and Controllability in Large Language Models

The advent of large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) marked a transformative era in artificial intelligence, granting machines the capacity to absorb broad world knowledge and develop rudimentary reasoning skills through exposure to massive datasets. Models such as PaLM, LLaMA, and others have demonstrated remarkable versatility, capable of completing texts, answering questions, and generating creative content with a fluency that mimics human output. However, a critical disconnect exists between what these models *know* and how they *behave*. Because these models are trained entirely on data generated by humans with diverse goals, priorities, and skill levels, they inherit the full spectrum of human variability. They learn to recognize patterns associated with high-quality expertise alongside those linked to errors, biases, misconceptions, or harmful behavior. Consequently, while a model may possess the capability to identify common programming mistakes or understand widespread misconceptions about scientific facts, it lacks the intrinsic mechanism to selectively suppress undesirable outputs while amplifying desirable ones.

Selecting specific desired responses from the vast array of possibilities encoded within a model's weights is essential for constructing AI systems that are safe, reliable, and controllable. Without intervention, a language model might inadvertently validate false beliefs held by half the population simply because that belief appears frequently in its training corpus. Similarly, an AI coding assistant might understand syntax errors perfectly yet fail to prioritize writing clean, secure code unless explicitly guided to do so. The fundamental challenge lies in steering the model's behavior away from merely imitating the statistical distribution of its training data—which includes noise and imperfections—and towards adhering to specific human values such as helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness. This requirement transforms the problem from one of pure pattern matching to one of behavioral alignment. Achieving precise control over these behaviors is notoriously difficult precisely because the underlying training process is completely unsupervised regarding human intent; the model knows nothing about what constitutes a "good" response versus a "bad" one unless external mechanisms intervene after pre-training.

## Why Supervised Fine-Tuning Alone Is Insufficient for Alignment

One intuitive approach to addressing this misalignment is Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where researchers curate datasets consisting of ideal inputs paired with perfect outputs—expert demonstrations—to teach the model how to behave correctly. While this method effectively narrows the model's focus toward high-quality responses for specific tasks like summarization or dialogue formatting, it faces severe limitations when applied broadly across complex domains requiring nuanced judgment. Collecting sufficient numbers of high-quality expert demonstrations for every conceivable scenario is prohibitively expensive and labor-intensive. Furthermore, relying solely on demonstrations assumes that experts always know exactly what output is required before seeing alternatives; however, in many cases involving subjective criteria like tone, style, or ethical nuance, determining superiority is difficult without comparison. Humans are often much better at judging which of two options is preferable rather than generating a flawless option from scratch every time.

Moreover, SFT struggles to capture relative preferences that define safety and alignment boundaries. If a dataset contains only "correct" answers derived from expert sources but fails to include examples of "incorrect" or "harmful" answers labeled as such during training pressure can be lost on distinguishing subtle distinctions between acceptable variations and unacceptable deviations. More critically, SFT treats all provided examples equally regardless of their quality relative to other potential outcomes not seen during training. It cannot inherently enforce constraints against generating harmful content if that content was never explicitly penalized in the dataset; it simply learns to replicate whatever distribution exists in the fine-tuning data. Therefore, while SFT serves as a necessary foundational step—often acting as an initialization point for more advanced techniques—it is fundamentally incapable of solving the broader alignment problem where we must actively shape behavior based on comparative human judgments rather than absolute demonstrations of perfection.

## Overview of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: SFT, Reward Modeling, and Policy Optimization

Given the insufficiency of purely supervised approaches for capturing complex human preferences regarding relative quality and safety standards today's dominant methodology has become Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This sophisticated pipeline operates in distinct stages designed first to ground the model in relevant domain knowledge via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), then quantify human preferences through explicit labeling followed by iterative optimization using reinforcement learning principles. Initially an unsupervised pre-trained language model undergoes SFT on high-quality demonstration data tailored for downstream tasks such as conversation or summarization yielding an intermediate policy denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This ensures that upon entering preference learning phases the model already possesses basic competence relevant to target scenarios reducing noise caused by hallucinations or irrelevant completions during early evaluation cycles.

Following SFT comes the pivotal reward modeling phase wherein pairs of responses $(y_1 y_2)$ generated by $\pi^\text{SFT}$ for identical prompts $x$ are presented to human annotators who indicate preference relationships typically expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$ indicating that response $y_w$ was preferred over $y_l$. These pairwise comparisons serve as proxies for an underlying latent reward function $r^*(x,y)$ reflecting true human utility although this function itself remains inaccessible directly Instead researchers approximate it using parametric networks $r_\phi(x,y)$ trained via Maximum Likelihood Estimation under probabilistic frameworks like Bradley-Terry models which posit that probability distributions over choices depend exponentially on differences between candidate scores specifically formulated as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x,y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x,y_1)\right)+\exp\left(r^*(x,y_2)\right)}$.
