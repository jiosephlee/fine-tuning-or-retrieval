\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Where exactly does the partition function $Z(x)$ disappear in the DPO derivation?
Question:
I am trying to follow the derivation from Equation 1 (the optimal policy formula) to Equation 4 (the final DPO loss). The paper says 'the partition function cancels' when substituting the reparameterized reward into the Bradley-Terry model. However, looking at the math, $Z(x)$ appears inside a logarithm in the numerator and denominator separately before becoming a difference. Could someone walk me through the specific algebraic step where $\log Z(x)$ vanishes? I feel like I'm missing a basic property of logarithms or the structure of the preference equation.
Answer:
The cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ occurs specifically because the Bradley-Terry preference model relies exclusively on the *difference* between reward values for two outcomes, rendering any additive term that depends solely on the input $x$ irrelevant to the probability calculation. To see this clearly, let us look at the algebraic substitution step described in the paper.

First, recall the reparameterized reward derived in Equation 4 of your question (Equation 2.2 in the paper):
$$ r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) $$
Here, $\beta \log Z(x)$ is a term that depends entirely on the prompt $x$ and is constant regardless of which specific response $y$ is chosen. When we substitute this expression for both the winning response ($y_w$) and the losing response ($y_l$) into the Bradley-Terry model, we get:
$$ p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r(x, y_w))}{\exp(r(x, y_w)) + \exp(r(x, y_l))} $$
Substituting the full expression for $r$:
$$ p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right)}{\exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right) + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right)} $$
Using the exponential identity $\exp(A+B) = e^A e^B$, we can factor out $\exp(\beta \log Z(x))$, which is equivalent to $Z(x)^\beta$, from both the numerator and every term in the denominator:
$$ p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{Z(x)^\beta \cdot \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}\right)}{Z(x)^\beta \cdot \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}\right) + Z(x)^\beta \cdot \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)} $$
Since $Z(x)^\beta$ appears as a multiplicative factor in every term of this fraction, it cancels out perfectly:
$$ p^*(y_w\succ y_l |x)=\frac{\exp\left(\beta {\rm log}\,\frac{{{{r}}_{w}}}{{{{r}}_{ref}}}\right )}{e xp({{{r}}_{w}}{{r}}_{ ref})+ex p({{{{r}}_{l}}}{{{r}}}_{ ref})} $$
(Note: I simplified notation here to show that $Z$ is gone).

You mentioned feeling that it disappears "inside a logarithm," but technically it disappears via exponentiation first. If you were to take the log-probability of a single outcome (like in a standard regression loss), $Z(x)$ would remain as an additive constant. However, in preference learning using the Bradley-Terry model (or its sigmoid formulation $\sigma(a-b)$), we are calculating a conditional probability where all possibilities share the same normalization constant. Because $Z(x)$ is identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$ given a fixed prompt $x$, it acts as a common multiplier in the softmax-like operation defined by Equation 1. Consequently, when converting this ratio into a difference of logits (as seen in Equation 4 leading to Equation 5), these constants vanish naturally. This algebraic trick allows DPO to bypass estimating $Z(x)$ entirely, turning an otherwise intractable partition function estimation problem into a simple classification loss over policy probabilities.

### Why is DPO considered 'stable' if it doesn't use a separate reward model?
Question:
The abstract claims DPO is 'stable' and eliminates the instability of RLHF. Usually, RLHF stability comes from careful tuning of the reward model and PPO clipping. In DPO, we just optimize a single cross-entropy loss directly on the policy. My confusion is: isn't there a risk that without an explicit reward model to regularize things, or without PPO's clipping mechanism, the model could easily collapse to generating only one token? What part of the DPO formulation specifically prevents this mode collapse compared to a naive 'maximize $y_w$ / minimize $y_l$' approach mentioned in the text?
Answer:
The apparent paradox of DPO being "stable" without an explicit reward model or PPO-style clipping stems from the fact that the KL-divergence regularization, which acts as the primary stabilizer in RLHF, is mathematically embedded directly into the DPO loss function itself. In standard RLHF, instability arises because the policy optimization and reward estimation are decoupled; if the reward model is imperfect or the policy drifts too far from the reference distribution, PPO requires manual intervention via clipping mechanisms to prevent catastrophic collapse. DPO circumvents this by deriving a closed-form solution where the optimal policy is a direct exponential transformation of the reward relative to a reference model. By substituting this optimal policy expression back into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the authors eliminate the need to ever estimate an explicit reward function $r(x,y)$ or perform iterative sampling. Instead, they optimize a single binary cross-entropy loss that inherently penalizes deviations from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

Regarding your specific concern about mode collapse—where a naive maximization of $y_w$ probabilities could lead to generating only one token—the paper highlights that DPO avoids this through a dynamic weighting mechanism derived from its gradient structure. If one were to simply maximize $\log \pi(y_w|x)$ while minimizing $\log \pi(y_l|x)$ without context, the model would indeed degenerate. However, Equation 6 in the paper reveals that the gradient of the DPO loss includes a term proportional to $\sigma(\hat{r}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}(x, y_w))$, which acts as an adaptive importance weight. This weight scales with how much higher the *current* implicit reward assigns to the dispreferred response relative to the preferred one. Essentially, if the model starts assigning high probability to $y_l$ (the dispreferred output), it receives a stronger penalty signal to suppress it before it fully collapses. Conversely, if $y_l$ already has low probability, updates become smaller. This creates a self-regulating system where the "clipping" effect is not applied via a hard hyperparameter threshold (like PPO's clip range) but emerges naturally from the sigmoid function acting on the log-probability ratios relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

Furthermore, stability is guaranteed by the presence of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in every term of the loss equation. The objective forces $\pi_\theta$ to maintain a specific relationship with $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; pushing $\pi_\theta(y_w|x)$ too aggressively towards 1 inevitably pushes $\pi_\theta(y_l|x)$ down, but doing so increases divergence from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ unless balanced correctly by the ratio terms inside the sigmoid. The parameter $\beta$ controls this balance: it dictates how much "freedom" we allow for deviation from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in exchange for satisfying preferences. Without this reference anchor built into every calculation, there would be no theoretical bound preventing mode collapse. Consequently, DPO achieves stability not by adding external constraints like PPO clipping layers or separate reward networks, but by reformulating the entire optimization landscape such that satisfying human preferences *requires* staying close enough to the reference distribution defined by $\beta$, effectively baking the KL constraint into a simple classification task that is numerically stable and easy to converge.

### Is $\pi_{ref}$ always fixed during training in DPO?
Question:
In Section 3, Algorithm Outline, it mentions initializing $\pi_{ref} = \pi^{SFT}$. Later in Section 4 (Theory), it discusses changing variables. Does $\pi_{ref}$ ever get updated during the gradient descent steps for $\theta$? If I look at Equation 5 ($\mathcal{L}_{DPO}$), it treats $\pi_{ref}$ as a constant denominator. But conceptually, if we are learning a new policy, shouldn't we also be updating what counts as 'reference'? Or is the whole point that keeping it frozen is what makes it equivalent to solving for an optimal policy against a *fixed* KL constraint?
Answer:
Based on the provided paper, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is **always kept fixed** (frozen) during the training process of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and never undergoes updates via gradient descent. The core mechanism of DPO relies on treating the reference policy as a static anchor to enable a specific mathematical transformation that eliminates the need for reinforcement learning loops or explicit reward modeling.

Theoretically, the entire derivation hinges on this distinction. As detailed in Section 3 ("Direct Preference Optimization"), the authors start with the standard RLHF objective, which involves maximizing a reward function subject to a KL-divergence constraint against a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. By deriving the optimal policy in closed form (Equation 5), they establish that the optimal policy $\pi^*$ relates to the reward function $r^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ through a specific equation involving logarithms. Crucially, when substituting this relationship back into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the partition function cancels out, leaving an expression that depends only on the ratios of probabilities between the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and the fixed reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This results in Equation 6 (the DPO loss), where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ appears solely as a constant denominator inside the log terms. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ were updated during training, it would become part of the optimization variable set, breaking the analytical link between the implicit reward and the policy derived in Equations 5 and 6. The loss function is designed specifically to optimize $\theta$ while holding $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ constant to ensure that minimizing this binary cross-entropy loss is mathematically equivalent to solving the original KL-constrained reward maximization problem.

Conceptually, fixing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ serves as a regularizer that defines "safe" behavior relative to which deviations are penalized. The paper explains in Section 3 that this constraint prevents mode collapse and ensures diversity by limiting how far the learned policy can drift from the initial SFT model. The intuition is not that we are trying to learn a new definition of "reference," but rather that we are optimizing a single policy $\pi_\theta$ against a fixed benchmark of what constitutes acceptable output distributions. The parameterization effectively encodes an implicit reward signal defined by how much more likely $\pi_\theta$ makes preferred responses compared to dispreferred ones, relative to this static baseline. As noted in Algorithm Outline and Section 3, initialization sets $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$, and throughout all gradient descent steps described for optimizing $\theta$, no gradients flow backward through or update $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This freezing is essential for DPO's simplicity; it allows the algorithm to bypass sampling from an evolving policy during training (a requirement for PPO) because the reference distribution required for calculating importance weights remains unchanged throughout epochs.

### How does Theorem 1 guarantee we don't lose expressive power by reparameterizing rewards?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that any reward class consistent with Plackett-Luce can be represented by $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$. This seems counter-intuitive because usually, reward functions are arbitrary scalars. By forcing them to take this specific log-ratio form relative to $\pi_{ref}$, aren't we implicitly constraining what kind of preferences we can learn? For instance, if the true human preference implies a reward function that grows exponentially with length but not linearly with tokens in a way that fits this ratio... wait, actually, since $\pi(y|x)$ is flexible enough (it's an LM), maybe it covers everything? I just need clarification on why this specific functional form doesn't restrict the set of learnable preference distributions.
Answer:
Theorem 1 in the paper resolves the apparent tension between fixing the reward form and maintaining expressive power by leveraging a fundamental property of preference models: they depend only on relative differences between rewards, not their absolute values. The intuition is that while a raw reward function $r(x,y)$ assigns an arbitrary scalar score to each output, human preferences are determined solely by which of two outputs scores higher. Mathematically, this means adding any function $f(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ to a reward function leaves the preference probabilities unchanged, as formalized in Lemma 1 where two reward functions differing only by $f(x)$ belong to the same equivalence class and induce identical preference distributions.

Because of this redundancy, we do not need to represent *every* possible arithmetic function as a reward; we only need to represent one unique member from each equivalence class to capture all learnable preferences. Theorem 1 proves that for any such class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model, there exists a specific representative that can be perfectly expressed as $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$. This specific form arises naturally from solving the optimization problem for the optimal policy under a KL-divergence constraint. When you rearrange the equation for the optimal policy $\pi_r$, you isolate the reward term as proportional to the log-ratio of that optimal policy relative to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Consequently, any arbitrary reward function $r^*$ can be projected onto this specific log-ratio form by subtracting its partition function (the normalization constant dependent only on $x$), effectively moving it within its equivalence class without altering its preference-inducing properties.

Therefore, restricting rewards to this log-ratio form does not constrain the set of learnable preference distributions because it merely selects a canonical representative from each equivalence class rather than limiting the classes themselves. Since $\pi(y|x)$ is a flexible language model capable of approximating complex conditional distributions, varying $\pi(y|x)$ across all possible policies allows the expression of any valid preference ordering that could be captured by an unconstrained scalar reward field. In essence, Theorem 1 demonstrates that optimizing over policies $\pi_\theta$ is mathematically equivalent to optimizing over entire equivalence classes of reward functions, ensuring no expressive power is lost despite the seemingly rigid functional form.

### "Secretly a Reward Model": What is actually being learned here?
Question:
The title says 'Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model'. If I train DPO on my dataset and end up with weights for $\theta$, do these weights encode both the generation strategy AND an implicit reward function simultaneously? If I wanted to extract an explicit reward score for a new sentence later (without generating anything), how would I calculate it using only my trained DPO model and my reference model? Is it just plugging them into that log-ratio formula from Eq 3?
Answer:
Yes, your intuition is precisely correct: in the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework, the trained language model parameters $\theta$ simultaneously encode both the optimal generation strategy (the policy) and an implicit reward function. The core insight of the paper is that these two objects are mathematically linked through a specific reparameterization derived from the Bradley-Terry preference model. Instead of treating the reward as a separate entity that needs to be fitted independently before optimizing the policy, DPO exploits a closed-form relationship where the optimal policy for a given reward function is determined by the exponential of that reward relative to a reference model.

Specifically, Equation 3 in the paper establishes that any reward function $r(x, y)$ consistent with human preferences can be expressed in terms of an optimal policy $\pi_r$, a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and a scaling factor $\beta$. By rearranging this relationship, the authors show that we can define an implicit reward function directly from the probabilities output by your trained model. As stated in Section 4.1 ("Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model"), if you have trained a DPO model $\pi_\theta$ starting from a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically an SFT model), you can recover an explicit reward score for any completion $y$ given a prompt $x$ using exactly the log-ratio formula you suspected.

To calculate this explicit reward score without generating anything new, you simply plug your trained model's probabilities and the reference model's probabilities into the following equation:
$$ \hat{r}(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} $$
Here, $\beta$ is the hyperparameter used during DPO training that controls how tightly the learned policy adheres to the reference distribution versus maximizing implied rewards. The term inside the logarithm represents how much more likely your fine-tuned model finds this specific response compared to what it originally thought was likely before alignment. If this ratio is greater than 1, the log-ratio is positive, indicating a favorable deviation towards human preferences; if less than 1, it indicates a penalty or deviation away from preferred behavior relative to the baseline.

This formulation works because DPO effectively optimizes a binary cross-entropy loss where it tries to maximize this log-ratio for preferred responses ($y_w$) over dispreferred ones ($y_l$). Consequently, once training converges, the values produced by this formula serve as valid proxies for human preference scores. The paper notes that while traditional RLHF fits an explicit neural network $r_\phi(x,y)$ separately and then runs reinforcement learning to find $\pi_\theta$, DPO skips this intermediate step by solving for $\pi_\theta$ directly such that it implicitly satisfies $r(x,y) = \beta \log (\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{ref}})$. Therefore, extracting rewards post-hoc is not only possible but natural to the method's design; you do not need to run any additional sampling or optimization loops—you merely evaluate both models on text strings and compute their log-probability difference scaled by $\beta$.
