# From Tokens to Sequences: How GSPO Stabilizes Large Language Model Training

## The Instability Crisis in Scaling Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the cornerstone for pushing language models beyond simple chatbots into agents capable of solving complex math problems and writing advanced code. However, as models grow in size—particularly with the advent of massive Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures—the training process has become increasingly fragile. State-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), while popular, suffer from severe stability issues when scaled. These instabilities often manifest as "catastrophic collapse," where the model's performance degrades irreversibly, rendering further training futile. This fragility has historically forced researchers to rely on complex, ad-hoc stabilization tricks just to keep training running.

The root cause of this instability lies in a fundamental mismatch within the algorithm's design. Current methods calculate importance ratios—the weights used to correct for differences between the old and new policies—at the token level. They essentially ask, "How likely was this specific word under the new policy compared to the old one?" However, since rewards are assigned to entire responses rather than individual words, this granular approach introduces high-variance noise. As response lengths increase, these errors accumulate and amplify through the clipping mechanism, eventually causing the training dynamics to spiral out of control.

## The Core Innovation: Aligning Units of Reward and Optimization

To resolve this, we introduce **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, a new algorithm designed specifically for stable, large-scale RL training. The central philosophy of GSPO is elegantly simple yet profound: **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward.**

Since a verifier assigns a score to a whole sequence (a complete answer or thought process), GSPO shifts the calculation of importance ratios from tokens to sequences. Instead of multiplying thousands of noisy token-level weights together, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio based on the total likelihood of the entire response under both policies. By doing so, it adheres strictly to the principles of importance sampling, which requires averaging over samples from a distribution rather than relying on single-point estimates that can be wildly inaccurate.

Mathematically, while GRPO sums token-level ratios $w_{t}$ across a sequence, GSPO utilizes a sequence-level ratio $s_i$:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
Here, $y_i$ represents an entire response string generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ is its likelihood under the new policy $\pi_\theta$. Crucially, we apply length normalization via the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ to ensure that responses of different lengths remain comparable and that small variations in token probability do not cause drastic swings in the overall ratio.

## Why Sequence-Level Matters More Than You Think

The shift from token-level to sequence-level processing solves several critical problems simultaneously:

*   **Variance Reduction:** Token-level importance weights can fluctuate wildly because they depend on single next-token predictions. In contrast, sequence likelihood aggregates probabilities over many steps, smoothing out local anomalies and providing a stable signal for gradient updates.
*   **Robust Clipping:** In GRPO, clipping happens per token. If one token has an extreme weight but is part of an otherwise good response, it can distort the update or trigger unnecessary clipping elsewhere. GSPO clips entire responses; if a whole response is deemed "too off-policy" based on its aggregate likelihood, it is excluded entirely from that update step. This ensures that only coherent candidate answers influence the model's direction.
*   **Elimination of Noise Accumulation:** Because GSPO avoids summing up potentially divergent token-level terms over long chains of reasoning (common in math or coding tasks), it prevents the exponential accumulation of error that leads to model collapse in GRPO.

## Solving MoE Instability Without Workarounds

One of the most significant achievements of GSPO is its ability to stabilize Reinforcement Learning training for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without requiring complex engineering hacks like "Routing Replay." In MoE models, different subsets of parameters ("experts") activate depending on input content. When using GRPO with MoEs, slight changes in weights during training can cause completely different experts to activate for similar inputs between updates ($\pi_\theta$ vs $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$). This volatility breaks token-level importance ratios because comparing probabilities computed by different active networks yields meaningless results. Previously, teams had to force both policies to use identical routing paths manually—a costly and restrictive workaround known as Routing Replay.

GSPO renders Routing Replay obsolete because it relies solely on sequence likelihoods ($\pi(y|x)$). Even if different experts activate at intermediate steps within a generation path, as long as the final sequence remains plausible under language modeling standards—which MoEs are trained to ensure—the aggregate likelihood remains stable enough for optimization. Consequently, GSPO allows MoE models to utilize their full capacity dynamically without artificial constraints on expert routing consistency. Empirical results show that GSPO converges smoothly where GRPO fails catastrophically unless heavily patched with Routing Replay strategies that add memory overhead and communication latency.

## Practical Impact and Future Outlook

The implications of GSPO extend beyond mere stability; it offers tangible gains in efficiency and infrastructure simplicity. Our experiments indicate that GSPO delivers superior performance metrics on benchmarks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench compared to GRPO while consuming less computational resources per successful iteration. Interestingly, despite clipping a significantly larger fraction of tokens/responses than GRPO due to stricter criteria at the sequence level, GSPO trains faster because every retained sample contributes a cleaner gradient signal rather than noisy ones requiring correction later.

Furthermore, GSPO simplifies RL infrastructure architecture traditionally plagued by precision mismatches between training engines (like Megatron-LM) and inference servers (like vLLM).
