Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Diagnosing Instability in Current Methods

## Introduction to Algorithmic Stability in Large-Scale Reinforcement Learning

As researchers strive to push the boundaries of large language models (LLMs) through reinforcement learning (RL), the ability to maintain stable training dynamics becomes paramount. While recent advancements have demonstrated that massive computational investments can unlock capabilities akin to solving competition-level mathematics or coding problems, the path to these breakthroughs is fraught with perilous instabilities. State-of-the-art algorithms designed specifically for LLMs, most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), were expected to facilitate this scaling efficiently by removing the need for separate value models.然而，empirical observations reveal that when trained on gigantic architectures—particularly those involving high degrees of sparsity like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)—these methods frequently succumb to "catastrophic model collapse." This phenomenon represents a complete degradation of model quality that renders subsequent training futile, regardless of hyperparameter tuning or architectural adjustments. Understanding why these failures occur requires dissecting the fundamental statistical assumptions underlying GRPO's design, revealing deep-seated conflicts between the algorithmic machinery used for small-scale problems and the realities of modern, ultra-large-scale generative systems.

The central thesis emerging from recent investigations is that the instability inherent in GRPO is not merely a matter of poor hyperparameter selection or insufficient computation; it arises from a structural flaw in how probability distributions are approximated during optimization. Specifically, the algorithm attempts to apply principles of importance sampling—a technique originally developed for estimating expectations over broad populations—to individual token predictions within a single sentence or code snippet. When scaled up to generate thousands of tokens or train billions of parameters, this local application breaks down statistically. The very mechanism meant to stabilize learning—the calculation of importance ratios—becomes a source of overwhelming noise. By tracing the lineage of this error back to its roots in probability theory and observing its destructive interaction with clipping mechanisms, we gain critical insight into why current pipelines fail and what must be replaced to achieve future scalability. This diagnosis forms the necessary groundwork for understanding proposed solutions like Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

## The Principle of Importance Sampling: Behavior vs. Target Distributions

At the heart of many offline reinforcement learning techniques lies the concept of importance sampling, a rigorous method used to evaluate policies or estimate outcomes when direct access to the optimal policy is unavailable. Fundamentally, importance sampling addresses a scenario where one wishes to calculate the expected value of some function $f(z)$ under a *target* distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$, yet all available data comes exclusively from a *behavior* distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. If one were naively averaged values from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ without adjustment, the results would systematically bias toward regions favored by $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ rather than reflecting reality under $\pi_{\text{tar}}$. Corrective weighting is therefore required to bridge this gap. Mathematically, if $Z$ is a random variable representing a trajectory or outcome sampled from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, then $Y'$ can be viewed as having been drawn from $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ via reweighting:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z) \right].
$$
Here, the ratio $\rho = \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ acts as an importance weight that compensates for discrepancies between how likely an event was under the two different distributions. Crucially, this equality holds strictly because it assumes we possess enough samples—or rely on averaging properties—that account for global differences between $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ and $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. The validity of this estimator depends heavily on reducing variance; ideally, we want samples drawn frequently enough that rare events under one distribution but common under another are adequately represented and corrected proportionately. Without sufficient sampling density relative to population heterogeneity, these corrections become unstable estimators prone to extreme outliers that distort gradients entirely away from useful directions.

When translating abstract importance sampling logic into concrete Deep Learning applications like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or GRPO, practitioners map $z$ onto sequences generated by language models and utilize ratios computed locally at every decision point along those trajectories. In PPO-style approaches applied sequentially word-by-word or character-by-character over text generation steps $t=1,\dots,T$, each step involves calculating an immediate transition-specific weight based solely upon conditional probabilities predicted immediately prior ($t-1$). Herein lies a subtle yet profound conceptual shift: whereas classical statistics might demand aggregate averages across vast swaths of possibilities before drawing conclusions about shifts in expectation densities... real-world implementations attempt instantaneous recalibrations conditioned purely upon single stochastic draws occurring moments ago during rollouts performed earlier ("cold" policies versus live ones now undergoing modifications).
