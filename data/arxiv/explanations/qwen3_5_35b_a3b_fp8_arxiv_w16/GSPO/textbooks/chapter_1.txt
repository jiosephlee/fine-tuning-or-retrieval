Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Reinforcement Learning in Large Language Models

## The Evolution of LLM Training Paradigms: From Pre-training to RLHF/RLAIF

The journey toward developing intelligent language models has been characterized by a steady expansion of objectives, moving from simple pattern matching to the nuanced deployment of reasoning capabilities. Initially, the training pipeline was dominated by pre-training on vast corpora of text, allowing models to learn statistical regularities and general world knowledge. However, as researchers sought to align these powerful generative systems with human intent, values, and safety guidelines, the industry transitioned toward post-pretraining refinement methods known collectively as RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback). Traditionally, this stage relied heavily on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an algorithm designed to constrain policy updates within a safe vicinity of a reference distribution. As described in the literature surrounding these advancements, PPO operates by estimating advantages via a separate value model—a neural network approximating expected future returns—and then optimizing the policy probability mass through a surrogate objective involving importance ratios calculated at every single token step. While effective for smaller scales or simpler alignment tasks, this architecture introduced significant complexity; the necessity of maintaining a value model nearly as large as the policy itself created substantial memory burdens and computational costs. More critically, the fidelity of PPO became entirely dependent on the accuracy of this auxiliary value estimator. If the value model failed to accurately predict return distributions—particularly as task difficulty escalated—the entire optimization process would become unstable or biased. Consequently, while PPO laid the groundwork for aligned models, its structural dependencies limited its scalability when pushing beyond elementary instruction-following behaviors into domains requiring deep logical deduction or extended planning chains.

As research progressed into the era of frontier reasoning models, practitioners identified that relying solely on static human preference datasets was insufficient for unlocking true cognitive depth. This realization catalyzed a shift away from pure supervised fine-tuning and early forms of RL toward dynamic feedback loops where models could generate diverse outputs evaluated verifiably against ground truth solutions in mathematics or code execution environments. This led to variants such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which attempted to eliminate the expensive value model bottleneck by calculating relative advantages directly from groups of responses generated for a single prompt. By comparing scores assigned by automated verifiers across multiple generations ($G$ responses per query), GRPO computed advantages based on mean-centered differences in verification rewards rather than predicted state values. Despite removing the explicit value head requirement—which simplified architectural demands somewhat—the underlying mechanics of GRPO retained the fundamental flaw inherited from earlier token-centric approaches: it performed importance weighting and clipping operations individually for every token position $t$ within a response $y$. While computationally lighter than traditional PPO in terms of parameters required during training steps, this granular approach obscured a deeper probabilistic inconsistency inherent in scaling up system sizes and output lengths. The evolution thus moved from generic supervision to direct outcome-based reinforcement learning but remained trapped in an implementation detail where local token decisions were treated independently despite global rewards being contingent upon complete sequence correctness. Understanding this lineage reveals why simply adding more compute or expanding dataset diversity yielded diminishing returns if the optimization machinery itself suffered from intrinsic instabilities rooted in how probabilities were aggregated across variable-length trajectories.
