Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Foundations of Policy Optimization Algorithms

## Introduction to Reinforcement Learning Scaling

As you delve into the cutting-edge applications of deep reinforcement learning (RL) for Large Language Models (LLMs), understanding the foundational algorithms is critical before exploring novel methodologies. Modern LLM development increasingly relies on RL paradigms to unlock capabilities beyond supervised pre-training, enabling models to solve complex mathematical proofs, generate intricate code, and engage in multi-step logical reasoning. However, translating classical RL success stories—originally designed for discrete Markov Decision Processes—to the realm of massive autoregressive generative models requires careful adaptation of existing techniques. The transition involves reconciling the infinite action spaces inherent in text generation with finite horizon episodes and managing the statistical properties of vast datasets efficiently.

Two primary pillars support the current landscape of RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and subsequent direct preference optimization variants: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its recent successor, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Both methods attempt to navigate the treacherous waters of "off-policy" learning, where data collected from an older version of the model must be used to train a newer version without causing destructive divergence or catastrophic forgetting. This chapter aims to deconstruct these foundational approaches, providing you with a rigorous grasp of their mathematical structures, intuitive motivations, and operational mechanics. By thoroughly analyzing how these algorithms handle probability distributions, advantage estimation, and constraint enforcement, we lay the necessary groundwork for appreciating why certain instabilities arise in extreme scales and what principles drive the evolution toward sequence-level optimizations.

Your journey begins with establishing a precise formalism for autoregressive generation. Without a shared vocabulary regarding policies, probabilities, and rewards, distinguishing between subtle variations in algorithmic design would be impossible. Consequently, we start by defining the core components of the environment—the query space—and how an autoregressive model acts as a stochastic policy mapping inputs to outputs. From there, we trace the lineage from PPO’s reliance on auxiliary value networks to GRPO’s elegant elimination thereof via group-relative comparisons. Each step reveals trade-offs between bias reduction and implementation complexity, culminating in a critique that sets the stage for identifying fundamental flaws in token-centric importance weighting schemes prevalent in earlier iterations. As you read through this chapter, keep in mind that every equation presented represents a deliberate engineering choice made to balance computational feasibility with statistical consistency—a theme that recurs throughout modern AI research literature.

# Notation and Problem Formulation in Autoregressive Generation

To reason about reinforcement learning agents operating within language domains, we must first establish a standardized notation system rooted in probabilistic graphical models. Consider an autoregressive language model parametrized by $\theta$, which functions as our policy $\pi_\theta$. This policy maps input prompts or queries $x$ drawn from some dataset $\mathcal{D}$ to output sequences $y$, known as responses. The generation process is inherently sequential: starting from scratch or conditioned on initial prompt tokens $x$, the model predicts each successive token $y_t$ based solely on preceding history up to index $t-1$. Mathematically, this conditional independence assumption implies that the joint probability mass function over an entire response string decomposes into a product of marginal probabilities at each time step:

$$ \pi_\theta(y|x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t}) $$
