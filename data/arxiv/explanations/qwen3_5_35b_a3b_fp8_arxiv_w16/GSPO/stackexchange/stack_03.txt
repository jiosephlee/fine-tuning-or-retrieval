\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is GSPO-token mathematically equivalent to GSPO if advantages are uniform?
Question:
Section 4.3 introduces 'GSPO-token', which looks like a token-level objective but uses sequence-level weights via stop-gradient operations. The text states: 'GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical... when we set the advantages of all the tokens in the response $y_i$ to the same value.' If this is true, why bother with the complex stop-gradient formulation ($sg[s_i]$)? Does it offer any practical benefit for multi-turn RL or dynamic masking where advantages might *not* be uniform across tokens?
Answer:
The short answer is yes: when the advantages are uniform across all tokens in a response (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), the **GSPO-token** objective is mathematically and numerically identical to the standard **GSPO** objective. As explicitly stated in Section 4.3 of the paper, under this condition, the complex stop-gradient formulation yields the exact same gradient updates because the term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ reduces to $s_i(\theta)$, and summing identical terms simply scales the result by the sequence length, which cancels out with the $1/|y_i|$ normalization factor already present in both formulations.

So, why implement the more complex stop-gradient machinery if it collapses to GSPO in simple cases? The primary reason is not mathematical equivalence in that specific scenario, but rather **architectural flexibility for non-uniform advantage signals**, which is crucial for advanced applications like multi-turn reinforcement learning or dynamic masking. In standard GSPO, every token in a generated response shares a single global advantage score derived from the total sequence reward. However, in many realistic scenarios—such as a multi-turn conversation where only specific turns matter, or tasks where early errors should penalize later generations differently—we may wish to assign different advantage values to different tokens. Without GSPO-token, enforcing such fine-grained adjustments would be impossible within this framework because GSPO treats the entire sequence as a single atomic unit for optimization.

The stop-gradient operation ($\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$) plays a critical engineering role here that goes beyond mere numerical convenience. By detaching the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ before multiplying it by token-specific advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, GSPO-token ensures that the gradient flow remains consistent with the original GSPO philosophy: we want to weight all tokens in a sequence equally regarding *policy deviation*, but we want to weight them differently regarding *reward credit*. Specifically, Equation 16 shows that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is constructed such that its gradient contribution comes solely from the explicit $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$ term in the numerator, while $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a constant scalar multiplier determined by the overall sequence quality. This prevents high-variance interactions between local token probabilities and global sequence penalties that could destabilize training if calculated naively.

Furthermore, this design choice offers significant practical benefits for infrastructure and MoE training. As noted in Section 5.3 and 5.4, GSPO was designed specifically to avoid sensitivity to individual token likelihood fluctuations caused by Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing volatility or precision mismatches between inference and training engines. While standard GSPO ignores token-level probability variations entirely by averaging them into a sequence metric, GSPO-token preserves this stability for the importance weighting component (via $s_i$) while allowing researchers to inject custom reward signals at arbitrary granularities without breaking the algorithm's theoretical grounding. Essentially, GSPO-token acts as a generalized interface: it defaults perfectly to standard GSPO when rewards are uniform but unlocks sophisticated reward shaping capabilities when they are not, making it a versatile tool for future RL research without sacrificing the stability guarantees of the base algorithm.
