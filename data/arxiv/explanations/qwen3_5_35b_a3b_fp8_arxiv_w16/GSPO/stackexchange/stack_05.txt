\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Does GSPO actually solve the off-policy bias problem or just hide it?
Question:
Standard PPO/GRPO rely on clipping to bound off-policy errors. In GSPO, we clip entire sequences based on their geometric mean likelihood ratio. If a sequence has very low probability under $	heta$ but was sampled from $	heta_{old}$ (highly off-policy), clipping removes it entirely. However, isn't there still a risk that we are systematically ignoring regions of high-reward space simply because they require many small deviations from $	heta_{old}$ (which sum up to a low geometric mean)? Does this make GSPO conservative compared to methods that might accept slightly lower probabilities per token if they yield high rewards?
Answer:
The concern you raise touches on a critical trade-off in reinforcement learning: the balance between exploration and stability. Based on the provided paper, GSPO does not merely "hide" the off-policy bias; rather, it fundamentally redefines what constitutes a valid off-policy sample by shifting the unit of analysis from tokens to sequences. The authors argue that GRPO's token-level clipping is actually *more* prone to systematic error because it creates an ill-posed objective where high-variance noise accumulates, whereas GSPO's sequence-level approach acts as a rigorous filter that preserves the statistical integrity of the importance sampling estimator.

To understand why GSPO avoids the trap of ignoring high-reward regions, we must look at how the paper characterizes the failure mode of GRPO. The authors explicitly state that GRPO's objective is "ill-posed" because it applies importance weights at each token position $t$ independently. As noted in Section III (Motivation), this misapplication introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. When a sequence contains even a single token with a highly divergent probability ratio (due to stochasticity or minor policy shifts), GRPO's clipping mechanism struggles to handle it correctly because the variance explodes locally. The paper posits that this accumulation of noise eventually leads to "catastrophic model collapse," which is often irreversible. In this light, GSPO's aggressive filtering is not an arbitrary conservatism but a necessary corrective measure to eliminate these unstable gradients before they corrupt the policy update.

Regarding your specific worry about sequences requiring "many small deviations": GSPO addresses this via its definition of the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. As shown in Equation 6 of the paper, this ratio is calculated as the geometric mean of the token likelihood ratios:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By using the geometric mean, GSPO ensures that if *every* token deviates slightly from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, their cumulative effect results in a moderate deviation in $s_i(\theta)$ rather than an infinite explosion or immediate rejection. Conversely, if a sequence requires many small deviations that sum up to a very low total probability under $\pi_\theta$, then $s_i(\theta)$ will indeed drop below the clipping threshold $(1-\varepsilon)$, and the sequence will be excluded. However, from an importance sampling perspective described in Section III, excluding such samples is theoretically sound. Importance sampling relies on re-weighting samples drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to estimate expectations under $\pi_\theta$. If a sample has extremely low probability under $\pi_\theta$, it contributes almost nothing to the true expectation anyway; weighting it heavily would introduce massive variance, while discarding it reduces variance without losing significant information about high-probability regions under $\pi_\theta$.

Furthermore, the paper argues that GSPO is actually *less* conservative regarding useful exploration than GRPO because GRPO's token-level clipping often retains "noisy" gradients from sequences that are statistically unreliable under $\pi_\theta$. By clamping individual tokens while keeping others un-clamped within a bad sequence, GRPO can inadvertently optimize towards artifacts caused by random chance rather than genuine reward signals. GSPO enforces consistency across the entire response: either the whole trajectory represents a plausible shift in policy (and thus receives credit), or it represents an outlier that should be discarded entirely. This ensures that when a sequence passes through clipping and contributes to training, its contribution is robust and aligned with both the reward structure and the underlying probability distribution changes.

Finally, regarding your intuition about missing high-reward spaces: if a region yields high rewards but requires many small deviations from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO will likely capture these if those deviations remain within reasonable bounds such that $s_i(\theta)$ stays near 1. If they fall outside these bounds, it implies those trajectories are already so improbable under $\pi_\theta$ that they offer little signal for stable gradient descent regardless of their raw reward score. The paper emphasizes in Section IV.B (Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions) that despite clipping significantly more tokens than GRPO (by two orders of magnitude), GSPO achieves higher efficiency and performance precisely because it filters out these unstable gradients rather than trying to learn from them. Thus, GSPO solves the off-policy bias problem by ensuring that every gradient step comes from a statistically coherent sequence update, preventing the accumulation of noise that causes model collapse in prior methods like GRPO and PPO when scaled to large LLMs and MoE architectures.
