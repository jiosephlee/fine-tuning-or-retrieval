# Why Your AI Model Collapses: The Hidden Flaw in Current Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the secret sauce behind the most advanced capabilities of modern Large Language Models (LLMs). By allowing models to learn from trial and error, we have unlocked abilities to solve complex math problems and write intricate code. However, as we attempt to scale these models to unprecedented sizes—especially with architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)—a persistent nightmare emerges: training instability. Despite careful tuning, models trained with popular algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) often suffer from catastrophic collapse, rendering hours of computation useless. In this post, we explore the root cause of this instability and introduce a new solution called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) that fundamentally fixes the problem.

## The Illusion of Stability in Token-Level Updates

Current state-of-the-art RL algorithms, including GRPO, rely heavily on a technique called importance sampling to ensure safe policy updates. Imagine you are trying to estimate the average height of people in a room by measuring just one person at a time; if that person is unusually tall or short, your estimate is unreliable. Similarly, GRPO calculates an "importance ratio" for every single token generated by the model. This ratio measures how likely a specific token is under the new policy versus the old policy.

The critical flaw lies in treating these ratios independently for each token. When generating long responses, the algorithm applies this ratio at every step $t$. Because each individual token probability can fluctuate wildly between policies, multiplying these unstable factors together creates what researchers call "high-variance noise." As the response gets longer, these tiny errors do not cancel out; instead, they accumulate exponentially. The clipping mechanism designed to prevent extreme updates actually amplifies this noise rather than dampening it. Consequently, the training signal becomes so corrupted that the model collapses irreversibly, unable to recover even when reverting to previous checkpoints or tweaking hyperparameters.

## Aligning Rewards with Optimization Units

The core philosophical shift required to fix this issue is recognizing that rewards are assigned to whole sequences, not individual words. If you receive a bonus for writing a perfect essay, it makes no sense to penalize or reward specific letters within that essay based on their independent probabilities. The paper argues that the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward.

This insight led to the development of **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**. Instead of calculating importance ratios for every token $y_t$, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio for the entire sequence $y$. Mathematically, this involves looking at the geometric mean of the likelihoods across all tokens in a response:

$$
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
$$

By defining the importance ratio at the sequence level, GSPO ensures that the "weight" applied during optimization reflects how much the *entire* response deviates from expectations. This approach naturally aligns with sequence-level rewards and eliminates the accumulation of token-level noise. Furthermore, GSPO clips entire responses rather than individual tokens, preventing outliers from skewing gradients while preserving valid learning signals from shorter or more typical sequences.

## Solving the MoE Mystery and Simplifying Infrastructure

One of the most compelling validations of GSPO comes from its application to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In dense models, stability issues are already difficult; in MoEs, where different subsets of parameters activate for different inputs ("expert-activation volatility"), traditional methods struggle immensely. Previously, engineers had to employ complex workarounds like "Routing Replay," which forced new policies to mimic old routing decisions just to calculate stable gradients. This added significant memory overhead and limited model capacity.

GSPO removes this dependency entirely because it relies on sequence likelihood rather than token-level routing paths. Since a language model generally maintains its overall generative capability even as internal experts switch slightly between updates, sequence-level likelihoods remain stable enough for reliable optimization without artificial constraints. Beyond MoEs, GSPO offers practical benefits for infrastructure engineering as well. Because it operates on sequence-level metrics rather than precise token-by-token calculations, it is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training engines and inference servers. This opens doors for simplified training pipelines where inference engines can provide likelihoods directly without costly recomputation steps in specialized training environments.

## Conclusion: A New Foundation for Scaling Intelligence

The journey toward truly intelligent AI systems requires not just bigger datasets or faster chips, but robust algorithms capable of handling massive scales without breaking down. The transition from GRPO to GSPO represents exactly this kind of necessary evolution—from fragile token-level corrections to stable sequence-level optimization. Empirical results show that GSPO delivers superior training efficiency and performance while completely eliminating the catastrophic collapse seen in previous attempts at scaling RL for large models like Qwen3. By addressing the fundamental misalignment between how rewards are measured and how gradients are calculated, GSPO lays a solid foundation for future breakthroughs in reinforcement learning and large-scale intelligence development



# From Tokens to Sequences: How GSPO Stabilizes Large Language Model Training

## The Instability Crisis in Scaling Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the cornerstone for pushing language models beyond simple chatbots into agents capable of solving complex math problems and writing advanced code. However, as models grow in size—particularly with the advent of massive Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures—the training process has become increasingly fragile. State-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), while popular, suffer from severe stability issues when scaled. These instabilities often manifest as "catastrophic collapse," where the model's performance degrades irreversibly, rendering further training futile. This fragility has historically forced researchers to rely on complex, ad-hoc stabilization tricks just to keep training running.

The root cause of this instability lies in a fundamental mismatch within the algorithm's design. Current methods calculate importance ratios—the weights used to correct for differences between the old and new policies—at the token level. They essentially ask, "How likely was this specific word under the new policy compared to the old one?" However, since rewards are assigned to entire responses rather than individual words, this granular approach introduces high-variance noise. As response lengths increase, these errors accumulate and amplify through the clipping mechanism, eventually causing the training dynamics to spiral out of control.

## The Core Innovation: Aligning Units of Reward and Optimization

To resolve this, we introduce **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, a new algorithm designed specifically for stable, large-scale RL training. The central philosophy of GSPO is elegantly simple yet profound: **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward.**

Since a verifier assigns a score to a whole sequence (a complete answer or thought process), GSPO shifts the calculation of importance ratios from tokens to sequences. Instead of multiplying thousands of noisy token-level weights together, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio based on the total likelihood of the entire response under both policies. By doing so, it adheres strictly to the principles of importance sampling, which requires averaging over samples from a distribution rather than relying on single-point estimates that can be wildly inaccurate.

Mathematically, while GRPO sums token-level ratios $w_{t}$ across a sequence, GSPO utilizes a sequence-level ratio $s_i$:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
Here, $y_i$ represents an entire response string generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ is its likelihood under the new policy $\pi_\theta$. Crucially, we apply length normalization via the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ to ensure that responses of different lengths remain comparable and that small variations in token probability do not cause drastic swings in the overall ratio.

## Why Sequence-Level Matters More Than You Think

The shift from token-level to sequence-level processing solves several critical problems simultaneously:

*   **Variance Reduction:** Token-level importance weights can fluctuate wildly because they depend on single next-token predictions. In contrast, sequence likelihood aggregates probabilities over many steps, smoothing out local anomalies and providing a stable signal for gradient updates.
*   **Robust Clipping:** In GRPO, clipping happens per token. If one token has an extreme weight but is part of an otherwise good response, it can distort the update or trigger unnecessary clipping elsewhere. GSPO clips entire responses; if a whole response is deemed "too off-policy" based on its aggregate likelihood, it is excluded entirely from that update step. This ensures that only coherent candidate answers influence the model's direction.
*   **Elimination of Noise Accumulation:** Because GSPO avoids summing up potentially divergent token-level terms over long chains of reasoning (common in math or coding tasks), it prevents the exponential accumulation of error that leads to model collapse in GRPO.

## Solving MoE Instability Without Workarounds

One of the most significant achievements of GSPO is its ability to stabilize Reinforcement Learning training for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without requiring complex engineering hacks like "Routing Replay." In MoE models, different subsets of parameters ("experts") activate depending on input content. When using GRPO with MoEs, slight changes in weights during training can cause completely different experts to activate for similar inputs between updates ($\pi_\theta$ vs $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$). This volatility breaks token-level importance ratios because comparing probabilities computed by different active networks yields meaningless results. Previously, teams had to force both policies to use identical routing paths manually—a costly and restrictive workaround known as Routing Replay.

GSPO renders Routing Replay obsolete because it relies solely on sequence likelihoods ($\pi(y|x)$). Even if different experts activate at intermediate steps within a generation path, as long as the final sequence remains plausible under language modeling standards—which MoEs are trained to ensure—the aggregate likelihood remains stable enough for optimization. Consequently, GSPO allows MoE models to utilize their full capacity dynamically without artificial constraints on expert routing consistency. Empirical results show that GSPO converges smoothly where GRPO fails catastrophically unless heavily patched with Routing Replay strategies that add memory overhead and communication latency.

## Practical Impact and Future Outlook

The implications of GSPO extend beyond mere stability; it offers tangible gains in efficiency and infrastructure simplicity. Our experiments indicate that GSPO delivers superior performance metrics on benchmarks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench compared to GRPO while consuming less computational resources per successful iteration. Interestingly, despite clipping a significantly larger fraction of tokens/responses than GRPO due to stricter criteria at the sequence level, GSPO trains faster because every retained sample contributes a cleaner gradient signal rather than noisy ones requiring correction later.

Furthermore, GSPO simplifies RL infrastructure architecture traditionally plagued by precision mismatches between training engines (like Megatron-LM) and inference servers (like vLLM). # Unlocking Mixture-of-Experts: Why GSPO Makes Sparse Models Trainable Without Hacks

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the cornerstone for pushing Large Language Models (LLMs) toward solving complex reasoning tasks, yet scaling this training remains fraught with instability. Nowhere is this challenge more pronounced than in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. While MoEs offer massive capacity through sparse activation, their very nature introduces a critical hurdle known as **expert-activation volatility**. Recent research introduces **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, an algorithm that naturally resolves this volatility, eliminating the need for complex engineering workarounds and enabling stable, efficient training of the largest models.

### The Instability of Expert Activation

To understand why GSPO matters for MoEs, we must first look at the mechanics of RL training. Standard algorithms like GRPO rely on comparing a new policy against an old one using importance ratios calculated at the token level. In dense models, this works reasonably well because the underlying parameters remain relatively stable between updates. However, in MoE models, only a subset of "experts" is active for any given token.

When a model undergoes a gradient update in an RL step, the policy shifts slightly. In an MoE architecture, even a tiny shift can cause the router to select completely different experts for the same input sequence. Empirical observations show that in deep MoE models like Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, approximately 10% of the activated experts change after just a single update for the same rollout sample. This creates a paradox: if the set of active experts changes drastically between the old and new policies, calculating a valid token-level importance ratio becomes mathematically unsound. The resulting noise propagates through the training loop, leading to high-variance gradients and eventual model collapse—a failure mode that is often irreversible once triggered.

### The Old Fix: Routing Replay

Historically, researchers attempted to mitigate this volatility with a technique called **Routing Replay**. This method involves caching which experts were active under the old policy and forcing the new policy to use those exact same routing decisions when calculating importance ratios. By artificially freezing the routing path during calculation, engineers could stabilize training and force convergence.

While effective enough to get things running, Routing Replay comes with significant costs. It requires storing additional routing states in memory and communicating them across devices during training steps. More importantly, it artificially constrains the model's capacity; by preventing the router from exploring new paths during the calculation phase, we limit how freely the model can adapt its internal structure to optimize performance. It is essentially a bandage that fixes symptoms but restricts potential growth.

### How GSPO Solves Volatility Naturally

GSPO offers a cleaner solution by fundamentally changing *what* is being optimized and *how* importance ratios are computed. Instead of relying on token-level probabilities where expert switches cause chaos, GSPO calculates importance ratios based on **sequence likelihood**.

The core innovation lies in defining the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By focusing on the probability of generating an entire response $y_i$ rather than summing up unstable token-by-token weights, GSPO smooths out local fluctuations caused by individual expert swaps. Even if specific experts activate differently at certain positions within a sequence, the overall sequence likelihood remains robust because it aggregates information across all tokens while respecting global consistency principles like importance sampling.

Consequently, GSPO does not suffer from expert-activation volatility in its gradient calculations because it does not depend on matching individual token distributions between policies. As demonstrated in empirical evaluations on Qwen3 models trained with GSPO:
*   **No Routing Replay Needed:** The algorithm converges stably without requiring cached routing modes or forced expert reuse.
*   **Full Capacity Utilization:** Because there are no artificial constraints on which experts can be used during optimization calculations, the model leverages its full architectural potential without interference from external replay buffers.
*   **Reduced Overhead:** Eliminating Routing Replay removes unnecessary memory consumption and inter-device communication overhead associated with storing and managing routing logs.

### Beyond Stability: Efficiency and Simplicity

The benefits of GSPO extend beyond merely fixing MoE instability; it streamlines RL infrastructure generally. Traditional methods often require recompiling likelihoods using specialized training engines to avoid precision mismatches between inference and training systems since they operate at granular token levels. Since GSPO operates at the sequence level with aggregated likelihoods, it is far more tolerant of minor precision discrepancies between inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) and training backends (like Megatron). This opens doors for more flexible deployment strategies, such as using pre-computed inference likelihoods directly for optimization or facilitating partially rolled-out multi-turn RL scenarios without expensive recomputation loops.

Ultimately, GSPO represents a shift toward more principled optimization where theoretical soundness drives practical success. By aligning the unit of reward (the whole sequence) with the unit of optimization (the whole sequence), it sidesteps the pitfalls that plagued previous approaches like GRPO when applied to sparse architectures. For practitioners aiming to scale RL on next-generation MoE models, GSPO provides a path forward that is not only stable but also simpler to implement and free from ad-hoc hacks like Routing Replay.



# Simplifying AI Infrastructure: How GSPO Bridges the Gap Between Training and Inference Engines

## The Hidden Cost of Precision Discrepancies in RL Training

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the gold standard for pushing Large Language Models (LLMs) toward human-level reasoning in complex domains like mathematics and coding. However, behind the scenes, implementing these algorithms requires a delicate balancing act between training engines and inference engines. Historically, this separation has introduced significant friction. When training models using algorithms like GRPO, engineers must rely on precise token-level probability calculations to determine importance ratios. This creates a bottleneck because training engines (such as Megatron) and inference engines (like SGLang or vLLM) often handle floating-point arithmetic differently. Consequently, practitioners are forced to recompute likelihoods within the training environment to maintain consistency, adding substantial computational overhead and complexity to the pipeline.

Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new algorithm designed not just for better performance, but for architectural simplicity. As detailed in recent research on Qwen3 models, GSPO shifts the fundamental unit of calculation from the token level to the sequence level. By defining importance ratios based on the overall likelihood of a generated response rather than individual tokens, GSPO inherently tolerates minor precision discrepancies between different software environments. This shift transforms a fragile engineering requirement into a robust feature, allowing teams to leverage pre-computed inference logs directly for optimization without expensive recomputation steps.

## Why Sequence-Level Likelihood Changes Everything

To understand why this matters, we must first look at how traditional methods handle probability distributions. Algorithms like GRPO calculate an importance ratio for every single token $t$ in a response $y$. Mathematically, this involves comparing $\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ against $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})$. While theoretically sound in isolation, this approach assumes perfect numerical consistency across every step of generation. If an inference engine returns a slightly different float value for a specific token probability compared to what the training engine expects due to precision differences or hardware variations, the cumulative error across thousands of tokens can destabilize the gradient estimates. This forces engineers to implement complex safeguards or simply force all computations back into the training engine's native format.

GSPO sidesteps this issue entirely by aggregating probabilities at the sequence level. The algorithm defines its importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ using the geometric mean of token probabilities:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

By focusing on the aggregate likelihood $\pi(y|x)$ rather than individual terms $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, GSPO smooths out local numerical noise. A small deviation in a single token's probability has a dampened effect on the final sequence-level ratio because it is averaged over the length of the response $|y_i|$. This mathematical property means that if an inference engine calculates a sequence likelihood with slight floating-point drift compared to a training engine, that drift does not catastrophically skew the advantage estimation used for policy updates. The system remains stable even when using approximate values provided by external inference services.

## Unlocking Efficient Deployment Pipelines

The practical implications of this stability are profound for modern AI infrastructure. In many advanced RL setups, such as partial rollouts or multi-turn conversations where data flows rapidly between systems, recomputing likelihoods in a separate training pass is prohibitively slow and resource-intensive. Previously, researchers had to build intricate bridges between their inference clusters and training clusters to ensure exact mathematical parity before feeding data into the optimizer.

With GSPO, this constraint vanishes. Because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision errors:
*   **Direct Integration:** Engineers can now feed raw outputs and associated likelihood scores directly from inference engines (like vLLM) into the RL trainer without needing a second pass through Megatron or similar frameworks solely for verification.
*   **Reduced Latency:** Eliminating the recomputation step significantly reduces latency between generating data and updating model parameters, accelerating feedback loops during training cycles.
*   **Cost Efficiency:** Running dual passes—one for generation and one for precise recalculations—consumes massive GPU hours unnecessarily; GSPO removes this redundancy entirely.
*   **Disaggregated Frameworks:** This capability paves the way for true train-inference disaggregation architectures where specialized nodes handle generation while others handle optimization without strict synchronization requirements regarding numerical precision.

Furthermore, this simplification extends beyond just efficiency; it enhances stability in other areas as well. The same logic that makes GSPO robust against precision errors also helps stabilize Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models by removing sensitivity to volatile expert activation patterns at individual token levels—a problem that previously required complex "Routing Replay" strategies just to achieve convergence.

## Conclusion: A Foundation for Scalable Intelligence

The introduction of Group Sequence Policy Optimization represents more than just an incremental improvement in loss functions; it is a structural evolution in how we build reinforcement learning systems for LLMs. By anchoring optimization decisions in sequence-level statistics rather than granular token-by-token comparisons, GSPO decouples algorithmic success from rigid engineering constraints around numerical precision. 

This shift lowers the barrier to entry for deploying large-scale RL pipelines and allows organizations to focus their resources on scaling capabilities rather than debugging infrastructure fragility. As seen in its application within Qwen3 models, GSPO offers a path toward simpler codebases, lower operational costs, and faster iteration speeds—all critical components needed to sustainably scale intelligence in future generations of AI systems.
