\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What is the exact mathematical reason why token-level importance sampling fails in GRPO for long sequences?
Question:
I am trying to understand the authors' claim that GRPO's objective is 'ill-posed'. They argue that because the importance weight $w_{i,t}$ is calculated for a single token $y_{i,t}$ sampled from $	heta_{old}$, it fails to perform proper distribution correction. Could someone explain this intuition using the formal definition of importance sampling provided in Section 3? Why exactly does averaging over a single sample per token lead to high variance accumulation over the sequence length $|y|$?
Answer:
The fundamental mathematical reason why token-level importance sampling fails in GRPO for long sequences, rendering the objective ill-posed, lies in the violation of the statistical independence required for valid importance sampling when applied to sequential dependencies. As established in the paper's Motivation section, the principle of importance sampling relies on estimating an expectation under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution, specifically $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} [\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)]$. Crucially, this estimator requires averaging over multiple independent samples from the behavior distribution to ensure the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ correctly captures the density ratio between distributions.

In GRPO, however, this principle is misapplied at the token level. The algorithm calculates an importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for each individual token $y_{i,t}$ based solely on that single sample: $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$. Mathematically, a single sample cannot represent a probability distribution; therefore, calculating a ratio of probabilities for just one outcome does not constitute a valid correction for the underlying distribution shift. Instead of correcting for off-policy bias, this single-sample ratio acts as a high-variance random variable. When you sum these terms over a sequence of length $|y|$ in the objective function $\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}$, you are essentially accumulating these independent sources of noise. Even if the variance at each step is moderate, the cumulative effect over many steps causes the total variance to explode as $|y|$ increases.

Furthermore, this issue is exacerbated by the structure of autoregressive generation. Each token depends on all preceding tokens ($y_{<t}$), creating strong correlations along the sequence path. By treating each token's transition as an isolated event with its own importance weight, GRPO ignores the joint probability mass of the entire trajectory. The paper argues that since rewards are assigned to complete sequences ($r(x,y)$), optimizing via token-level corrections creates a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization. This mismatch forces the optimizer to rely on noisy gradient estimates where small deviations in early tokens can disproportionately amplify errors later in the chain due to compounding effects. Consequently, as noted in the text, this accumulated noise leads to unstable training dynamics and eventually catastrophic model collapse, particularly in long-response scenarios where $|y|$ is large enough for these variances to become dominant factors rather than negligible noise. GSPO resolves this by shifting from token-level weights $w_{i,t}$ to sequence-level weights $s_i$, which correctly treat the entire response as a single sample from a joint distribution, thereby restoring statistical validity and reducing variance accumulation.

### How does the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ in GSPO's importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ affect the interpretation of clipping?
Question:
In Equation 6, GSPO defines the sequence-level importance ratio as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The paper mentions this is done to reduce variance and unify numerical ranges. However, since we clip $s_i(\theta)$ directly in Equation 5, doesn't raising the ratio to a fractional power compress extreme values differently depending on sequence length? How do we know that clipping at a fixed range (e.g., $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$) corresponds to a consistent 'degree of off-policyness' regardless of whether $|y_i|$ is short or long?
Answer:
The exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ in GSPO's importance ratio fundamentally transforms the metric from a cumulative probability product into an **average per-token likelihood ratio**, which ensures that the concept of "off-policyness" remains consistent regardless of sequence length. Without this normalization, the raw sequence likelihood ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}$ would behave multiplicatively: for independent token distributions, the expected value of the product scales exponentially with length, causing extremely long sequences to yield massive importance ratios even if every individual token was only slightly shifted. By taking the geometric mean via the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ power, GSPO converts this accumulation into an arithmetic average of log-probabilities, as explicitly shown in the exponential form $\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \dots \right)$. This normalization guarantees that a value of $s_i(\theta) = 1.05$ represents exactly a 5% increase in average per-token probability density for both a short response and a long one. Consequently, clipping $s_i(\theta)$ within a fixed range like $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ enforces a uniform constraint on the *average* deviation between policies across all steps in the sequence. If one were to clip without this exponent, a short sequence might be allowed to drift significantly in total probability mass because its cumulative ratio stays small simply due to having few terms, whereas a long sequence would trigger clipping immediately for minor per-token shifts due to compounding errors. Therefore, the fractional power aligns the scale of the importance ratio with the intuition behind standard PPO/GRPO clipping ranges (which operate on single-step ratios), allowing a fixed $\epsilon$ to represent a consistent degree of trustworthiness or "safe" off-policyness irrespective of how many tokens constitute the response. This is crucial for stability because it prevents long responses from dominating the loss landscape simply due to their length while simultaneously preventing short responses from being treated as highly reliable when they might actually contain significant deviations at specific critical tokens.

### Is GSPO-token mathematically equivalent to GSPO if advantages are uniform?
Question:
Section 4.3 introduces 'GSPO-token', which looks like a token-level objective but uses sequence-level weights via stop-gradient operations. The text states: 'GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical... when we set the advantages of all the tokens in the response $y_i$ to the same value.' If this is true, why bother with the complex stop-gradient formulation ($sg[s_i]$)? Does it offer any practical benefit for multi-turn RL or dynamic masking where advantages might *not* be uniform across tokens?
Answer:
The short answer is yes: when the advantages are uniform across all tokens in a response (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), the **GSPO-token** objective is mathematically and numerically identical to the standard **GSPO** objective. As explicitly stated in Section 4.3 of the paper, under this condition, the complex stop-gradient formulation yields the exact same gradient updates because the term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ reduces to $s_i(\theta)$, and summing identical terms simply scales the result by the sequence length, which cancels out with the $1/|y_i|$ normalization factor already present in both formulations.

So, why implement the more complex stop-gradient machinery if it collapses to GSPO in simple cases? The primary reason is not mathematical equivalence in that specific scenario, but rather **architectural flexibility for non-uniform advantage signals**, which is crucial for advanced applications like multi-turn reinforcement learning or dynamic masking. In standard GSPO, every token in a generated response shares a single global advantage score derived from the total sequence reward. However, in many realistic scenarios—such as a multi-turn conversation where only specific turns matter, or tasks where early errors should penalize later generations differently—we may wish to assign different advantage values to different tokens. Without GSPO-token, enforcing such fine-grained adjustments would be impossible within this framework because GSPO treats the entire sequence as a single atomic unit for optimization.

The stop-gradient operation ($\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$) plays a critical engineering role here that goes beyond mere numerical convenience. By detaching the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ before multiplying it by token-specific advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, GSPO-token ensures that the gradient flow remains consistent with the original GSPO philosophy: we want to weight all tokens in a sequence equally regarding *policy deviation*, but we want to weight them differently regarding *reward credit*. Specifically, Equation 16 shows that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is constructed such that its gradient contribution comes solely from the explicit $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$ term in the numerator, while $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a constant scalar multiplier determined by the overall sequence quality. This prevents high-variance interactions between local token probabilities and global sequence penalties that could destabilize training if calculated naively.

Furthermore, this design choice offers significant practical benefits for infrastructure and MoE training. As noted in Section 5.3 and 5.4, GSPO was designed specifically to avoid sensitivity to individual token likelihood fluctuations caused by Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing volatility or precision mismatches between inference and training engines. While standard GSPO ignores token-level probability variations entirely by averaging them into a sequence metric, GSPO-token preserves this stability for the importance weighting component (via $s_i$) while allowing researchers to inject custom reward signals at arbitrary granularities without breaking the algorithm's theoretical grounding. Essentially, GSPO-token acts as a generalized interface: it defaults perfectly to standard GSPO when rewards are uniform but unlocks sophisticated reward shaping capabilities when they are not, making it a versatile tool for future RL research without sacrificing the stability guarantees of the base algorithm.

### Why does MoE expert-volatility invalidate token-level ratios but not sequence-level ratios?
Question:
The paper claims that in MoE models, changing experts between $	heta$ and $	heta_{old}$ causes massive fluctuations in token-level ratios $w_{i,t}$, breaking GRPO. Yet, they claim sequence-level ratios $s_i$ remain stable because 'the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability.' Intuitively, if an expert switches for just one critical token in a chain-of-thought, shouldn't that cause a massive drop in total sequence probability $rac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{old}(y|x)}$? Why is the aggregate sequence likelihood less sensitive to individual expert swaps than the pointwise token likelihood?
Answer:
The intuition behind why expert-volatility destabilizes token-level ratios but leaves sequence-level ratios intact lies in the fundamental difference between pointwise probability products and their geometric mean, combined with the concept of functional redundancy in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. As described in the paper, when training MoE models with GRPO, the router selects different subsets of experts for the same token between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the new policy $\pi_\theta$. This switch causes the individual conditional probabilities $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ to fluctuate wildly because a completely different set of parameters is now responsible for generating that specific token. If a critical token happens to be routed to an expert that produces a low-probability output under the new configuration compared to the old one, the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can become extremely small or large, introducing massive variance into the gradient updates and potentially causing model collapse.

However, sequence-level ratios remain stable because they represent an aggregate measure of overall language modeling capability rather than sensitivity to specific routing decisions. Mathematically, GSPO defines the sequence importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as the geometric mean of the token-level ratios raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$, as shown in Equation 6: $$s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right).$$ Even if a single expert swap causes one or two tokens to have highly volatile ratios, these extreme values are averaged out across the entire sequence length. More importantly, as noted in Section 4.3 ("Benefit of GSPO for MoE Training"), an MoE model maintains its underlying language modeling capability regardless of which specific experts are active at any given moment. The model's ability to generate coherent text depends on the collective knowledge distributed across all experts; therefore, swapping one expert for another rarely results in a catastrophic drop in the *total* probability assigned to a valid response sequence. The product of many probabilities tends to smooth out local irregularities caused by routing volatility, whereas GRPO treats every local irregularity as a direct signal for optimization without smoothing. Consequently, while $w_{i,t}$ acts as a fragile indicator that breaks under expert-switching noise, $s_i$ acts as a robust indicator that reflects whether the response itself remains high-quality under the new policy, allowing GSPO to train MoE models stably without requiring complex workarounds like Routing Replay.

### Does GSPO actually solve the off-policy bias problem or just hide it?
Question:
Standard PPO/GRPO rely on clipping to bound off-policy errors. In GSPO, we clip entire sequences based on their geometric mean likelihood ratio. If a sequence has very low probability under $	heta$ but was sampled from $	heta_{old}$ (highly off-policy), clipping removes it entirely. However, isn't there still a risk that we are systematically ignoring regions of high-reward space simply because they require many small deviations from $	heta_{old}$ (which sum up to a low geometric mean)? Does this make GSPO conservative compared to methods that might accept slightly lower probabilities per token if they yield high rewards?
Answer:
The concern you raise touches on a critical trade-off in reinforcement learning: the balance between exploration and stability. Based on the provided paper, GSPO does not merely "hide" the off-policy bias; rather, it fundamentally redefines what constitutes a valid off-policy sample by shifting the unit of analysis from tokens to sequences. The authors argue that GRPO's token-level clipping is actually *more* prone to systematic error because it creates an ill-posed objective where high-variance noise accumulates, whereas GSPO's sequence-level approach acts as a rigorous filter that preserves the statistical integrity of the importance sampling estimator.

To understand why GSPO avoids the trap of ignoring high-reward regions, we must look at how the paper characterizes the failure mode of GRPO. The authors explicitly state that GRPO's objective is "ill-posed" because it applies importance weights at each token position $t$ independently. As noted in Section III (Motivation), this misapplication introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. When a sequence contains even a single token with a highly divergent probability ratio (due to stochasticity or minor policy shifts), GRPO's clipping mechanism struggles to handle it correctly because the variance explodes locally. The paper posits that this accumulation of noise eventually leads to "catastrophic model collapse," which is often irreversible. In this light, GSPO's aggressive filtering is not an arbitrary conservatism but a necessary corrective measure to eliminate these unstable gradients before they corrupt the policy update.

Regarding your specific worry about sequences requiring "many small deviations": GSPO addresses this via its definition of the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. As shown in Equation 6 of the paper, this ratio is calculated as the geometric mean of the token likelihood ratios:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By using the geometric mean, GSPO ensures that if *every* token deviates slightly from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, their cumulative effect results in a moderate deviation in $s_i(\theta)$ rather than an infinite explosion or immediate rejection. Conversely, if a sequence requires many small deviations that sum up to a very low total probability under $\pi_\theta$, then $s_i(\theta)$ will indeed drop below the clipping threshold $(1-\varepsilon)$, and the sequence will be excluded. However, from an importance sampling perspective described in Section III, excluding such samples is theoretically sound. Importance sampling relies on re-weighting samples drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to estimate expectations under $\pi_\theta$. If a sample has extremely low probability under $\pi_\theta$, it contributes almost nothing to the true expectation anyway; weighting it heavily would introduce massive variance, while discarding it reduces variance without losing significant information about high-probability regions under $\pi_\theta$.

Furthermore, the paper argues that GSPO is actually *less* conservative regarding useful exploration than GRPO because GRPO's token-level clipping often retains "noisy" gradients from sequences that are statistically unreliable under $\pi_\theta$. By clamping individual tokens while keeping others un-clamped within a bad sequence, GRPO can inadvertently optimize towards artifacts caused by random chance rather than genuine reward signals. GSPO enforces consistency across the entire response: either the whole trajectory represents a plausible shift in policy (and thus receives credit), or it represents an outlier that should be discarded entirely. This ensures that when a sequence passes through clipping and contributes to training, its contribution is robust and aligned with both the reward structure and the underlying probability distribution changes.

Finally, regarding your intuition about missing high-reward spaces: if a region yields high rewards but requires many small deviations from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO will likely capture these if those deviations remain within reasonable bounds such that $s_i(\theta)$ stays near 1. If they fall outside these bounds, it implies those trajectories are already so improbable under $\pi_\theta$ that they offer little signal for stable gradient descent regardless of their raw reward score. The paper emphasizes in Section IV.B (Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions) that despite clipping significantly more tokens than GRPO (by two orders of magnitude), GSPO achieves higher efficiency and performance precisely because it filters out these unstable gradients rather than trying to learn from them. Thus, GSPO solves the off-policy bias problem by ensuring that every gradient step comes from a statistically coherent sequence update, preventing the accumulation of noise that causes model collapse in prior methods like GRPO and PPO when scaled to large LLMs and MoE architectures.

Answer:
The intuition behind your observation is partially correct: the gradient formula for GSPO does resemble the structure of REINFORCE with baselines, specifically looking like an expectation of a scalar weight times an advantage times a score vector. However, GSPO is not merely REINFORCE with a different weighting scheme; it represents a fundamental shift in the **unit of analysis** from the token level to the sequence level to resolve a critical statistical flaw inherent in token-level importance sampling used by GRPO and standard PPO.

In standard REINFORCE or GRPO, the importance ratio is computed per token as $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$. As explained in the paper's Motivation section, applying this ratio at every token position creates high-variance noise because it treats each next-token prediction as an independent importance sampling event. When these ratios are multiplied together along a long sequence or summed across many tokens, small deviations compound rapidly. The paper argues that this misalignment—applying token-level corrections to a sequence-level reward—is what causes the instability and eventual model collapse seen in GRPO, especially for long responses or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models where expert activation volatility exacerbates these fluctuations.

GSPO resolves this by defining the importance ratio at the **sequence level**. The term $s_i(\theta)$ in your equation is not just a simple scalar weight; it is the geometric mean of the per-token ratios raised to a power that normalizes for length: $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|}$. By using this sequence-level likelihood ratio, GSPO ensures that the importance sampling correction aligns perfectly with the unit of the reward (which is assigned to the entire response). This alignment satisfies the theoretical requirement of importance sampling where the weight must reflect the deviation between distributions over the *same* random variable—in this case, the full sequence $y$, not individual tokens $y_t$.

Regarding variance reduction, it is precisely this shift from token-to-sequence that lowers variance compared to standard REINFORCE-style implementations. In GRPO/REINFORCE with token-level weights, if a single unlikely token generates a massive importance ratio (a common occurrence in long chains), it disproportionately skews the gradient for that specific trajectory. In GSPO, because $s_i(\theta)$ aggregates probabilities over the whole sequence before being normalized by length, extreme outliers at single positions are smoothed out. The paper explicitly notes that while GRPO clips individual tokens based on these volatile local weights, GSPO clips entire responses based on their global likelihood ratio. This means GSPO rejects trajectories that are globally "off-policy" rather than penalizing specific tokens within otherwise good trajectories. Consequently, even though GSPO might clip more tokens overall (as noted in their "Curious Observation" section), it retains fewer but much higher-quality gradient signals per update step because each retained sample carries a stable, low-variance weight representing its true probability mass relative to the old policy.

Furthermore, regarding your question about why GSPO outperforms GRPO so dramatically despite REINFORCE generally suffering from high variance: GSPO avoids the accumulation error entirely by never summing unstable per-token weights. The gradient derivation shows that while both methods eventually multiply an advantage term by log-probability gradients ($\nabla \log \pi$), GRPO sums terms like $\sum_t w_{i,t} \nabla \log \pi_{i,t}$ where each $w_{i,t}$ varies wildly. In contrast, GSPO pulls out a constant weight for all tokens in that sequence: $\sum_t s_i(\theta) \nabla \log \pi_{i,t} = s_i(\theta) \sum_t \nabla \log \pi_{i,t}$. Here, $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a uniform scaling factor for all tokens in response $y_i$, eliminating the multiplicative noise introduced by product-of-ratios dynamics. This structural simplification makes GSRO inherently more robust to long sequences and MoE architecture instabilities without requiring auxiliary tricks like "Routing Replay," which GRPO needs to artificially stabilize its token-level ratios. Thus, GSPO isn't just "better weighted" REINFORCE; it is REINFORCE reformulated at the correct granularity to satisfy statistical consistency principles for sequential decision making.
