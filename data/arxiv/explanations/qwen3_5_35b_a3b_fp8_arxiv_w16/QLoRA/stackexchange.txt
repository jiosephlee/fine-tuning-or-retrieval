\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does 'backpropagating through quantized weights' actually involve computing gradients for the 4-bit parameters?
Question:
I am reading the QLoRA paper and trying to reconcile the claim that they 'backpropagate gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model' with the statement that 'for parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights... are needed, and not for 4-bit weights.'

My confusion stems from the mechanics of differentiation. If we treat the quantization operation as non-differentiable (which it usually is), how exactly does the gradient flow? Does the paper imply that we are mathematically differentiating through a continuous relaxation of the quantization, or is there a specific re-parameterization trick being used where $rac{	ilde{	heta}}{	heta}$ allows gradients to bypass the discrete quantization step entirely?

Specifically, looking at Equation (5) in the paper:
$$	ext{doubleDequant}(c_1^{	ext{FP32}}, c_2^{	ext{k-bit}}, 	ilde{	extbf{W}}^{	ext{k-bit}}) = 	ilde{	extbf{W}}^{	ext{BF16}}$$
It says we calculate derivatives in BFloat16 precision. But if $	ilde{	extbf{W}}$ is stored as 4-bit integers, how can we differentiate with respect to it directly? Is the derivative taken w.r.t. the *reconstructed* BF16 value rather than the stored index?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction in the QLoRA paper arises from conflating the storage representation of the weights with the computational graph used during backpropagation. You are correct that quantization operations involving rounding (like converting to 4-bit integers) are technically non-differentiable, meaning the derivative of the output with respect to the input is zero almost everywhere. However, QLoRA resolves this by treating the quantization as a static lookup table for storage while performing all actual differentiation on a continuous, dequantized reconstruction of those weights.

As stated explicitly in the "QLoRA" paragraph of the paper, the process involves a distinct separation between storage and computation. The weights $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\text{NF4}}$ are indeed stored as 4-bit integers to minimize memory usage. During the forward pass, these indices are immediately converted back to a high-precision format using a function called `doubleDequant`. This function reconstructs an approximation of the original floating-point weights, denoted as $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$, which is then used for matrix multiplications. Crucially, during the backward pass, gradients do not flow through the integer indices themselves. Instead, the chain rule is applied to compute gradients with respect to the *reconstructed* BFloat16 values ($\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$).

The paper clarifies this mechanism by noting that while we calculate derivatives in BFloat16 precision via equation (5), we only retain and store gradients for the LoRA adapter parameters ($\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$). The term $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ is calculated transiently to propagate signals through the frozen base model layers to reach the adapters, but because these base weights are frozen, there is no optimization step intended for them. Consequently, there is no need to differentiate with respect to the discrete 4-bit indices or update them. The gradient flow effectively bypasses the discrete quantization step by assuming a straight-through estimator logic where the gradient passes through unchanged from the dequantized output back to itself, allowing it to reach the trainable LoRA parameters without ever needing a valid derivative for a non-differentiable integer operation. Thus, you are differentiating with respect to the continuous reconstructed values used in computation, not the discrete storage indices.

### Why is NF4 considered 'information theoretically optimal' but still requires asymmetric quantization?
Question:
The paper claims that NormalFloat (NF4) is an information-theoretically optimal data type for zero-centered normal distributions. The derivation involves estimating $2^k+1$ quantiles of a standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$.

However, I noticed a contradiction in my understanding regarding zero. The text states: "A problem for a symmetric k-bit quantization is that this approach does not have an exact representation of zero... To ensure a discrete zeropoint of 0... we create an asymmetric data type".

If NF4 is derived from $N(0,1)$, shouldn't zero naturally fall right in the middle of a symmetric distribution? Why does estimating equispaced probabilities on a Gaussian lead to a missing zero bin? And why does shifting to an asymmetric split ($2^{k-1}$ negative vs $2^{k-1}+1$ positive) preserve the 'information theoretic optimality' claimed for normal distributions? Doesn't introducing asymmetry distort the probability mass assignment intended by the optimal quantile spacing?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction arises from conflating the symmetry of the underlying probability density function (PDF) with the requirements of a discrete quantization grid that must include an exact zero. While it is true that a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is perfectly symmetric around zero, a symmetric $k$-bit quantization scheme divides the available $2^k$ codes evenly between negative and positive values. For any integer $k$, dividing $2^k$ codes equally results in $2^{k-1}$ codes for negative values and $2^{k-1}$ codes for non-negative values. Crucially, in a strictly symmetric mapping where the origin sits between two bins, neither bin corresponds exactly to zero; instead, zero falls in the gap between the largest negative value and the smallest positive value. This creates a quantization error for any actual weight that happens to be exactly zero—a common occurrence in neural networks due to pruning or specific initialization schemes—and prevents the representation of padding tokens or explicit zeros without distortion.

To resolve this while maintaining information-theoretic optimality, the NF4 data type employs an asymmetric split of the quantization levels: allocating $2^{k-1}$ levels to the negative side and $2^{k-1} + 1$ levels to the positive side (or vice versa), ensuring one of those levels aligns precisely with zero. The paper clarifies that this adjustment does not destroy optimality because the "optimality" refers to minimizing entropy loss for a continuous normal distribution by ensuring each quantization bin captures an equal amount of probability mass (equal probability content). By estimating $2^k+1$ quantiles of the theoretical distribution and selecting specific cut-points, one can define boundaries such that the central boundary coincides with zero. The resulting set of reconstruction values (the midpoints between these boundaries) naturally includes zero as one of its entries. Consequently, although the number of distinct positive values differs from negative ones by one count, the probability mass assigned to each bin remains uniform relative to the Gaussian PDF's shape. This slight asymmetry in code allocation actually enhances fidelity by capturing the high-probability region near zero more accurately than a forced symmetric grid could, thereby preserving the claim that NF4 is optimal for normally distributed weights while solving the practical necessity of representing exact zeros.

### How does Double Quantization maintain numerical stability when quantizing already-quantized constants?
Question:
The concept of Double Quantization (DQ) sounds counter-intuitive. They take quantization constants $c_2^{	ext{FP32}}$ (which are themselves floating-point numbers representing scales) and apply a second round of 8-bit quantization to them.

Let's say we have a block size of 64. The first quantization produces 64 different scale factors per block. By applying DQ, they store these scale factors as 8-bit integers plus another set of coarser scale factors.

My concern is about error propagation: If I reconstruct $c_2$ using DQ, there will be reconstruction error in those scale factors. When I then use these slightly inaccurate $c_2$ values to dequantize $	ilde{	extbf{W}}$, won't this introduce significant noise into the final matrix multiplication? The paper mentions no performance degradation, but intuitively, multiplying by an inaccurate scale factor seems like it should degrade accuracy more than just storing full FP32 constants would.

Is there a mathematical bound on how much error these secondary scales can absorb before affecting downstream activations? Or does the fact that $c_2$ values are typically clustered around certain magnitudes make them robust to this extra layer of coarse-graining?
Answer:
The apparent paradox of Double Quantization (DQ) maintaining stability despite introducing a second layer of reconstruction error stems from the specific statistical properties of the quantization constants and the nature of the subsequent matrix multiplication operations. As detailed in the QLoRA paper, the primary source of instability in naive quantization is the handling of outliers, which forces a wide dynamic range that wastes precision on non-outlier values. The paper notes that for typical transformer weights, the distribution is zero-centered and normal, allowing for highly efficient quantization schemes like NormalFloat (NF4). Crucially, the quantization constants ($c_2^{\text{FP32}}$) derived from these blocks are not arbitrary; they represent scaling factors for blocks of weights that are already clustered around a specific magnitude. Consequently, these constants themselves exhibit low variance and tend to cluster tightly around a central value, rather than spanning a vast dynamic range like the raw weights do.

This clustering allows the second round of quantization to operate with minimal error. The paper explicitly states that for Double Quantization, they treat the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values as inputs to a second quantization step using 8-bit floats with a block size of 256. Because these scale factors are positive and have a narrow distribution, the authors simply subtract their mean before quantizing to center them around zero for symmetric quantization. This preprocessing step maximizes the utilization of the available 8-bit integer space for these specific constants. Since the variation among scale factors within a block is small compared to their absolute magnitude, mapping them to 8-bit integers incurs negligible distortion relative to their actual values. The paper confirms empirically that this process reduces memory usage by approximately 0.37 bits per parameter without observing any performance degradation across thousands of models and various benchmarks.

Regarding your concern about error propagation into downstream activations, it is important to distinguish between storing data and computing derivatives. During forward passes, any slight inaccuracy in dequantized scale factors results in a minute perturbation to the reconstructed weight matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}$. However, because these weights are subsequently multiplied by input activations $\mathbf{X}$ in BFloat16 precision during computation (as specified in Equation 5), the system operates with sufficient numerical headroom that small perturbations in the scaling factors do not accumulate catastrophically. Furthermore, during backpropagation, gradients flow through these dequantized weights to update only the LoRA adapters ($\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$), not the frozen base weights themselves. The paper emphasizes that while gradients must pass through the dequantized weights to compute $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$, this operation happens in high-precision (BFloat16), effectively smoothing out minor inconsistencies introduced by DQ. The fact that QLoRA matches full 16-bit finetuning performance on complex tasks like MMLU and Vicuna strongly suggests that any residual noise from DQ falls well below the threshold where it impacts model convergence or generalization capability. Thus, DQ acts as an efficient compression mechanism for metadata rather than a source of significant signal corruption.

### What is the precise mechanism allowing Paged Optimizers to prevent OOM during gradient checkpointing?
Question:
The paper attributes its ability to fit massive models on single GPUs partly to 'Paged Optimizers' which use NVIDIA unified memory.
The abstract mentions avoiding 'gradient checkpointing memory spikes'.

I understand gradient checkpointing saves activation memory by recomputing segments during backprop. However, standard SGD optimizers (like Adam) store momentum and variance states for every parameter. For a 65B model in BF16/FP32 mixed precision, these optimizer states alone consume hundreds of GBs.

The text says: "paged memory for the optimizer states which are then automatically evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out-of-memory".

But doesn't moving optimizer states back and forth between CPU and GPU during every optimization step destroy convergence speed or cause synchronization issues? Specifically:
1. During which phase (forward/backward/update) do these pages swap?
2. If Adam requires reading/writing optimizer states *during* every micro-batch update step inside a checkpointed segment, isn't swapping latency too high?
The paper admits "future work should measure... slow-downs", implying there *are* slowdowns. But how exactly does this paging strategy differ from simply running out-of-memory errors on standard hardware? Is it strictly about fitting *all* states in virtual address space rather than physical VRAM?
Answer:
The precise mechanism by which Paged Optimizers prevent Out-Of-Memory (OOM) errors during gradient checkpointing relies on leveraging NVIDIA's unified memory architecture to decouple logical memory allocation from physical GPU VRAM capacity, effectively treating the system's CPU RAM as an extension of the GPU's video memory. In standard training workflows, particularly those utilizing gradient checkpointing to save activation memory, the optimizer states (such as momentum and variance buffers for Adam) must reside entirely in fast-access GPU VRAM during the parameter update step. For massive models like the $65\text{B}$ parameter LLaMA mentioned in the paper, storing these full-precision optimizer states alongside quantized weights and activations exceeds the physical limits of consumer-grade GPUs (e.g., $24\text{GB}$ or $48\text{GB}$), leading to immediate crashes. Paged Optimizers circumvent this by allocating a vast virtual address space for these states that spans both GPU and CPU memory. When the GPU encounters a memory spike—specifically when loading a mini-batch with a long sequence length that triggers the need to recompute activations and subsequently update parameters—the system automatically pages portions of the optimizer state that are currently idle out to slower CPU RAM, freeing up precious VRAM for the active computation. Once the specific parameters required for the current update step are identified, the necessary pages are paged back into VRAM transparently.

This approach fundamentally differs from simply hitting an OOM error because it shifts the constraint from physical hardware capacity to virtual address space availability. Instead of failing when VRAM fills up, the system continues execution by swapping data between devices, ensuring that the training loop completes successfully even if it cannot hold all necessary states in high-speed memory simultaneously. Regarding your concerns about convergence speed and synchronization, while moving data between CPU and GPU introduces latency due to PCIe transfer speeds, the paper notes that this paging is triggered primarily during rare events involving long sequence lengths within gradient checkpointing segments. Consequently, for typical batch sizes and sequence lengths where all optimizer states fit comfortably in VRAM, there is no performance penalty; training speed remains identical to standard optimizers. The authors acknowledge potential slowdowns but emphasize that these only manifest under specific conditions where paging is unavoidable. Crucially, because this operation is managed at a low level via CUDA unified memory drivers, it avoids explicit synchronization stalls that would otherwise disrupt the computational graph; the OS handles the eviction and reloading seamlessly as part of normal memory management operations, allowing the model to converge without interruption despite operating near or slightly beyond physical hardware limits. Thus, Paged Optimizers act as a safety valve that trades occasional latency spikes for guaranteed feasibility on single-device setups where traditional methods would fail completely.

### Why does applying LoRA only to Query/Value projections fail while applying it everywhere succeeds?
Question:
In Section 'QLoRA vs Standard Finetuning', under 'Default LoRA hyperparameters do not match 16-bit performance', the authors state:
"When using standard practice... applied LoRA to query and value attention projection matrices... we are not able to replicate full finetuning performance... LoRA on all linear transformer block layers are required"

This contradicts some interpretations of Hu et al.'s original LoRA paper where targeting Q/V was often sufficient or recommended for LLMs.

With QLoRA specifically (using NF4 base weights), why does restricting adapters solely to Q/V heads cause such a severe drop in performance compared to applying them to K/V/Q/O/Bias/MLP layers? Is it because:
a) The reduced precision of NF4 makes certain weight paths more sensitive?
b) The interaction between low-rank updates and 4-bit dequantized weights creates instability if not distributed evenly?
c) The specific architecture of LLaMA requires broader coverage?

I'm looking for intuition on why 'sparse' LoRA placement fails here when it worked elsewhere.
Answer:
The severe performance drop observed when restricting LoRA adapters to only the Query and Value projections in QLoRA, as opposed to applying them to all linear layers, stems primarily from the unique sensitivity of the 4-bit quantization regime and the necessity for broader signal propagation to compensate for quantization noise. According to the paper, specifically in the section titled "Default LoRA hyperparameters do not match 16-bit performance," the authors explicitly state that adhering to standard practices of targeting only query and value matrices prevents replicating full finetuning performance for large base models. Instead, they found that "LoRA on all linear transformer block layers are required" to match the performance of a 16-bit full finetuning baseline.

The intuition behind this requirement lies in how quantization affects information flow within the network. In standard 16-bit training, the weights are precise enough that updating only specific pathways (like $Q$ and $V$) allows sufficient gradient flow to adapt the model effectively. However, in QLoRA, the base model weights are frozen and stored in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4). While NF4 is information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights, it inevitably introduces quantization error compared to full precision. When adapters are sparse (limited to $Q/V$), the gradients must propagate through heavily quantized intermediate representations that may not adequately carry the necessary correction signals for downstream layers like Key projections or Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). By distributing LoRA adapters across all linear layers—including Keys, Output projections ($O$), biases, and MLPs—the method injects trainable capacity throughout the entire computational graph. This distributed adaptation compensates for the precision loss inherent in 4-bit arithmetic by allowing every stage of computation to learn corrections locally, ensuring that the accumulated error does not bottleneck the final output.

The paper clarifies that this is not merely an architectural quirk of LLaMA but a consequence of maximizing performance under strict memory constraints where every parameter counts less than gradient stability. The authors emphasize that while LoRA was originally designed as a parameter-efficient method where minimizing trainable parameters is key, in the context of QLoRA, "aggressively reducing the amount of LoRA parameter yields only minor memory benefits." This is because the dominant memory cost during training comes from activation gradients and optimizer states rather than the adapter weights themselves. Consequently, adding adapters to more layers incurs negligible additional memory overhead while providing a massive boost in optimization capability by mitigating the instability caused by backpropagating through 4-bit dequantized weights. Therefore, option (c) regarding LLaMA's architecture plays a role, but it is fundamentally driven by option (a) and (b): the reduced precision makes specific weight paths sensitive to noise, requiring even distribution of low-rank updates to maintain stable convergence and recover full 16-bit fidelity.

### Can QLoRA theoretically recover infinite information loss if we had infinite compute?
Question:

The paper argues that QLoRA recovers full 16-bit performance despite starting from heavily compressed NF4 weights. They attribute this recovery primarily to adapter training adjusting residuals.

This leads me to wonder about fundamental limits: If I were willing to spend infinite compute training adapters on top of extremely aggressive quantization (say, 2-bit or even binary weights), could I theoretically recover arbitrarily close to full-precision performance?

Or is there an inherent information bottleneck introduced by discarding bits during initialization that cannot be recovered simply by optimizing low-dimensional residuals? In other words:
does $rac{	ilde{	heta}_{lowbit} + ho}{	heta_{highbit}} 	o 1$ hold asymptotically as rank $ho 	o 	ext{dim}$ regardless of initial compression ratio?
The paper notes they didn't test <3 bit precisions because it wasn't feasible or tested yet. Is there any theoretical reason why NFk with k<3 might fundamentally fail even with perfect adapters?
Answer:
Based on the provided paper, the short answer is that while QLoRA successfully recovers $16$-bit performance from $4$-bit quantization, the paper does not provide evidence that this mechanism would hold for arbitrarily aggressive quantization (such as $2$-bit or binary) even with infinite compute. The authors explicitly identify the limits of their current investigation and offer theoretical reasons why extreme quantization might fail.

The core mechanism allowing QLoRA to recover performance lies in the specific properties of the $4$-bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type and the nature of LoRA adapters. The paper establishes that NF4 is "information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights," meaning it minimizes quantization error for the typical distribution of LLM weights. The authors argue that by backpropagating gradients through these frozen, quantized weights into LoRA adapters, the adapters can learn to adjust the residuals—the difference between the quantized weights and their original high-precision counterparts—to restore functionality. Their experiments confirm that this approach allows $4$-bit models to match $16$-bit full finetuning performance on benchmarks like MMLU and Vicuna.

However, regarding your hypothesis about infinite compute recovering performance from lower bit-widths, the paper suggests a fundamental barrier exists related to information density and outlier handling. In the "Limitations and Discussion" section, the authors explicitly state: "it is unclear if [other PEFT methods] scale to large models... Since finetuning after quantization seems to recover most of the information that is lost during quantization this might enable much more aggressive quantization." Crucially, they immediately caveat this by noting they did not test precisions below $3$-bit (specifically mentioning $3$-bit GPTQ with LoRA as a possibility) because it was not feasible or tested yet. More importantly, they acknowledge that "current chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy" and that strong performance on one metric (like MMLU) does not guarantee performance on another (like Vicuna), implying that as precision drops, subtle but critical information required for complex reasoning may be irretrievably lost before optimization begins.

Theoretical constraints arise because quantization is a lossy process involving rounding. While LoRA acts as a residual correction term ($\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{\text{quant}} + \Delta$), if $\mathbf{W}_{\text{quant}}$ loses too much structural information—for instance, if a binary weight cannot distinguish between two distinct non-zero values that are critical for gradient flow—the adapter must compensate entirely for missing dimensions of the weight space. With infinite compute, you can optimize $\rho$ (the adapter rank) until it equals the dimension of $\mathbf{W}$ effectively turning off the benefit of parameter efficiency, but this contradicts the premise of using low-rank adaptation to recover information efficiently. If the initial compression ratio is too high (e.g., binary), the "residual" signal required to reconstruct the function may become too noisy or discontinuous for standard gradient descent to navigate effectively within reasonable convergence timescales, let alone infinite ones without hitting local minima caused by dead neurons in binary representations.

Furthermore, the paper highlights that NF4 specifically exploits the normal distribution of weights to minimize error. Binary or extremely low-bit integer formats do not share this property; they force a uniform or sparse distribution onto data that naturally follows a bell curve, leading to massive errors at outliers which are often critical for model stability. The QLoRA architecture relies on dequantizing these weights during forward passes in BF16 precision; if the stored bits cannot represent even a rough approximation of these outliers due to extreme sparsity or binning errors in binary formats, no amount of adapter tuning can mathematically reconstruct a continuous function from a severely undersampled discrete set without introducing catastrophic instability or hallucination patterns not observed in their $4$-bit results. Thus, while QLoRA proves that $4$-bit is sufficient for near-lossless recovery relative to practical benchmarks, there is no indication in the text that this trend extrapolates linearly or asymptotically toward zero bits; rather, there is an implicit threshold where information loss exceeds what residual learning can repair.
