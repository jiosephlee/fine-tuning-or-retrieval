Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Foundational Concepts – From Quantization to LoRA

Before engaging with the specific mechanics of \textsc{QLoRA}, it is essential to ground oneself in the fundamental principles that govern the manipulation of neural network representations. Modern large language models (LLMs) operate on vast scales, necessitating rigorous management of memory and computational resources. Two primary pillars support the architecture of parameter-efficient finetuning within constrained environments: quantization, which compresses static weights, and adapter modules, which introduce dynamic, trainable parameters. This chapter establishes the intuitive and mechanical foundations for these concepts as presented in the foundational literature regarding \textsc{QLoRA}. Specifically, we must understand how quantization transforms continuous floating-point data into discrete integers without catastrophic loss of information, how block-wise strategies address numerical instability caused by extreme values, and how Low-Rank Adapters leverage factorized projections to minimize the surface area of trainable parameters while maintaining expressivity. Finally, we will dissect the memory economics of these processes to appreciate why traditional parameter counting fails to capture the true constraints of deep learning training pipelines.

## Principles of Block-Wise K-Bit Quantization and Rescaling Strategies

At its core, quantization is a process of mapping a high-dimensional representation space onto a coarser grid with reduced resolution. In the context of deep learning, this typically involves transforming data stored in high-precision formats, such as 32-bit Floating Point ($\text{FP32}$), into formats utilizing significantly fewer bits per element, such as 8-bit Integer ($\text{INT8}$) or 4-bit formats. The objective is to store more parameters within a fixed physical memory capacity. Mathematically, let us denote an input tensor represented in $\text{FP32}$ format as $\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}$. To convert this into a lower-precision integer representation, such as an $\text{INT8}$ tensor denoted as $\mathbf{X}^{\text{INT8}}$, one must apply a transformation that maps the continuous range of values in $\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}$ to a discrete set of integers bounded by the limits of the target dtype.

The standard mechanism for this conversion involves a rescaling operation governed by a multiplicative constant known as the quantization constant or scale factor, denoted herein as $c$. The transformation begins by identifying the absolute maximum magnitude present within the input tensor, expressed as $\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})$. This value defines the dynamic range that must fit within the target integer bounds. For an $\text{INT8}$ representation operating within a signed range of $[-127, 127]$, every element $x_{ij}$ in the original tensor is scaled by dividing by this maximum magnitude and multiplying by the upper bound limit (127). Formally, this operation is written as:

$$
\mathbf{X}^{\text{INT8}} = \text{round}\left( \frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})} \cdot \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}} \right)
$$

By defining $c^{\text{FP32}} = \frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}$, we can view this succinctly as applying a global scale $c$ to reshape the entire data distribution before snapping it to nearby integers:

$$
\mathbf{T}_{target\_bits} = \lfloor c_{scale} \cdot X_{original\_dtype} + offset \rfloor
$$

This rescaling strategy ensures that if any valid number exists in our original distribution near zero or near infinity (within reason), there corresponds a distinct bucket or index in our lower-precision domain capable representing it. Without this scaling—if one attempted to map raw values directly—the dynamic range would be misaligned entirely outside the representable limits of an 8-bit integer unless all numbers happened to naturally fall between 0 and 1 or -1 and 0 depending on encoding conventions. Thus, calculating $c$ acts as calibrating instrument; it stretches or shrinks our signal so that it occupies precisely one unit interval inside our digital container before compression artifacts are introduced via rounding.
