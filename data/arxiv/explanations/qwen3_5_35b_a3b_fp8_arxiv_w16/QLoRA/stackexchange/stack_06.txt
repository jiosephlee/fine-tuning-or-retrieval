\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Can QLoRA theoretically recover infinite information loss if we had infinite compute?
Question:

The paper argues that QLoRA recovers full 16-bit performance despite starting from heavily compressed NF4 weights. They attribute this recovery primarily to adapter training adjusting residuals.

This leads me to wonder about fundamental limits: If I were willing to spend infinite compute training adapters on top of extremely aggressive quantization (say, 2-bit or even binary weights), could I theoretically recover arbitrarily close to full-precision performance?

Answer:
Based on the provided paper, the short answer is that while QLoRA successfully recovers $16$-bit performance from $4$-bit quantization, the paper does not provide evidence that this mechanism would hold for arbitrarily aggressive quantization (such as $2$-bit or binary) even with infinite compute. The authors explicitly identify the limits of their current investigation and offer theoretical reasons why extreme quantization might fail.

The core mechanism allowing QLoRA to recover performance lies in the specific properties of the $4$-bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type and the nature of LoRA adapters. The paper establishes that NF4 is "information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights," meaning it minimizes quantization error for the typical distribution of LLM weights. The authors argue that by backpropagating gradients through these frozen, quantized weights into LoRA adapters, the adapters can learn to adjust the residuals—the difference between the quantized weights and their original high-precision counterparts—to restore functionality. Their experiments confirm that this approach allows $4$-bit models to match $16$-bit full finetuning performance on benchmarks like MMLU and Vicuna.

However, regarding your hypothesis about infinite compute recovering performance from lower bit-widths, the paper suggests a fundamental barrier exists related to information density and outlier handling. In the "Limitations and Discussion" section, the authors explicitly state: "it is unclear if [other PEFT methods] scale to large models... Since finetuning after quantization seems to recover most of the information that is lost during quantization this might enable much more aggressive quantization." Crucially, they immediately caveat this by noting they did not test precisions below $3$-bit (specifically mentioning $3$-bit GPTQ with LoRA as a possibility) because it was not feasible or tested yet. More importantly, they acknowledge that "current chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy" and that strong performance on one metric (like MMLU) does not guarantee performance on another (like Vicuna), implying that as precision drops, subtle but critical information required for complex reasoning may be irretrievably lost before optimization begins.

Theoretical constraints arise because quantization is a lossy process involving rounding. While LoRA acts as a residual correction term ($\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{\text{quant}} + \Delta$), if $\mathbf{W}_{\text{quant}}$ loses too much structural information—for instance, if a binary weight cannot distinguish between two distinct non-zero values that are critical for gradient flow—the adapter must compensate entirely for missing dimensions of the weight space. With infinite compute, you can optimize $\rho$ (the adapter rank) until it equals the dimension of $\mathbf{W}$ effectively turning off the benefit of parameter efficiency, but this contradicts the premise of using low-rank adaptation to recover information efficiently. If the initial compression ratio is too high (e.g., binary), the "residual" signal required to reconstruct the function may become too noisy or discontinuous for standard gradient descent to navigate effectively within reasonable convergence timescales, let alone infinite ones without hitting local minima caused by dead neurons in binary representations.

Furthermore, the paper highlights that NF4 specifically exploits the normal distribution of weights to minimize error. Binary or extremely low-bit integer formats do not share this property; they force a uniform or sparse distribution onto data that naturally follows a bell curve, leading to massive errors at outliers which are often critical for model stability. The QLoRA architecture relies on dequantizing these weights during forward passes in BF16 precision; if the stored bits cannot represent even a rough approximation of these outliers due to extreme sparsity or binning errors in binary formats, no amount of adapter tuning can mathematically reconstruct a continuous function from a severely undersampled discrete set without introducing catastrophic instability or hallucination patterns not observed in their $4$-bit results. Thus, while QLoRA proves that $4$-bit is sufficient for near-lossless recovery relative to practical benchmarks, there is no indication in the text that this trend extrapolates linearly or asymptotically toward zero bits; rather, there is an implicit threshold where information loss exceeds what residual learning can repair.
