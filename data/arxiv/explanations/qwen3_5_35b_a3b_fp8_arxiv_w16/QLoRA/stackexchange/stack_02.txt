\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why is NF4 considered 'information theoretically optimal' but still requires asymmetric quantization?
Question:
The paper claims that NormalFloat (NF4) is an information-theoretically optimal data type for zero-centered normal distributions. The derivation involves estimating $2^k+1$ quantiles of a standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$.

However, I noticed a contradiction in my understanding regarding zero. The text states: "A problem for a symmetric k-bit quantization is that this approach does not have an exact representation of zero... To ensure a discrete zeropoint of 0... we create an asymmetric data type".

If NF4 is derived from $N(0,1)$, shouldn't zero naturally fall right in the middle of a symmetric distribution? Why does estimating equispaced probabilities on a Gaussian lead to a missing zero bin? And why does shifting to an asymmetric split ($2^{k-1}$ negative vs $2^{k-1}+1$ positive) preserve the 'information theoretic optimality' claimed for normal distributions? Doesn't introducing asymmetry distort the probability mass assignment intended by the optimal quantile spacing?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction arises from conflating the symmetry of the underlying probability density function (PDF) with the requirements of a discrete quantization grid that must include an exact zero. While it is true that a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is perfectly symmetric around zero, a symmetric $k$-bit quantization scheme divides the available $2^k$ codes evenly between negative and positive values. For any integer $k$, dividing $2^k$ codes equally results in $2^{k-1}$ codes for negative values and $2^{k-1}$ codes for non-negative values. Crucially, in a strictly symmetric mapping where the origin sits between two bins, neither bin corresponds exactly to zero; instead, zero falls in the gap between the largest negative value and the smallest positive value. This creates a quantization error for any actual weight that happens to be exactly zero—a common occurrence in neural networks due to pruning or specific initialization schemes—and prevents the representation of padding tokens or explicit zeros without distortion.

To resolve this while maintaining information-theoretic optimality, the NF4 data type employs an asymmetric split of the quantization levels: allocating $2^{k-1}$ levels to the negative side and $2^{k-1} + 1$ levels to the positive side (or vice versa), ensuring one of those levels aligns precisely with zero. The paper clarifies that this adjustment does not destroy optimality because the "optimality" refers to minimizing entropy loss for a continuous normal distribution by ensuring each quantization bin captures an equal amount of probability mass (equal probability content). By estimating $2^k+1$ quantiles of the theoretical distribution and selecting specific cut-points, one can define boundaries such that the central boundary coincides with zero. The resulting set of reconstruction values (the midpoints between these boundaries) naturally includes zero as one of its entries. Consequently, although the number of distinct positive values differs from negative ones by one count, the probability mass assigned to each bin remains uniform relative to the Gaussian PDF's shape. This slight asymmetry in code allocation actually enhances fidelity by capturing the high-probability region near zero more accurately than a forced symmetric grid could, thereby preserving the claim that NF4 is optimal for normally distributed weights while solving the practical necessity of representing exact zeros.
