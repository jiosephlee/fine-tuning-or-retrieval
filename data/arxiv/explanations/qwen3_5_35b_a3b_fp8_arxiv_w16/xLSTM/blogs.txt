# The Resurrection of the LSTM: Why xLSTM is Here to Challenge Transformers

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was the undisputed king of sequence modeling. Introduced in the 1990s to solve the vanishing gradient problem, LSTMs powered early breakthroughs in speech recognition, machine translation, and even the first generations of Large Language Models (LLMs). Their architecture relied on a clever concept called the "constant error carousel," allowing information to flow through time without degrading. However, with the arrival of Transformers in 2017, which offered parallelizable self-attention mechanisms, LSTMs seemed destined for obsolescence. Transformers quickly outpaced LSTMs in scalability and speed, leading many to believe that recurrent architectures had reached their limit. But a new development suggests that history may repeat itself: researchers have revitalized the LSTM by addressing its fundamental flaws, creating a model called xLSTM that competes directly with modern giants like GPT and Mamba.

The primary reason LSTMs fell behind wasn't necessarily a flaw in their core logic, but rather three specific limitations that hindered their evolution at scale. First, traditional LSTMs struggled to revise storage decisions; once a value was written into memory, it was difficult for the model to overwrite it if a better candidate appeared later in the sequence. Second, they suffered from limited storage capacity because their cell states were restricted to scalars or simple vectors, forcing them to compress complex information into narrow channels. Finally, and perhaps most critically for modern hardware efficiency, standard LSTMs lacked parallelizability due to their reliance on hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections that forced strictly sequential processing. The xLSTM architecture tackles each of these issues head-on with novel mathematical innovations.

To fix the inability to revise storage decisions and improve stability, xLSTM introduces **exponential gating**. While classic LSTMs use sigmoid functions to modulate information flow between 0 and 1, xLSTM replaces these with exponential activation functions for input and forget gates. This change allows for more dynamic updates but introduces numerical instability risks due to potentially exploding values. To counteract this without altering the network's output or gradients, the authors incorporate stabilizer states that track log-sums of gate activations. Furthermore, they introduce a normalizer state that tracks the cumulative influence of gates over time. This combination ensures that the model can effectively rewrite memories—a capability essential for tasks requiring precise state tracking—and maintains robustness during training.

Addressing the issue of limited storage capacity requires looking beyond scalar values entirely. Traditional LSTMs store information in single numbers per neuron, which acts as a bottleneck when trying to remember intricate relationships or rare tokens within long contexts. xLSTM solves this by introducing **matrix memory**, specifically within its variant known as mLSTM (matrix LSTM). Instead of updating a scalar cell state $c_t$, mLSTM updates a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ representing correlations between feature dimensions. This update follows a covariance rule similar to associative memories: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. By storing key-value pairs as outer products in a matrix rather than compressed scalars, mLSTM dramatically increases its ability to retrieve specific information from long sequences without losing fidelity. This structure mimics parts of Transformer attention but retains the linear complexity advantages of recurrent models.

However, expanding memory alone isn't enough; we must also ensure these models can run efficiently on modern GPUs alongside Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs). The introduction of matrix memory naturally leads to full parallelizability because mLSTM eliminates hidden-to-hidden dependencies between time steps regarding memory mixing. In contrast, another variant called sLSTM (scalar LSTM) retains some memory mixing capabilities across cells using exponential gating but sacrifices direct parallelization due to its sequential nature. Fortunately, researchers have engineered fast CUDA kernels for sLSTM that keep it within twice the latency of mLSTM while offering superior expressiveness for certain state-tracking tasks where mixing across cells is beneficial. By integrating these blocks into residual backbones similar to those used in transformers—specifically utilizing pre-up-projection for mLSTM and post-up-projection for sLSTM—the resulting xLSTM architecture achieves both high performance and architectural flexibility.

The experimental results validate this theoretical leap forward significantly surpassing expectations for recurrent models trained on billions of parameters. When evaluated on synthetic tasks involving formal languages and associative recall (where models must remember key-value pairs over long distances), xLSTM outperformed standard RNNs and even matched specialized linear attention mechanisms like RetNet and RWKV on tasks requiring true state tracking—a feat previously thought impossible for non-recurrent models lacking memory mixing capabilities on long ranges. More impressively, in real-world language modeling benchmarks using 300 billion tokens from SlimPajama datasets across various model sizes ranging from 125 million to 1.3 billion parameters, xLSTM consistently achieved lower perplexity scores than comparable Transformers (like Llama), State Space Models (like Mamba), and other RNN variants (like RWKV). Notably, xLSTM maintained strong performance even when extrapolating context lengths far beyond their training windows up to 16k tokens where others began struggling with degradation curves typical of finite-context architectures relying solely on positional encodings or sliding windows without explicit memory revision mechanisms.

# Inside the Black Box: Understanding sLSTM vs. mLSTM

The landscape of artificial intelligence recently witnessed a shift away from the dominance of Transformers, driven by the need for more efficient and scalable sequence models. While Transformers revolutionized natural language processing with their parallelizable self-attention mechanisms, they come with significant computational costs as context windows grow. Enter **xLSTM** (Extended Long Short-Term Memory), a research breakthrough that revitalizes the classic LSTM architecture by addressing its historical bottlenecks. By integrating modern techniques like exponential gating and advanced memory structures, xLSTM challenges the status quo, offering performance comparable to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) while retaining the efficiency of recurrent networks.

At the heart of this innovation lies a dual-engine design consisting of two distinct variants: **sLSTM** and **mLSTM**. These are not merely slight tweaks to the original Long Short-Term Memory but fundamental architectural evolutions designed to solve specific limitations inherent in traditional RNNs. The original LSTM struggled with three main issues: it could not easily revise storage decisions once made, it had limited storage capacity due to scalar memory cells, and it lacked parallelizability due to hidden-state dependencies. xLSTM tackles these problems head-on by introducing two specialized blocks that complement each other within a unified framework.

### The Power of Revision: sLSTM

The first engine, **sLSTM**, focuses on enhancing the expressiveness of memory through a mechanism called **exponential gating**. Traditional LSTMs use sigmoid gates to control information flow, which limits their ability to drastically alter stored information once committed. sLSTM replaces these with exponential activation functions for the input and forget gates ($i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$). This modification allows the model to effectively "revise" storage decisions, solving what researchers call the *Nearest Neighbor Search* problem where finding a better match requires overriding previous entries.

However, exponential functions can lead to numerical instability if left unchecked. To prevent overflow errors during training, sLSTM employs a sophisticated stabilization technique involving a max-stabilizer state ($m_t$). Furthermore, unlike standard LSTMs where memory is just updated via addition, sLSTM introduces a unique **memory mixing** capability across multiple cells within a head. This allows the model to create complex internal representations by blending information between different memory units. While this makes sLSTM powerful for tasks requiring state tracking—such as understanding code or following long narratives—it comes at the cost of serial processing speed because hidden states depend on previous hidden states in a chain-like fashion.

### Scaling Capacity: mLSTM

If sLSTM is designed for precision and revision, **mLSTM** is engineered for sheer capacity and speed. The primary limitation of standard LSTMs is that their memory cell $c_t$ is a single scalar value per unit. To break this bottleneck, mLSTM upgrades the memory cell from a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a full matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This transformation turns the memory cell into a dynamic repository capable of storing key-value pairs simultaneously.

The update rule for mLSTM mimics the concept of associative memories using a **covariance update**:
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t (\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top)
$$
Here, $\mathbf{v}_t$ represents values and $\mathbf{k}_t$ represents keys projected from the input at time $t$. The forget gate $f_t$ acts as a decay factor (similar to forgetting old associations), while the input gate $i_t$ controls how strongly new associations are written into the matrix. Because this matrix update relies on outer products rather than hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections, mLSTM eliminates the dependency on previous hidden states for memory mixing. Consequently, mLSTM is **fully parallelizable**, allowing it to process entire sequences simultaneously on GPUs without waiting for sequential steps—a critical advantage over traditional RNNs and even some newer SSMs regarding context length handling.

### Synergy in Action: The xLSTM Architecture

The true magic of xLSTM emerges when these two architectures are integrated into residual blocks and stacked into deeper networks. Researchers found that neither variant alone was sufficient to dominate all tasks; instead, they offer complementary strengths suitable for different parts of an architecture. The resulting hybrid blocks are denoted as `xLSTM[a:b]`, representing the ratio of mLSTM blocks ($a$) to sLSTM blocks ($b$).

In practice, architectures often employ mostly mLSTM blocks (e.g., `xLSTM[7:1]`) to leverage high-throughput parallel processing for general language modeling tasks where massive context retention is key. However, incorporating even a few sLSTM blocks (`xLSTM[1:0]` or mixed ratios) injects essential state-tracking capabilities that pure parallel models sometimes lack in formal language tasks or scenarios requiring strict entity tracking over long distances. Experimental results show that these hybrid models significantly outperform standalone LSTMs and rival Transformers on benchmarks ranging from synthetic formal languages (like parity checks) to real-world datasets like SlimPajama and PALOMA. Notably, xLSTM maintains low perplexity even when extrapolating beyond training context lengths (up to 16k tokens), demonstrating robustness that Transformers struggle with due to quadratic complexity growth.

### Conclusion

The introduction of xLSTM marks a pivotal moment in sequence modeling history by proving that recurrent architectures can compete with modern Transformers when properly evolved. By splitting responsibilities between sLSTM's exponential gating for revisiting memories and mLXM's matrix-based covariance updates for massive capacity, xL STM offers a flexible toolkit tailored for diverse AI needs—from efficient edge computing with constant memory complexity to high-performance large language models capable of scaling into billions of parameters as evidenced by their superior scaling laws compared to Mamba or RWKV counterparts in initial trials. As optimization efforts continue on CUDA kernels specifically designed for these structures, we may soon see xL STM become a foundational component in the next generation of AI systems where efficiency meets raw predictive power without sacrificing interpretability or flexibility.



# Beyond Parallelism: How xLSTM Achieves Speed Without Sacrificing Accuracy

For years, the deep learning community has viewed the choice between accuracy and efficiency as a zero-sum game. While Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) like the classic Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) offered linear time complexity relative to sequence length, they struggled with training speed due to their inherent sequential dependencies. Conversely, Transformers revolutionized language modeling with massive parallelization but paid a steep price in quadratic memory and compute costs as context windows grew. The xLSTM paper asks a pivotal question: Can we retain the interpretability and linear scalability of RNNs while matching the raw performance of Transformers? The answer lies in a dual-path architecture that cleverly balances memory mixing with parallelizable matrix operations.

At its core, xLSTM addresses three historical limitations of traditional LSTMs: the inability to revise storage decisions, limited scalar storage capacity, and a lack of parallelizability caused by hidden-hidden connections. To solve these issues, the authors introduce two distinct architectural variants: sLSTM and mLSTM. These variants are integrated into residual blocks and stacked to form complete architectures capable of competing with billion-parameter models. The innovation here is not just in adding new layers, but in fundamentally rewriting how information flows through the memory cell using exponential gating and novel update rules.

The sLSTM variant focuses on enhancing expressiveness through "memory mixing." By introducing exponential gating functions alongside a stabilizer state to prevent numerical overflow, sLSTM gains the ability to revisit and correct previous storage decisions—a capability often referred to as state tracking. This is achieved through recurrent connections between hidden states from one time step to the next ($h_{t-1}$ influencing $h_t$). While this makes sLSTM theoretically more powerful for tasks requiring strict state tracking, such as solving formal grammar problems or evaluating code logic, it comes at a cost: it remains inherently sequential. You cannot process all steps in parallel because calculating step $t$ requires the exact output of step $t-1$. However, the authors note that despite this limitation, they have engineered a fast CUDA implementation that runs less than twice as slow as their parallel counterpart, making it viable for many applications where memory mixing is critical.

On the other side of the spectrum lies mLSTM, designed specifically to shatter the parallelization barrier without sacrificing capacity. Instead of storing information in a single scalar value per neuron, mLSTM upgrades the memory cell to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This structural shift allows for a covariance update rule where key-value pairs are stored via outer products ($\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$). Because this formulation eliminates hidden-hidden recurrent connections (memory mixing), the entire sequence can be computed simultaneously on modern hardware like GPUs. The trade-off here is computational intensity; updating a $d \times d$ matrix is heavier than updating a scalar. Yet, because these matrix operations map efficiently onto tensor cores and avoid memory bandwidth bottlenecks associated with large caches in Transformers, mLSTM achieves near-constant wall-clock time regardless of sequence length during inference.

The true power of xLSTM emerges when combining these two approaches into hybrid architectures denoted as xLSTM[$a:b$], representing the ratio of mLSTM blocks to sLSTM blocks. Experimental results on datasets ranging from 15 billion to 300 billion tokens reveal that these hybrids consistently outperform state-of-the-art Transformers (like Llama) and State Space Models (like Mamba) in terms of validation perplexity. For instance, on long-range context tasks involving up to 16k tokens, xLSTM models maintained significantly lower perplexity than their Transformer counterparts, whose performance degrades quadratically with length. Furthermore, in direct comparisons on 571 diverse text domains from the PALOMA benchmark, xLSTM achieved lower perplexity in nearly every category compared to leading RNNs and SSMs.

Perhaps most compelling for real-world deployment is the inference profile described in the paper regarding generation times and throughput. Unlike Transformers which suffer from quadratic scaling during autoregressive decoding due to growing key-value caches, xLSTM exhibits linear scaling behavior similar to other recurrent models but with superior stability over long contexts. When measuring decoding throughput at varying batch sizes, xLSTM demonstrated its ability to handle much larger batches than Llama models thanks to its constant memory footprint per step ($O(d^2)$ vs $O(L^2)$ where $L$ is sequence length). This suggests that for applications requiring low-latency responses over long conversations or documents—such as AI assistants or real-time analysis tools—xLSTM offers a path toward high-throughput systems that do not compromise on intelligence or accuracy.

Ultimately, xLSTM proves that recurrent models are far from obsolete; they simply needed modern engineering techniques to unlock their potential again. By leveraging exponential gating for robustness and matrix memories for capacity while strategically choosing between sequential mixing and full parallelization, xLSTM bridges the gap between theoretical optimality and practical speed. As research continues to optimize CUDA kernels further—as acknowledged by the authors who noted room for improvement—the gap between recurrent efficiency and transformer-like performance may soon close entirely, offering developers a versatile new tool for building scalable language models without paying an astronomical computational tax for long contexts.



# Scaling Up: Can LSTMs Really Compete with Billions-Parameter Models?

The rise of the Transformer architecture revolutionized natural language processing, largely displacing the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks that once dominated the field. While Transformers offered superior scalability and parallelization, LSTMs remained a robust choice for specific tasks involving sequential state tracking. However, a fundamental question lingered: if we were to apply modern scaling techniques and fix the historical limitations of LSTMs, could they compete with billion-parameter models? The introduction of xLSTM (extended Long Short-Term Memory) suggests the answer is a resounding yes. By integrating exponential gating and novel memory structures, xLSTM challenges the dominance of Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) in large-scale language modeling.

To understand why xLSTM matters, we must first revisit the constraints that led to the decline of traditional LSTMs. Classic LSTMs suffer from three primary issues: an inability to revise storage decisions once made, limited storage capacity due to scalar memory cells, and a lack of parallelizability caused by hidden-hidden recurrent connections. These limitations meant that while LSTMs excelled at learning abstractions and state tracking, they struggled with massive datasets and long-range dependencies compared to the parallel-friendly attention mechanisms of Transformers. xLSTM addresses these pain points directly by introducing two distinct architectural variants: sLSTM and mLSTM.

The sLSTM variant retains a scalar memory cell but replaces standard sigmoid gating with exponential gating coupled with a normalization mechanism. This modification empowers the network to revise its storage decisions dynamically. Imagine trying to remember a fact but realizing halfway through a sentence that a better piece of information appeared earlier; standard LSTMs struggle to overwrite previous memories effectively, whereas sLSTM can adjust its internal state to prioritize newer or more relevant data. Furthermore, sLSTM introduces a new form of memory mixing across multiple cells within a single head, allowing it to solve complex state-tracking problems that purely feed-forward or non-recurrent models often miss.

On the other hand, mLSTM tackles the issue of limited storage capacity by upgrading the memory cell from a scalar value to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Instead of simply adding values to a running sum, mLSTM utilizes a covariance update rule similar to associative memory systems. When storing information, it records key-value pairs as outer products within this matrix. This allows for significantly higher information density and retrieval precision. Crucially, because mLSTM abandons hidden-hidden connections in favor of this matrix update rule, it becomes fully parallelizable during training, removing one of the biggest bottlenecks associated with recurrent architectures.

Experimental evidence strongly supports the efficacy of these upgrades when scaled up. In synthetic tasks designed to test state tracking capabilities—such as recognizing formal languages like parity tasks—models lacking memory mixing fail completely. In contrast, xLSTM architectures utilizing sLSTM blocks achieve near-perfect accuracy where others falter. Similarly, in Multi-Query Associative Recall tasks where models must retrieve specific key-value pairs from long sequences containing up to 256 pairs over 2048 tokens, xLSTM matches or exceeds the performance of Transformers despite having no explicit attention mechanism. These results confirm that xLSTM possesses unique inductive biases essential for handling complex sequential logic without relying on quadratic complexity attention layers.

When moved from synthetic toys to real-world language modeling at scale, xLSTM continues to impress. Researchers trained models on 15 billion tokens from the SlimPajama dataset using various architectures including GPT-3-style Transformers (like Llama), State Space Models (like Mamba), and other RNNs like RWKV. The results were striking: xLSTM configurations consistently achieved lower validation perplexity than all competing baselines across different parameter counts ranging from 350 million upwards. Notably, when scaling up further to 300 billion tokens—a scale comparable to major industry models—the gap widened rather than closed. On this massive dataset, xLSTM outperformed Llama and Mamba on nearly every metric tested, including general down-stream reasoning tasks measured by benchmarks like ARC and MMLU.

Perhaps most compelling is xLSTM's performance on extremely long contexts without degradation in quality. While Transformers often suffer from increased perplexity as context length grows beyond their training window due to quadratic attention costs or cache management issues, xLSTM maintains stable performance up to context lengths of 16k tokens trained only on 2k windows. This capability stems from its linear computational complexity relative to sequence length ($O(N)$) compared to the quadratic complexity ($O(N^2)$) typical of self-attention mechanisms in standard Transformers like Llama or GPT-3 variants during inference with growing context sizes.

Furthermore, efficiency metrics reveal another advantage for deployment scenarios where latency matters. Although mLSTM involves heavy matrix multiplications for updates ($d \times d$), these operations are highly parallelizable on GPUs using standard libraries like cuBLAS or custom CUDA kernels similar to FlashAttention optimizations mentioned in related literature. Benchmarks indicate that while current implementations may lag slightly behind highly optimized FlashAttention kernels for Transformers due to lack of specialized tuning yet being under development for mLSTM specifically), sLSTM offers competitive speeds thanks to optimized CUDA kernels that run less than twice as slow as mLSTM despite being inherently sequential in nature regarding memory mixing logic alone (though modern hardware mitigates much of this).
