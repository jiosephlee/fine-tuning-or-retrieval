# Chapter 1: The Legacy and Limitations of Classical LSTMs

## Historical Context: From RNNs to LSTMs

The journey toward understanding sequential data began with the development of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), which were designed to handle sequences where the order of elements matters. Unlike feed-forward networks that treat inputs independently, RNNs possess internal loops allowing them to retain information about previous steps in a sequence. This capability made them theoretically ideal for tasks ranging from speech recognition to natural language processing. However, despite their elegant design, classical RNNs suffered from a catastrophic failure mode known as the vanishing gradient problem. During the training process, specifically when using Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), gradients are propagated backward through the sequence. As these gradients traverse many time steps, they tend to shrink exponentially towards zero if the activation functions involve saturating nonlinearities or if the spectral radius of the weight matrices is less than one. Consequently, early parts of a sequence become invisible to the learning algorithm, preventing the network from capturing long-term dependencies—a fatal flaw for modeling real-world phenomena like sentences or financial time series where context spans hundreds or thousands of tokens.

It was in response to this fundamental instability that Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber introduced the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture in the mid-1990s. Their seminal work provided a structural solution to preserve gradient flow over long durations by introducing a dedicated memory pathway called the cell state ($c_t$). Rather than relying solely on repeated nonlinear transformations of hidden states, LSTMs created a "highway" for information flow that allowed errors to propagate unchanged across arbitrary distances in time. This innovation effectively neutralized the vanishing gradient issue, enabling networks to learn relationships between events separated by significant temporal gaps. Over the subsequent decades, LSTMs evolved from academic curiosities into industry standards, powering some of the earliest and most successful Large Language Models (LLMs). They excelled in diverse domains including handwriting generation, machine translation, program evaluation, and even reinforcement learning agents like AlphaStar for StarCraft II and OpenAI Five for Dota 2. The robustness of LSTMs established them as a cornerstone of deep learning history before the paradigm shift brought about by Transformers.

However, while LSTMs dominated sequence modeling for nearly two decades following their introduction around 1997, their reign eventually faced competition from a new architectural contender: the Transformer. Introduced in 2017 by Vaswani et al., Transformers utilized a mechanism called self-attention which allowed every position in a sequence to attend directly to every other position simultaneously. This design eliminated recursion entirely, granting massive advantages in parallelization during training on modern hardware accelerators like GPUs and TPUs. While Transformers quickly surpassed LSTMs in raw speed and scalability at billion-parameter levels, leaving many researchers wondering if RNN-based models had reached their ceiling, it remained an open question whether these limitations were intrinsic to recurrence itself or merely artifacts of outdated LSTM designs. The xLSTM paper posits that by addressing specific mechanical flaws within the LSTM framework—specifically regarding gating dynamics and memory representation—one could revive recurrent architectures and compete with modern transformers at scale without sacrificing their unique strengths in state tracking and efficiency.

## Mechanism Deep Dive: Constant Error Carousel and Gates

To understand how LSTMs succeeded where traditional RNNs failed, one must dissect their core updating equation for the cell state $c_t$. The heart of the LSTM lies in what is termed the "constant error carousel." Mathematically expressed as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, this equation describes an additive update rule rather than a multiplicative transformation typical of standard RNNs. In this formulation, $c_{t-1}$ represents the cell state from the previous time step, serving as a carrier for persistent information. The term $f_t c_{t-1}$ acts as a controlled retention mechanism governed by a forget gate $f_t$, while $i_t z_t$ represents new information injected into memory via an input gate $i_t$ modulating a candidate value $z_t$. Because addition preserves magnitude significantly better than multiplication under iteration (especially near identity), gradients flowing backward through this chain remain stable over long horizons unless explicitly suppressed by saturation effects elsewhere in the network. This additive nature ensures that important signals can persist indefinitely unless actively overwritten or forgotten by learned gating mechanisms.

Complementing this cellular highway are three distinct gating units—the input gate ($i_t$), forget gate ($f_t$), and output gate ($o_t$)—which regulate access to and from this memory channel using sigmoid activation functions defined as $\sigma(x) = 1 / (1 + \exp(-x))$. These gates produce values strictly bounded between zero and one acting as continuous switches that determine how much information flows through each component at every timestep. # Chapter 2: Introducing xLSTM Architecture Overview

## Core Philosophy: Revitalizing LSTMs for the Scale Era

The trajectory of deep learning in natural language processing has historically oscillated between the dominance of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and the rise of Transformer architectures. While Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, introduced in the 1990s with concepts like the constant error carousel and gating mechanisms, served as the foundation for early Large Language Models (LLMs), they eventually faced significant hurdles when attempting to compete with Transformers at massive scales. The primary catalyst for this shift was the advent of Transformer technology, specifically its self-attention mechanism, which allowed for unprecedented parallelization during training. Consequently, LSTMs were often relegated to niche applications or smaller models, perceived as unable to handle the sheer volume of parameters and data required for modern generative AI. However, this historical dismissal overlooks fundamental structural advantages inherent to recurrent architectures. The core philosophy driving the development of Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM) is not to abandon the proven efficacy of LSTMs but to rigorously interrogate their limitations and apply modern engineering solutions derived from contemporary Large Language Model practices.

The central inquiry motivating xLSTM is straightforward yet profound: How far can we push language modeling performance if we scale traditional LSTM principles to billions of parameters while simultaneously addressing their intrinsic bottlenecks? The authors posit that by integrating the latest scaling techniques—such as robust residual backbones and advanced normalization—with targeted architectural modifications designed to mitigate specific LSTM weaknesses, it is possible to create a hybrid model that rivals or exceeds current state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs). This approach acknowledges that while Transformers excelled in parallelizability, LSTMs possess unique strengths in managing temporal dependencies through their gated memory updates. By revitalizing these mechanisms, xLSTM seeks to bridge the gap between the interpretability and stability of recurrent units and the scalability demands of modern deep learning.

Furthermore, this revitalization effort challenges the assumption that sequential processing is inherently incompatible with high-performance computing at scale. The xLSTM framework demonstrates that with careful modification of internal dynamics—specifically regarding how information is stored, updated, and retrieved—it is possible to retain the desirable properties of RNNs without succumbing to their historical limitations. The resulting architecture aims to offer a viable alternative path for developing efficient, scalable language models that do not strictly adhere to the Transformer paradigm. By proving that LSTMs can be adapted to perform competitively in billion-parameter regimes, xLSTM opens new avenues for research in areas ranging from reinforcement learning to time-series analysis, reinforcing the enduring relevance of recurrent logic when properly augmented.

## The Two Pillars of xLSTM: Exponential Gating and Modified Memory Structures

To transcend the boundaries imposed by classical LSTM designs, xLSTM introduces two foundational pillars: exponential gating strategies and novel memory structures. These modifications directly target three critical deficiencies identified in standard LSTMs: the inability to revise storage decisions once made, limited storage capacity constrained by scalar representations, and a lack of parallelizability caused by hidden-state dependencies. The first pillar addresses the rigidity of information retention through exponential gating coupled with rigorous normalization and stabilization techniques. In traditional LSTMs governed by sigmoid activations ($\sigma(x)$), gates operate within a bounded range [0, 1], which limits their dynamic range and makes revisiting earlier storage choices difficult once a decision has been encoded into a fixed point along a curve. By transitioning input gates ($i_t$) and potentially forget gates ($f_t$) to exponential activation functions ($\exp(\cdot)$), xLSTM expands this dynamic range significantly. This expansion allows for finer granularity in controlling information flow over long sequences, effectively enabling the model to overwrite previous memories when superior candidates emerge—a capability essential for solving tasks like nearest neighbor search where initial assumptions may need correction upon discovering closer matches later in a sequence.

However, simply applying exponentials introduces numerical instability risks due to rapid growth leading to overflow errors. To counteract this without altering network functionality or gradient propagation characteristics, xLSTM employs sophisticated stabilizers utilizing auxiliary states denoted as $m_t$. This stabilizer tracks running maximums related logarithmic transformations of gate values ($\log(f_t)$ and $\log(i_t)$), ensuring that computed exponents remain within safe bounds relative to their cumulative history. Specifically, stabilized versions $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ are derived by subtracting these max-logarithms before exponentiation guarantees numerical safety while preserving exact equivalence in output values compared to unstable raw calculations. This mathematical elegance ensures that despite aggressive scaling behaviors intended for improved expressivity, training remains stable even under extreme parameter counts or lengthy contexts where # Chapter 3: The sLSTM Unit — Exponential Gating and Memory Mixing

## Introduction to the Scalar Extension

The evolution of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture began with the seminal introduction of the constant error carousel and gating mechanisms, which effectively solved the vanishing gradient problem plaguing earlier recurrent neural networks. While these innovations allowed LSTMs to dominate early deep learning landscapes and serve as foundational components for the first Large Language Models (LLMs), they eventually encountered fundamental bottlenecks when faced with modern scaling requirements. Specifically, traditional LSTMs struggled with three critical limitations: the inability to revise storage decisions once made, restricted storage capacities inherent to scalar cell states, and a lack of parallelizability due to tight coupling between hidden states across time steps. To address these deficiencies, particularly the rigidity of storage updates, researchers introduced the Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM). Within this expanded family lies the scalar LSTM, or sLSTM, a specialized unit designed to retain the interpretability of scalar memory while unlocking new capabilities through exponential gating and sophisticated memory mixing strategies. Unlike its counterpart, the matrix-valued mLSTM, which prioritizes massive storage capacity through outer-product updates, the sLSTM focuses on enhancing expressivity and revisibility through nonlinear transformations of its internal signals. This chapter provides a rigorous examination of the sLSTM unit, exploring its mathematical underpinnings, stability mechanisms, and architectural nuances that distinguish it from classical LSTM designs. By understanding these components, one gains insight into how modern recurrent architectures can compete with Transformers in complex sequence modeling tasks requiring precise state tracking and adaptive memory management.

## Mathematical Formulation of Exponential Gating in sLSTM

The cornerstone modification distinguishing sLSTM from its predecessor is the replacement of sigmoid-gated multiplicative interactions with exponential activation functions for both input and forget gates. In the classic LSTM formulation defined by Hochreiter et al., the cell state update relies heavily on sigmoid activations $\sigma(\cdot)$ for gates $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$. These sigmoids constrain gate values strictly between 0 and 1, enforcing a soft selection mechanism where information flow is either permitted or suppressed based on bounded probabilities. While effective for preventing exploding gradients historically, this bounded nature limits the dynamism of the memory system. The sLSTM liberates these constraints by adopting exponential activation functions for at least one or both of these gates. Mathematically, if we denote the pre-activation log-values as $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$, the input gate $i_t$ is computed directly as $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ rather than $\sigma(\tilde{i}_t)$. Similarly, the forget gate may follow this path or remain sigmoid depending on configuration choices: $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ OR $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$.

This shift from saturation-based gating to exponential growth fundamentally alters how information enters and persists within the cell state $c_t$. The standard additive update equation remains structurally similar but operates under vastly different dynamics:
$$ c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t $$
Here, $z_t$ represents the candidate cell content generated via a nonlinearity $\varphi$ (typically tanh) applied to a linear combination of current inputs $\mathbf{x}_t$ and previous hidden states $h_{t-1}$. Crucially, because $i_t$ grows exponentially with its log-input $\tilde{i}_t$, small variations in pre-synaptic activity can lead to orders-of-magnitude differences in actual gate magnitude. This property grants sLSTM unprecedented flexibility in modulating information flow. Instead of merely selecting whether to include a piece of information (binary-like behavior near 0 or 1), exponential gating allows for continuous amplification or attenuation factors that are not capped at unity. When combined with normalization techniques described below, this mechanism enables "dynamic revision" of stored memories—a capability largely absent in standard LSTMs where forgetting rates are rigidly bounded by 0–1 sigmoid outputs. Consequently, if a newly observed pattern suggests that previously stored information was erroneous or outdated relative to current evidence, sLSTM can aggressively overwrite or dampen old entries without waiting for gradual decay dictated by fixed forget thresholds.
