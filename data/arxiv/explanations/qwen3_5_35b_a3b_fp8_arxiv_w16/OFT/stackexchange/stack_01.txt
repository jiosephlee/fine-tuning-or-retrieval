\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is 'hyperspherical energy' and why must it be preserved rather than minimized?
Question:
I am reading the section on 'Orthogonal Finetuning' and I am getting confused about the definition and purpose of 'hyperspherical energy'. The paper defines it as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Intuitively, this looks like a repulsive force between neurons. Why does the paper argue that *preserving* this value is crucial for maintaining semantic ability, whereas previous work (like Liu et al., 2021) tries to *minimize* it? Doesn't minimizing the energy (spreading neurons evenly) lead to better generalization in classification, so shouldn't we do that here too?
Answer:
Based on the paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning," the distinction between preserving and minimizing hyperspherical energy stems from the fundamentally different goals of training a model from scratch versus finetuning a pre-existing model. Hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ represents the $i$-th normalized neuron vector. Intuitively, this formula acts as a measure of repulsion: because the term $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ grows larger as neurons get closer, a lower total energy corresponds to neurons being spread out more uniformly on the hypersphere.

Previous work, specifically citing Liu et al. (2021), focuses on training classification networks from random initialization. In that context, starting with random weights results in a specific expected distribution of angles. The goal there is to minimize hyperspherical energy to ensure that all neurons are equally informative and non-redundant, effectively creating a uniform packing of vectors that maximizes generalization for classification tasks. Minimizing this energy forces the network to avoid clusters of redundant features early in training.

However, in the context of finetuning text-to-image diffusion models described in this paper, the situation is reversed. The pretrained model already possesses a highly optimized configuration of neuronal angles that encode complex semantic relationships acquired during its massive pre-training phase. Here, the absolute value of the hyperspherical energy is less important than its relative stability compared to the original model. The authors argue that minimizing this energy during finetuning would force the neurons to rearrange themselves into a new uniform configuration, thereby destroying the delicate pairwise angular relationships established during pretraining. Since these angles carry the bulk of the semantic information required for generating high-fidelity images (similar to how phase information carries semantics in Fourier analysis), disrupting them leads to a loss of generative capability or "model collapse."

Therefore, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) does not attempt to drive the hyperspherical energy toward zero or any theoretical minimum. Instead, it strictly enforces an equality constraint: $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{finetuned}}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{pretrained}})$. This is achieved by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}}$. Mathematically, multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves all pairwise distances between vectors on the unit sphere; consequently, $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_j\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$, ensuring that every term in the summation defining hyperspherical energy remains identical before and after finetuning. By preserving this value rather than minimizing it, OFT allows the model to adapt its orientation to new tasks (like subject-driven generation) while rigorously protecting the internal structural integrity and semantic knowledge embedded in the original weight geometry.
