Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Foundations of Neural Network Geometry and Semantics

## Introduction to Geometric Principles in Fine-tuning Large Models

As we delve deeper into the realm of modern deep learning, particularly concerning massive text-to-image diffusion models, it becomes evident that successful application requires more than merely applying gradient descent updates to existing weights. The prevailing paradigm of fine-tuning relies heavily on heuristic assumptions about how changes in weight vectors correlate with the retention of semantic meaning. Before introducing the specific methodology known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), it is essential to dismantle the limitations inherent in traditional fine-tuning strategies and establish a robust geometric foundation. This foundational perspective shifts our view from treating neural network weights as mere scalar values accumulating numerical adjustments to viewing them as directional entities residing on complex manifolds. The core insight driving advanced finetuning techniques is that the relative orientation—specifically the angles between neurons—is paramount for encoding semantic knowledge, while absolute magnitudes serve a subsidiary role. Understanding this distinction is critical for grasping why standard approaches often fail to maintain generative capability during adaptation and how newer geometric constraints offer a principled solution.

Traditional fine-tuning methodologies operate under a tacit assumption that keeping the updated model close to the pretrained model in Euclidean space ensures the preservation of functionality. This philosophy manifests practically through the adoption of extremely small learning rates or low-rank adaptations like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). The rationale behind limiting step sizes or restricting parameter dimensions is to induce a "perturbation" rather than a fundamental restructuring of the network's internal logic. Mathematically, practitioners attempt to minimize the Frobenius norm, denoted as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, where $\bm{M}$ represents the weights after fine-tuning and $\bm{M}^0$ represents the pretrained weights. The belief is straightforward: if the distance between old and new weights is negligible, then the function performed by the network should remain nearly identical, thereby retaining its pre-trained generalization abilities while acquiring slight modifications for downstream tasks. However, as explored in recent literature addressing this exact issue, relying solely on Euclidean proximity is fundamentally flawed. It ignores the topological arrangement of neurons and presupposes that linear interpolation in weight space corresponds intuitively to functional continuity in probability space. Consequently, many attempts at aggressive fine-tuning result in catastrophic forgetting or mode collapse, precisely because they ignore the geometric integrity required to sustain complex generative behaviors found in diffusion models.

To rectify this oversight, we must shift our analytical lens toward spherical geometry. Instead of focusing on whether two weight matrices are numerically similar, we should investigate whether they possess similar internal relational structures. This transition begins with normalizing every individual neuron (weight vector) onto a unit hypersphere. Once normalized, each neuron becomes a point on a surface where position is determined entirely by direction rather than length. This normalization process reveals that neural networks encode vast amounts of information through the interplay between these directional components—their angles relative to one another—and their collective distribution across this high-dimensional sphere. By abstracting away magnitude initially, we isolate pure angular relationships, allowing us to define quantities such as hyperspherical energy, which measures how densely packed or spread out these neurons are on their respective spheres. This conceptual leap sets the stage for understanding why preserving these specific relational properties allows for safe adaptation without destroying previously acquired knowledge bases embedded within millions of parameters. Furthermore, recognizing that rotations preserve distances facilitates discussions about orthogonal transformations later on; thus establishing early definitions here paves roads toward rigorous proofs involving stability and convergence characteristics necessary for practical deployment scenarios today [reference: Liu et al., 2021].
