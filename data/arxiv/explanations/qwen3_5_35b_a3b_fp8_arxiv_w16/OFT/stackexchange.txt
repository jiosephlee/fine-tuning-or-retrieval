\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is 'hyperspherical energy' and why must it be preserved rather than minimized?
Question:
I am reading the section on 'Orthogonal Finetuning' and I am getting confused about the definition and purpose of 'hyperspherical energy'. The paper defines it as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Intuitively, this looks like a repulsive force between neurons. Why does the paper argue that *preserving* this value is crucial for maintaining semantic ability, whereas previous work (like Liu et al., 2021) tries to *minimize* it? Doesn't minimizing the energy (spreading neurons evenly) lead to better generalization in classification, so shouldn't we do that here too?
Answer:
Based on the paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning," the distinction between preserving and minimizing hyperspherical energy stems from the fundamentally different goals of training a model from scratch versus finetuning a pre-existing model. Hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ represents the $i$-th normalized neuron vector. Intuitively, this formula acts as a measure of repulsion: because the term $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ grows larger as neurons get closer, a lower total energy corresponds to neurons being spread out more uniformly on the hypersphere.

Previous work, specifically citing Liu et al. (2021), focuses on training classification networks from random initialization. In that context, starting with random weights results in a specific expected distribution of angles. The goal there is to minimize hyperspherical energy to ensure that all neurons are equally informative and non-redundant, effectively creating a uniform packing of vectors that maximizes generalization for classification tasks. Minimizing this energy forces the network to avoid clusters of redundant features early in training.

However, in the context of finetuning text-to-image diffusion models described in this paper, the situation is reversed. The pretrained model already possesses a highly optimized configuration of neuronal angles that encode complex semantic relationships acquired during its massive pre-training phase. Here, the absolute value of the hyperspherical energy is less important than its relative stability compared to the original model. The authors argue that minimizing this energy during finetuning would force the neurons to rearrange themselves into a new uniform configuration, thereby destroying the delicate pairwise angular relationships established during pretraining. Since these angles carry the bulk of the semantic information required for generating high-fidelity images (similar to how phase information carries semantics in Fourier analysis), disrupting them leads to a loss of generative capability or "model collapse."

Therefore, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) does not attempt to drive the hyperspherical energy toward zero or any theoretical minimum. Instead, it strictly enforces an equality constraint: $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{finetuned}}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{pretrained}})$. This is achieved by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}}$. Mathematically, multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves all pairwise distances between vectors on the unit sphere; consequently, $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_j\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$, ensuring that every term in the summation defining hyperspherical energy remains identical before and after finetuning. By preserving this value rather than minimizing it, OFT allows the model to adapt its orientation to new tasks (like subject-driven generation) while rigorously protecting the internal structural integrity and semantic knowledge embedded in the original weight geometry.

### How does the Cayley parameterization restrict the search space to Special Orthogonal Group SO(d)?
Question:
In the 'Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization' section, the authors propose using $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. They mention that this only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 (rotations), excluding reflections (det = -1). I understand mathematically why $(I-Q)^{-1}$ combined with $(I+Q)$ yields det=1, but I am unsure if this restriction hurts performance. Did they explicitly test cases where a reflection might be necessary to align features? Or is the intuition that rotations alone are sufficient for the degrees of freedom needed in finetuning?
Answer:
The restriction of the search space to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$—matrices with determinant $+1$ corresponding to pure rotations—is indeed an inherent consequence of using the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. Mathematically, the determinant of this transformation is always $+1$ because the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix come in conjugate imaginary pairs, causing their contribution to the determinant product to be positive, and thus $\det(\bm{R}) = \prod \frac{1+i\lambda_k}{1-i\lambda_k} = 1$. This excludes reflections (where $\det(\bm{R}) = -1$) from the reachable set of orthogonal matrices.

However, according to the paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*, this theoretical limitation does not negatively impact performance for two primary reasons rooted in the specific goals of their finetuning framework. First, the authors explicitly state in Section 3.3 ("Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization") that "we find that such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice." Their motivation for enforcing orthogonality is not merely to allow arbitrary rigid body transformations (which include reflections), but specifically to preserve **hyperspherical energy**, which characterizes the pairwise angular relationships between neurons. Since both rotations and reflections preserve angles between vectors equally well, restricting the transformation to rotations alone retains all necessary properties for maintaining the semantic structure encoded in these angles. The paper argues that "changing neuron directions is the key to semantically modifying," and since rotations provide sufficient degrees of freedom to explore these directional changes effectively, excluding reflections is unnecessary for their objectives.

Furthermore, the paper highlights that standard orthogonalization allows for both rotations and reflections, but OFT focuses on preserving the relative geometry of neurons rather than flipping them globally. The authors note that while a reflection could theoretically be required if a specific alignment demanded a parity flip, their extensive empirical evaluations on subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks demonstrate that rotations are entirely sufficient to achieve superior results compared to methods like LoRA or DreamBooth. In fact, they emphasize that OFT strikes a "sweet spot between flexibility and regularity," suggesting that the extra symmetry offered by reflections (the full orthogonal group $O(d)$) introduces redundant complexity without providing tangible benefits for stabilizing diffusion model finetuning. Consequently, while $O(d)$ would technically offer a superset of transformations including reflections, the authors conclude that $SO(d)$ provides enough expressiveness to adapt the model's canonical coordinate system effectively without destroying pre-trained knowledge or hindering convergence.

### Can someone clarify the exact forward pass difference between OFT and Re-scaled OFT?
Question:
There seems to be a contradiction or at least confusion in Section 4.3 regarding Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning. The text states the forward pass is $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$, but then includes a footnote saying: "In the NeurIPS camera ready version, the forward pass... was mistakenly written as $\bm{z}=(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$". Since matrix multiplication is not commutative, applying $\bm{D}$ before vs after $\bm{R}$ changes which dimensions are scaled (input features vs output features). Could you confirm which order is actually implemented in their code and whether scaling happens before or after the orthogonal rotation?
Answer:
Based on the erratum explicitly stated in Section 4.3 of the paper, the forward pass for Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (Rescaled OFT) is indeed $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$, confirming that the scaling operation occurs *after* the orthogonal rotation in the linear algebra formulation before the transpose. The footnote clarifies that the alternative expression $(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ found in the NeurIPS camera-ready version was a typographical error. To understand why this distinction matters intuitively, consider how matrix multiplication interacts with transposition: expanding the correct formula gives $\bm{z} = \bm{D}^\top(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{R}^\top\bm{x}$ (assuming symmetric properties or careful dimension matching), whereas the erroneous version would imply a different ordering of operations relative to the input vector $\bm{x}$.

In practical implementation terms described by the authors, $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weight matrix where columns correspond to individual neurons. The term $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to these neurons, effectively rotating them in their high-dimensional space while preserving their pairwise angles and thus maintaining hyperspherical energy. Crucially, because $\bm{R}$ acts on the rows of $\bm{W}^0$ (which are feature dimensions) or equivalently transforms the column vectors (neurons) depending on perspective, it mixes features globally across all neurons. The subsequent multiplication by $\bm{D}$ on the right side of $\bm{W}^0$ means that $\bm{D}$ operates directly on the columns of $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$. Since each column corresponds to a specific neuron's weight vector, multiplying by a diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ scales each neuron individually by its own scalar factor $s_i$. This allows Rescaled OFT to adjust the magnitude of each neuron independently after they have been rotated by $\bm{R}$, providing extra flexibility without breaking the orthogonality-induced preservation of angular relationships established by $\rm R$.

If one were to implement it as $(\boldsymbol{\mathrm { D }}\boldsymbol{\mathrm { R }} \boldsymbol{\mathrm { W }}^{0})$, mathematically this would place $\boldsymbol{\mathrm { D }}$ immediately next to $\boldsymbol{\mathrm { R }}$, meaning it would scale the rows of $\boldsymbol{\mathrm { R }}$ or interact differently with the feature dimensions before they reach the weights, which contradicts the paper's goal of per-neuron magnitude adjustment. The authors confirm that their code implements the corrected version where scaling happens post-rotation on the neuron axis. This subtle ordering ensures that while every neuron is rotated together as a rigid body via $\boldsymbol{\mathrm { R }}$, their lengths can subsequently be tuned individually via $\boldsymbol{\mathrm { D }}$, combining structural stability with adaptive magnitude control for improved finetuning performance.

### Why does OFT claim to avoid inference overhead while requiring matrix multiplication at runtime?
Question:
The paper claims in Section 5 ('Intriguing Insights') that 'Unlike ControlNet, our OFT framework introduces no additional inference overhead.' They justify this by saying one can pre-multiply $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ offline to get an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}$. However, isn't multiplying two dense matrices ($d \times d$) computationally expensive if done per-layer? If we merge them into a single weight matrix during training/finetuning setup, doesn't that increase memory usage for storing the merged weights compared to just storing $\bm{W}^0$ plus a small low-rank factor like LoRA? How does this scale compared to keeping separate parameters?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction between claiming "no additional inference overhead" and requiring a matrix multiplication at runtime stems from the distinction between *architectural* changes and *re-parameterization*. As explained in the "Intriguing Insights" section of the paper, OFT modifies the forward pass during training to be $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights. During this training phase, the computation indeed involves multiplying $\bm{R}$ with $\bm{W}^0$. However, the authors explicitly state that in the inference stage, one can pre-multiply these two matrices offline to create a single equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}_{\text{final}} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Once this fusion is complete, the model effectively becomes a standard linear layer with weights $\bm{W}_{\text{final}}$, meaning the runtime computation is identical to running the original pretrained model without any extra operations.

Regarding your concern about memory usage and storage costs compared to methods like LoRA: yes, merging $\bm{R}$ (size $d \times d$) into $\bm{W}^0$ results in a dense $d \times n$ weight matrix that occupies more memory than storing just $\bm{W}^0$ plus a small low-rank factor. The paper acknowledges that standard orthogonal parameterization requires $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which scales quadratically with dimension $d$. To mitigate this during the *training* phase (where memory matters most for optimization), OFT employs a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$ with $r$ blocks. This reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even lower if blocks are shared. Crucially, however, once training concludes and the weights are fused into $\bm{W}_{\text{final}}$ for deployment, there is no longer any need to store $\bm{R}$ or maintain separate parameters. The deployed model stores only one dense weight matrix per layer, exactly as it did before finetuning. Therefore, while OFT might require more memory than LoRA *during* training due to its specific parameterization strategy, it incurs **zero** additional memory footprint or computational cost *at inference* because it collapses back into a standard architecture. This contrasts sharply with methods like ControlNet or adapters that must retain extra modules (like skip connections or parallel branches) permanently during inference to function correctly.

### Is COFT strictly a subset of OFT or an independent method with different optimization objectives?
Question:
Looking at Equation ~eq:coft_general~, COFT adds a constraint $|| \bm{R}- \bm{I} || \leq \epsilon$ on top of orthogonality. This seems like it restricts OFT further. But then they say COFT allows us to tune flexibility via $\\epsilon$. If I set $\\epsilon$ very large, does COFT become identical to standard OFT? And conversely, if I set $\\epsilon=0$, does it revert to doing nothing (just using pretrained weights)? I want to make sure I understand if COFT is just OFT with a regularization term added to the loss function or if it fundamentally changes how gradients flow through the Cayley parameterization.
Answer:
Based on the paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*, COFT is indeed a constrained variant of OFT, functioning as a strict subset in terms of feasible solutions when $\epsilon$ is finite, but it represents a distinct optimization regime rather than merely adding a soft regularization term to the loss function.

To understand the relationship, we must look at how the constraints are implemented. Standard OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, allowing the model to explore the entire orthogonal group manifold. COFT, however, explicitly restricts this search space by imposing an additional hard constraint: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. As described in Section 3.4, this is not treated as a penalty added to the loss function (like a Lagrange multiplier would suggest); instead, it is incorporated directly into the optimization procedure via projected gradient descent on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ derived from the Cayley parameterization. The authors approximate $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ using a Neumann series and enforce the constraint by projecting $\bm{Q}$ back onto a ball of radius $\epsilon'$ whenever it exceeds the threshold. Consequently, gradients cannot flow freely to push $\bm{R}$ outside this specific neighborhood of the identity matrix; the optimization landscape itself is physically clipped.

Regarding your specific questions about limiting cases: yes, conceptually, if you were to set $\epsilon$ to infinity (or sufficiently large relative to the scale of valid rotations), COFT would theoretically collapse back into standard OFT because the deviation constraint would become inactive. Conversely, if you set $\epsilon = 0$, the constraint forces $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| = 0$, meaning $\bm{R}$ must remain exactly equal to the identity matrix throughout training. In this extreme case, COFT effectively reverts to "doing nothing," leaving the weights at their pretrained values ($\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0$) without any updates.

This distinction highlights that COFT is not just OFT with a loose penalty but a method designed specifically to control flexibility versus stability. The paper argues that while OFT preserves hyperspherical energy (pairwise angles) globally, it might still deviate too far from the pretrained configuration for delicate tasks. By making the distance from initialization an explicit bound rather than an implicit result of a small learning rate or soft regularization, COFT ensures that the finetuned model stays within a "safe" region where semantic integrity is maximized while still allowing enough movement (controlled by $\epsilon$) to adapt to downstream tasks like subject-driven or controllable generation. Thus, while both methods share the same underlying mechanism of applying orthogonal transformations via Cayley parameterization and maintaining gradient flow through those parameters differently than standard finetuning or LoRA, COFT fundamentally alters the feasible solution space of the optimizer by treating proximity to initialization as a hard boundary condition.
