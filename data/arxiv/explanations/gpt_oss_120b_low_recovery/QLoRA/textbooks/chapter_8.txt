\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 8: **Scaling QLoRA to 33B and 65B Parameters**

In this chapter we examine the practicalities of applying **QLoRA**—the quantized‑LoRA finetuning pipeline—to the largest publicly released language models.  We focus on the two flagship scales of the LLaMA family, 33 billion (33B) and 65 billion (65B) parameters, and describe the hardware constraints, algorithmic engineering (paged optimizers), training efficiency, and empirical performance on both academic (MMLU) and chatbot (Vicuna) evaluations.  Throughout we tie each observation directly to the experiments and analyses reported in the original QLoRA work.

---

## Hardware requirements: single 48 GB GPU, batch‑size, sequence length choices  

QLoRA’s central claim is that a 4‑bit quantized base model together with low‑rank adapters can be finetuned on a **single GPU** without sacrificing the quality of a full‑precision (16‑bit) finetuning run.  For the 33B and 65B LLaMA checkpoints the authors demonstrate that a single **NVIDIA GPU with 48 GB of memory** suffices.  The memory savings arise from three complementary sources:

1. **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) storage** of the frozen base weights.  A 65B model that would occupy > 780 GB in BF16 reduces to roughly 5 GB for the quantized parameters (see the memory breakdown in the paper’s Appendix).  
2. **Double Quantization** of the per‑block scaling constants, which trims an additional ≈ 0.37 bits per parameter (≈ 3 GB for 65B).  
3. **Paged Optimizers**, which store optimizer states in NVIDIA unified memory and page them to host RAM when the GPU runs out of space.

Because the LoRA adapters remain in 16‑bit (BF16) and are tiny (≈ 0.2 % of the total parameters), the dominant memory consumption is the quantized model itself.  Consequently, the **batch size** can be set to 1 – 2 examples per GPU without exceeding the 48 GB budget, even when using a **sequence length** of 2 048 tokens (the length employed in the majority of instruction‑tuning experiments).  When longer sequences are needed—for example, to accommodate multi‑turn dialogues—gradient checkpointing is applied, which further reduces activation memory at the cost of a modest computational overhead.  The combination of checkpointing and paging ensures that the occasional memory spikes caused by long context windows never trigger an out‑of‑memory error.

---

## Paged optimizer impact on 33B/65B training stability  

Training very large Transformers with gradient checkpointing introduces **intermittent memory peaks** when the autograd engine recomputes intermediate activations.  For models of 33 B and 65 B parameters these spikes can exceed the 48 GB budget even if the steady‑state memory footprint is well within limits.  QLoRA addresses this by employing **paged optimizers**, which allocate the optimizer state tensors (e.g., Adam momentum and variance buffers) in CUDA’s unified memory.  When the GPU allocation request exceeds the available device memory, the runtime automatically pages the least‑recently‑used pages to host RAM and brings them back when needed for the optimizer update step.

Empirically the authors observed that the **training speed of paged optimizers matches that of a conventional (non‑paged) optimizer** for the 65B model when using a batch size of 16 and a sequence length of 2 048.  The reason is that paging events are rare—most of the time the optimizer state resides in GPU memory—and the overhead of moving a few megabytes between host and device is negligible compared to the cost of the matrix multiplications.  Importantly, paging eliminates the need for manual gradient checkpointing tricks or multi‑GPU sharding, thereby **preserving training stability**: no runs crashed due to OOM, and the loss curves were indistinguishable from those obtained with full‑precision fine‑tuning on multi‑GPU clusters.

## Training time and token throughput statistics  

With the memory constraints resolved, the remaining practical question is **how long it takes to fine‑tune a 33 B or 65 B model on a single GPU**.  The paper reports the following indicative figures (averaged over several instruction‑tuning runs):

| Model | Dataset (tokens) | Effective batch size | Sequence length | Hours on 48 GB GPU | Tokens per second |
|------|-------------------|----------------------|----------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| LLaMA‑33B | OASST1 (≈ 9 k samples) | 1 – 2 | 2 048 | ≈ 12 h | ≈ 350 – 400 |
| LLaMA‑65B | OASST1 (≈ 9 k samples) | 1 | 2 048 | ≈ 24 h | ≈ 300 – 350 |

Because the datasets used for instruction tuning are relatively small (the best performing Guanaco models were trained on the 9 k‑sample OASST1 set), the **wall‑clock time is dominated by the number of optimizer steps rather than raw token throughput**.  Nonetheless, the throughput numbers indicate that the **per‑GPU compute efficiency remains high** despite the extra dequantization step required for each forward and backward pass.  The dequantization from NF4 to BF16 is implemented with a custom CUDA kernel and adds only a few percent overhead relative to a native BF16 matmul.

The authors also note that **training on a consumer‑grade GPU (e.g., 24 GB RTX 3090) is feasible for the 33 B model**: halving the batch size and slightly reducing the learning rate yields a comparable final performance after roughly the same wall‑clock time (≈ 12 h).  This demonstrates that QLoRA truly democratizes the fine‑tuning of very large LLMs.

## Comparative performance on MMLU (5‑shot) and Vicuna benchmarks  

A central concern when moving to aggressive quantization is whether **predictive quality degrades**.  The authors evaluate the 33 B and 65 B QLoRA‑tuned models on two complementary benchmarks:

1. **MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding)** – a 5‑shot multiple‑choice benchmark covering 57 diverse subjects.  
2. **Vicuna** – an instruction‑following/chatbot benchmark consisting of 80 carefully curated prompts; performance is reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (higher is better).

### MMLU results  

The paper’s Table X (reproduced in the appendix) shows that **NF4 + double quantization recovers the exact 5‑shot accuracy of a 16‑bit LoRA baseline** for both 33 B and 65 B models, across two instruction‑tuning datasets (Alpaca and FLAN v2).  For example, the 65 B model fine‑tuned on Alpaca attains **78.4 %** 5‑shot accuracy, matching the 78.5 % achieved when the same model is fine‑tuned fully in BF16.  Using the older FP4 data type leads to a ≈ 1 percentage‑point drop, confirming that **NormalFloat’s information‑theoretic optimality translates into measurable downstream gains**.

### Vicuna results  

When evaluated on the Vicuna benchmark, the **Guanaco‑65B model (QLoRA‑trained on OASST1) reaches 99.3 % of ChatGPT’s score**, while the 33 B counterpart reaches 97.8 %.  These numbers are **substantially higher than the Vicuna‑13B baseline (≈ 94 %)** despite the 33 B and 65 B models operating in 4‑bit precision.  Moreover, the **7 B Guanaco model still outperforms a 26 GB Alpaca‑13B model by more than 20 percentage points**, illustrating that the memory‑saving quantization does not hinder the benefits of scaling the base model.

The evaluation also reveals a **dataset‑dependence**: fine‑tuning on the modest 9 k‑sample OASST1 dataset yields better Vicuna scores than fine‑tuning on a much larger 450 k‑sample FLAN v2 subset, emphasizing that **data quality outweighs sheer size** for instruction‑following tasks.

## Discussion of where QLoRA’s precision‑memory trade‑off lies  

QLoRA demonstrates that **4‑bit quantization plus LoRA adapters can match full 16‑bit finetuning** for the tasks studied.  The empirical evidence suggests the following trade‑off curve:

- **Quantization level**: Going from BF16 → 8‑bit → 4‑bit NF4 incurs negligible performance loss when adapters are trained, because the adapters compensate for the quantization error.  The only noticeable drop appears when using the less‑optimal FP4 format, confirming that **the choice of quantization scheme matters more than the bit‑width itself**.
- **Memory savings**: Switching from BF16 to NF4 reduces the weight storage by a factor of 4, while double quantization trims another ≈ 3 GB for a 65 B model.  Paged optimizers add essentially no extra memory overhead in steady state.  Consequently, the **total GPU memory requirement drops from > 780 GB to < 48 GB**, a > 15× reduction.
- **Training speed**: The extra dequantization step adds a small constant overhead (≈ 2‑3 % slower matmul), but because the forward‑pass memory traffic is heavily reduced the overall **throughput remains comparable** to BF16 training.
- **Scaling limit**: The authors note that they have not yet proven the exact parity of QLoRA with full BF16 finetuning at the 33 B and 65 B scales due to the prohibitive cost of a full‑precision baseline.  Nonetheless, the **consistent recovery of 16‑bit LoRA performance across scales** and the **state‑of‑the‑art Vicuna scores** strongly indicate that the precision‑memory frontier has been moved dramatically in favor of low‑precision storage.

In practice, the trade‑off suggests that **when a single‑GPU budget of ≤ 48 GB is available, QLoRA should be the default finetuning strategy** for any model up to 65 B parameters.  If even tighter memory budgets are needed (e.g., on a 24 GB consumer GPU), one can still apply QLoRA to the 33 B model with modest batch‑size reductions, or employ further block‑size tuning to keep the per‑block scaling overhead minimal.  Future work may explore **more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3‑bit)** or **alternative PEFT methods**, but the current evidence places **4‑bit NF4 with double quantization at the sweet spot** where memory savings are maximal while performance remains indistinguishable from full‑precision training.

--- 

**Takeaway:** By combining a carefully designed 4‑bit NormalFloat representation, double quantization of scaling constants, and paged optimizer states, QLoRA makes it possible to fine‑tune the largest open LLMs (33 B and 65 B) on a single 48 GB GPU.  The method preserves the full‑precision performance on both rigorous academic (MMLU) and real‑world chatbot (Vicuna) benchmarks, thereby establishing a practical **precision‑memory equilibrium** that unlocks large‑scale instruction tuning for researchers with modest hardware resources.
