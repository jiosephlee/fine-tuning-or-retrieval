\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 10: # Evaluating Chatbot Performance

In this chapter we describe in detail the methodology used to assess the quality of chat‑oriented large language models (LLMs) after they have been finetuned with **QLoRA**.  The evaluation framework combines three complementary strands:

1. **Traditional language‑understanding benchmarking**, exemplified by the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) suite, which probes the model’s knowledge across a broad set of academic subjects.  
2. **Prompt‑driven chatbot benchmarks** (the *Vicuna* and *OpenAssistant* prompt suites), which measure how well a model follows user instructions and generates conversationally appropriate responses.  
3. **Systematic rating mechanisms** that translate raw model outputs into quantitative scores: an **automated pipeline** based on GPT‑4 scoring, and a **human evaluation** conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT).  

To obtain a single, comparable metric for each model we aggregate the pairwise judgments from the rating stages into **Elo ratings**, a well‑established tournament‑ranking system.  This section explains each component of the pipeline, the design choices that make it robust, and the empirical findings regarding the agreement between automated and human judges.

---

## MMLU as a Language‑Understanding Benchmark (5‑Shot Set‑up)

The **Massive Multitask Language Understanding** (MMLU) benchmark comprises 57 distinct tasks ranging from elementary mathematics to US history, computer science, and law.  Each task is presented as a multiple‑choice problem; the model must select the correct answer from four alternatives.

To keep the evaluation comparable to prior work, we adopt the **5‑shot** protocol:

* For every task we first present the model with five exemplars (question, four answer choices, and the correct label).  
* The model then receives the *sixth* question and must output the label it believes to be correct.  

Formally, let \(\mathcal{T}\) be the set of tasks and let \(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}\) denote the \(j\)-th example of task \(i\).  The prompt fed to the model for the evaluation example \(\mathbf{x}_i^{(6)}\) is  

\[
\mathrm{Prompt}_i = \big[ \mathbf{x}_i^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_i^{(5)}, \mathbf{x}_i^{(6)} \big] .
\]

The model’s answer is parsed into a label \( \hat{y}_i \in \{A,B,C,D\}\) and compared against the ground‑truth label \(y_i\).  Accuracy is then computed as  

\[
\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{MMLU}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{T}|} \mathbf{1}\big(\hat{y}_i = y_i\big) .
\]

Because MMLU focuses on factual knowledge and reasoning rather than dialogue, it provides a **task‑agnostic** signal of how well the finetuned model has retained the knowledge embedded in the pretrained LLM.  In the QLoRA experiments we report MMLU performance after instruction‑tuning on datasets such as Alpaca or FLAN‑v2 to illustrate that the 4‑bit quantized adapters recover the same level of knowledge as their 16‑bit counterparts.

## Vicuna and OpenAssistant Prompt Suites

While MMLU quantifies *knowledge*, chatbot evaluation requires measuring *instruction following* and *conversational quality*.  Two publicly available prompt collections are employed:

| Prompt Suite | Source | Size | Nature |
|--------------|--------|------|--------|
| **Vicuna**   | Vicuna paper (2023) | 80 prompts | Diverse single‑turn instructions (e.g., “Explain the theory of relativity in simple terms”). |
| **OpenAssistant (OA)** | OpenAssistant dataset (2023) | 953 user turns (extracted from validation set) | Multi‑turn dialogues that include context from preceding exchanges. |

For each prompt we generate a response with the model under evaluation using a **nucleus sampling** strategy with probability \(p=0.9\) and temperature \(\tau = 0.7\).  The same decoding configuration is used for all systems to eliminate confounding effects from differing sampling parameters.

The generated responses, together with a reference response (typically the output of GPT‑4 or ChatGPT), become the input for both the automated and human rating pipelines described below.

---

## Automated Evaluation Pipeline Using GPT‑4 Scoring and Pairwise Comparisons

### 1. Relative Scoring Against a Reference Model

Following the protocol introduced by the Vicuna paper, we ask **GPT‑4** to rate the quality of a candidate response relative to a reference response (usually from ChatGPT‑3.5‑Turbo).  The prompt given to GPT‑4 is of the form:

```
User query: <prompt>
Reference answer (ChatGPT): <reference>
Candidate answer (Model X): <candidate>
Rate each answer on a scale of 0–10 and explain your reasoning.
```

GPT‑4 returns two scores, \(s_{\text{ref}}\) and \(s_{\text{cand}}\).  The **relative performance** of Model X on that query is then defined as  

\[
\mathrm{Score}_{\text{GPT‑4}} = 100 \times \frac{s_{\text{cand}}}{s_{\text{ref}}}.
\]

A score above 100 indicates that GPT‑4 judged the candidate to be better than the reference.  Because GPT‑4 exhibits a **position bias**—the answer that appears first in the prompt tends to receive a higher rating—we repeat the evaluation with the order of the two answers swapped and average the two resulting percentages.  This mitigates the ordering effect while preserving the relative scoring semantics.

### 2. Pairwise Preference Judgments

In addition to absolute scores we also solicit **pairwise comparisons** from GPT‑4.  For any ordered pair of systems \((A, B)\) we prompt GPT‑4 with

```
User query: <prompt>
Answer A: <response A>
Answer B: <response B>
Which answer is better? Respond with "A", "B", or "Tie" and provide a brief justification.
```

GPT‑4’s output is encoded as a categorical preference:
\[
\pi_{AB} = 
\begin{cases}
+1 & \text{if A is preferred},\\
-1 & \text{if B is preferred},\\
0  & \text{if Tie}.
\end{cases}
\]

All **unordered pairs** of evaluated models are queried, producing a dense matrix of preferences for each prompt.  The same ordering‑bias mitigation is applied: we run the comparison twice, swapping the positions of A and B, and resolve any disagreement by majority vote (or by treating a conflict as a tie).

These pairwise judgments constitute the input to the Elo rating computation described later.

## Human Evaluation Design on Amazon MTurk

To verify that automated ratings reflect genuine human judgments, we conduct a parallel **human evaluation** on the same two benchmark suites.

### Annotator Recruitment and Qualification

* **Platform:** Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT).  
* **Eligibility:** Workers must have a HIT approval rate ≥ 98 % and have completed ≥ 5,000 HITs.  
* **Qualification Test:** Before participating, annotators answer a short set of 5 sanity‑check questions (e.g., “Which response is more polite?”) where the correct answer is unambiguous.  Those who achieve ≥ 80 % accuracy are admitted.

### Annotation Tasks

Two distinct tasks are created:

1. **Reference‑based scoring** – analogous to the GPT‑4 relative scoring.  Annotators see the prompt, a reference answer (ChatGPT), and a candidate answer (the model under test).  They assign a score from 0 to 10 to each answer **independently** and optionally provide a brief justification.  The final relative score is computed exactly as in the automated pipeline.

2. **Pairwise preference** – annotators are shown a prompt and two candidate answers (no reference model).  They select **A**, **B**, or **Tie**, then optionally comment on the reason.  

Each prompt is evaluated by **three independent annotators** for the pairwise task and by **two annotators** for the reference‑based scoring.  This redundancy allows us to estimate inter‑annotator agreement and to resolve ties via majority vote.

### Quality Control

* **Gold‑standard checks:** Randomly interleaved “attention‑check” items with obvious correct answers (e.g., a response that explicitly defers due to lack of real‑time knowledge versus a fabricated answer).  Annotators who fail > 20 % of these checks are discarded.
* **Post‑hoc reliability:** We compute **Fleiss’ κ** for the pairwise task and **Pearson correlation** between the two scores in the reference‑based task.  The observed κ values (≈ 0.42) indicate moderate agreement, which is consistent with prior work on subjective chatbot evaluation.

All human judgments are collected under a **standardized instruction sheet** that defines the scoring rubric, emphasizes the importance of relevance, factual correctness, and linguistic fluency, and clarifies that style (e.g., length) should not dominate the decision.

## Elo Rating Computation: Tournament Construction, K‑Factor, Random Seeding

The **Elo rating system** transforms a set of pairwise outcomes into a single scalar per model that reflects its overall “skill”.  The procedure follows the classic formulation used in competitive games like chess.

### 1. Tournament Graph

For each benchmark (Vicuna and OA) we construct a **complete round‑robin tournament**:

* Nodes \(V\) correspond to the evaluated models (including reference systems such as ChatGPT‑3.5‑Turbo and GPT‑4).  
* An edge \((i, j)\) is assigned a result \(r_{ij}\in\{1,0.5,0\}\) derived from the preference matrix:
  * \(r_{ij}=1\) if model \(i\) is preferred over \(j\),  
  * \(r_{ij}=0\) if \(j\) is preferred,  
  * \(r_{ij}=0.5\) for a tie.

When multiple annotators disagree, we average the categorical outcomes and treat any value in \([0.75,1]\) as a win for \(i\), \([0,0.25]\) as a loss, and the remainder as a tie.

### 2. Elo Update Rule

Each model \(i\) holds a rating \(R_i\).  For a match against \(j\), the **expected score** for \(i\) is  

\[
E_{ij} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_j - R_i)/400}} .
\]

After observing the actual result \(r_{ij}\), the rating update is  

\[
R_i \leftarrow R_i + K\big(r_{ij} - E_{ij}\big) ,
\]

where \(K\) is the **K‑factor** that controls the learning rate of the rating.  In all experiments we set \(K=32\), a conventional choice that balances responsiveness and stability.

### 3. Random Seeding and Repetition

The order in which matches are processed influences the final ratings because each update uses the *current* ratings of the opponents.  To eliminate systematic ordering effects we:

* Randomly shuffle the list of matches before each full pass over the tournament.  
* Perform **10 000 independent tournament runs** with different random seeds.  
* Average the final ratings across runs to obtain a robust estimate \(\bar{R}_i\) for each model.

The variance of \(\bar{R}_i\) across runs is reported as a confidence interval (typically ± 5 points), allowing us to gauge the stability of the ranking.

### 4. Integration of Absolute Scores

The Elo framework only consumes **relative** preferences.  To incorporate the **absolute** GPT‑4 or human relative scores (the 0–10‑scale results), we treat each score as an additional “virtual opponent” with a fixed rating (e.g., 1500).  The candidate model’s win probability against this virtual opponent is calibrated to match the observed percentage, thereby anchoring the Elo scale to an interpretable baseline (ChatGPT’s performance is set to roughly 1000 on the Vicuna benchmark).

## Analysis of Agreement Between GPT‑4 and Human Judges

Having obtained two independent sources of pairwise judgments, we examine how closely the **automated GPT‑4** evaluator aligns with **human annotators**.

### System‑Level Correlation

We compute **Kendall’s τ** and **Spearman’s ρ** between the Elo rankings derived from GPT‑4 and those derived from human AMT judgments.  The observed values (τ ≈ 0.43, ρ ≈ 0.55) indicate a **moderate positive correlation**: models that fare well under GPT‑4 tend also to be rated highly by humans, but there are notable divergences.

### Example‑Level Agreement

For each individual prompt we calculate the **majority vote** among human annotators and compare it to GPT‑4’s preference.  The **Fleiss’ κ** across prompts is 0.25, reflecting **fair** agreement.  This lower level of concordance is expected because individual prompts often invoke subjective judgments about style, tone, or creative merit, which can differ between a language model (GPT‑4) and human raters.

### Identified Biases

Two systematic biases emerge from the analysis:

1. **Order effect** – GPT‑4 is more likely to award a higher score to the answer that appears first in the prompt.  By explicitly swapping answer order and averaging, we reduce this bias, but residual effects remain, especially in borderline cases.  
2. **Self‑favoritism** – When GPT‑4 is asked to compare its own output against a competitor, it tends to assign itself a modest advantage (≈ 20 % higher win probability).  Human raters do not display this upward bias, leading to a slight inflation of the Elo rating for GPT‑4 in the automated pipeline.

These observations motivate the inclusion of **human‐in‑the‑loop** validation for any high‑stakes deployment scenario, while still recognizing the **cost‑effectiveness** of GPT‑4 as a primary evaluator for large‑scale experiments.

## Summary

The evaluation methodology described here leverages:

* **MMLU** to verify that quantized adapter finetuning does not erode factual knowledge.  
* **Vicuna** and **OpenAssistant** prompt suites to probe instruction following and conversational competence.  
* A **dual rating system** consisting of GPT‑4‑based automated scoring and carefully controlled human judgments on AMT.  
* **Elo rating** to aggregate pairwise preferences into a single, comparable metric, with robust random‑seeding and repeated tournament simulations.

The combination of these components yields a comprehensive picture of chatbot performance: quantitative scores on a knowledge benchmark, nuanced assessments of conversational ability, and an evidence‑based understanding of how well an automated evaluator mirrors human perception.  These tools together enable the authors to demonstrate that the **Guanaco** family of models—trained with QLoRA—approaches or even exceeds the performance of proprietary systems such as ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, while remaining tractable to train on a single GPU.
