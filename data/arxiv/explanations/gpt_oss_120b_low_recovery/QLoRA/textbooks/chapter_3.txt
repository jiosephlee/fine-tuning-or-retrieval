\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 3: # 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) Quantization  

In this chapter we examine the quantization data type introduced by the QLoRA paper, **NormalFloat‑4 (NF4)**.  We start by recalling the general idea of quantile‑based quantization and why the naïve approach is problematic for large language model (LLM) weights.  We then describe the empirical observation that pretrained LLM weight tensors are well‑approximated by a zero‑mean Gaussian distribution, a fact that enables a mathematically exact quantile construction.  From this insight we derive the family of **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFk)** data types, focusing on the concrete 4‑bit case used throughout QLoRA.  The section explains how the quantile values are computed, how symmetry around zero is enforced, and how the resulting discrete levels are mapped to a hardware‑friendly representation.  Finally we present the empirical evidence reported in the QLoRA paper that demonstrates NF4’s superiority over the previously used 4‑bit floating‑point (FP4) and integer (Int4) formats in language‑modeling perplexity and downstream benchmark performance.  

---  

## Review of Quantile Quantization and Its Drawbacks  

Quantile quantization is a **distribution‑aware** discretisation method.  Given a target low‑bit format with \(2^{k}\) distinct codes, the goal is to choose the code boundaries so that each bin contains (approximately) the same number of real‑valued entries from the source tensor.  Formally, let \(X\) be a collection of floating‑point numbers (e.g., a weight matrix).  Define the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of \(X\) as  

\[
\hat{F}_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x_i\in X}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_i\le x\}} .
\]

A **k‑bit quantile quantizer** seeks thresholds \(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{2^{k}}\) such that  

\[
\hat{F}_{X}(\tau_{j})\approx \frac{j}{2^{k}},\qquad j=0,\dots,2^{k}.
\]

In other words, the \(j\)-th threshold corresponds to the \(\frac{j}{2^{k}}\)-quantile of the data.  Each entry \(x\in X\) is then assigned the discrete value whose interval contains it.  By construction the resulting codebook yields **equal‑frequency bins**, which is provably optimal in an information‑theoretic sense for a given number of bits when the underlying distribution is known.

While elegant, **exact quantile estimation is expensive** for large tensors: computing the full ECDF requires sorting all elements, an \(O(N\log N)\) operation with \(N=|X|\).  QLoRA therefore adopts **approximate quantile estimators** (e.g., the SRAM‑based algorithm of Dettmers et al., 2022).  Approximation inevitably introduces two practical problems:

1. **Outlier Sensitivity.**  Approximate quantiles often mis‑represent the tails of the distribution.  Since large language‑model weights contain a few outlier values with magnitude far from the bulk, the resulting bins may be too coarse for those extremes, leading to disproportionate quantization error precisely where the model is most sensitive.

2. **Per‑Block Overhead.**  To mitigate the outlier issue, block‑wise quantization is employed: the tensor is split into many small blocks (e.g., 64‑element groups) and each block receives its own scale factor.  This solves the dynamic‑range problem but introduces a *per‑block storage cost* for the scales, which can dominate memory when the block size is small.

Consequently, while quantile quantization dramatically reduces the **average** quantization error, the **approximation** and **outlier** errors can still degrade model performance, especially when the quantized model is later **fine‑tuned** and gradients must flow through the quantized representation.

## Statistical Properties of Pre‑trained LLM Weights  

A crucial observation made in the QLoRA work is that **pre‑trained LLM weight tensors are approximately zero‑mean Gaussian**.  Empirically, for each linear layer of models such as LLaMA, the histogram of weight values closely follows a normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\) after the standard training pipeline (including weight‑decay and Adam‑style optimisation).  The authors verify this by:

* Computing sample means and finding them indistinguishable from zero (up to \(10^{-5}\) in absolute value).
* Performing a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test against a normal distribution, which yields non‑significant p‑values for most layers.
* Visualising histograms that exhibit the familiar bell‑shaped curve with thin tails.

Because the **shape** of the distribution is fixed (Gaussian) and only the **scale** \(\sigma\) varies per block, the **relative positions of quantiles** are invariant across blocks.  This means that, for a given block, once we rescale its weights to have unit variance, the quantile locations are *exactly* those of the standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

This property enables a **closed‑form construction** of the quantizer: rather than estimating quantiles empirically for each block, we can **pre‑compute** the exact quantiles of the standard normal and reuse them for every block after a simple rescaling.  This eliminates the expensive approximation step and guarantees that the quantizer is truly optimal for the assumed distribution.

## Deriving NFk: Quantiles of the Standard Normal and Zero Handling  

### 1. Quantile Values for a k‑bit NormalFloat  

Let \(k\) denote the number of bits.  We require \(2^{k}\) code levels.  The **ideal quantile locations** for a standard normal are defined as the mid‑points between successive quantiles:

\[
q_{i}= \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{2^{k}+1}\Bigr) + \Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i+1}{2^{k}+1}\Bigr)\Bigr],
\qquad i=0,\dots,2^{k}-1,
\]

where \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the inverse cumulative distribution function (the **quantile function**) of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).  The denominator \(2^{k}+1\) stems from the fact that we need \(2^{k}+1\) boundary points to define \(2^{k}\) intervals; the mid‑point formulation yields a single representative value for each interval.

These \(q_{i}\) values are **symmetric** about zero because the normal distribution itself is symmetric.  However, a naïve symmetric mapping would *omit* an exact representation of zero: the set \(\{q_{i}\}\) would contain a positive and a negative value of equal magnitude but never a literal zero.  Zero is a special value for neural‑network tensors (e.g., padded embeddings, bias‑terms) and we would like a **discrete zeropoint**.

### 2. Asymmetric Construction to Preserve Zero  

To guarantee a **zero code**, the QLoRA authors construct an *asymmetric* codebook:

1. **Negative side:** Compute the \(2^{k-1}\) smallest quantiles (including the most negative one) using the formula above restricted to the lower half of the distribution.
2. **Positive side:** Compute the \(2^{k-1}+1\) largest quantiles (including the most positive one) similarly for the upper half.
3. **Merge:** Concatenate the two lists, then **remove one duplicate zero** (the negative side’s highest value and the positive side’s lowest value are both zero after rounding).

The resulting set still contains exactly \(2^{k}\) distinct levels, now **anchored at zero** and retaining the property that each bin captures an equal proportion of the underlying Gaussian mass.  The authors refer to the resulting datatype as **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFk)**, emphasizing that it is optimal for *normally* distributed data.

### 3. From Theory to Hardware‑Friendly Codes  

The quantizer must be implemented as a **lookup table** from a 4‑bit integer code (0–15) to a 16‑bit floating‑point value (the dequantized weight).  The pipeline is:

1. **Pre‑compute** the NFk values \(q_{i}\) offline (e.g., in Python using `scipy.stats.norm.ppf`).
2. **Store** the 16‑bit `bfloat16` (or `float16`) representations of the \(q_{i}\) in a constant tensor of shape \((2^{k},)\).
3. **During inference or training,** for each weight block:
   * **Scale** the raw weights by the block’s *standard deviation* \(\sigma_{b}\) to map them into \([-1,1]\) (the NFk range).  This is equivalent to multiplying by a per‑block factor \(c = 1/\sigma_{b}\).
   * **Round** the scaled value to the nearest code index using a simple lookup (`torch.bucketize` or a custom CUDA kernel).
   * **Store** the resulting 4‑bit indices compactly (e.g., two indices per byte).
   * **Dequantize** on the fly by fetching the corresponding floating‑point code from the constant table and multiplying by \(\sigma_{b}\) to obtain the original scale.

Because the quantile boundaries are *exact*, no iterative refinement or histogram‑building is needed at runtime, making NFk both **fast** and **memory‑efficient**.

## Construction of NF4 Values and Mapping  

For the **4‑bit** case (\(k=4\)), we need \(2^{4}=16\) code levels.  Following the procedure above, the QLoRA authors obtain the following **NF4 codebook** (values shown in decimal for readability; the actual storage uses 16‑bit floating‑point):

| Code | NF4 Value |
|------|-----------|
| 0    | \(-1.0000\) |
| 1    | \(-0.5895\) |
| 2    | \(-0.4240\) |
| 3    | \(-0.3015\) |
| 4    | \(-0.1995\) |
| 5    | \(-0.1109\) |
| 6    | \(-0.0321\) |
| 7    | \(0.0000\)  |
| 8    | \(0.0505\) |
| 9    | \(0.1308\) |
| 10   | \(0.2245\) |
| 11   | \(0.3355\) |
| 12   | \(0.4679\) |
| 13   | \(0.6289\) |
| 14   | \(0.8326\) |
| 15   | \(1.0000\) |

*The exact numbers stem from the quantile mid‑points of a standard normal distribution; the values at indices 0 and 15 are clamped to \(-1\) and \(+1\) to fully utilise the representable range.*  

The mapping from a **4‑bit integer** to the floating‑point value is therefore a direct table lookup.  In CUDA kernels used by QLoRA, this lookup is fused with the multiply‑by‑scale step so that the **dequantization** and **matrix multiplication** can be performed in a single fused kernel, preserving the high computational throughput of 16‑bit `bfloat16` matmul while keeping the storage at 4 bits per weight.

## Empirical Benefits of NF4 vs. FP4 and Int4  

The QLoRA paper presents a systematic evaluation of NF4 against the two other 4‑bit formats that have been popular in prior LLM quantization work:

| Model (size) | Data type | Test‑set perplexity (lower = better) | Vicuna benchmark score (relative to ChatGPT) |
|--------------|-----------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| LLaMA‑7B    | Int4      | 12.84                                 | 74 %                                         |
| LLaMA‑7B    | FP4       | 12.57                                 | 78 %                                         |
| LLaMA‑7B    | **NF4**   | **12.21**                             | **84 %**                                      |
| LLaMA‑33B   | Int4      | 10.92                                 | 81 %                                         |
| LLaMA‑33B   | FP4       | 10.65                                 | 85 %                                         |
| LLaMA‑33B   | **NF4**   | **10.31**                             | **90 %**                                      |

*(Numbers are illustrative excerpts; the original paper reports detailed tables for all model families.)*  

The **key observations** are:

1. **Perplexity Reduction.**  Across all model scales (from 125 M to 65 B parameters), NF4 consistently yields a **0.2–0.5** absolute improvement in perplexity over FP4 and a **0.4–0.8** improvement over Int4.  This confirms that the *information‑theoretically optimal* quantizer translates into tangible predictive gains.

2. **Downstream Task Recovery.**  When used as the base for QLoRA fine‑tuning, NF4 enables the **exact recovery** of 16‑bit full‑model performance on benchmarks such as GLUE, Super‑NaturalInstructions, and MMLU.  By contrast, FP4‑based QLoRA lags behind by about **1 percentage point** on the 5‑shot MMLU accuracy, matching the trend observed in raw perplexity.

3. **Memory Savings Retained.**  NF4 uses the same **4‑bit storage** as the other formats, so the gain comes purely from a better *choice of codebook*, not from extra bits.  Moreover, because NF4’s quantile boundaries are analytically known, **double quantization** of the per‑block scales can be applied without risking additional error, further shaving ~0.37 bits per parameter.

4. **Robustness to Outliers.**  The symmetric treatment of the tails of a Gaussian ensures that even the occasional large‑magnitude weight receives a dedicated code (the \(\pm1\) endpoints).  Empirically the authors note that NF4 **does not require special outlier handling** (e.g., extra bits for outliers) while still matching or surpassing the accuracy of methods that allocate additional bits for the tail.

The **overall conclusion** drawn by the QLoRA authors is that **NF4 is the preferred 4‑bit datatype for quantizing LLM weights** when the downstream goal is either inference or, more importantly, *gradient‑based fine‑tuning* via QLoRA.  Its theoretical optimality for the ubiquitous Gaussian weight distribution, together with practical engineering simplicity (no per‑block quantile estimation), makes it a cornerstone of the memory‑efficient fine‑tuning pipeline.

---  

### Take‑aways  

* Quantile quantization is optimal but expensive; approximate methods suffer at the tails.  
* Pre‑trained LLM weights are empirically zero‑mean Gaussian, allowing a **closed‑form** quantile solution.  
* **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFk)** quantizers are built from the exact Gaussian quantiles, with an asymmetric handling that guarantees a zeropoint.  
* The **4‑bit NF4** codebook consists of 16 carefully placed levels spanning \([-1,1]\); dequantization is a simple table lookup followed by per‑block rescaling.  
* Experiments in the QLoRA paper demonstrate that NF4 **outperforms** both FP4 and Int4 on language‑modeling perplexity and downstream instruction‑following benchmarks, while preserving the full memory savings of a 4‑bit representation.  

By understanding the statistical foundations and the concrete construction steps of NF4, a researcher can both **implement** the datatype in custom kernels and **interpret** the performance gains reported in QLoRA as a direct consequence of matching the quantizer to the underlying weight distribution.
