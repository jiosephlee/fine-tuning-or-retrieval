# Evaluating Chatbots Without Paying for Human Labels: GPT‑4 vs. Humans in QLoRA Benchmarks  

Large language models (LLMs) have become powerful conversational agents, but measuring *how good* a chatbot is remains expensive. Human annotators are the gold standard, yet scaling human evaluation to thousands of model variants quickly becomes prohibitive. The recent **QLoRA** paper proposes an elegant workaround: use **GPT‑4** as a cheap, model‑based evaluator and compare its judgments against a limited set of human ratings. In this post we unpack their evaluation pipeline, highlight where GPT‑4 and humans agree or diverge, and give practical advice for researchers who want to rely on model‑based evaluation without sacrificing reliability.

---

## 1. Why a New Evaluation Protocol?  

Traditional chatbot benchmarks (e.g., Vicuna, OpenAssistant) rely on **absolute scores** (a 0–10 rating) or on **multiple‑choice correctness**. Those methods have two major drawbacks:

1. **Subjectivity & Ambiguity** – A “8 out of 10” means different things to different raters, making cross‑paper comparisons noisy.  
2. **Cost** – Human annotation at scale (thousands of prompts × many model outputs) quickly exceeds budgets, especially for academic labs.

The QLoRA authors therefore built a *pairwise* evaluation framework that mitigates both issues:

- **Elo Tournament**: Instead of absolute scores, models face each other in head‑to‑head matches. The outcome (“model A wins vs. model B”) feeds into an Elo rating that naturally accounts for relative strength.
- **Model‑Based Scoring**: GPT‑4 is prompted to select the better response (or call a tie). Because GPT‑4 can be queried at scale for a few cents per thousand calls, this eliminates the bulk of human labor.
- **Human Spot Checks**: A modest number of human annotators (2–3 per prompt) provide a benchmark to verify that GPT‑4’s rankings are trustworthy.

The combination gives us the best of both worlds: cheap large‑scale rankings plus an anchor to real human preferences.

---

## 2. The Pipeline in Detail  

### 2.1 Prompt Sets  

The authors evaluate on two complementary corpora:

| Benchmark | Size | Nature |
|-----------|------|--------|
| **Vicuna** | 80 hand‑crafted prompts covering diverse topics | Short, often single‑turn QA |
| **OpenAssistant (OA)** | 953 user turns drawn from multi‑turn dialogues | Realistic conversational context |

Both sets are publicly available, making the pipeline reproducible.

### 2.2 Generating Model Outputs  

For each prompt the authors generate responses from all candidate chatbots (Guanaco family, Vicuna 13B, OpenAssistant 33B, GPT‑3.5‑Turbo, GPT‑4, etc.) using the same decoding parameters (nucleus sampling *p* = 0.9, temperature = 0.7). This standardization prevents confounding effects due to different sampling strategies.

### 2.3 Pairwise Comparisons  

Every ordered pair of systems is compared on every prompt:

1. **GPT‑4 Evaluation**  
   - Prompt structure: “Given the user query and two assistant replies, rate each on a scale of 0–10 and explain why.”  
   - To neutralize ordering bias, the two replies are swapped and the process repeated; scores are then averaged.

2. **Human Evaluation**  
   - For the Vicuna set they use Amazon Mechanical Turk with two annotators per comparison; OA comparisons receive three annotators.
   - Annotators are asked to pick the better response or declare a tie, providing a short justification.

This yields three possible outcomes per pair: *A wins*, *B wins*, or *Tie*.

### 2️⃣3 Elo Computation  

All pairwise outcomes are fed into an Elo rating system (starting at 1 000 points; K = 32). The Elo algorithm updates each model’s rating after every match based on the expected win probability:

\[
\Delta R = K \times ( \text{actual outcome} - \text{expected outcome} )
\]

Because matches are sampled randomly and repeated across many seeds (10 000 runs), the final Elo scores are robust to ordering effects.

## 3. Where Do GPT‑4 and Humans Agree?  

### 3.1 System‑Level Correlation  

When aggregating over all prompts and matches:

- **Kendall’s τ ≈ 0.43**  
- **Spearman’s ρ ≈ 0.55**

These moderate correlations indicate that GPT‑4 and humans rank models similarly *on average*. In practice this means that if GPT‑4 places Guanaco 65B just behind ChatGPT and ahead of Vicuna 13B, humans are likely to produce a comparable ordering.

### 3.2 High‑Confidence Wins  

For clear performance gaps (>15 Elo points), both evaluators almost always agree:

| Pair | GPT‑4 Verdict | Human Verdict |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| Guanaco 65B > Alpaca 13B | ✔ | ✔ |
| GPT‑4 > all open‑source models | ✔ | ✔ |
| Vicuna 13B ≈ Guanaco 7B | ✔ (tie) | ✔ (tie) |

The consistency on large margins gives confidence that GPT‑4 can be used as a *screening tool*—e.g., ruling out clearly inferior configurations before committing human resources.

---

## 4. Where Do They Diverge?  

### 4.1 Fine‑Grained Differences  

When models differ by only a few Elo points (e.g., Guanaco 33B vs. Vicuna 13B), the agreement drops:

- **Example:** GPT‑4 gave Guanaco 33B a small edge; human annotators split roughly evenly.
- This reflects human subjectivity—some raters prioritize factual correctness while others value fluency or tone.

### 4.2 Order Effects in GPT‑4  

The authors observed that GPT‑4 tends to score the response presented first *higher*, even after swapping order and averaging. Although averaging reduces bias, a residual effect remains, especially for borderline cases.

### 4.3 Sample‑Level Disagreement  

Using Fleiss’ κ to measure inter‑annotator agreement:

- **Human vs. Human:** κ ≈ 0.42 (moderate)
- **GPT‑4 vs. Human Majority Vote:** κ ≈ 0.25 (fair)

Thus, on an individual prompt level, GPT‑4 can make different choices than humans—sometimes favoring more “polished” language over factual accuracy.

---

## 5. Guidelines for Using Model‑Based Evaluation Responsibly  

1️⃣ **Treat GPT‑4 Rankings as *Relative* Signals**  
   - Use them to prune large candidate sets or detect major regressions.
   - Do not treat a single Elo score as an absolute quality metric.

2️⃣ **Validate with Human Spot Checks**  
   - Allocate human effort to a random subset (~5–10% of prompts).  
   - If the correlation between GPT‑4 and humans falls below ~0.5, reconsider reliance on automatic scores.

3️⃣ **Mitigate Order Bias**  
   - Always run both orderings and average results (as done in QLoRA).  
   - Optionally shuffle prompts across runs to further dilute systematic effects.

4️⃣ **Combine Multiple Metrics**  
   - Pairwise Elo captures relative preference but ignores calibrated confidence levels.
   - Complement with automated metrics such as perplexity or factuality detectors for a fuller picture.

5️⃣ **Beware of Domain Shift**  
   - The evaluation was performed on Vicuna and OA prompts; using it on unrelated domains (e.g., legal reasoning) may inflate divergences.
   - Conduct a quick human validation when moving to new task distributions.

6️⃣ **Document All Settings**  
   - Prompt template used for GPT‑4 matters; even small phrasing changes can shift its judgments.
   - Record temperature/generation parameters; otherwise comparisons become noisy.

---

## 6. Takeaways  

The QLoRA paper demonstrates that with careful design—pairwise Elo tournaments plus a modest amount of human verification—it is feasible to replace most costly human labeling with **GPT‑4 scoring** without severely compromising reliability. The key insights are:

- **Relative rankings work better than absolute scores**, especially when many models compete.
- **GPT‑4 is trustworthy for large performance gaps**, but its judgments become noisy when models are close in quality.
- **Human checks remain essential**, not as a primary evaluation engine but as an anchor that validates model bias and identifies failure modes.

For researchers who lack the budget for massive crowdsourced studies, this evaluation recipe offers an accessible middle ground: scale up experiments cheaply with GPT‑4 while still preserving scientific rigor through systematic human spot checks.
