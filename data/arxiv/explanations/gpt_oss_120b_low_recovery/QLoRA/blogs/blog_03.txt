# Low‑Rank Adapters Meet Quantized Weights: The LoRA Tricks That Recover Full‑Precision Accuracy  

Fine‑tuning gigantic language models used to be a luxury reserved for labs with dozens of high‑end GPUs.  A 65 B‑parameter model, for example, required **more than 780 GB** of GPU memory if you tried to update every weight in the usual 16‑bit format.  The recent paper *QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs* shows that you can now train the same model on a single 48 GB GPU **without losing any performance**.  The secret sauce?  A clever combination of **4‑bit NormalFloat quantization** and **Low‑rank Adapter (LoRA) layers placed in every transformer block**, plus a few engineering tricks to keep the memory budget in check.

In this post we unpack how LoRA works, why sprinkling adapters across all layers matters for QLoRA, and how back‑propagating through frozen, quantized weights restores the accuracy you’d expect from a full‑precision finetune.

---

## 1. A Quick Recap: What Is LoRA?

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) was introduced as a parameter‑efficient way to specialize a giant pretrained model without touching its massive weight matrix.  
Consider a standard linear projection  

\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W},
\]

where \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) and \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\).  
Instead of updating \(\mathbf{W}\) directly, LoRA adds a **pair of low‑rank matrices** \(\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) (with \(r\ll h,o\)):

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}+ s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\;}
\]

where \(s\) is a scaling factor.  
During training only \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\) receive gradients; the original \(\mathbf{W}\) stays frozen.  Because the adapters are tiny (often < 0.2 % of total parameters), the memory footprint for *learnable* weights is negligible.

---

## 2. Why “Adapters Everywhere” Is Crucial for QLoRA  

Traditional LoRA recipes often target **just a few matrices**—most commonly the query and value projections in each attention block.  The QLoRA authors discovered that this sparse placement **fails to close the gap** to full‑precision finetuning, especially for larger models (33 B and 65 B).

Their extensive ablations (see Figure 2 in the paper) reveal two key observations:

| Adapter placement | Observed effect on a 7 B LLaMA finetune |
|-------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Only Q/K/V projections | Significant drop (~1–2 % absolute) compared to full finetune |
| **All linear layers** (attention + feed‑forward) | **Matches** 16‑bit full finetuning performance |

Why does this happen?  

1. **Gradient flow through every layer** – When you back‑propagate through a frozen weight matrix, the gradient signal that reaches an adapter depends on the *local Jacobian* of that matrix. If many layers are left untouched, their Jacobians act as bottlenecks, limiting how much useful information reaches the adapters. Adding adapters to *every* linear transformation (including the MLPs inside each transformer block) gives each layer its own learnable degree of freedom, effectively “amplifying” gradient information throughout the network.

2. **Compensating quantization error** – Quantizing weights down to 4 bits introduces small but systematic distortions (e.g., rounding of outlier values). By giving each layer its own low‑rank correction term, the model can locally re‑calibrate those distortions, restoring the representation quality that would otherwise be lost.

The takeaway: **When training with quantized weights, put LoRA adapters everywhere**. It costs almost nothing extra—LoRA parameters still occupy < 0.5 % of total memory—but it is essential for recovering full‑precision accuracy.

---

## 3. Backpropagating Through Frozen 4‑Bit Weights

QLoRA’s main novelty lies in letting gradients flow *through* a frozen, **4‑bit quantized** version of the pretrained model. This sounds risky: after all, gradients are usually computed with floating‑point numbers while our stored weights live in an ultra‑compact format like NF4 (NormalFloat‑4). The solution involves two steps:

### 3.1 Dequantize On‑The‑Fly  
During both forward and backward passes, QLoRA **temporarily dequantizes** each weight block from its compact NF4 representation to bfloat16 (BF16). The dequantization formula is simply

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} = \text{dequant}\bigl(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\bigr),
\]

where \(c_1,c_2\) are the per‑block scaling constants (the latter themselves double‑quantized to save memory). Once in BF16, regular matrix multiplications proceed as if we were training a full‑precision model.

### 3.2 Update Only LoRA Parameters  
Crucially, we never compute or store gradients w.r.t. \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\). The backward pass still needs \(\partial \mathcal L / \partial \mathbf W^{\text{BF16}}\) because this derivative propagates *through* the dequantization step to compute gradients for the adapters:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \mathbf L_i}
    = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \mathbf Y}
      \cdot
      \frac{\partial (\mathbf X \mathbf L_1 \mathbf L_2)}{\partial \mathbf L_i},
      \qquad i=1,2.
\]

Since all other terms involve only fixed weights (now in BF16), they don’t require updates. This means memory consumption stays dominated by activation storage and optimizer states—not by the massive original parameter matrix.

### 3.3 Why No Accuracy Loss?  
Empirically, QLoRA with NF4 plus double quantization *exactly matches* 16‑bit full finetuning on GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, and even on large-scale benchmarks like MMLU up to 65 B parameters (see Table 3 in the paper). The intuition is that LoRA adapters act as **learnable residuals** that can correct any discrepancy introduced by quantization, provided they have enough capacity—hence why we need adapters in every layer.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together: The QLoRA Pipeline  

1. **Quantize** the pretrained checkpoint to NF4 per weight block (blocksize = 64).  
2. **Double quantize** scaling constants from FP32 → FP8 → FP32 (blocksize = 256) – saves ~0.37 bits/parameter.  
3. Insert LoRA adapters (\(r=64\) or similar) into **every linear layer** of the transformer stack.  
4. During training:
   - Dequantize each block to BF16 on demand.
   - Compute forward activations normally.
   - Backpropagate through dequantized tensors; compute gradients only for LoRA matrices.
   - Use *paged optimizers* (CUDA unified memory) so optimizer states can spill safely when training long sequences on limited GPU RAM.

The result? A **65 B model fine‑tuned on a single RTX 4090 (48 GB)** in under 24 hours that reaches **99.3 % of ChatGPT’s Vicuna benchmark score**, while consuming < 5 GB at inference time.

---

## 5. Takeaways for Practitioners  

| Insight | Practical Recommendation |
|---------|---------------------------|
| LoRA adapters must be placed everywhere | When using QLoRA (or any quantized fine‑tuning), add adapters to *all* linear transformations—not just attention heads. |
| Only adapters are trainable | Freeze the quantized base model completely; this dramatically cuts optimizer memory usage. |
| Use NF4 + double quantization | NF4 is information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights; double quantization further trims memory without harming accuracy. |
| Paged optimizers are optional but helpful | For very long sequences or batch sizes that temporarily exceed GPU RAM, enable unified memory paging for optimizer states to avoid OOM crashes. |

---

## 6. Looking Ahead  

QLoRA demonstrates that **low‑rank adaptation + smart quantization = full‑precision performance at fraction of the cost**. Several exciting directions follow:

- Exploring even lower precisions (e.g., 3‑bit or mixed precision) combined with richer adapter families.
- Mixing LoRA with other PEFT techniques like IA³ or Prompt Tuning to see if synergy yields better tradeoffs.
- Leveraging QLoRA on edge devices: early experiments suggest a 7 B model could be fine‑tuned on a phone overnight while charging.

The core message is clear: you no longer need an entire GPU farm to adapt state-of-the-art LLMs to your niche task or domain. By embracing low-rank adapters everywhere and letting gradients pass through frozen quantized weights, you can recover *exactly* what full-precision fine-tuning would give—only cheaper and more accessible.

--- 

*TL;DR*: In QLoRA you freeze a 4‑bit NF4‐quantized model, add LoRA adapters to every transformer layer, and backpropagate through the dequantized weights while updating only those tiny adapters. The result is full-precision accuracy with < 48 GB GPU memory—a game changer for anyone who wants to fine-tune today’s biggest language models without breaking the bank.*
