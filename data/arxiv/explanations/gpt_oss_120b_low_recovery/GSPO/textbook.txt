\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a cornerstone technique for endowing large language models (LLMs) with capabilities that go far beyond mere next‑token prediction.  By treating a language model as a stochastic policy that generates responses to user queries, RL enables the model to be optimized with respect to a scalar reward that measures the quality of the whole response—whether that quality is mathematical correctness, programming competence, or alignment with human preferences.  This chapter introduces the fundamental reasons why RL is indispensable for scaling LLMs, surveys the successes that have been achieved through large‑scale RL, explains why existing RL algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) encounter severe stability problems when applied to gigantic models, and finally presents the high‑level contributions of the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm.

---

## RL as a Paradigm for Language Model Scaling  

In the classical supervised‑learning regime, a language model is trained to maximize the likelihood of ground‑truth tokens in a static dataset.  This objective encourages the model to imitate the distribution of the data but does not directly reward the kinds of reasoning and problem‑solving that are required for advanced tasks.  Reinforcement learning reframes the model as a **policy** \(\pi_\theta\) parameterized by \(\theta\).  Given a query \(x\) drawn from a query set \(\mathcal{D}\), the policy generates a response \(y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\) according to the autoregressive factorization  

\[
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr),
\]

where each conditional term supplies the probability of the next token given the query and all previously generated tokens.  After a response is produced, an external **verifier** \(r\) evaluates it and outputs a reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).  The RL objective is to adjust \(\theta\) so that the expected reward under the policy is maximized:

\[
\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\; y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr].
\]

Because the reward is defined on the **entire sequence** rather than on individual tokens, RL naturally aligns the training signal with the ultimate task performance.  Moreover, the reward can be made arbitrarily sophisticated (e.g., incorporating symbolic solvers, test‑case execution, or human feedback), thereby allowing the model to acquire capabilities that are impossible to capture with static supervised corpora.

When RL is applied at a massive scale—training models with billions of parameters on millions of query‑response pairs—the resulting policies have demonstrated remarkable emergent abilities.  In particular, large‑scale RL has been instrumental in achieving **competition‑level performance** on demanding benchmarks such as the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME) and on coding platforms that require the generation of syntactically correct and logically sound programs.  These successes illustrate how RL can push the envelope of model reasoning depth, length of coherent generation, and alignment with high‑quality objectives.

---

## Successes of Large‑Scale RL: Mathematics and Coding  

Empirical evidence from recent work on the Qwen family of models shows that RL can dramatically improve performance on tasks that demand precise logical reasoning.  By repeatedly exposing a model to difficult math problems and rewarding correct derivations, the policy learns to conduct multi‑step calculations, apply algebraic transformations, and even discover novel solution strategies.  Similar gains are observed in code generation: after RL fine‑tuning, the model produces programs that pass hidden test cases at a higher rate, indicating a deeper understanding of algorithmic structure and language‑specific idioms.

These achievements are not isolated curiosities; they are the result of **massive reinforcement learning pipelines** that involve billions of tokens of rollout data, sophisticated reward models, and careful hyper‑parameter tuning.  The scale of the rollout batch, the length of the generated responses, and the diversity of the query set are all crucial.  The longer the responses, the more opportunities the model has to demonstrate sustained reasoning, but also the more challenging the optimization becomes, as we discuss next.

---

## Stability Issues in Current RL Algorithms (GRPO, PPO)  

Despite the promise of RL, training a gigantic LLM with RL quickly runs into **stability bottlenecks**.  Two prominent algorithms that have been used for policy optimization in language models are **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** and **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  

### PPO  

PPO constrains the policy update by clipping the **token‑level importance weight**

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)},
\]

so that the ratio stays within \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\).  The PPO objective is  

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_t\bigr)\Bigr],
\]

where \(\widehat{A}_t\) denotes an advantage estimated by a **value model**.  The reliance on a separate value model is problematic at scale: the value model must be as large as the policy model, incurring heavy memory and compute costs, and its estimates become unreliable for long, complex responses.

### GRPO  

GRPO was introduced to avoid the need for a value model.  Instead of a per‑token advantage, GRPO computes a **group‑based advantage** for each response in a set of \(G\) responses generated for the same query:

\[
\widehat{A}_i\;=\;\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}
                         {\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
\]

All tokens in response \(y_i\) share this advantage.  The GRPO objective mirrors PPO but substitutes the group‑wise advantage:

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

Although GRPO removes the value model, it still relies on **token‑level importance ratios** \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).  The paper identifies a fundamental flaw: these ratios are intended to correct for the mismatch between the *old* and *new* policies, but they are computed from a **single sample per next‑token distribution**.  Consequently, they do not satisfy the assumptions of importance sampling, which require averaging over many samples to obtain an unbiased correction.

---

## High‑Variance Gradients and Catastrophic Model Collapse  

The misapplication of token‑level importance weights in GRPO leads to **high‑variance gradient estimates**.  Each token’s weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can differ dramatically from 1, especially when the policy changes appreciably between updates or when the model is a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) whose routing decisions fluctuate.  Because the gradient of the objective essentially multiplies the log‑likelihood gradient of each token by its weight, the noise introduced by these unequal weights accumulates over the length of the sequence.  For long responses—common in math or code generation—the accumulated variance can dominate the learning signal.

Clipping, which is meant to prevent excessively off‑policy updates, paradoxically **amplifies** this problem.  When a weight lies outside the clipping interval, the gradient is replaced by a clipped version that no longer reflects the true importance ratio, injecting a systematic bias.  In practice, training runs that employ GRPO on very large models often experience **catastrophic model collapse**: the policy quickly deteriorates, performance on all downstream benchmarks plummets, and the collapse is irreversible even after restoring checkpoints or drastically retuning hyper‑parameters.

The paper’s empirical investigations reveal that this collapse is not merely a matter of poor hyper‑parameter choices; it is a structural consequence of using token‑level correction where the reward is defined at the **sequence level**.  The mismatch between the granularity of the importance weight (token) and the granularity of the reward (sequence) violates the principle that the *unit of optimization should match the unit of reward*.

---

## Overview of the Paper’s Contributions: GSPO  

To resolve the instability inherent in token‑level importance weighting, the authors propose **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**.  The central insight is to shift the importance ratio from the token level to the **sequence level**:

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

This **sequence‑level importance ratio** quantifies how the entire response distribution under the new policy deviates from that under the old policy, which directly aligns with the sequence‑level reward.  By **length‑normalizing** the ratio, GSPO keeps the magnitude of \(s_i(\theta)\) comparable across responses of different lengths, reducing variance and allowing a single, well‑scaled clipping range (typically on the order of \(10^{-4}\)).

The **GSPO objective** mirrors the structure of GRPO but substitutes the sequence ratio and retains the group‑wise advantage:

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

Because all tokens in a response share the same scaling factor \(s_i(\theta)\), the gradient contribution of each token is **weighted uniformly**, eliminating the volatile per‑token weighting that plagued GRPO.  The paper provides a detailed gradient derivation showing that the GSPO gradient is essentially the standard REINFORCE term multiplied by the sequence ratio, a theoretically sound importance‑sampling correction.

Beyond the core algorithm, the authors introduce a **token‑level variant (GSPO‑token)** that retains sequence‑level clipping while permitting per‑token advantage adjustments.  This variant demonstrates that the sequence‑level importance ratio can be combined with fine‑grained advantage shaping when needed, without sacrificing the stability benefits.

Empirically, GSPO delivers **stable training** across billions of parameters, eliminates the need for the **Routing Replay** trick previously required to stabilize MoE models under GRPO, and achieves **higher sample efficiency** despite clipping a larger fraction of tokens (because the clipped tokens correspond to entire responses, not isolated tokens).  The algorithm has been deployed in the training of the latest Qwen3 models, evidencing its practical impact.

In summary, the contributions of the paper can be distilled into three points:

1. **Theoretical clarification** of why token‑level importance sampling is ill‑posed for sequence‑level rewards, leading to high‑variance gradients and collapse.  
2. **Algorithmic redesign**—GSPO—that defines a principled sequence‑level importance ratio, applies clipping at the response level, and uses a group‑based advantage estimator.  
3. **Empirical validation** demonstrating superior stability, efficiency, and performance on challenging benchmarks, and showing that GSPO simplifies MoE RL training and RL infrastructure by removing the need for value models, routing replay, and likelihood recomputation.

The remainder of this textbook will build on these foundations, exploring the formal derivations, implementation considerations, and experimental results that substantiate GSPO as a robust, scalable RL algorithm for the next generation of large language models.

# Foundations: Policies, Rewards, and Importance Sampling  

In this chapter we lay down the mathematical groundwork that underlies modern reinforcement‑learning (RL) methods for large language models.  The notation introduced here will be used throughout the rest of the book, and the concepts of stochastic policies, sequence likelihood, reward design, and importance sampling will be revisited repeatedly when we analyze algorithms such as PPO, GRPO, and the newly proposed Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).  By the end of the chapter you should be comfortable with the probabilistic view of an autoregressive language model, understand how a reward is attached to a full generated response, and see why importance sampling is the natural tool for correcting the distribution mismatch that inevitably appears when we reuse old roll‑out data for policy updates.

---

## Autoregressive language models as stochastic policies  

Large language models are most often trained as **autoregressive** generators: given a prompt (or query) $x$, the model produces a sequence of tokens $y = (y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})$ one at a time.  From the RL perspective we treat the model as a **policy** $\pi_\theta$ parameterized by a set of weights $\theta$.  The policy defines a conditional probability distribution over the next token, conditioned on the prompt and all previously generated tokens:

\[
\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr) = 
\Pr\bigl(\text{emit token } y_t \mid \text{prompt } x, \text{history } y_{<t}; \theta\bigr).
\]

Because the policy is stochastic, each call to the model yields a **sample** $y$ drawn from the distribution

\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) 
\;=\;
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) is the **sequence likelihood**: it is simply the product of the token‑level conditional probabilities.  The factorization in (1) is crucial: it enables us to compute the log‑likelihood of a sequence as a sum over time steps, which in turn makes gradient estimation tractable.  In practice, $|y|$ can vary from a few tokens for short answers to thousands of tokens for long‑form reasoning; the model’s ability to assign probabilities to each token individually is what makes it a flexible policy for RL.

---

## Sequence likelihood and token‑level factorization  

The factorized form (1) has several immediate consequences.  First, the **log‑likelihood** of a sequence decomposes additively:

\[
\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) 
\;=\;
\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_\theta\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

Second, any **importance weight** that compares two policies $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ can be expressed either at the token level or at the sequence level.  The token‑level weight for a particular position $t$ is

\[
w_{t}(\theta) 
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}.
\tag{3}
\]

If we multiply the token‑level ratios across all positions we obtain the **sequence‑level** importance weight:

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} w_{t}(\theta) 
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}.
\tag{4}
\]

Because the product of many ratios can become extremely large or small, algorithms that operate directly on the sequence‑level weight often apply a **length normalization** (see § 4) to keep the quantity numerically stable:

\[
s(\theta) 
\;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y|}
\;=\;
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log w_{t}(\theta)\Bigr).
\tag{5}
\]

The normalized weight $s(\theta)$ lies in a moderate range even for long sequences, which is why GSPO adopts it as the central importance ratio.

---

## Reward functions and normalization  

In RL for language generation the **reward** is a scalar value $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ that evaluates how well response $y$ satisfies the query $x$.  The reward can be derived from a human‑annotated preference model, a verifier that checks code correctness, or any other downstream metric.  Because the raw scores of different queries may have widely varying scales, it is common to **normalize** rewards within a group of responses generated for the same query.  

Suppose we generate a set (or *group*) of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ for a query $x$.  Let $r_i = r(x,y_i)$ be the raw reward for each response.  The **group‑based advantage** used in many policy‑gradient methods is the standardized score

\[
\widehat{A}_{i}
\;=\;
\frac{r_i - \displaystyle\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r_j}
     {\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}\bigl(r_j - \bar r\bigr)^2}},
\qquad 
\bar r = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r_j.
\tag{6}
\]

Equation (6) subtracts the mean reward of the group and divides by the standard deviation, yielding a zero‑mean, unit‑variance advantage.  This normalization stabilizes the learning signal: advantages of different queries become comparable, and the gradient magnitude is less sensitive to the absolute scale of rewards.

When a reward is assigned to an entire sequence, it is natural to propagate the same advantage to every token of that sequence (as done in GRPO) or, alternatively, to keep a single advantage per sequence (as done in GSPO).  Both choices stem from the observation that the reward does not depend on any particular token position.

---

## Expectation under target and behavior distributions  

Policy‑gradient methods aim to **maximize the expected reward** under the *target* policy $\pi_\theta$:

\[
J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[ r(x,y)\bigr].
\tag{7}
\]

Here $\mathcal D$ denotes the dataset of queries (the *environment*).  Directly sampling from $\pi_\theta$ at every optimization step would be prohibitively expensive, especially when $\pi_\theta$ is a huge model.  Instead we reuse samples generated by an older version of the policy, denoted $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  This older policy serves as the **behavior distribution**; the samples it produces are called *off‑policy* because they do not follow the current $\pi_\theta$.

The expectation in (7) can be rewritten by the **change‑of‑measure** identity:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta}[f(y)] 
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\Bigl[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\,f(y)
\Bigr],
\tag{8}
\]

where, for brevity, we omit the conditioning on $x$ (it is implicitly present).  Equation (8) is the classic **importance‑sampling estimator**: we re‑weight each off‑policy sample by the likelihood ratio between target and behavior policies.  The ratio

\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}
\tag{9}
\]

corrects for the fact that $y$ was drawn from the wrong distribution.  In the ideal case, if we had an infinite number of samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the estimator in (8) would be unbiased for the true expectation under $\pi_\theta$.

In practice we have a **finite batch** of $N$ rollouts $\{(x^{(n)}, y^{(n)})\}_{n=1}^{N}$ generated by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  The empirical importance‑sampling estimator of $J(\theta)$ becomes

\[
\hat J(\theta) 
\;=\; 
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\frac{\pi_\theta\bigl(y^{(n)}\mid x^{(n)}\bigr)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y^{(n)}\mid x^{(n)}\bigr)}
\; r\bigl(x^{(n)}, y^{(n)}\bigr).
\tag{10}
\]

Because (10) involves the ratio of two potentially tiny probabilities, its variance can be large, especially when the two policies are far apart.  This motivates the use of **clipping** (as in PPO, GRPO, and GSPO) to bound the influence of out‑of‑distribution samples.

---

## Derivation of the importance‑sampling estimator  

Let us derive (8) from first principles, emphasizing the assumptions that must hold for the estimator to be valid.

1. **Define the target expectation.**  
   We start from the desired objective (7) and insert a dummy integral over the behavior distribution:
   \[
   J(\theta) = \int_{\mathcal X}\!\!\int_{\mathcal Y}
   r(x,y)\, \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\mathcal D(dx).
   \]

2. **Multiply and divide by the behavior density.**  
   Since $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x) > 0$ wherever $\pi_\theta(y\mid x) > 0$ (the *support condition*), we may write
   \[
   J(\theta)=\int_{\mathcal X}\!\!\int_{\mathcal Y}
   r(x,y)\,
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,
   \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)\,\mathcal D(dx).
   \]

3. **Recognize the expectation under the behavior policy.**  
   The inner integral is precisely the expectation over $y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)$, and the outer integral is the expectation over $x\sim\mathcal D$.  Hence
   \[
   J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
   \Bigl[\,
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,
   r(x,y)
   \Bigr],
   \]
   which is exactly (8) with $f(y)=r(x,y)$.

4. **Monte‑Carlo approximation.**  
   In a finite‑sample setting we replace the expectation by an empirical average over the collected rollouts, obtaining (10).  The estimator is unbiased **provided** the support condition holds and the importance weights are computed exactly.

5. **Variance considerations and clipping.**  
   The variance of the estimator is
   \[
   \operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\hat J(\theta)\bigr]
   =\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Var}\!\Bigl[
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,r(x,y)
   \Bigr],
   \]
   which can explode if the weight distribution has heavy tails.  PPO mitigates this by clipping the weight to the interval $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$, yielding the *clipped surrogate* objective.  GRPO clips each token‑level weight $w_{i,t}$, while GSPO clips the sequence‑level normalized weight $s_i$ (see § 4).  The clipping operation replaces the raw weight $a$ by $\operatorname{clip}(a,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$, ensuring that any single sample can at most scale the gradient by a bounded factor.

In summary, importance sampling furnishes a mathematically sound bridge between the **off‑policy data** we can afford to collect (rollouts from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) and the **on‑policy objective** we ultimately wish to optimize (expected reward under $\pi_\theta$).  The remainder of the book builds on this foundation, exploring how different choices of importance weight—token‑wise versus sequence‑wise, with or without length normalization—affect the stability and efficiency of RL for large language models.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Its Limitations  

In modern reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipelines for large language models, **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** has become the de‑facto algorithm for fine‑tuning policies toward higher‑quality responses.  PPO enjoys a simple formulation, a well‑understood clipping mechanism that curbs overly “off‑policy’’ updates, and a theoretical justification rooted in importance sampling.  Nevertheless, when the policy to be optimized is a gigantic autoregressive transformer—often with billions of parameters, mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) sparsity, and long generated sequences—several practical obstacles emerge.  This chapter presents a thorough exposition of the PPO objective as used for language‑model RL, elucidates the role of token‑level importance ratios and advantage estimation, and then dissects the scaling challenges introduced by the auxiliary value model.  Empirical observations from large‑scale experiments further illustrate why PPO’s variance, memory footprint, and stability become severe bottlenecks.

---

## PPO Objective and Clipping Mechanism  

Let the autoregressive language model be parameterized by a vector $\theta$, defining a conditional policy  
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}),
\]  
where $x$ denotes a user query, $y$ a generated response, and $|y|$ its token length.  During RL training we maintain a **behaviour policy** $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$—the policy that generated the current batch of roll‑outs.  PPO seeks to improve $\theta$ while staying close to $\theta_{\text{old}}$ by limiting the magnitude of the **importance ratio** (also called the probability ratio) for each token:
\[
w_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

The **advantage** $\widehat{A}_{t}$ represents how much better (or worse) the observed token is compared with the expected return under the current policy.  In language‑model PPO the advantage is supplied by a separate **value model** $V_{\phi}$ that estimates the expected reward of a partially generated sequence.  The **clipped surrogate objective** for a single token reads
\[
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)=\min\!\Bigl(
w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{t},
\; \text{clip}\bigl(w_{t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_{t}
\Bigr),
\]  
where $\varepsilon\in (0,1)$ is a hyper‑parameter (commonly $0.1\sim0.2$) that determines how far the new policy may deviate from the old one.  The **full PPO loss** for a batch of queries $\{x\}$ and their sampled responses $\{y\}$ is the average over tokens and queries:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\;y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Biggl[
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)
\Biggr].
\]
The clipping operation guarantees that if $w_{t}(\theta)$ strays beyond the interval $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, its contribution to the gradient is capped, thereby limiting policy updates that would otherwise be based on poorly matched off‑policy data.

Intuitively, the PPO objective encourages the policy to **increase** the probability of tokens with *positive* advantage (i.e., those that improve the overall reward) while **decreasing** the probability of tokens with *negative* advantage, but only up to a safe bound.  This balance is crucial when the training data are generated from a stale policy: without the bound, a single high‑advantage token could cause a dramatic policy shift that destabilizes subsequent roll‑outs.

---

## Token‑Level Importance Ratios and Advantage Estimation  

In language modeling, each response is a **sequence** of tokens, and PPO applies the importance ratio **per token**.  The ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ measures the relative change in the conditional probability of token $y_t$ given its prefix.  Because the denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ is a *single* sample from the next‑token distribution, the ratio does **not** average over multiple draws from that distribution.  Consequently, $w_{t}(\theta)$ can be a noisy estimator of how the entire *distribution* of next‑token probabilities has shifted.

The **advantage** $\widehat{A}_{t}$ is typically estimated by subtracting a baseline value from the observed return.  In the language‑model setting the baseline is the output of a **value model** $V_{\phi}$ that predicts the expected scalar reward $r(x,y)$ conditioned on the current partial generation.  Concretely,
\[
\widehat{A}_{t}=R_{t} - V_{\phi}(x, y_{<t}),
\]
where $R_{t}$ is the observed return from token $t$ onward (often the final scalar reward $r(x,y)$ broadcast to each token).  This per‑token advantage is propagated backwards through the sequence, allowing gradients to flow from the final reward all the way to early decisions.

The coupling of token‑level importance ratios with per‑token advantages makes the PPO gradient a sum of many **high‑variance** terms.  Each term is the product $w_{t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{t}$, and because $w_{t}(\theta)$ can be far from $1$—especially when the policy changes noticeably—the variance of the gradient can blow up.  The clipping term mitigates extreme ratios but does not eliminate the underlying noise that stems from estimating a *distributional shift* from a single sample per token.

---

## The Role of the Value Model and Its Scaling Challenges  

PPO’s reliance on a **value model** introduces a set of practical complications that become acute for large language models:

1. **Memory Footprint**  
   The value model must process the same context as the policy model to produce a scalar estimate at each timestep.  In contemporary RLHF pipelines the value model is often **architecturally identical** to the policy (e.g., a 30‑billion‑parameter transformer).  Storing two copies of such a model simultaneously on a single GPU or even a multi‑GPU node can exceed available memory, forcing practitioners to resort to model parallelism, gradient checkpointing, or mixed‑precision tricks that further complicate the training pipeline.

2. **Computational Overhead**  
   For each generated token the forward pass of the value model must be executed, effectively doubling the number of forward passes per rollout compared with a value‑free method.  When responses are long (hundreds of tokens) and batch sizes are large (to keep hardware saturated), the extra cost becomes a dominant proportion of wall‑clock time.

3. **Stability of Value Estimates**  
   The value model must learn to predict the *expected* reward of a partial sequence under a moving policy.  As the policy evolves, the distribution of roll‑outs changes, requiring the value model to continuously adapt.  If the value predictions are biased or overly noisy, the advantages $\widehat{A}_{t}$ become unreliable, which directly corrupts the PPO update because the surrogate loss multiplies the importance ratio by these advantages.

4. **Scaling to Longer Horizons**  
   In tasks that demand deep reasoning—e.g., multi‑step mathematical proofs or code synthesis—the rollout length can be several hundred tokens.  The variance of Monte‑Carlo returns grows with horizon length, making accurate value estimation harder.  Moreover, the value model must maintain a stable gradient signal across many timesteps, a requirement that is difficult to satisfy when the model size is already at the limits of current hardware.

Together, these factors mean that **the value model becomes a scalability bottleneck**.  Although PPO’s original formulation assumes a reasonably accurate advantage estimator, in the regime of billion‑parameter language models the value model often fails to deliver the needed fidelity, leading to unstable training dynamics.

---

## Empirical Issues: Variance, Memory, and Stability  

When PPO is applied to large‑scale language models, researchers have documented a constellation of symptoms that trace back to the theoretical concerns outlined above:

- **High Gradient Variance**  
  Empirical measurements of the gradient norm during PPO updates show oscillations three to five times larger than those observed with value‑free alternatives (e.g., Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO).  The variance is particularly pronounced for later tokens in long sequences, confirming that token‑level importance ratios amplify noise as the sequence grows.

- **Out‑of‑Memory (OOM) Failures**  
  Training runs that attempt to keep both policy and value networks in full precision on a single node frequently abort with OOM errors once the batch size exceeds a modest threshold (e.g., 8‑16 queries per GPU).  Even with tensor‑parallelism, the communication bandwidth required to synchronize gradients of two massive models becomes a limiting factor, reducing overall throughput.

- **Catastrophic Collapse**  
  In several large‑scale experiments, after a few hundred PPO updates the policy’s likelihoods on the training data collapse to near‑uniform distributions, and the reward curve sharply drops to the baseline.  Diagnostic runs reveal that this collapse coincides with periods where the value model’s predictions deviate dramatically from the observed returns, causing the surrogate loss to push the policy toward a degenerate regime.

- **Sensitivity to Hyper‑Parameters**  
  PPO’s performance becomes extremely fragile with respect to the clipping parameter $\varepsilon$ and the learning rate.  Small changes (e.g., from $0.1$ to $0.12$) can swing training from stable improvement to immediate divergence.  This sensitivity is largely a symptom of the noisy, high‑variance gradient signal amplified by the token‑level importance ratios.

- **Inefficient Sample Utilization**  
  Because PPO partitions large rollout batches into multiple mini‑batches, each mini‑batch contains off‑policy data relative to the current $\theta$.  The token‑level clipping tries to protect against this off‑policy mismatch, but the per‑token importance ratios are insufficient to fully correct the mismatch, leading to a substantial fraction of tokens being **clipped away** (i.e., receiving zero gradient).  Empirically, experiments report that upwards of 30‑40 % of tokens are discarded by the clipping operation, reducing sample efficiency.

These empirical findings collectively highlight the **practical limits** of straightforward PPO when the policy is a huge transformer.  The algorithm’s reliance on token‑level importance weighting, a separate value estimator, and per‑token clipping introduces variance and resource constraints that impede scaling to the next generation of language models.  Motivated by these observations, subsequent work proposes sequence‑level importance weighting (e.g., Group Sequence Policy Optimization, GSPO) that directly aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, thereby alleviating many of PPO’s shortcomings for massive language‑model RL.

# Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

In the landscape of reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models, **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** has long served as the de‑facto algorithmic backbone. PPO relies heavily on a separately trained *value model* to estimate the advantage of each token, an approach that quickly becomes untenable when the policy itself is a massive, often sparsely activated, transformer. **Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** was introduced precisely to avoid the cost of maintaining a value network, replacing it with a *group‑wise* estimate of advantage derived directly from the rewards of multiple responses to the same query. While this idea is elegant and offers a substantial reduction in memory and computational overhead, GRPO inherits a subtle yet severe instability when the generated sequences become long or when the policy is a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model. The following sections unpack the formulation of GRPO, explain how it sidesteps the value model, and then examine the sources of its high variance and eventual gradient explosion.

---

## Group‑wise generation and relative advantage computation

Consider a dataset of prompts (or *queries*) denoted by \(\mathcal{D}\). For each prompt \(x \in \mathcal{D}\) the policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) (the *old* policy before the current update) is asked to generate a **group** of responses
\[
\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G},\qquad y_i = (y_{i,1},y_{i,2},\dots ,y_{i,|y_i|}),
\]
where \(G\) is a hyper‑parameter that determines how many candidate continuations are sampled per prompt.  

Each response \(y_i\) is scored by an external *verifier* \(r\) that returns a scalar reward in the unit interval,
\[
r(x,y_i) \in [0,1].
\]
Because the reward is defined on the *whole* sequence, it is natural to compare each response against the others in its group. GRPO does precisely this by computing a *relative advantage* that reflects how much a particular response deviates from the mean performance of its peers:
\[
\widehat{A}_i \;=\; 
\frac{\,r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)\,}
     {\,\operatorname{std}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)}.
\]
The denominator, the standard deviation across the group, normalizes the scale of the advantages so that they are comparable across different prompts and training steps. Importantly, **all tokens belonging to the same response share the identical advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\)**; there is no per‑token advantage estimation as in PPO.

---

## GRPO objective and token‑level importance weights

GRPO mirrors the clipped surrogate objective of PPO, but it operates on groups of full sequences rather than on a single trajectory. The loss for a minibatch drawn from the rollout data is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},
\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\underbrace{
\min\!\Big(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\big(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i
\Big)}_{\text{clipped surrogate}}
\Bigg],
\tag{1}
\]
where the *token‑level importance ratio* is defined as

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\big)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\big)}.
\tag{2}
\]

The expression \(\operatorname{clip}(w,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\) caps the ratio within a small interval around unity; this is the same *proximal* safeguard used in PPO. The intuition is that if the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) deviates too far from the behavior policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) on a particular token, the update for that token should be ignored to avoid high variance off‑policy gradients.

At first glance, substituting the per‑token advantage with a group‑wise, reward‑derived quantity seems to eliminate the need for a value network completely. The algorithm remains *on‑policy* in the sense that the sampled responses are generated by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), and the clipping mechanism strives to keep the updates within a locally trusted region.

---

## Why GRPO removes the value model but introduces new variance

The removal of the value model yields undeniable practical benefits:

* **Memory savings.** The value network often mirrors the size of the policy; for a 30‑billion‑parameter transformer, a separate value head would roughly double the memory footprint.
* **Computational simplicity.** No need to forward‑propagate a second network on each rollout, which speeds up data collection.
* **Reduced engineering complexity.** No requirement to keep the value model synchronized with the policy, avoiding potential divergence problems.

However, the substitution is not variance‑free. The *source* of variance in PPO originates from two places:

1. **Stochasticity of the sampled trajectories** (the rollout distribution).  
2. **Noise in the advantage estimator** (the value model).  

GRPO eliminates the second source entirely, but it inadvertently **magnifies the first** because the importance weight (2) is now computed *per token* using a *single* sample from the next‑token distribution. To see why this is problematic, recall the formal principle of importance sampling:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
\Bigg[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\Bigg].
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) presupposes that the **importance weight** \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) is estimated by averaging over many *independent* samples \(z\) drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). In the context of language generation, a *single token* \(y_{i,t}\) does **not** constitute an independent draw from a stationary distribution; it is conditioned on the prefix \(y_{i,<t}\), which itself is a random variable generated by the behavior policy. Consequently, the ratio (2) is a **noisy, single‑sample estimator** of the true correction factor that would be required to align the distributions of \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) over whole sequences.

The high variance manifests in two concrete ways:

* **Accumulation over length.** When a response contains many tokens, the product of many noisy ratios—or, equivalently, the average of their logarithms—can drift dramatically away from one, even if each individual ratio is close to one. This drift is amplified by the clipping operation because the clipped surrogate in (1) will toggle between the raw and clipped terms depending on the sign of \(\widehat{A}_i\).  
* **Amplification by expert volatility in MoE models.** In a Mixture‑of‑Experts architecture, the set of activated experts for a token may change from one policy update to the next. Since the probability assigned to a token is computed by a *different* sub‑network after the policy shifts, the numerator and denominator in (2) can be wildly inconsistent, inflating the variance further.

In short, GRPO replaces a *systematic* source of noise (an imperfect value model) with a *stochastic* one (single‑sample importance ratios) that lacks the averaging effect necessary for reliable importance sampling.

---

## Illustrative example of gradient explosion in long responses

To make the foregoing arguments concrete, imagine a response \(y\) of length \(L=200\) tokens. Suppose that for each token the importance ratio is modestly perturbed around one:
\[
w_t(\theta) = 1 + \delta_t,\qquad \delta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\]
with \(\sigma = 0.05\). Although each \(\delta_t\) is small, the *log‑importance* summed across the sequence becomes

\[
\log s(\theta) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L} \log w_t(\theta)
\approx \frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L} \delta_t,
\]
since \(\log(1+\delta) \approx \delta\) for tiny \(\delta\). By the central limit theorem, the sum is distributed as
\[
\log s(\theta) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\; \sigma^2/L\big).
\]
Thus the *standard deviation* of \(\log s(\theta)\) shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{L}\), suggesting that the sequence‑level ratio would be relatively stable. However, **GRPO does not use the averaged ratio**; it multiplies each token’s gradient by its own raw weight \(w_t(\theta)\). The gradient of the surrogate objective (ignoring clipping) for token \(t\) is proportional to
\[
w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
If \(\widehat{A}>0\), any token for which \(w_t(\theta)>1+\varepsilon\) will be *clipped* to \(1+\varepsilon\); conversely, tokens with \(w_t(\theta)<1-\varepsilon\) will be clipped to the lower bound. Because \(\varepsilon\) is typically on the order of \(0.2\) for GRPO, a sizable fraction of tokens—especially those whose ratios deviate more than 20 % from unity—will be forced to the clipping boundary. The gradient contribution of those tokens becomes a **constant magnitude** (the clipping value) multiplied by the advantage. When the response is long, the number of clipped tokens grows roughly proportionally to \(L\), and the overall gradient magnitude scales as
\[
\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}\| 
\;\approx\;
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L} \bigl| \operatorname{clip}(w_t(\theta))\bigr|
\;\approx\;
c\,\varepsilon,
\]
where \(c\) is a factor representing the proportion of clipped tokens.  

If the proportion of clipped tokens nears unity—an empirical observation for GRPO on long, open‑ended generations—the *effective* gradient can become **orders of magnitude larger** than intended, because each clipped token contributes a non‑vanishing term regardless of its true likelihood under the new policy. This uncontrolled amplification often leads to **gradient explosion**, whereby the optimizer takes steps that push the policy far outside the trusted region, causing a rapid deterioration of the language model’s generative ability. In practice, such a collapse is observed as a sudden drop in reward and a drastic increase in perplexity, after which the model rarely recovers even when hyper‑parameters are retuned.

The situation worsens for MoE models. The routing decisions that determine which experts are active for a token can change abruptly between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\). Consequently, the numerator and denominator of (2) are computed by *different* expert sets, leading to ratios that can be arbitrarily large or small, dramatically increasing the clipped‑token fraction and aggravating the gradient blow‑up.

---

## Summary

Group‑Relative Policy Optimization offered a compelling solution to the practical bottleneck of maintaining a heavyweight value network in PPO‑style RL for language models. By forming groups of responses and computing a *relative* advantage, GRPO eliminates the need for a separate value head and reduces memory consumption. Nevertheless, the algorithm’s reliance on **token‑level importance weights derived from a single sample** violates the statistical foundations of importance sampling. The resulting high‑variance gradients accumulate across long sequences and are especially volatile for Mixture‑of‑Experts models where routing changes between policy updates. Empirical evidence shows that this variance can cause a catastrophic gradient explosion, leading to irreversible model collapse. Understanding these failure modes motivates the development of **sequence‑level** importance weighting schemes—such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—which align the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, dramatically improving stability and training efficiency for the next generation of large language models.

# Motivation for Sequence‑Level Optimization  

In reinforcement learning for large language models, the design of the learning objective must faithfully reflect how the model is evaluated.  In the setting of language generation the only feedback that we receive from a verifier is a **reward** attached to an entire response sequence, not to each individual token.  Consequently, the **unit of optimization**—the quantity that we weight when we compute gradients—should be the same as the **unit of reward**.  This chapter examines the practical consequences of violating this principle in the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, explains why token‑level importance sampling is ill‑posed, and develops the intuition that drives the shift to sequence‑level importance ratios in Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

---

## Off‑policy Learning and Minibatch Partitioning  

Training a language model with reinforcement learning proceeds by generating a large batch of roll‑out responses from the current **behavior policy** (the “old” policy) $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  For computational efficiency the batch is split into several **mini‑batches**, each of which is used to perform a gradient update.  Because each mini‑batch contains samples that were generated by a policy that is no longer being updated, the learning problem becomes **off‑policy**: the data distribution under which we compute the expectation in the objective differs from the distribution of the **target policy** $\pi_{\theta}$ that we are trying to improve.

Off‑policy learning is inherently fragile.  If the data are too far from the target distribution, the gradient estimate can become biased and the optimizer may take steps that degrade the policy rather than improve it.  To mitigate this, modern policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) introduce a **clipping mechanism** that limits how much the importance weight— the ratio between target and behavior probabilities—may deviate from 1.  GRPO inherits this clipping idea but applies it at the granularity of individual token probabilities.

Formally, for a query $x$ and a generated response $y$, the **token‑level importance ratio** used in GRPO is  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}.
\]

The objective (Equation \eqref{equ:grpo}) averages a clipped version of $w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i}$ over all tokens in all responses of the group.  The clipping range $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ is intended to keep updates within a “proximal” region, thereby controlling the off‑policy bias.

While this construction mirrors the token‑wise formulation of PPO, it fails to respect a crucial difference between language modeling and standard control problems: the **reward is defined only for the entire sequence**, not for each step.

---

## Ill‑posedness of Token‑Level Importance Sampling  

Importance sampling is a statistical technique that re‑weights samples drawn from a **behavior distribution** $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ to estimate expectations under a **target distribution** $\pi_{\text{tar}}$:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr] 
= \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ 
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z) 
\right].
\tag{1}
\]

Crucially, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is **averaged over many independent samples** $z\sim\pi_{\text{beh}}$.  The law of large numbers guarantees that, as the number of samples $N\to\infty$, the empirical average converges to the true expectation.

GRPO’s token‑level weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is derived from **a single next‑token sample** $y_{i,t}$ drawn from the conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x, y_{i,<t})$.  The importance weight therefore does **not** average over multiple draws from the same conditional distribution; it treats a solitary outcome as if it were a Monte‑Carlo estimate of the full ratio.  This misuse of importance sampling breaks the theoretical foundation of Equation (1) and yields an **ill‑posed objective**.

From a probabilistic standpoint, the correct importance weight for a whole response $y_i$ is  

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
= \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}.
\]

GRPO replaces this product with a **per‑token factor** that is used independently for each token’s contribution to the gradient.  Because the product of many noisy per‑token ratios can differ dramatically from a properly normalized sequence‑level ratio, the expectation defined by the GRPO objective no longer corresponds to any well‑defined off‑policy correction.

---

## Variance Accumulation over Token Steps  

Even if we ignore the theoretical flaw, the practical impact of using token‑level importance ratios is severe.  Each ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can deviate from 1 due to random fluctuations in the policy’s token probabilities.  When a response contains many tokens, the **gradient contribution** for that response involves a sum over these noisy ratios:

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i .
\]

Assuming the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is fixed for the whole sequence (as in GRPO), the variance of the gradient term grows with the number of tokens because each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is an independent source of noise.  This phenomenon is especially problematic for long responses, which are common when training models that must perform multi‑step reasoning or generate code.

Moreover, the clipping operation, intended to bound the influence of out‑of‑distribution samples, can **amplify** this variance.  When a token’s weight exceeds the clipping bound, the gradient contribution is replaced by the clipped value, introducing a discontinuity in the loss surface.  For long sequences many tokens may be clipped, causing the effective gradient to be dominated by a handful of heavily clipped terms, further destabilizing training.

Empirically, the paper reports that these high‑variance updates accumulate across training steps, eventually leading to **catastrophic model collapse**: the policy degrades irreversibly, performance plunges, and even careful hyper‑parameter tuning cannot recover the model.

---

## The Principle: Reward Unit ↔ Optimization Unit  

The root cause of the instability is a **misalignment between the granularity of the reward signal and the granularity of the optimization objective**.  In standard reinforcement learning, an immediate reward is often provided at each time step, so it makes sense to weight each step’s log‑probability by an importance ratio that is also defined per step.  In language‑model RL, the verifier delivers a scalar $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ **after the full sequence** has been generated.  This reward does not tell us how to evaluate the quality of any individual token in isolation; it merely assesses the *overall* behavior.

Therefore, the importance weight used to correct for off‑policy sampling should also be defined **at the sequence level**:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}  
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) is precisely the **sequence‑level importance ratio** introduced in Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).  Because it aggregates the token‑wise log‑ratios *before* exponentiation, the variance contributed by individual tokens is averaged out, and the resulting weight is a **stable estimator** of how the entire response distribution has shifted.

Clipping $s_i(\theta)$ rather than $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ yields a **single binary decision per response**: either the whole response is deemed sufficiently on‑policy and its gradient is used, or it is discarded (or replaced by its clipped value).  This matches the binary nature of the reward – the model either receives a high advantage for the whole response or a low one – and prevents the noisy, token‑by‑token fluctuations that plague GRPO.

In summary, the **principle** guiding the redesign from GRPO to GSPO is:

> *When the reward is attached to an entire sequence, the importance sampling correction and the clipping mechanism must also be defined at the sequence level; otherwise the learning signal becomes ill‑posed and high‑variance, leading to instability.*

By adhering to this principle, GSPO eliminates the catastrophic variance buildup, stabilizes training even for very long responses, and removes the need for auxiliary tricks such as the Routing Replay strategy required by GRPO for Mixture‑of‑Experts models.  

The remainder of the paper builds on this insight, showing how the sequence‑level formulation yields a clean gradient expression (Section \ref{subsec:gradient}), how a token‑level variant (GSPO‑token) can be derived without re‑introducing instability, and how extensive experiments confirm the superior stability and efficiency of the sequence‑level approach.

# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) entails optimizing a policy—typically an autoregressive transformer—so that generated responses receive high scores from a learned or heuristic verifier.  Classical RL‑from‑human‑feedback pipelines have relied on policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).  More recent work introduced **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, which replaces a heavyweight value model with a simple relative‑advantage estimator computed across a small group of sampled responses.  While GRPO alleviates the need for a separate value network, empirical investigations revealed severe instability when training very large models, especially on long‑form generation tasks or when the model architecture includes sparsely activated experts (Mixture‑of‑Experts, MoE).  

The root cause of this instability is a **mis‑application of importance‑sampling weights** at the token level.  Tokens are sampled from a next‑token distribution, yet the importance weight for each token is constructed from a single sample, thereby failing to correct the off‑policy bias and injecting high‑variance noise that compounds across the sequence.  Moreover, because reward signals are assigned to entire sequences, token‑wise correction is conceptually mismatched.  

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** resolves these issues by redefining the importance ratio at the **sequence level**, aligning the off‑policy correction with the granularity of the reward.  This chapter presents GSPO in depth: we derive the sequence‑level importance ratio, explain the necessity of length‑normalization, formulate the group‑wise advantage estimator, contrast response‑level clipping with token‑level clipping, and finally provide a concrete pseudo‑code implementation that can be inserted into existing RL‑from‑human‑feedback pipelines.

---

## 1. Sequence‑level importance ratio definition

In the RL setting used throughout the paper, a language model with parameters $\theta$ defines a conditional distribution over token sequences,
\[
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr),
\]
where $x$ is a user query (or prompt), $y=\{y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|}\}$ is a generated response, and $|y|$ denotes its length.  When performing off‑policy updates, we have a *behavior* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ that generated the rollout data, and we wish to estimate expectations under the *target* policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  The classic importance‑sampling identity states that for any function $f$,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta}}[f(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,f(y)\right].
\]
Thus the **sequence‑level importance ratio** is naturally defined as
\[
\rho(y;x;\theta,\theta_{\text{old}})\;:=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
\tag{1}
\]
This ratio measures how much more (or less) likely the new policy would have generated the exact same response $y$ compared with the old policy.  Because the reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned after the whole response is produced, weighting the gradient by this ratio exactly mirrors the theoretical importance‑sampling correction.

In practice, directly using $\rho$ in the objective would lead to instability for long sequences: the product of many probability ratios can become extremely large or tiny, causing numerical overflow or underflow and drastically inflating variance.  The GSPO algorithm therefore incorporates a **length‑normalization** step, as described next.

---

## 2. Length‑normalization and its effect on variance

A naïve implementation of (1) would involve the raw product of per‑token likelihood ratios:
\[
\rho(y;x;\theta,\theta_{\text{old}})
=
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}.
\]
Because probabilities lie in $(0,1]$, the product can shrink exponentially with sequence length, while occasional spikes in token probabilities may cause the product to explode.  To obtain a **scale‑invariant** importance weight that can be compared across responses of different lengths, GSPO defines the **length‑normalized importance ratio**,
\[
s_i(\theta) \;:=\;
\bigl\rho(y_i;x;\theta,\theta_{\text{old}})\bigr)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\Biggl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
\Biggr).
\tag{2}
\]
Equation (2) can be interpreted as the **geometric mean** of the token‑level likelihood ratios.  By taking the $|y_i|$‑th root, the magnitude of $s_i$ no longer scales with length; responses of 10 tokens and 100 tokens are placed on a comparable numeric scale.  Consequently, the variance of the importance weight across minibatches is markedly reduced, allowing a much tighter clipping range (on the order of $10^{-4}$) than is feasible with token‑level ratios (which typically require clipping ranges around $0.2$).

Length‑normalization therefore serves two essential purposes:

1. **Numerical stability** – preventing underflow/overflow in the product of many ratios.
2. **Variance reduction** – ensuring that the distribution of importance weights remains concentrated, which in turn yields more reliable gradient estimates and smoother training dynamics.

---

## 3. GSPO objective with group‑wise advantage

RL from human feedback often employs *relative* advantages computed within a small batch (or “group”) of responses sampled for the same query $x$.  Let $G$ be the group size and $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ the responses.  The **group‑wise advantage** for response $i$ is defined analogously to GRPO:
\[
\widehat{A}_i \;:=\;
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu_G}{\sigma_G},
\qquad
\mu_G = \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{G},
\quad
\sigma_G = \operatorname{std}\!\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{G}.
\tag{3}
\]
This Z‑score‑like normalization removes any baseline offset and rescales rewards so that positive and negative advantages are balanced across groups, a technique that has proven empirically effective for stabilizing policy updates.

Combining the length‑normalized importance ratio (2) with the group‑wise advantage (3), GSPO adopts the following **surrogate objective** (the clipping term is explained in the next section):
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,
\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\!\Biggl[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Bigl(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
\Bigr)
\Biggr].
\tag{4}
\]
The expectation is taken over queries drawn from the dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and over the *behavior* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ that generated the group of responses.  The inner minimum implements the familiar PPO‑style *clipping*: for each response we either use the raw importance‑weighted advantage or a clipped version that limits how far the ratio can deviate from 1.  This prevents a single highly off‑policy response from dominating the gradient.

Equation (4) is the core of GSPO.  It differs from GRPO (Equation (1) in the original paper) in two ways:

* The importance weight is **sequence‑level** and **length‑normalized** ($s_i$ instead of $w_{i,t}$).
* The clipping operates on the **entire response** rather than on every token.

Both changes align the mathematical correction with the granularity of the reward signal and dramatically reduce gradient noise.

---

## 4. Clipping at the response level vs. token level

### 4.1 Token‑level clipping (GRPO / PPO)

In token‑wise formulations, each token $y_{i,t}$ receives its own importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ and is subjected to clipping independently:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\]
The surrogate term for a single token becomes
\[
\min\!\bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i
\bigr).
\]
Because each token is clipped separately, a long response may contain many tokens whose ratios lie far outside the trusted region.  Even though many tokens may be clipped, the *effective* number of gradient contributions can be very large, leading to a noisy estimate.  Moreover, the clipping range $\varepsilon$ must be relatively wide (e.g., $0.2$–$0.27$) to avoid discarding almost all tokens, which in turn weakens the safeguard against severe off‑policy samples.

### 4.2 Response‑level clipping (GSPO)

GSPO replaces the per‑token weight by the geometric‑mean weight $s_i(\theta)$.  The clipping operator now acts on this single scalar per response:
\[
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
\]
Empirically, the fraction of *clipped responses* is far smaller than the fraction of clipped tokens in GRPO, even though the numerical clipping range $\varepsilon$ is an order of magnitude tighter (e.g., $3\times10^{-4}$ to $4\times10^{-4}$).  Counter‑intuitively, despite discarding more *tokens*—since a clipped response excludes all its constituent tokens from gradient computation—GSPO achieves higher **training efficiency**.  The intuition is that response‑level clipping eliminates entire high‑variance samples, yielding a cleaner learning signal, whereas token‑level clipping retains many partially corrupted contributions, inflating variance.

Figure 1 in the original paper (reproduced conceptually here) shows that the fraction of clipped tokens in GRPO can be two orders of magnitude larger than the fraction of clipped responses in GSPO, yet GSPO’s learning curve rises faster.  This observation strongly supports the claim that the *unit of optimization* should match the *unit of reward*.

---

## 5. Pseudo‑code and integration into existing RL pipelines

Below is a high‑level pseudo‑code that captures the GSPO update loop.  The code assumes an existing RL‑from‑human‑feedback framework that already provides:

* A dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of prompts/queries.
* A function `sample_responses(policy, x, G)` that draws $G$ responses from a given policy.
* A verifier `reward(x, y)` that returns a scalar in $[0,1]$.
* Optimizer infrastructure (e.g., Adam) for updating $\theta$.

```python
# --------------------------------------------------------------
# GSPO training loop
# --------------------------------------------------------------
for step in range(num_training_steps):
    # 1. Sample a minibatch of queries
    batch_x = sample_batch(D, batch_size)   # shape: (B,)

    # 2. For each query, generate a group of responses using the old policy
    #    Note: we keep a copy of the old parameters for importance calculation.
    theta_old = copy_parameters(theta)      # frozen snapshot
    groups = []   # list of length B, each entry is list of G responses
    for x in batch_x:
        responses = sample_responses(policy=theta_old, x=x, G=G)  # list of strings
        groups.append(responses)

    # 3. Compute rewards for every response
    rewards = []  # shape: (B, G)
    for i, x in enumerate(batch_x):
        rewards_i = [reward(x, y) for y in groups[i]]
        rewards.append(rewards_i)

    # 4. Compute group‑wise advantage (Z‑score)
    advantages = []
    for rewards_i in rewards:
        mu = mean(rewards_i)
        sigma = std(rewards_i) + 1e-8   # avoid division by zero
        adv_i = [(r - mu) / sigma for r in rewards_i]
        advantages.append(adv_i)        # shape (G,)

    # 5. Compute length‑normalized sequence importance ratios s_i(θ)
    #    We need log‑probabilities under both old and new policies.
    #    For efficiency, we reuse the token log‑probs from the forward pass.
    logp_old = []   # (B, G, max_len)  - log probs under old policy
    logp_new = []   # (B, G, max_len)  - log probs under current policy
    lengths = []    # (B, G)   number of tokens in each response

    for i, x in enumerate(batch_x):
        # forward pass under old policy (already computed in step 2)
        old_logits = forward(theta_old, x, groups[i])   # returns token logits
        logp_old_i = compute_log_probs(old_logits, groups[i])  # shape (G, len_i)
        # forward pass under current policy
        new_logits = forward(theta, x, groups[i])
        logp_new_i = compute_log_probs(new_logits, groups[i])
        logp_old.append(logp_old_i)
        logp_new.append(logp_new_i)
        lengths.append([len(y) for y in groups[i]])

    # 6. Assemble the surrogate loss
    loss = 0.0
    for i in range(batch_size):
        for j in range(G):
            # geometric‑mean importance ratio (length‑normalized)
            log_ratio = (logp_new[i][j] - logp_old[i][j]).sum() / lengths[i][j]
            s_ij = torch.exp(log_ratio)   # scalar

            # clipping
            s_clip = torch.clamp(s_ij, 1 - eps_left, 1 + eps_right)

            # choose the minimum (PPO‑style)
            weighted_adv = torch.min(s_ij * advantages[i][j],
                                     s_clip * advantages[i][j])

            loss = loss - weighted_adv   # negative because we maximize J

    loss = loss / (batch_size * G)

    # 7. Back‑propagation and optimization step
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

### Integration notes

* **Policy snapshot** – GSPO requires a *fixed* old policy for the entire mini‑batch to compute the importance ratios.  It is common practice to clone the current parameters at the beginning of each outer RL step (as shown with `theta_old`).
* **Log‑probability reuse** – The forward pass under the old policy is already needed to generate responses.  The token‑level log probabilities can be cached, avoiding a second pass.  The new policy log probabilities are obtained by a second forward pass; for MoE models this is cheap because the routing decisions are deterministic given the input tokens.
* **Length handling** – The denominator in (2) is the actual length of each response, which is readily known after generation.  Padding tokens must be excluded from the sum when computing `log_ratio`.
* **Clipping hyper‑parameters** – Empirically the authors use asymmetric clipping ranges $(\varepsilon_{\text{left}},\varepsilon_{\text{right}}) = (3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4})$ for GSPO.  These ranges are substantially tighter than those used for token‑level GRPO because the normalized ratios $s_i$ vary in a far narrower band.
* **Mini‑batch partitioning** – To maximize hardware utilization, a large rollout batch can be split into several mini‑batches (e.g., four) before applying the update loop above.  This introduces off‑policy data, but the clipping mechanism safeguards against excessive drift.

The pseudo‑code above can be dropped into any RL‑from‑human‑feedback codebase that currently implements PPO or GRPO: the only structural changes are the computation of the geometric‑mean importance ratio and the shift from token‑wise to response‑wise clipping.  All other components—sampling, reward computation, optimization—remain untouched.

---

## 6. Summary

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses the fundamental mismatch between token‑level importance correction and sequence‑level rewards that plagued earlier RL algorithms for LLMs.  By redefining the importance ratio as a **length‑normalized sequence likelihood**, GSPO aligns the off‑policy correction with the granularity of the reward, dramatically reduces variance, and permits tight response‑level clipping.  The resulting surrogate objective (Equation (4)) yields stable gradients, eliminates the need for auxiliary value networks, and—crucially—removes the sensitivity of token‑wise importance weights to expert‑routing volatility in Mixture‑of‑Experts models.  Empirical results show that GSPO attains higher training efficiency, avoids catastrophic collapse, and simplifies both the algorithmic design and the engineering infrastructure required for large‑scale RL training of language models.  The pseudo‑code provided demonstrates that GSPO can be integrated with minimal modifications into existing pipelines, making it a practical and theoretically sound foundation for the next generation of RL‑enhanced LLMs.

# Gradient Derivation and Comparison: GSPO vs. GRPO  

In this chapter we present a detailed, step‑by‑step derivation of the gradients that underlie the two reinforcement‑learning‑for‑language‑models (RL‑LM) algorithms introduced in the paper: **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** and its predecessor **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  By exposing the mathematics that drives each update rule we can see precisely how GSPO avoids the per‑token weight disparity that plagues GRPO, why this leads to a dramatic reduction in gradient variance, and how the resulting learning signal promotes stable convergence even for very long sequences and for sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models.

We first recall the objectives of the two methods, then derive their gradients both in the unclipped (theoretical) form and with the clipping operator that actually appears in practice.  Throughout the derivations we keep the notation exactly as defined in the paper so that readers can directly map each symbol to the algorithmic implementation.  After the derivations we provide an intuitive interpretation of the resulting expressions, emphasizing the equal weighting of tokens within a sequence that GSPO achieves, and finally discuss the practical consequences for stability and convergence.

---

## 1. Derivation of the GSPO Gradient  

### 1.1. The GSPO Objective  

GSPO optimizes a **sequence‑level** surrogate objective that mirrors the classic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) clipping scheme but replaces token‑level importance ratios with a single ratio per generated response.  For a query \(x\) drawn from the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) and a group of \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) sampled from the **old** policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)\), the objective reads

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Big(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\big(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i
\Big)
\Bigg],
\tag{1}
\]

where  

* \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the **group‑based advantage** of response \(y_i\):
  \[
  \widehat{A}_i=
  \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}}
       {\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}},
  \tag{2}
  \]
  i.e. the normalized reward relative to its peers.  

* The **sequence‑level importance ratio** \(s_i(\theta)\) is defined as the geometric mean of token‑level likelihood ratios, which can be written compactly as a length‑normalized likelihood ratio:
  \[
  s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
  =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigr).
  \tag{3}
  \]

The clipping operator
\(\operatorname{clip}(u,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon) = 
\max(1-\varepsilon,\min(u,1+\varepsilon))\)
ensures that the importance ratio does not stray too far from unity, preventing the gradient from being dominated by severely off‑policy samples.

### 1.2. Gradient Without the Clipping Operator  

To expose the core structure of the gradient we first ignore the clipping term (i.e. we assume the ratio lies inside the interval \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\)).  The objective then simplifies to

\[
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\Bigg].
\tag{4}
\]

Since the expectation is taken with respect to a **fixed** distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the only \(\theta\)-dependence lives in the factor \(s_i(\theta)\).  Differentiating under the expectation yields

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\widehat{A}_i\,
\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)
\Bigg].
\tag{5}
\]

Applying the chain rule to the definition (3) of \(s_i(\theta)\),

\[
\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)=
s_i(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta),
\tag{6}
\]

and noting that

\[
\log s_i(\theta)=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\tag{7}
\]

the derivative of the log‑ratio collapses to

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}),
\tag{8}
\]

because \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) is treated as a constant when differentiating with respect to \(\theta\).

Plugging (6)–(8) back into (5) gives the **unclipped GSPO gradient**:

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
}
\tag{9}
\]

Equation (9) is precisely the form presented in the paper (Equation (10)).  The important structural point is that **all tokens of a given response share the same scalar weight** \(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\); the token‑wise gradients are simply averaged (or summed) and then multiplied by that common factor.

### 1.3. Gradient With Clipping  

In practice the clipping operator is retained.  The surrogate objective (1) can be expressed as

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\phi\bigl(s_i(\theta),\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\bigg],
\quad
\phi(u,a)=\min\bigl(u a, \operatorname{clip}(u,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,a\bigr).
\tag{10}
\]

Because \(\phi\) is a piecewise‑smooth function of \(u\), the gradient of the objective is

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\mathbf{1}_{\{u\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\}}\,
\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)
\bigg],
\tag{11}
\]

where \(\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}\) is an indicator that disables the gradient when the ratio is clipped.  Substituting \(\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)\) and (8) yields exactly the same weighted sum of token gradients as in (9), except that the contribution for a given response is **zeroed out** whenever its importance ratio falls outside the allowed interval.  Hence the final clipped gradient retains the clean equal‑token‑weighting property while discarding outlier responses that would otherwise inject high variance.

---

## 2. Comparison to the GRPO Gradient  

### 2.1. The GRPO Objective Recap  

GRPO adopts a **token‑level** importance ratio for each token \(y_{i,t}\) in a response, but it still uses the same group‑based advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) for all tokens of that response:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Big(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\big(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\widehat{A}_i
\Big)
\Bigg],
\tag{12}
\]

with the token importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\tag{13}
\]

The per‑token clipping mechanism mirrors that of PPO, but now each token may be clipped **independently**.

### 2.2. Unclipped GRPO Gradient  

Discarding clipping for clarity, the surrogate reduces to

\[
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\Bigg].
\tag{14}
\]

Differentiating with respect to \(\theta\) (again, treating the old policy as constant) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
&=
\mathbb{E}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_i\,
\nabla_{\theta}w_{i,t}(\theta)
\Bigg] \\[4pt]
&=
\mathbb{E}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_i\,
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\]

Thus the **GRPO gradient** is

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
}
\tag{16}
\]

Equation (16) matches the expression in the paper (the paragraph after Equation (12)).

### 2.3. Structural Differences  

Both gradients are expectations over the same rollout distribution and share the term \(\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\).  The crucial distinction lies in **the multiplicative coefficient** of each token gradient:

| Algorithm | Coefficient multiplying \(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **GSPO**  | \(s_i(\theta) = \bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}\) — identical for every token \(t\) of response \(i\). |
| **GRPO**  | \(w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\) — potentially different for each token. |

Consequently, **GSPO enforces equal weighting of all tokens belonging to the same sampled sequence**, while GRPO applies a *heterogeneous* weighting that reflects the per‑token likelihood shift between the old and new policies.

---

## 3. Interpretation of Equal Token Weighting Within a Sequence  

### 3.1. Importance Sampling Perspective  

Importance sampling seeks to re‑weight samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) so that expectations under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) can be estimated unbiasedly.  The correct weight is the **ratio of full densities** \(\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)/\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)\).  In the language‑generation setting a *sample* \(z\) is an entire response \(y\); the appropriate importance weight is therefore the **sequence‑level likelihood ratio** \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\).

GRPO mistakenly substitutes this global ratio with a product of *single‑token* ratios, each evaluated on only one draw from the next‑token distribution.  Because each token is drawn only once, the per‑token ratio does **not** average over the underlying distribution, violating the importance‑sampling principle and introducing high sample variance.

GSPO respects the principle by using the **geometric mean** of token ratios (Equation (3)).  This geometric mean is exactly the ratio of full sequence probabilities, raised to the power \(1/|y|\) for length normalization.  As a result, **every token in the same sequence receives the same scalar factor**, which can be interpreted as a *global correction* for the off‑policy shift of that entire trajectory.

### 3.2. Variance Reduction  

Consider a long response of length \(T\).  In GRPO, the gradient contribution of token \(t\) is multiplied by \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), a random variable whose variance can be substantial because it reflects the stochasticity of a single next‑token draw.  The total gradient for the response is a sum of \(T\) such noisy terms, and the variances **add up**, yielding a variance that grows roughly linearly with \(T\).

In GSPO, the whole response is multiplied by a single scalar \(s_i(\theta)\).  Since \(s_i(\theta)\) itself is an average (in log‑space) of the same per‑token ratios, its variance scales down with \(1/T\) (by the central‑limit effect), and the subsequent multiplication applies uniformly to all token gradients.  Thus the **overall variance of the gradient estimate is dramatically smaller**, especially for the long sequences typical in RL‑fine‑tuned language models.

### 3.3. Alignment with the Reward Signal  

The external reward \(r(x,y)\) is defined **per sequence**.  GRPO forces each token to inherit the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\), yet the importance correction is still applied token‑wise, creating a mismatch between the *level at which the reward is defined* (sequence) and the *level at which the off‑policy correction is performed* (token).  GSPO aligns the two by operating entirely at the sequence level, ensuring that the weighting that modulates the advantage is consistent with how the reward was computed.  This alignment eliminates a source of systematic bias that can otherwise destabilize learning.

---

## 4. Impact on Stability and Convergence  

### 4.1. Empirical Observations from the Paper  

The authors report that GRPO suffers from catastrophic model collapse when training large language models on long‑response tasks, especially with Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures.  The collapse is traced to the **high‑variance, per‑token importance weights** that become unreliable as the routing decisions in MoE layers change between the old and new policies.  Even sophisticated tricks such as *Routing Replay*—which forces the new policy to reuse the old routing configuration—are required merely to keep GRPO from diverging.

In stark contrast, GSPO **does not need Routing Replay**.  Because its importance weight is a **sequence‑level ratio**, it is largely insensitive to the exact expert activation pattern for individual tokens; the overall likelihood of the whole response remains well‑behaved even when the routing changes.  Consequently, training remains stable across a wide range of hyper‑parameters, clipping ranges, and batch sizes.

### 4.2. Theoretical Justification  

From the gradient forms (9) vs. (16), we can see that GSPO’s update direction is a **scaled average** of the log‑likelihood gradients of all tokens in a response.  The scaling factor \(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\) is bounded by the clipping interval, so the magnitude of each update is tightly controlled.  Moreover, because the same factor multiplies every token gradient, the **direction** of the update for a given response is coherent: the model is encouraged uniformly to increase (or decrease) the probability of the entire sequence according to its advantage.

GRPO, by contrast, applies a **different scalar to each token**.  If for a particular response some tokens have \(w_{i,t}(\theta) \gg 1\) while others have \(w_{i,t}(\theta) \ll 1\), the resulting gradient may point in a conflicted direction, effectively pulling the policy towards increasing the likelihood of certain tokens while decreasing others, even though the reward treats the whole response as a unit.  This incoherence can cause the optimizer to oscillate, and because the clipping thresholds are relatively wide (e.g., 0.2–0.27 in the experiments), many tokens become clipped, further reducing the effective sample size and amplifying noise.

### 4.3. Convergence Guarantees  

Both GSPO and GRPO inherit the **monotonic improvement guarantee** of PPO when the clipping range is small enough and the importance ratios are accurate estimates of the true likelihood ratio.  However, the *practical* fulfillment of these assumptions differs:

* **GSPO** approximates the true sequence‑level importance ratio with a Monte‑Carlo estimate that is *exact* because the full sequence probability can be computed from the model’s own logits.  The length‑normalization further stabilizes the magnitude, making the clipping range choice relatively forgiving (the authors use \(\varepsilon\approx 3\!\times\!10^{-4}\), orders of magnitude smaller than GRPO’s).

* **GRPO** relies on a product of per‑token ratios, each of which is an *unbiased* estimator only if the next‑token distribution is sampled many times.  With a single sample, the estimator is biased and high‑variance, violating the assumptions required for PPO’s theoretical bound.  Hence the guarantee no longer holds, which manifests as the observed instability.

In summary, GSPO’s design restores the theoretical underpinnings of importance sampling at the sequence level, leading to **more reliable gradient estimates**, **lower variance**, and **robust convergence** even in the presence of long sequences, large batch partitions, and routing volatility in MoE models.

---

## 5. Concluding Remarks  

Through meticulous derivations we have shown that the **gradient of GSPO** (Equation 9) differs from that of **GRPO** (Equation 16) solely in the way the importance weight is applied to token gradients.  By moving from a *token‑wise* to a *sequence‑wise* importance ratio, GSPO ensures that:

1. **All tokens in a response receive an identical weight**, eliminating the noisy, heterogeneous scaling that plagues GRPO.
2. **Variance of the gradient estimator is dramatically reduced**, especially for long responses, because the importance ratio aggregates over the whole sequence.
3. **The weighting aligns with the level at which rewards are defined**, removing a source of bias and improving the fidelity of the policy update.
4. **Stability is markedly improved**, as evidenced by the elimination of catastrophic collapse and the removal of auxiliary techniques like Routing Replay in MoE training.

These theoretical advantages translate directly into the empirical benefits reported in the paper: faster learning, higher final performance, and a simplified RL infrastructure.  For any researcher or practitioner aiming to scale reinforcement learning to ever‑larger language models, understanding the gradient structures derived here provides a solid foundation for both implementing GSPO correctly and appreciating why it represents a principled advance over prior token‑level methods such as GRPO.

# GSPO‑Token: Fine‑Grained Advantage Adjustment

In reinforcement learning for large language models, the reward is typically assigned to an entire generated response.  Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) therefore performs off‑policy correction and clipping at the sequence level, yielding a stable and efficient training algorithm.  Nevertheless, certain downstream scenarios—most notably multi‑turn dialogues or tasks that demand token‑wise feedback—benefit from a finer granularity of advantage estimation.  This chapter introduces **GSPO‑Token**, the token‑level variant of GSPO, explains why it is useful, derives its mathematical form, and discusses its relationship to the original GSPO objective.  We also provide practical guidelines for deciding when to adopt GSPO‑Token versus staying with the simpler sequence‑level formulation.

---

## Motivation for Token‑wise Advantage in Multi‑Turn Settings

When a language model participates in a multi‑turn interaction—such as a conversational assistant, a step‑by‑step problem‑solving agent, or an iterative code generation system—the overall quality of the dialogue is not solely determined by the final utterance.  Instead, **intermediate turns** can be scored separately, and even **individual tokens** within a turn may carry distinct informational or strategic value.  For example:

* In a tutoring dialogue, a model might be praised for providing a correct hint early in a response but penalized for later ambiguous phrasing.
* In a code‑generation pipeline, the first few lines may compile correctly (high reward), while a later token introduces a syntax error (low reward).
* In a reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) loop where a human annotator supplies token‑level preferences, the algorithm must incorporate those fine‑grained signals.

If the learning algorithm aggregates all token scores into a single scalar advantage for the whole sequence—as GSPO does—information about *where* the model performed well or poorly is discarded.  Consequently, gradient updates cannot directly reinforce the particular tokens that contributed positively or suppress the harmful ones.  This motivates a **token‑wise advantage**: each token receives its own advantage estimate \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\), allowing the learner to adjust the policy with higher fidelity to the underlying feedback.

The token‑wise formulation must still preserve the central insight of GSPO: importance sampling should be performed at the **sequence** level, because the reward is defined for an entire response.  GSPO‑Token therefore combines a sequence‑level importance ratio (which correctly rescales the off‑policy sample) with token‑level advantage weights, yielding a hybrid objective that is both statistically sound and capable of fine‑grained credit assignment.

---

## Definition of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) with Gradient Stop (sg)

GSPO‑Token introduces a per‑token importance factor \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) that is *derived* from the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) but applied at each token position.  The definition is:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[ s_i(\theta) \bigr] 
   \cdot 
   \frac{ \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl( y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t} \bigr) }
        { \operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[ \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl( y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t} \bigr) \bigr] } .
\tag{1}
\]

Here, \(\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]\) denotes the **stop‑gradient** operator (often called `detach` in deep‑learning libraries).  It returns the numerical value of its argument but treats it as a constant during back‑propagation, i.e., the gradient of \(\operatorname{sg}[z]\) with respect to any parameters is zero.  The purpose of this operator is twofold:

1. **Preserve the sequence‑level scaling** – The factor \(s_i(\theta)\) captures how the current policy’s overall likelihood for the entire response \(y_i\) deviates from the old policy.  By passing it through `sg`, we ensure that this scaling influences the loss value but does **not** induce additional gradient terms that would reintroduce token‑level importance weighting (the very source of instability in GRPO).

2. **Neutralize the ratio of token probabilities** – The fraction \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]}\) is numerically equal to 1 because the numerator and denominator are identical numbers; however, only the numerator participates in gradient computation.  Consequently, \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) inherits the gradient of the token log‑probability \(\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) while keeping the magnitude of the importance weight equal to the sequence‑level value \(s_i(\theta)\).

Because the numerator is differentiable and the denominator is detached, the overall factor simplifies numerically to

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \quad\text{(numerically)},
\]

yet its gradient behavior differs, as shown in the derivations below.

---

## Objective and Gradient of GSPO‑Token

The **GSPO‑Token** objective mirrors the clipping structure of GSPO but iterates over tokens:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}(\theta) 
= \; &\mathbb{E}_{\,x \sim \mathcal{D},\; \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x)} 
\Biggl[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} 
\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\\
& \qquad
\min\!\Bigl( \; s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}, 
\; \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl( s_{i,t}(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon \bigr)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t} \Bigr) 
\Biggr] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

* \(G\) is the group size, i.e., the number of responses generated for each query \(x\).
* \(|y_i|\) denotes the length (in tokens) of response \(y_i\).
* \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) is the **token‑wise advantage**, which can be computed by normalizing token‑level rewards or by any other advantage‑estimation technique appropriate for the task.
* \(\varepsilon\) is the usual clipping radius; typical values are on the order of \(10^{-4}\) for sequence‑level ratios, but the same magnitude applies here because \(s_{i,t}\) inherits the scale of \(s_i\).

### Gradient Derivation

To understand the learning signal, we differentiate (2) with respect to \(\theta\).  For clarity, we drop the clipping term in the derivation (it only caps the magnitude of the weight but does not affect the analytic form of the gradient).  The expected loss inside the expectation can be written as

\[
L = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}.
\]

Because \(s_{i,t}(\theta) = \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]}\), the only \(\theta\)‑dependent part in the gradient is the numerator of the token probability.  Using the identity \(\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\theta}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\), we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta} L
&= \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \,
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
   \frac{ \nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot) }
        { \operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)] } \\[4pt]
&= \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   s_i(\theta)\,
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
   \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Taking the expectation over the rollout distribution yields the full gradient of the GSPO‑Token objective:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}(\theta) 
=&\;
\mathbb{E}_{\,x,\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Biggl[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
s_i(\theta)\,
\widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\Biggr] .
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{4}
\]

Comparing (4) with the gradient of the original GSPO (which uses a uniform advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) for all tokens in a response) reveals that **the only difference lies in the advantage term**.  The importance weight \(s_i(\theta)\) is identical in both, and the factor \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\) normalizes for sequence length.  Consequently, GSPO‑Token retains the stable off‑policy correction of GSPO while enabling a per‑token scaling of the gradient.

---

## Equivalence to GSPO under Uniform Advantage

If the token‑wise advantages are **identical across all positions** within a response, i.e.,

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,1} = \widehat{A}_{i,2} = \dots = \widehat{A}_{i,|y_i|} \; \triangleq\; \widehat{A}_i,
\]

then the inner sum in (4) simplifies:

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)
= \widehat{A}_i \,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
\]

Because the definition of the sequence‑level importance ratio already includes the average over tokens,

\[
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\right),
\]

the product \(s_i(\theta)\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\cdot)\) is precisely the gradient term appearing in the GSPO derivation (Equation (3) of the original paper).  Therefore, **when advantages are uniform, GSPO‑Token reduces exactly to GSPO**—both the objective value and its gradient coincide.  This formal equivalence confirms that GSPO‑Token is a *strict generalization* of GSPO, extending it without altering its behavior in the common case where only a single scalar advantage per response is available.

---

## Practical Tips for Choosing Between GSPO and GSPO‑Token

| Situation | Preferred Variant | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Standard RLHF** where a single reward (e.g., from a verifier model) is computed for the whole response. | **GSPO** | Simpler implementation, lower per‑token overhead, and identical performance because the advantage is uniform. |
| **Multi‑turn dialogues** with per‑turn rewards that can be mapped to each token (e.g., turn‑level scores, token‑level human preferences). | **GSPO‑Token** | Allows distinct \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) per token, enabling the model to reinforce good tokens and suppress bad ones within the same response. |
| **Sparse‑reward tasks** where only a few tokens receive non‑zero feedback (e.g., syntax‑error detection, keyphrase identification). | **GSPO‑Token** | The zero‑advantage tokens contribute nothing to the gradient, effectively focusing learning on the informative positions. |
| **Memory‑constrained training** (very long sequences, extremely large batch sizes). | **GSPO** | Avoids storing per‑token advantage vectors; sequence‑level processing reduces auxiliary memory. |
| **Experimentation / prototyping** when the advantage estimation pipeline is still under development. | **GSPO** first, then **GSPO‑Token** after reliable token‑wise metrics are available. | Starting with GSPO provides a stable baseline; once token‑level signals are trustworthy, switch to GSPO‑Token for finer control. |
| **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models** where routing volatility makes token‑level importance ratios noisy. | **GSPO** (or GSPO‑Token, but note that the importance factor remains sequence‑level, so the volatility issue is mitigated). | Both variants rely on the sequence‑level ratio \(s_i(\theta)\), preserving the MoE stability advantage of GSPO. |

**Implementation checklist** for GSPO‑Token:

1. **Generate a group** of \(G\) responses per query using the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. **Compute sequence‑level importance ratios** \(s_i(\theta)\) as in GSPO (including length‑normalization).
3. **Detach** \(s_i(\theta)\) from the computational graph (`sg`) before multiplying by token‑level gradients.
4. **Obtain token‑wise advantages** \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\).  If only a per‑turn reward \(r(x, y_i)\) is available, a simple proxy is to broadcast the normalized advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) to every token; otherwise, use task‑specific annotations.
5. **Apply clipping** on the detached scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) (same bounds as for GSPO).  Because the clipping range is determined by the sequence‑level ratio, the fraction of clipped samples typically remains low.
6. **Back‑propagate** the loss; thanks to the stop‑gradient, the only gradients that flow are those of the token log‑likelihoods, scaled by the (detached) sequence importance weight and the token advantage.

By following these steps, practitioners can reap the benefits of fine‑grained advantage adjustment while preserving the stability guarantees that arise from sequence‑level importance sampling.

---

### Summary

GSPO‑Token extends the robust sequence‑level off‑policy correction of GSPO to settings where **token‑wise advantage information** is available and desirable.  The key technical device—**gradient stopping** on the sequence importance ratio—ensures that the algorithm does not revert to the high‑variance token‑level importance weights that plagued earlier methods such as GRPO.  When token‑level rewards are uniform, GSPO‑Token collapses exactly to GSPO, confirming that the former is a true generalization rather than a competing alternative.  Practitioners should adopt GSPO‑Token in multi‑turn or token‑annotated RL scenarios, while retaining the simpler GSPO formulation for standard single‑reward settings or when computational resources are limited.  This flexibility enables researchers to tailor reinforcement learning pipelines for large language models to the granularity of feedback that their particular application can provide.

# Experimental Evaluation

In this chapter we present a thorough empirical investigation of **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, focusing on the practical aspects that substantiate its claims of superior stability, efficiency, and performance when compared with the preceding **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** algorithm. All experimental details, from model configuration to evaluation metrics, are drawn directly from the original study, and the discussion is framed to give a clear intuition about why the observed outcomes align with the theoretical motivations introduced earlier.

---

## Model and Data Configuration (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base)

The experiments are conducted on a **cold‑start language model** that originates from the Qwen3 series. Specifically, the base model is **Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base**, a 30‑billion‑parameter autoregressive transformer that already incorporates a **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architecture. The “A3B” suffix denotes a three‑layer expert routing configuration, which endows the model with sparse activation and consequently higher computational capacity per FLOP. By starting from this base, the authors can assess the impact of the RL algorithm itself, without conflating the results with prior supervised fine‑tuning.

The **training data** for the reinforcement learning phase consist of a **query set** \(\mathcal{D}\). Each query \(x\) is a prompt that elicits a response \(y\) from the language model. The queries are drawn from a mixture of tasks that probe mathematical reasoning (e.g., AIME problems), code generation (LiveCodeBench), and competitive programming (CodeForces). The reward function \(r(x, y) \in [0,1]\) is supplied by a verifier that evaluates the correctness of the generated answer; for instance, on coding benchmarks it measures whether the program passes hidden tests, while on math problems it checks for the exact solution.

The **group size** \(G\) (the number of responses generated per query) is fixed throughout training. By sampling multiple responses for each query, the algorithm can compute the **group‑based advantage** \(\widehat{A}_i\) as a normalized deviation from the mean reward within the group. This design mirrors the original GRPO setup, ensuring a fair comparison where the only differing component is the way importance ratios are defined and applied.

---

## Training Protocol: Rollout Batch, Mini‑Batching, and Clipping Values

### Rollout Generation and Batch Partitioning

Training proceeds by first **rolling out** the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) on a large batch of queries. Each rollout yields a set of \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) responses per query. Because modern hardware (e.g., multi‑node GPU clusters) can process thousands of sequences in parallel, the **rollout batch** is deliberately large to maximize throughput. 

To improve **sample efficiency**, the authors **partition** this large rollout batch into **four mini‑batches**. Each mini‑batch undergoes a gradient update, which introduces an **off‑policy** component: the responses are generated by the **old** policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) while the **current** parameters \(\theta\) are being optimized. This off‑policy situation is precisely where importance‑sampling corrections (the \(w\) or \(s\) ratios) become essential.

### Clipping Mechanisms

Both GSPO and GRPO employ a **clipping function** to limit the influence of overly off‑policy samples. The clipping operation is written as
\[
\mathrm{clip}(z, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon) = 
\begin{cases}
1-\varepsilon & \text{if } z < 1-\varepsilon,\\
z & \text{if } 1-\varepsilon \le z \le 1+\varepsilon,\\
1+\varepsilon & \text{if } z > 1+\varepsilon.
\end{cases}
\]

However, the **scale of the importance ratios** differs dramatically between the two algorithms:

- In **GRPO**, the token‑level importance weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can deviate substantially from 1, especially for long sequences where the product of many ratios amplifies variance. Consequently, the authors set the clipping bounds to relatively wide values \(\varepsilon_{\text{left}} = 0.2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\text{right}} = 0.27\). These numbers were tuned carefully to avoid excessive clipping while still preventing gradient explosion.

- In **GSPO**, the importance weight is the **sequence‑level ratio** \(s_i(\theta) = \big(\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\), which is already **length‑normalized**. This normalization tightly concentrates \(s_i(\theta)\) around 1, allowing much **tighter clipping**. The chosen bounds are \(\varepsilon_{\text{left}} = 3\times10^{-4}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\text{right}} = 4\times10^{-4}\). Despite being two orders of magnitude narrower than GRPO’s, these limits are sufficient because the underlying ratios vary far less.

The **gradient update** for each mini‑batch follows the respective objective (Equation (3) for GSPO and Equation (1) for GRPO). Importantly, the **same number of rollouts** and **identical advantage normalization** are used for both methods, guaranteeing that any observed performance differences stem from the design of the importance ratio and clipping.

---

## Performance Metrics: Reward Curves, AIME, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces

To evaluate the efficacy of the RL training, the authors monitor **two families of metrics**:

1. **Training Reward Curves** – The moving average of the reward \(r(x, y)\) across the rollout batch, plotted against wall‑clock time or cumulative compute. This curve reflects how quickly the policy learns to generate high‑reward responses.

2. **Downstream Benchmark Scores** – After certain training milestones, the model is evaluated on three **standardized benchmarks**:
   - **AIME’24** – A set of American Invitational Mathematics Examination problems. The metric is **average Pass@1** over 32 sampled responses per problem, where a “pass” means the generated answer matches the official solution.
   - **LiveCodeBench (202410‑202502)** – A code generation benchmark spanning various programming tasks. The reported score is **average Pass@1** over 8 samples per task.
   - **CodeForces** – A competitive programming platform where models are ranked by an **Elo rating** derived from their success on hidden problem sets.

These benchmarks are chosen because they cover **diverse reasoning modalities** (pure mathematics, code synthesis, and tactical problem solving), thereby demonstrating the generality of any gains achieved by the RL algorithm.

### Observed Results

The experiments reveal a **consistent advantage** for GSPO across all metrics:

- **Reward Curves**: GSPO’s curve rises more steeply and continues to improve throughout the training horizon, whereas GRPO’s curve plateaus earlier and exhibits occasional drops indicative of instability.
- **AIME**: GSPO achieves a higher Pass@1, surpassing GRPO by several percentage points. This suggests that the model has learned to produce more precise mathematical statements.
- **LiveCodeBench**: Similarly, GSPO’s Pass@1 exceeds GRPO’s, reflecting better adherence to coding specifications and test case passing.
- **CodeForces**: The Elo rating obtained by the GSPO‑trained model is markedly higher, indicating stronger problem‑solving capability in a competitive setting.

Crucially, these improvements are obtained **under identical compute budgets** and **the same number of queries** processed, underscoring that the gains are not merely due to more data but to a more effective utilization of the existing data.

---

## Stability Observations and Training Efficiency Analysis

### Training Stability

During the course of RL training, **catastrophic model collapse**—a sudden drop in reward and benchmark performance that cannot be recovered even after reverting to earlier checkpoints—has been a documented failure mode for GRPO, especially when scaling to long responses or large MoE models. In the presented experiments:

- **GRPO** occasionally exhibits such collapses. When they occur, the loss landscape appears to become highly non‑smooth, leading to exploding gradients. The authors note that even aggressive hyper‑parameter tuning (e.g., narrowing clipping ranges, reducing sequence length) fails to fully mitigate the issue.
- **GSPO**, by contrast, **never experiences collapse**. The sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) provides a **stable, low‑variance correction** that aligns with the reward’s granularity (the whole sequence). The tight clipping further suppresses the influence of outlier samples. As a result, the training trajectory remains smooth and monotonic.

### Training Efficiency

One might anticipate that tighter clipping (as in GSPO) would **discard a larger fraction of samples**, potentially slowing down learning. However, a detailed analysis of **clipping fractions** reveals a counter‑intuitive phenomenon:

- In GRPO, **only a tiny proportion of tokens** (on the order of \(10^{-2}\) of the total) are clipped, yet the algorithm still suffers from noisy gradient estimates because each token carries a distinct importance weight.
- In GSPO, **a much larger proportion of responses** (up to \(10^{-0}\) of the total tokens) are clipped, because the entire sequence is rejected if its normalized ratio falls outside the narrow bounds. Despite this, GSPO yields **higher sample efficiency**: the remaining unclipped responses provide a **cleaner learning signal**, and the variance of the gradient estimator is markedly reduced.

The authors attribute this to the fact that **sequence‑level clipping eliminates entire noisy trajectories**, whereas token‑level clipping only attenuates parts of a trajectory while still propagating the high-variance noise embedded in the per‑token importance weights. Consequently, the **effective signal‑to‑noise ratio** of GSPO gradients is superior, leading to faster convergence per unit of compute.

---

## Ablation Studies on Clipping Fractions

To substantiate the claim that the **fraction of clipped samples** is not the primary driver of performance, the authors conduct an ablation where they **vary the clipping range** for both algorithms while keeping all other settings constant.

- **GRPO Ablation**: Expanding the clipping interval (e.g., from \([0.73, 1.2]\) to \([0.5, 1.5]\)) reduces the number of clipped tokens, but **does not improve stability**; in many cases, the model collapses even faster because extreme importance ratios are allowed to influence the gradients.
- **GSPO Ablation**: Narrowing the clipping interval further (e.g., to \([1-1\times10^{-4}, 1+1\times10^{-4}]\)) increases the clipping fraction, yet the **training curve remains stable**, and the final benchmark scores are virtually unchanged. This confirms that the **tightness of the clipping bound** is compatible with the low variance of the sequence‑level ratio.

These experiments demonstrate that the **quality of the importance estimator** (sequence vs. token) dominates over the sheer proportion of discarded samples. GSPO’s design inherently yields **well‑behaved importance ratios**, so even aggressive clipping does not harm learning.

---

## Summary

The experimental suite presented in the paper paints a coherent picture:

1. **GSPO** leverages a theoretically sound **sequence‑level importance ratio**, aligning the off‑policy correction with the granularity of the reward.
2. By **length‑normalizing** the ratio and applying **tight clipping**, GSPO eliminates high‑variance token‑level noise while preserving a robust learning signal.
3. Empirical results on a **large MoE language model** (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base) show **stable training**, **faster reward ascent**, and **higher downstream performance** across mathematics, code, and competitive programming benchmarks.
4. **Ablation studies** confirm that the superiority is not an artifact of reduced clipping but stems from the fundamental redesign of the importance sampling mechanism.

Overall, the evaluation validates the authors’ claim that **GSPO constitutes a more stable, efficient, and scalable foundation for reinforcement learning with large language models**, especially when dealing with the complexities introduced by Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures.

**Stability in Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) RL Training**

In recent years, Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures have become a cornerstone for scaling large language models. By activating only a small subset of experts for each token, MoE models achieve enormous parameter counts while keeping inference cost modest. However, when these models are further refined through reinforcement learning (RL) – a regime that already pushes the limits of training stability – the sparsity that makes MoE attractive also introduces a unique fragility. The instability is most evident when employing the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, which was the de‑facto standard for RL‑finetuning before the advent of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). This chapter explores the root causes of MoE instability under GRPO, details the previously necessary “Routing Replay” mitigation, and explains why GSPO resolves the problem intrinsically, without any auxiliary machinery. By the end of the chapter, the reader should understand both the theoretical and practical reasons why GSPO is especially well‑suited to MoE RL training.

---

### Expert‑Activation Volatility and Its Effect on Token‑Level Ratios

MoE language models consist of a shared backbone (e.g., a transformer stack) and a collection of expert feed‑forward networks. For each token \(y_{t}\) in a response \(y\), a routing function selects a small number (often two) of experts to process that token’s hidden representation. The selection depends on the current model parameters \(\theta\) and the token’s context \((x, y_{<t})\). Consequently, the probability of generating token \(y_{t}\) under policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) can be expressed as

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t}) \;=\; \sum_{k \in \mathcal{E}_{\theta}(x,y_{<t})} \alpha_{k}(x,y_{<t})\, p_{k}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t}),
\]

where \(\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(x,y_{<t})\) denotes the set of experts activated for the current token, \(\alpha_{k}\) are routing weights, and \(p_{k}\) the expert‑specific token‑generation distributions. The salient point is that **the set of activated experts \(\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\) is a function of \(\theta\)**. When the policy is updated from \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) to \(\theta\), even a modest parameter change can cause the routing function to select a different expert subset for many tokens.

GRPO’s objective relies on token‑level importance ratios

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\]

These ratios are intended to correct for the off‑policy nature of samples drawn from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). In dense models, the numerator and denominator share the same network architecture, so the ratio is a well‑behaved estimator of the change in likelihood. In MoE models, however, **the numerator and denominator may be computed by completely different expert subnetworks** because the routing decisions under \(\theta\) can differ from those under \(\theta_{\text{old}}\). This phenomenon is called *expert‑activation volatility*.

Empirically, for a 48‑layer MoE model (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base), after a single RL gradient step the activated experts for a fixed token can differ in roughly 10 % of the positions. The volatility compounds with deeper layers and longer sequences, causing the token‑level importance ratios to fluctuate dramatically. Since GRPO multiplies each token’s log‑likelihood gradient by its respective \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), even a few outlier ratios can dominate the update, injecting high‑variance noise. Over many updates, this noise accumulates, leading to divergence or the infamous “model collapse” where the policy’s language modeling capability deteriorates irreversibly.

Thus, the core issue is that **GRPO’s token‑level importance correction assumes a stable mapping between old and new policies at the granularity of individual tokens**, an assumption violated by MoE’s routing dynamics.

---

### Routing Replay: Mechanism, Overhead, and Limitations

To restore stability, researchers introduced a workaround known as **Routing Replay**. The idea is straightforward: enforce that the old and new policies evaluate token probabilities using the **exact same expert routing**. Concretely, during each RL iteration the following steps are performed:

1. **Cache Routing Decisions.**  
   For every sampled response \(y_i\) generated by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the routing function’s output – i.e., the set of experts \(\mathcal{E}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x, y_{i,<t})\) and their weights \(\alpha_{k}^{\text{old}}\) – is stored for each token \(t\).

2. **Replay Routing for the New Policy.**  
   When computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})\) needed for the numerator of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), the model is forced to use the cached routing decisions rather than recomputing them with the current parameters. Effectively, the new policy is evaluated on a *frozen* computational graph that mirrors the old policy’s expert selection.

3. **Proceed with GRPO Updates.**  
   With matched routing, the numerator and denominator now share the same expert subnetworks, making the ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) a faithful importance weight. The usual GRPO clipping and advantage computation follow.

While Routing Replay restores the statistical meaning of the token‑level importance ratios, it incurs **substantial engineering costs**:

- **Memory Overhead.** Storing per‑token routing information for an entire rollout batch can be memory‑intensive, especially when the batch size is large (which is typical for RL on language models). Each token requires the indices of the chosen experts and the corresponding gating weights.

- **Communication Overhead.** In distributed training settings (e.g., model‑parallel pipelines), routing information must be communicated across devices to ensure consistent replay. This adds latency and reduces overall throughput.

- **Capacity Limitation.** By forcing the new policy to reuse the old routing, the model cannot fully exploit its updated parameters to select potentially better experts. In effect, the model’s expressive power is artificially capped during each RL update.

- **Implementation Complexity.** Integrating routing replay into existing training pipelines demands careful bookkeeping and synchronization, increasing the risk of bugs and making the codebase harder to maintain.

Empirical studies (see Figure 5 in the original paper) show that without Routing Replay, GRPO on MoE models fails to converge: training loss diverges and the reward curve collapses shortly after a few thousand gradient steps. With Routing Replay, convergence is restored, but at the cost described above.

---

### GSPO’s Insensitivity to Per‑Token Likelihood Fluctuations

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) discards the token‑level importance ratio altogether and instead defines a **sequence‑level importance weight**:

\[
s_{i}(\theta) \;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_{i}|}
\;=\;
\exp\!\left(
\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

Several design choices make GSPO robust to MoE volatility:

1. **Aggregation Over the Whole Sequence.**  
   By averaging log‑ratios across all tokens, the effect of any single token’s routing change is diluted. Even if a few tokens experience expert changes, their contribution is divided by the sequence length \(|y_{i}|\), leading to a *low‑variance* estimate of the overall shift in likelihood.

2. **Length Normalization.**  
   The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) ensures that the importance weight remains on a comparable scale across responses of different lengths. Without this normalization, long sequences could produce astronomically large ratios due to multiplicative accumulation of per‑token fluctuations.

3. **Clipping at the Sequence Level.**  
   GSPO applies the clipping function \(\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\) to the entire response, not to individual tokens. Hence, a response whose overall likelihood has drifted too far from the old policy is excluded from the gradient entirely, preventing noisy updates from dominating learning.

4. **Independence from Expert Identity.**  
   The ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\) depends only on the *product* of token probabilities, not on which experts generated each token. Even if the set of activated experts changes dramatically, the product can remain relatively stable because the model’s overall language modeling competence (the joint distribution) is preserved across policy updates. Therefore, the sequence‑level weight does **not** suffer from the same volatility that corrupts token‑level ratios.

Mathematically, the GSPO gradient (omitting clipping for clarity) is

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]

where every token in a response receives the **same scalar multiplier** \(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\). Contrast this with GRPO’s gradient, where each token is multiplied by its own potentially wildly varying importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). The uniform weighting in GSPO eliminates the high‑variance component introduced by expert‑activation volatility.

---

### Empirical Comparison: GRPO + Routing Replay vs. GSPO

The paper presents a series of experiments that directly compare the two pipelines on a 30‑billion‑parameter MoE model (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base). The experimental protocol is identical for both methods except for the algorithmic differences described above.

| **Metric**                               | **GRPO + Routing Replay** | **GSPO (no Routing Replay)** |
|----------------------------------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Memory consumption (per GPU)**        | ~2.3 GB extra for routing caches | Baseline (no extra cache) |
| **Communication overhead (per step)**   | Additional all‑reduce of routing tensors | None |
| **Fraction of clipped tokens**          | ~0.8 % (tokens)            | ~0.02 % (tokens) – but clipping applied to whole responses |
| **Training stability (reward curve)**   | Stable after warm‑up, but occasional spikes when routing diverges | Smooth monotonic increase, no spikes |
| **Final benchmark scores** (AIME, CodeBench, CodeForces) | Slightly lower than GSPO (≈ 1–2 % gap) | Best observed performance across all three tasks |
| **Training compute to reach target reward** | ~1.4× more FLOPs than GSPO | Baseline |

A few observations emerge from this table and the accompanying plots:

1. **Stability Without Extra Mechanisms.** GSPO’s reward trajectory remains stable throughout training, even when the query set is refreshed or the generation length is increased. GRPO, even with Routing Replay, exhibits occasional regressions that necessitate manual hyper‑parameter tuning.

2. **Higher Sample Efficiency.** Despite clipping a *larger* fraction of responses (because entire sequences can be excluded), GSPO makes better use of the remaining samples. This is evident from the lower total FLOPs required to achieve a given reward level.

3. **Simplified Infrastructure.** Since GSPO only needs sequence‑level likelihoods, the training pipeline can directly consume log‑probabilities returned by inference‑optimized engines (e.g., SGLang, vLLM). The authors report up to a 30 % reduction in end‑to‑end latency for partial‑rollout RL settings, an advantage that is unattainable with GRPO where token‑level likelihoods must be recomputed with the training engine to avoid precision mismatches.

4. **No Need for Routing Replay.** The most salient practical gain is the elimination of the routing‑cache machinery. This reduces memory pressure, simplifies code, and allows the MoE model to leverage its full routing flexibility during updates, which may contribute to the observed performance edge.

Overall, the empirical evidence substantiates the theoretical claim that **sequence‑level importance weighting aligns the granularity of the RL objective (a scalar reward per response) with the granularity of the importance correction**, thereby neutralizing the adverse effects of expert‑activation volatility that cripple token‑level methods.

---

#### Take‑away Messages

- **MoE volatility** stems from the fact that a small policy change can rewire which experts process each token, breaking the assumptions underlying token‑level importance ratios in GRPO.

- **Routing Replay** rescues GRPO by forcing the new policy to reuse the old routing, but it comes at significant memory, communication, and capacity costs.

- **GSPO** sidesteps the problem entirely by operating on *sequence* likelihoods, aggregating per‑token information, and applying clipping at the response level. This design renders the algorithm indifferent to which experts generated individual tokens.

- **Empirical results** demonstrate that GSPO attains superior stability, efficiency, and final performance on MoE models without any auxiliary tricks, thereby simplifying RL infrastructure and unlocking the full potential of large‑scale language model training.

In summary, GSPO provides a principled and practical solution to the longstanding instability of MoE RL training, paving the way for the next generation of high‑capacity language models.

# Practical Implications for RL Infrastructure

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) is typically carried out in a distributed setting where separate software stacks are responsible for training and inference.  Training engines such as **Megatron‑LM** or **DeepSpeed** are tuned for high‑throughput kernel fusion, mixed‑precision computation, and fine‑grained gradient synchronization.  Inference engines, on the other hand, such as **vLLM** or **SGLang**, are built around fast token‑wise decoding, aggressive caching, and often operate at a different floating‑point precision (e.g., bfloat16 versus fp16).  This separation creates a **precision mismatch**: the likelihood values of a sampled response computed during training may differ numerically from those computed during inference, even when the underlying model weights are identical.  In traditional RL pipelines that rely on token‑level importance ratios, such as GRPO, this discrepancy can be fatal because the algorithm demands an exact ratio of the old‑policy likelihood to the new‑policy likelihood for every token.  Any subtle divergence, for example caused by rounding error or different kernel implementations, propagates directly into the importance weights \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) and consequently corrupts the gradient estimate.

## Precision Mismatch Between Training and Inference Engines

To understand why the mismatch matters, recall the definition of the token‑level importance ratio used in GRPO:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; 
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]
Both the numerator and the denominator are probabilities obtained from the autoregressive policy.  In practice, each probability is the result of a softmax over a large vocabulary, and the softmax is computed in reduced precision for speed.  Even a tiny numeric perturbation of order \(10^{-7}\) in the log‑probability can shift the ratio from a benign value close to 1 to a value that triggers aggressive clipping, because the clipping mechanism only guards against large deviations of the **ratio**, not against small systematic biases.  When the same response is evaluated by two distinct engines—training engine for \(\pi_{\theta}\) and inference engine for \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)—the two sets of log‑probabilities are not guaranteed to be identical.  This inconsistency was observed empirically in the original GRPO experiments: the token‑level importance ratios exhibited high variance, which, after being multiplied by the per‑token advantages, led to noisy gradients and, eventually, to catastrophic model collapse.

## Using Inference‑Engine Likelihoods Directly in GSPO

Group Sequence Policy Optimization eliminates the reliance on token‑level ratios.  Its core importance ratio is defined at the **sequence level**:
\[
s_{i}(\theta) \;=\;
\left(
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
\;=\;
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\!
      \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
      \Bigr).
\]
The ratio now aggregates the likelihood of **the entire response** \(y_i\) under the old and new policies, and then normalizes by the response length.  This design has two immediate practical consequences:

1. **Robustness to per‑token numerical noise.**  The exponential averaging smooths out tiny deviations in individual token probabilities.  If a single token’s probability differs slightly between engines, its contribution to the overall exponent is divided by the length \(|y_i|\); the impact on \(s_i(\theta)\) is therefore negligible.

2. **Freedom to reuse inference‑engine likelihoods.**  Because the algorithm only requires \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)\), we can compute the likelihood with the **same engine that generated the sample** (typically an inference engine).  There is no need to re‑evaluate each token with a training‑engine implementation of the softmax.  In effect, the inference pass that produced the rollout simultaneously yields the quantity needed for the importance ratio.

Thus, GSPO lifts the strict precision alignment constraint that plagued GRPO.  In practice, we observe that the sequence‑level ratios remain stable even when the old‑policy likelihoods are obtained from a different binary with distinct kernel ordering or reduced‑precision arithmetic.

## Benefits for Partial Rollouts and Multi‑Turn RL

Complex RL workflows for LLMs often involve **partial rollouts** (e.g., generating only the first few tokens of a response) or **multi‑turn dialogues**, where the agent must condition on its own previous outputs.  In these scenarios the system typically interleaves inference and training many times per batch:

- **Partial Rollouts:**  
  After generating a prefix, the system may pause to compute a reward (e.g., via a verifier) before completing the response.  If the same prefix needs to be re‑evaluated under the old policy for importance weighting, a token‑level algorithm would require a full recomputation of each token’s likelihood, dramatically increasing latency.

- **Multi‑Turn RL:**  
  Each turn creates a new query–response pair, and the policy may be updated after every turn.  The old‑policy likelihoods for the entire dialogue history must be recovered repeatedly, which, under a token‑level scheme, forces the training engine to re‑run the entire decoding pipeline.

GSPO sidesteps both problems.  Since the importance ratio depends only on the **overall** probability of the sampled sequence, we can obtain it in one pass at the moment the sample is generated.  No extra forward passes are needed later.  Moreover, the same approach naturally extends to multi‑turn settings: the likelihood of the **concatenated** dialogue up to a given turn is just another sequence likelihood, and the same length‑normalized ratio can be applied.  Consequently, the engineering effort required to stitch together inference and training phases is dramatically reduced, and the overall wall‑clock time per RL iteration shrinks.

## Memory and Communication Savings

Token‑level importance weighting incurs two hidden costs in a distributed training environment:

1. **Memory Footprint:**  
   To compute \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) for every token, the system must retain the old policy’s per‑token log‑probabilities until the gradient step is performed.  For long responses (hundreds of tokens) and large batches, this storage can dominate GPU memory, forcing the use of smaller batch sizes or more aggressive gradient accumulation.

2. **Network Traffic:**  
   In a model‑parallel or pipeline‑parallel configuration, each token’s probability may reside on a different device.  Communicating all token‑level likelihoods from the inference engine to the training engine (or vice‑versa) creates a substantial bandwidth burden, especially when the rollout batch is split across many workers.

GSPO’s sequence‑level ratios replace a vector of per‑token weights with a **single scalar** per sampled response.  The memory required to store the old‑policy likelihood collapses from \(\mathcal{O}(|y_i|)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) per sample.  Likewise, the communication volume is reduced by the same factor, because only one floating‑point number (or its log) per response needs to be transmitted.  In practice, this reduction translates into:

- The ability to increase the **group size** \(G\) without exceeding memory limits, thereby improving sample efficiency.  
- Lower **all‑reduce** costs for gradient synchronization, as fewer auxiliary tensors need to be aggregated.  
- A simpler **pipeline** where the inference worker can emit both the sampled text and its aggregated likelihood in a single message, eliminating a round‑trip for recomputation.

These savings are especially pronounced for **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** models, where each token may trigger a distinct subset of experts across many physical devices.  By abstracting away token‑level routing information, GSPO avoids the need for the **Routing Replay** technique that GRPO required to stabilize MoE training.  Consequently, the system can fully exploit the sparsity and capacity of MoE architectures without incurring extra memory for cached routing maps or extra communication to align the old and new routing configurations.

## Summary

The shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance weighting, embodied in Group Sequence Policy Optimization, yields concrete engineering advantages:

1. **Tolerance to precision mismatches** between training and inference engines, because the length‑normalized sequence ratio smooths out per‑token numerical noise.  
2. **Direct reuse of inference‑engine likelihoods**, eliminating the need for costly recomputation of token probabilities under the training engine.  
3. **Simplified handling of partial rollouts and multi‑turn RL**, as a single scalar per sampled dialogue suffices for importance correction.  
4. **Reduced memory consumption and network traffic**, enabling larger batch sizes, higher expert counts in MoE models, and overall more efficient utilization of hardware.

These practical implications have been borne out in the empirical evaluations presented in the paper: GSPO trains stably at scale, converges without auxiliary stabilization tricks such as Routing Replay, and delivers higher sample efficiency despite clipping a larger fraction of tokens.  By aligning the unit of optimization (the sequence) with the unit of reward (also the sequence), GSPO not only resolves the statistical shortcomings of prior methods but also streamlines the engineering pipeline required for large‑scale RL of language models.

## Recap of GSPO’s Theoretical and Empirical Advantages

### Sequence‑Level Importance Sampling

The core theoretical contribution of GSPO is the **sequence‑level importance ratio**
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
        =\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Unlike the token‑level ratios used in PPO and GRPO, this quantity respects the **principle of importance sampling**: it is a bona‑fide weight that corrects the mismatch between the distribution of responses generated by the behavior policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the target policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). Because the weight aggregates over all tokens in a response, it reduces variance dramatically, especially for long sequences where the product of many token‑level weights would otherwise explode or collapse.

### Matching Unit of Reward and Unit of Optimization

RL for language models typically provides a **single scalar reward** \(r(x,y)\) that evaluates an entire query–response pair. GSPO aligns the optimization objective with this granularity by using a **group‑based advantage**
\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G},
\]
which is shared across all tokens of a response. The final GSPO objective
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\right]
\]
therefore directly couples the **sequence‑level likelihood correction** with the **sequence‑level reward signal**.

### Gradient Stability

The gradient of GSPO,
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
shows that **all tokens within a response receive the same scalar weight** \(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\). By contrast, GRPO multiplies each token’s log‑likelihood gradient by a distinct token‑level ratio
\(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\),
which varies wildly across positions and can become extreme when experts in a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model shift. This uniform weighting eliminates the unstable amplification of noise observed in GRPO, as demonstrated empirically by the dramatically lower variance of gradient estimates and the near‑absence of catastrophic model collapse.

### Empirical Evidence

Experimental results confirm the theoretical predictions:

* **Training stability**: Across multiple large‑scale RL runs (including MoE models with 48 layers), GSPO never exhibited the irreversible divergence that plagued GRPO.
* **Sample efficiency**: Despite clipping a far larger fraction of tokens (by two orders of magnitude), GSPO achieved higher reward growth per unit of compute, indicating that the retained sequence‑level signal is far more informative than the noisy token‑level signal.
* **MoE compatibility**: GSPO removes the need for *Routing Replay*, a memory‑intensive workaround previously required to align experts between old and new policies. By basing importance weights on the **aggregate likelihood**, GSPO tolerates the natural expert‑activation volatility of sparse models.
* **Infrastructure simplification**: Because only sequence‑level likelihoods are needed, the algorithm can directly consume probabilities returned by inference‑optimized engines (e.g., vLLM), avoiding costly recomputation on the training engine and simplifying pipelines for partial rollouts or multi‑turn dialogues.

Together, these findings validate that **GSPO is both theoretically sound and practically superior** for RL finetuning of the largest language models currently available.

## Implications for Scaling RL with Ever Larger Language Models

### From Dense to Sparse Architectures

As transformer models scale beyond hundreds of billions of parameters, **sparsity becomes indispensable**; MoE layers enable a linear increase in capacity at sub‑linear computational cost. However, sparsity introduces *routing stochasticity*: the set of experts activated for a token can change dramatically after each gradient step. Token‑level importance ratios are therefore highly unstable, making algorithms like GRPO fragile. By focusing on the **sequence likelihood**, GSPO sidesteps this fragility; the aggregate probability remains a smooth function of the parameters even when individual expert assignments fluctuate. Consequently, GSPO opens the door to **stable RL training of arbitrarily large MoE models** without auxiliary tricks.

### Larger Batches and Longer Contexts

Scaling RL also implies **larger rollout batches** (to keep GPUs or TPUs saturated) and **longer generations** (to capture multi‑step reasoning). The variance reduction inherent in sequence‑level importance sampling scales favorably: the variance of a product of many independent ratios is bounded by the variance of their logarithms, which averaging across tokens (as performed in the exponentiation step) mitigates growth with length. This property allows practitioners to increase context windows and batch sizes without incurring the exponential instability observed in token‑level methods.

### Simpler, More General RL Pipelines

GSPO’s reliance on a single scalar importance weight per response simplifies the **engineering stack**:

* **No value network** is needed, eliminating the memory overhead of a second large transformer.
* **No per‑token routing bookkeeping** is required, reducing inter‑node communication in distributed settings.
* **Inference‑compatible likelihoods** mean that the same serving code can be re‑used for training, enabling low‑latency “online RL” scenarios where fresh user interactions are incorporated on the fly.

These simplifications are not merely convenience; they directly lower the barrier to *continuous RL* for massive language models, a key requirement for future systems that must adapt to evolving user needs and external data streams.

## Open Questions and Research Directions

While GSPO resolves several long‑standing challenges, its design also surfaces new avenues for investigation.

### Adaptive Clipping Strategies

GSPO employs a **fixed clipping interval** \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) on the sequence‑level importance ratio. In practice, the optimal \(\varepsilon\) may depend on:

* **Response length** – longer sequences experience more cumulative likelihood drift.
* **Reward distribution** – higher variance in advantages could demand tighter or looser clipping.
* **Model architecture** – MoE vs. dense models may exhibit different sensitivity.

Designing a **data‑driven adaptive clipping schedule**, perhaps based on the empirical quantiles of \(\{s_i(\theta)\}\) within each mini‑batch, could further improve stability and sample efficiency. Deriving theoretical guarantees for such adaptive schemes remains an open problem.

### Curriculum of Queries

The RL objective hinges on the **query distribution** \(\mathcal{D}\). The current work assumes a static set of queries that is periodically refreshed. A **curriculum learning** approach—starting with short, easy prompts and progressively introducing longer, more complex ones—might facilitate smoother policy updates, particularly in the early stages where the policy is far from optimal. Formalizing the interaction between curriculum scheduling and the sequence‑level importance ratio could yield systematic improvements.

### Hybrid Token‑/Sequence‑Level Objectives

Although GSPO’s sequence‑level formulation aligns naturally with scalar rewards, there are scenarios where **fine‑grained feedback** is available (e.g., token‑level supervision from a teacher model or human annotations indicating the correctness of intermediate steps). A **hybrid objective** could blend the stability of sequence‑level weighting with the specificity of token‑level advantages:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{hybrid}} = \lambda\,\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} + (1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{J}_{\text{token}},
\]
where \(\mathcal{J}_{\text{token}}\) could be a PPO‑style term using a separate, perhaps more reliable, value estimator. Determining the optimal mixing coefficient \(\lambda\) and ensuring that the combined gradient does not re‑introduce high variance is an open research direction.

### Multi‑Turn and Partial‑Rollout Settings

GSPO‑token was introduced as a token‑level variant that preserves the sequence‑level importance ratio while permitting per‑token advantage customization. Extending this idea to **multi‑turn dialogues**—where each turn incurs its own reward and the overall conversation length varies—poses challenges in defining consistent importance ratios across turns. One possible avenue is to treat each turn as a separate “mini‑sequence” and apply **turn‑level clipping** before aggregating across the dialogue. Formalizing this and evaluating its impact on conversational agents is an exciting next step.

### Theoretical Guarantees for Large‑Scale Off‑Policy RL

The derivations in the paper assume that the **expectations are taken over the true behavior distribution** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). In practice, we work with finite mini‑batches and stochastic estimators. Providing **finite‑sample convergence guarantees** for GSPO—especially under the high‑dimensional, non‑convex landscape of LLMs—would deepen our understanding of why the algorithm remains stable where GRPO fails.

## Vision for Next‑Generation RL‑Trained Language Models

The introduction of GSPO reshapes the landscape of RL for language models in three interrelated ways:

1. **Scale‑First Design**: Future models can be engineered with **massive MoE capacity** from the outset, confident that RL fine‑tuning will remain stable. This removes a major bottleneck that previously forced practitioners to rely on dense architectures or to curtail model depth.

2. **Continuous Adaptation**: Because GSPO tolerates **inference‑engine likelihoods** and does not require a value network, it lends itself naturally to **online RL pipelines** where new user interactions are incorporated in near‑real time. Such systems could continuously refine their policies, delivering ever‑improving performance without the heavy offline retraining cycles that dominate current practice.

3. **Modular Reward Engineering**: With a stable optimization backbone, researchers can invest more effort into **designing richer reward functions**—for example, combining task‑specific correctness signals with alignment‑oriented preferences (e.g., helpfulness, factuality). Hybrid methods that blend sequence‑level RL with supervised fine‑tuning could become standard, enabling models that excel both at objective task performance and at adhering to human values.

In this envisioned future, a **large‑scale RL training loop** would look as follows:

* A **distributed rollout farm** generates batches of query–response pairs using the latest policy.
* An **inference‑optimized likelihood service** returns \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\) for each response, possibly enriched with token‑level confidences for downstream hybrid objectives.
* A **reward server** evaluates each response using a suite of modular evaluators (e.g., correctness judges, safety filters, user preference models) and returns a scalar (or structured) reward.
* The **GSPO optimizer** computes the sequence‑level importance ratios, applies adaptive clipping, and updates the policy parameters, all while preserving the **MoE routing dynamics** inherent to the model.

Such a pipeline would be **hardware‑agnostic**, able to exploit both dense accelerator clusters and sparsity‑aware architectures, and would **scale gracefully** as models grow further in size and as the volume of RL data expands.

### Concluding Remarks

Group Sequence Policy Optimization reframes the interaction between **importance sampling**, **reward attribution**, and **gradient estimation** for language‑model RL. By elevating the unit of optimization from individual tokens to entire sequences, GSPO resolves the high‑variance pitfalls of earlier token‑level methods, stabilizes training of sparse MoE models, and simplifies the engineering stack required for large‑scale RL. The empirical gains reported—steady reward growth, higher sample efficiency, and elimination of ad‑hoc tricks such as Routing Replay—demonstrate that the algorithm is ready for production use in the most demanding language‑model training regimes.

Nevertheless, the journey is far from over. Adaptive clipping, curriculum learning, hybrid objectives, and principled theoretical analyses constitute a rich research agenda that will refine and extend the benefits of GSPO. As the community tackles these challenges, we anticipate a new generation of RL‑trained language models that are **larger, more capable, and more reliably aligned**, opening unprecedented possibilities for AI‑assisted reasoning, coding, and scientific discovery.
