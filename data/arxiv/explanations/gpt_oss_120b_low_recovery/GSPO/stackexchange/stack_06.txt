\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does expert‑activation volatility in MoE models break GRPO’s token‑level importance weights?
Question:
The authors state that after each update about 10 % of experts activated for a token change, causing large fluctuations in \( w_{i,t}(θ) \). Could someone explain mechanistically why a change in routing leads to an unstable importance ratio, and why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is immune to this effect?
Answer:
In a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) language model each token is processed by a *routing* function that selects a small subset of experts (the “activated experts”) from a large pool.  
During RL training the policy is updated after every minibatch, i.e. we replace the old parameters $ \theta_{\text{old}} $ by new parameters $ \theta $. The routing function depends on the current parameters (it uses the same hidden‑state that drives the token‑level logits), so after an update the set of experts that would fire for a given token can change.

### Why a change in routing makes $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $ volatile

The token‑level importance weight used by GRPO is  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Both the numerator and denominator are **conditional probabilities of a single token** given the same context, but they are computed by *different* expert subnetworks because the routing decisions differ between $ \theta_{\text{old}} $ and $ \theta $.  

When the routing changes, the forward‑pass that yields $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ may now involve completely different weight matrices and activation patterns. Even if the logits change only slightly, the *ratio* of two probabilities that are produced by distinct expert mixtures can swing dramatically: a token that was previously handled by a strong expert may now be handed to a weaker one, making the denominator large and the numerator small, or vice‑versa.  

Empirically the paper observes that about 10 % of the experts used for a token differ after each update; this is enough to cause **large fluctuations** in the per‑token ratios $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $. Since GRPO multiplies each token’s log‑gradient by its own $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $, these noisy, sometimes very large, scalars amplify variance in the gradient estimate. The effect compounds over long sequences because the variance accumulates across all token positions, which ultimately destabilises training and can lead to catastrophic collapse.

### Why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is immune

GSPO replaces the token‑wise weight with a **sequence‑level importance ratio**

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
= \exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
$$

Two facts make this ratio robust to routing volatility:

1. **Averaging over tokens** – The logarithms of the per‑token ratios are summed and then divided by the sequence length. Even if a few tokens experience abrupt routing changes, their contribution is diluted by the many tokens whose routing remains unchanged or changes only mildly. The law of large numbers therefore reduces variance: the sequence‑level weight is effectively an average of many noisy token ratios.

2. **Length normalisation** – By raising the product of probabilities to the power $1/|y_i|$ (or equivalently averaging log‑probabilities), GSPO keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a narrow numerical band irrespective of how long the response is. This prevents long sequences from exploding the importance weight, something that would happen if one multiplied raw token ratios without normalisation.

Because GSPO’s objective only needs **the overall likelihood of the whole response**, it does not require matching which experts were active for each individual token. The sequence likelihood is computed by summing log‑probabilities across tokens; even if some tokens pass through different expert subsets after an update, their log‑probabilities still contribute additively to the same scalar. Consequently, a 10 % change in expert activation does not translate into a large swing in $s_i(\theta)$; the ratio remains relatively stable and can be clipped reliably.

### In short

* In GRPO each token carries its own importance weight; when routing changes, those weights fluctuate because they compare two *different* expert mixtures.
* In GSPO we first aggregate all token probabilities into a single sequence probability, then normalise by length. The aggregation averages out the sporadic routing differences, so the resulting importance ratio is much less sensitive to which experts fired for any single token.

This explains mechanistically why expert‑activation volatility breaks GRPO’s token‑level importance weights while GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio remains stable.
