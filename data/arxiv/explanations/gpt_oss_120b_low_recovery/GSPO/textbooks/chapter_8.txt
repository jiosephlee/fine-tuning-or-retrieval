\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 8: # GSPO‑Token: Fine‑Grained Advantage Adjustment

In reinforcement learning for large language models, the reward is typically assigned to an entire generated response.  Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) therefore performs off‑policy correction and clipping at the sequence level, yielding a stable and efficient training algorithm.  Nevertheless, certain downstream scenarios—most notably multi‑turn dialogues or tasks that demand token‑wise feedback—benefit from a finer granularity of advantage estimation.  This chapter introduces **GSPO‑Token**, the token‑level variant of GSPO, explains why it is useful, derives its mathematical form, and discusses its relationship to the original GSPO objective.  We also provide practical guidelines for deciding when to adopt GSPO‑Token versus staying with the simpler sequence‑level formulation.

---

## Motivation for Token‑wise Advantage in Multi‑Turn Settings

When a language model participates in a multi‑turn interaction—such as a conversational assistant, a step‑by‑step problem‑solving agent, or an iterative code generation system—the overall quality of the dialogue is not solely determined by the final utterance.  Instead, **intermediate turns** can be scored separately, and even **individual tokens** within a turn may carry distinct informational or strategic value.  For example:

* In a tutoring dialogue, a model might be praised for providing a correct hint early in a response but penalized for later ambiguous phrasing.
* In a code‑generation pipeline, the first few lines may compile correctly (high reward), while a later token introduces a syntax error (low reward).
* In a reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) loop where a human annotator supplies token‑level preferences, the algorithm must incorporate those fine‑grained signals.

If the learning algorithm aggregates all token scores into a single scalar advantage for the whole sequence—as GSPO does—information about *where* the model performed well or poorly is discarded.  Consequently, gradient updates cannot directly reinforce the particular tokens that contributed positively or suppress the harmful ones.  This motivates a **token‑wise advantage**: each token receives its own advantage estimate \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\), allowing the learner to adjust the policy with higher fidelity to the underlying feedback.

The token‑wise formulation must still preserve the central insight of GSPO: importance sampling should be performed at the **sequence** level, because the reward is defined for an entire response.  GSPO‑Token therefore combines a sequence‑level importance ratio (which correctly rescales the off‑policy sample) with token‑level advantage weights, yielding a hybrid objective that is both statistically sound and capable of fine‑grained credit assignment.

---

## Definition of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) with Gradient Stop (sg)

GSPO‑Token introduces a per‑token importance factor \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) that is *derived* from the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) but applied at each token position.  The definition is:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[ s_i(\theta) \bigr] 
   \cdot 
   \frac{ \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl( y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t} \bigr) }
        { \operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[ \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl( y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t} \bigr) \bigr] } .
\tag{1}
\]

Here, \(\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]\) denotes the **stop‑gradient** operator (often called `detach` in deep‑learning libraries).  It returns the numerical value of its argument but treats it as a constant during back‑propagation, i.e., the gradient of \(\operatorname{sg}[z]\) with respect to any parameters is zero.  The purpose of this operator is twofold:

1. **Preserve the sequence‑level scaling** – The factor \(s_i(\theta)\) captures how the current policy’s overall likelihood for the entire response \(y_i\) deviates from the old policy.  By passing it through `sg`, we ensure that this scaling influences the loss value but does **not** induce additional gradient terms that would reintroduce token‑level importance weighting (the very source of instability in GRPO).

2. **Neutralize the ratio of token probabilities** – The fraction \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]}\) is numerically equal to 1 because the numerator and denominator are identical numbers; however, only the numerator participates in gradient computation.  Consequently, \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) inherits the gradient of the token log‑probability \(\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) while keeping the magnitude of the importance weight equal to the sequence‑level value \(s_i(\theta)\).

Because the numerator is differentiable and the denominator is detached, the overall factor simplifies numerically to

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \quad\text{(numerically)},
\]

yet its gradient behavior differs, as shown in the derivations below.

---

## Objective and Gradient of GSPO‑Token

The **GSPO‑Token** objective mirrors the clipping structure of GSPO but iterates over tokens:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}(\theta) 
= \; &\mathbb{E}_{\,x \sim \mathcal{D},\; \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x)} 
\Biggl[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} 
\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\\
& \qquad
\min\!\Bigl( \; s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}, 
\; \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl( s_{i,t}(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon \bigr)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t} \Bigr) 
\Biggr] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

* \(G\) is the group size, i.e., the number of responses generated for each query \(x\).
* \(|y_i|\) denotes the length (in tokens) of response \(y_i\).
* \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) is the **token‑wise advantage**, which can be computed by normalizing token‑level rewards or by any other advantage‑estimation technique appropriate for the task.
* \(\varepsilon\) is the usual clipping radius; typical values are on the order of \(10^{-4}\) for sequence‑level ratios, but the same magnitude applies here because \(s_{i,t}\) inherits the scale of \(s_i\).

### Gradient Derivation

To understand the learning signal, we differentiate (2) with respect to \(\theta\).  For clarity, we drop the clipping term in the derivation (it only caps the magnitude of the weight but does not affect the analytic form of the gradient).  The expected loss inside the expectation can be written as

\[
L = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}.
\]

Because \(s_{i,t}(\theta) = \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]}\), the only \(\theta\)‑dependent part in the gradient is the numerator of the token probability.  Using the identity \(\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta} = \pi_{\theta}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\), we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta} L
&= \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \,
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
   \frac{ \nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot) }
        { \operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)] } \\[4pt]
&= \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   s_i(\theta)\,
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
   \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Taking the expectation over the rollout distribution yields the full gradient of the GSPO‑Token objective:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}(\theta) 
=&\;
\mathbb{E}_{\,x,\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Biggl[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
s_i(\theta)\,
\widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\Biggr] .
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{4}
\]

Comparing (4) with the gradient of the original GSPO (which uses a uniform advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) for all tokens in a response) reveals that **the only difference lies in the advantage term**.  The importance weight \(s_i(\theta)\) is identical in both, and the factor \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\) normalizes for sequence length.  Consequently, GSPO‑Token retains the stable off‑policy correction of GSPO while enabling a per‑token scaling of the gradient.

---

## Equivalence to GSPO under Uniform Advantage

If the token‑wise advantages are **identical across all positions** within a response, i.e.,

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,1} = \widehat{A}_{i,2} = \dots = \widehat{A}_{i,|y_i|} \; \triangleq\; \widehat{A}_i,
\]

then the inner sum in (4) simplifies:

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)
= \widehat{A}_i \,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
\]

Because the definition of the sequence‑level importance ratio already includes the average over tokens,

\[
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\right),
\]

the product \(s_i(\theta)\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\cdot)\) is precisely the gradient term appearing in the GSPO derivation (Equation (3) of the original paper).  Therefore, **when advantages are uniform, GSPO‑Token reduces exactly to GSPO**—both the objective value and its gradient coincide.  This formal equivalence confirms that GSPO‑Token is a *strict generalization* of GSPO, extending it without altering its behavior in the common case where only a single scalar advantage per response is available.

## Practical Tips for Choosing Between GSPO and GSPO‑Token

| Situation | Preferred Variant | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Standard RLHF** where a single reward (e.g., from a verifier model) is computed for the whole response. | **GSPO** | Simpler implementation, lower per‑token overhead, and identical performance because the advantage is uniform. |
| **Multi‑turn dialogues** with per‑turn rewards that can be mapped to each token (e.g., turn‑level scores, token‑level human preferences). | **GSPO‑Token** | Allows distinct \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) per token, enabling the model to reinforce good tokens and suppress bad ones within the same response. |
| **Sparse‑reward tasks** where only a few tokens receive non‑zero feedback (e.g., syntax‑error detection, keyphrase identification). | **GSPO‑Token** | The zero‑advantage tokens contribute nothing to the gradient, effectively focusing learning on the informative positions. |
| **Memory‑constrained training** (very long sequences, extremely large batch sizes). | **GSPO** | Avoids storing per‑token advantage vectors; sequence‑level processing reduces auxiliary memory. |
| **Experimentation / prototyping** when the advantage estimation pipeline is still under development. | **GSPO** first, then **GSPO‑Token** after reliable token‑wise metrics are available. | Starting with GSPO provides a stable baseline; once token‑level signals are trustworthy, switch to GSPO‑Token for finer control. |
| **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models** where routing volatility makes token‑level importance ratios noisy. | **GSPO** (or GSPO‑Token, but note that the importance factor remains sequence‑level, so the volatility issue is mitigated). | Both variants rely on the sequence‑level ratio \(s_i(\theta)\), preserving the MoE stability advantage of GSPO. |

**Implementation checklist** for GSPO‑Token:

1. **Generate a group** of \(G\) responses per query using the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. **Compute sequence‑level importance ratios** \(s_i(\theta)\) as in GSPO (including length‑normalization).
3. **Detach** \(s_i(\theta)\) from the computational graph (`sg`) before multiplying by token‑level gradients.
4. **Obtain token‑wise advantages** \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\).  If only a per‑turn reward \(r(x, y_i)\) is available, a simple proxy is to broadcast the normalized advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) to every token; otherwise, use task‑specific annotations.
5. **Apply clipping** on the detached scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) (same bounds as for GSPO).  Because the clipping range is determined by the sequence‑level ratio, the fraction of clipped samples typically remains low.
6. **Back‑propagate** the loss; thanks to the stop‑gradient, the only gradients that flow are those of the token log‑likelihoods, scaled by the (detached) sequence importance weight and the token advantage.

By following these steps, practitioners can reap the benefits of fine‑grained advantage adjustment while preserving the stability guarantees that arise from sequence‑level importance sampling.

---

### Summary

GSPO‑Token extends the robust sequence‑level off‑policy correction of GSPO to settings where **token‑wise advantage information** is available and desirable.  The key technical device—**gradient stopping** on the sequence importance ratio—ensures that the algorithm does not revert to the high‑variance token‑level importance weights that plagued earlier methods such as GRPO.  When token‑level rewards are uniform, GSPO‑Token collapses exactly to GSPO, confirming that the former is a true generalization rather than a competing alternative.  Practitioners should adopt GSPO‑Token in multi‑turn or token‑annotated RL scenarios, while retaining the simpler GSPO formulation for standard single‑reward settings or when computational resources are limited.  This flexibility enables researchers to tailor reinforcement learning pipelines for large language models to the granularity of feedback that their particular application can provide.
