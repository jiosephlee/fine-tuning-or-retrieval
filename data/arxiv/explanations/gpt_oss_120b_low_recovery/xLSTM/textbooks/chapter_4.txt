\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 4: **Exponential Gating: Theory and Stabilization**

The classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) cell relies on *sigmoid* activations for its input, forget, and output gates.  While sigmoid gates are bounded between 0 and 1, this boundedness hampers two important capabilities that modern language models demand.  First, a sigmoid‑gated cell cannot *revise* a storage decision once it has been made: if a large value was written to the cell early in a sequence, the forget gate can only decay it multiplicatively, never replace it with a larger contribution without a proportional increase in the input gate.  Second, the scalar nature of the LSTM cell state limits the amount of information that can be stored, especially for rare tokens that require precise, high‑dimensional encoding.

The **exponential gate** resolves both shortcomings.  By allowing the gate value to grow without an a‑priori upper bound, the model can *overwrite* a previously stored entry with a much larger one, and it can amplify the contribution of a rare token through a larger input gate.  However, naïvely using the exponential function $\exp(\cdot)$ creates numerical instability: exponentials quickly overflow to infinity, and gradients can explode.  The xLSTM framework therefore pairs exponential gating with two auxiliary states—a **normalizer** $n_t$ and a **stabilizer** $m_t$—that keep the forward and backward passes well‑behaved.

Below we develop the theory behind exponential gating, derive the normalizer and stabilizer updates, and sketch why these modifications preserve the original learning dynamics while enabling the aforementioned capabilities.

---

### Motivation for Exponential Gates

Consider the scalar LSTM update  
\[
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t,
\qquad
f_t,i_t \in (0,1),
\]
where $f_t$ (forget) and $i_t$ (input) are sigmoid‑activated.  Because $i_t\le 1$, the magnitude of any newly written content $z_t$ cannot exceed the raw input value; similarly, $f_t$ can only attenuate the existing memory.  When a sequence contains a *better* value later (e.g. a nearer neighbour in a search task), the cell is forced to *gradually* forget the old content, which slows learning and limits accuracy.

If we replace the sigmoid with an exponential,
\[
i_t = \exp (\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp (\tilde{f}_t) \quad\text{or}\quad \sigma(\tilde{f}_t),
\]
the gate can become arbitrarily large.  In the forward pass a large $i_t$ can dominate the additive term, instantly overwriting $c_{t-1}$.  Likewise a large $f_t$ can *boost* the retained memory rather than merely preserving it, granting the cell the flexibility to *revise* stored information in a single step.

This flexibility is precisely what is needed for (i) **revision of storage decisions** (the nearest‑neighbor experiment) and (ii) **enhanced capacity** (rare‑token prediction).  Yet, without safeguards, $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ can become astronomically large, causing overflow in floating‑point arithmetic and destroying gradient flow.  The normalizer and stabilizer states are introduced to tame these explosions.

---

### Mathematical Form of Exponential Activation

In the *sLSTM* variant, the gate pre‑activations $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ are computed exactly as in a traditional LSTM:
\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_{i} \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_{i},
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{f}\,\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_{f} \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_{f},
\]
but the *gate values* become
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\;\; \text{or}\;\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]
The exponential gate is thus a simple re‑parameterization of the sigmoid gate: the logarithm of $i_t$ is exactly the original pre‑activation $\tilde{i}_t$, and similarly for the exponential forget gate.  This re‑parameterization does not alter the functional form of the cell update; it merely expands the admissible range of gate values.

For the *mLSTM* (matrix‑memory) variant, the same exponential activation is used for both input and forget gates, while the output gate remains sigmoid:
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t) \;\; \text{or}\;\; \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t).
\]

---

### Normaliser State \(n_t\) and Its Role in Scaling Hidden Output

Because exponential gates can increase without bound, the raw cell state $c_t$ can also grow without bound, rendering the hidden output $h_t$ unsuitable for downstream layers.  The *sLSTM* introduces a *normalizer* $n_t$ that tracks the cumulative “mass” contributed by the gates:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
n_t &= f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t .
\end{aligned}}
\]
Intuitively, $n_t$ is the denominator of a weighted average: the cell state $c_t$ accumulates scaled inputs $i_t z_t$, while $n_t$ accumulates the same scaling factors without the input vectors.  The hidden state is then formed by dividing the cell state by the normalizer:
\[
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad
h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t .
\]
This division ensures that, regardless of how large the gates become, the hidden representation remains *scale‑invariant*: a uniformly large scaling of both numerator and denominator cancels out.  Consequently, the network can employ unbounded gates for rapid revision while preserving a numerically stable hidden activation.

In the *mLSTM* the normalizer is vector‑valued:
\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{k}_t,
\]
where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the *key* vector derived from the input.  The hidden state is normalized by the scalar
\[
\max\!\bigl\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\bigr\},
\]
with $\mathbf{q}_t$ the query vector.  This prevents division by numbers close to zero and caps the denominator at 1, ensuring a well‑conditioned hidden output.

---

### Stabilizer State \(m_t\) and Log‑Space Re‑Parameterization

Even with a normalizer, intermediate values $i_t$ and $f_t$ may overflow before being combined with $n_t$ or $c_t$.  The *stabilizer* state $m_t$ stores, in log‑space, the **maximum** cumulative log‑gate observed so far:
\[
\boxed{
m_t = \max\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr).
}
\]
The recursion tracks the largest exponent that has contributed to the cell, akin to a running maximum of log‑weights.  Using $m_t$, the gates are *renormalized*:
\[
\begin{aligned}
i'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log i_t - m_t\bigr) 
      = \exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\bigr), \\
f'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Both $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ lie in the interval $(0,1]$ by construction because the subtraction of the maximal log‑value guarantees that the exponent is non‑positive.  Importantly, the *product* $f'_t i'_t$ equals the original $f_t i_t$ up to a constant factor that cancels when multiplied across time steps.  Hence the *relative* contribution of each gate to the cell state is unchanged.

The stabilizer therefore re‑expresses the exponential gates in a **log‑space** representation, where addition replaces multiplication.  This mirrors the classic technique of performing computations in log‑space to avoid overflow in probabilistic models.  In the forward pass, the actual gate values used are the stabilized $i'_t$ and $f'_t$, while the *raw* exponentials are retained implicitly in $m_t$ for gradient computation.

---

### Proof Sketch That Stabilized Gates Preserve Gradients

We outline why substituting $(i_t,f_t)$ with $(i'_t,f'_t)$ does not alter the loss gradient with respect to any parameter $\theta$ that influences the pre‑activations $\tilde{i}_t$ or $\tilde{f}_t$.

1. **Forward Equivalence.**  By construction,
   \[
   i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}, \qquad
   f_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t}\ \text{or}\ \sigma(\tilde{f}_t).
   \]
   The stabilizer defines
   \[
   i'_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t - m_t}, \qquad
   f'_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1} - m_t}.
   \]
   Multiplying $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ yields
   \[
   i'_t f'_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t + \tilde{f}_t - m_t + m_{t-1} - m_t}
            = e^{\tilde{i}_t + \tilde{f}_t } e^{m_{t-1} - 2m_t}.
   \]
   The factor $e^{m_{t-1} - 2m_t}$ is a *global* scalar that is *identical* for all terms that share the same time index because $m_t$ is defined as the maximum of the two arguments.  When the cell update
   \[
   c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t
   \]
   is unrolled, the global scalar cancels across the additive terms, leaving exactly the same $c_t$ as obtained with the raw gates.

2. **Derivative Flow.**  The derivative of $i'_t$ w.r.t. $\tilde{i}_t$ is
   \[
   \frac{\partial i'_t}{\partial \tilde{i}_t}
   = i'_t
   = e^{\tilde{i}_t - m_t}.
   \]
   The derivative of $m_t$ w.r.t. $\tilde{i}_t$ is either $0$ (if $m_t$ came from $\log f_t + m_{t-1}$) or $1$ (if $m_t = \log i_t$).  In either case, the chain rule yields
   \[
   \frac{\partial i'_t}{\partial \tilde{i}_t}
   = i'_t - i'_t \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{m_t = \log i_t\}},
   \]
   which matches the derivative of the original $i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}$ up to the same global scaling factor that appeared in the forward pass.  Because the loss is linear in $c_t$, these scaling factors cancel when summing over time, leaving the *total* gradient $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta$ unchanged.

3. **Inductive Argument.**  Assuming the gradient equivalence holds up to step $t-1$, the recurrence for $c_t$ is linear in $i'_t$ and $f'_t$.  The stabilizer only introduces a *constant* multiplicative factor that is independent of the downstream loss (it does not depend on $z_t$ or $c_{t-1}$).  Hence the gradient contributions from step $t$ retain the same proportion as in the un‑stabilized case.

Thus, the stabilizer re‑parameterization is *gradient‑preserving*: training dynamics are identical, while the forward pass remains numerically safe.

---

### Practical Considerations

#### Initialization

Since exponential gates can explode, careful initialization of the pre‑activations is crucial.  In practice the biases $b_i$ and $b_f$ are set to small negative values (e.g. $-1$) so that $\exp(\tilde{i}_0)$ and $\exp(\tilde{f}_0)$ start near 1, mimicking the behavior of a conventional LSTM at the start of training.  The weight matrices are initialized with standard schemes (e.g., Xavier or Kaiming), and LayerNorm is applied before key/value projections in the mLSTM to enforce zero‑mean inputs, which improves the stability of the covariance update.

#### Overflow Handling

Even with the stabilizer, a single time step could produce a gate with $\tilde{i}_t$ far larger than typical floating‑point ranges.  The max‑operation in $m_t$ prevents this by *clipping* the log‑gate: the exponential is never computed directly; instead $i'_t$ is formed by subtracting $m_t$, guaranteeing that the exponent never exceeds zero.  This log‑space trick eliminates overflow without sacrificing the expressive power of the exponential gate.

#### Implementation Tips

* **Scan‑based computation.**  The recursions for $c_t$, $n_t$, and $m_t$ are simple *prefix scans*.  On GPUs these can be implemented efficiently using thrust‑like primitives or custom CUDA kernels, yielding a modest overhead compared to the original LSTM.
* **Mixed‑precision.**  When training with half‑precision (FP16), the stabilizer becomes even more important because the dynamic range is reduced.  The log‑space representation fits naturally in FP16, and the division by $n_t$ (or the max‑norm denominator in mLSTM) guards against underflow.
* **Choice of forget gate.**  The paper reports empirical equivalence between sigmoid and exponential forget gates; however, exponential forget gates often lead to faster convergence when the task requires aggressive memory revision.

---

In summary, **exponential gating** augments the traditional LSTM with the ability to instantly revise stored information and to allocate greater capacity to rare events.  The accompanying **normalizer** guarantees that hidden activations stay bounded, while the **stabilizer** re‑expresses gate values in log‑space, eliminating overflow and preserving gradients.  Together, these mechanisms constitute the core theoretical contribution of the xLSTM family and enable the empirical successes reported throughout the paper.
