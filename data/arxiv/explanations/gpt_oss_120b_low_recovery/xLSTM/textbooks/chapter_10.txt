\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 10: # Synthetic Benchmarks: Verifying Core Capabilities

Understanding the benefits of the extensions introduced in the Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM) family requires careful isolation of each new component.  In the original paper a suite of synthetic experiments was designed expressly for this purpose.  By constructing tasks that stress a single functional aspect—revision of stored information, storage capacity, or the ability to track state over long horizons—we can observe how the proposed exponential gating, the new scalar‑mixing sLSTM, and the matrix‑memory mLSTM each contribute to performance.  This chapter details those benchmarks, explains the experimental setup, and interprets the results, highlighting why conventional Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs) struggle when memory mixing is absent.

---

## Formal‑Language / Parity and State‑Tracking Tasks

### 1. Task definition

Formal‑language benchmarks are classic probes of a model’s ability to implement deterministic finite‑state or push‑down automata.  The parity task, for instance, presents a binary sequence \(x_1,\dots,x_T\in\{0,1\}\) and asks the model to output the parity (i.e. the XOR) of all bits seen so far at each time step.  Formally the target hidden state satisfies
\[
y_t = \bigoplus_{k=1}^{t} x_k .
\]
More generally, a family of regular and context‑free grammars is generated (e.g., Dyck‑2, copy, reversal) and the model must predict the next symbol given the entire prefix.

### 2. Why this isolates memory mixing

All these languages require *state tracking*: the model must remember an abstract property of the prefix (parity, nesting depth, etc.) that cannot be retrieved from a single token alone.  In an LSTM‑style recurrence this property is stored in the cell state and continually updated, a process that relies on **hidden‑hidden connections** (the recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf{R}_{\ast}\) in the original formulation).  The sLSTM preserves these connections while switching the input and forget gates to exponential activations.  Consequently, the sLSTM can **mix** information across time steps more aggressively, revising its hidden representation as soon as new evidence arrives.

### 3. Experimental setup

The authors trained two‑block architectures (i.e., two residual blocks) for each method under identical hyper‑parameters: embedding dimension 1024, a context length of 512, and a training budget of 1 M tokens.  The models compared were:

- **xLSTM[0:1]** – only sLSTM blocks (scalar memory with exponential gating).
- **xLSTM[1:0]** – only mLSTM blocks (matrix memory, no mixing).
- **xLSTM[1:1]** – a hybrid of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks.
- **Transformer‑based LLMs** (e.g., Llama) and **SSM‑based models** (e.g., Mamba).
- **Pure LSTM** and **LSTM embedded in Transformer blocks**.

Performance was measured as *accuracy* on the task‑relevant positions, scaled from 0 (random) to 1 (perfect).

### 4. Results and interpretation

Figure 2 of the paper (reproduced in the appendix) shows that only the sLSTM‑containing variants (xLSTM[0:1] and xLSTM[1:1]) achieve near‑perfect accuracy on parity and other regular‑grammar tasks.  The pure mLSTM, despite its richer memory, fails because it lacks hidden‑hidden mixing; the recurrence reduces to a purely feed‑forward update of the matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\).  Transformers and SSMs also plateau at chance level, confirming prior theoretical work that these architectures cannot simulate a deterministic finite‑state machine without explicit recurrence.

The crucial takeaway is that **exponential gating together with memory mixing** endows the sLSTM with the ability to *revise* internal representations instantaneously, a capability absent in the matrix‑memory‑only mLSTM.

## Nearest‑Neighbor Search as a Revision Test

### 1. Problem statement

Given a sequence of vectors \(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and a **reference vector** \(\mathbf{r}\), the model must output the value attached to the *most similar* (nearest‑neighbor) vector in the sequence after the entire sequence has been processed.  Formally,
\[
\hat{y} = \text{value}\bigl(\arg\min_{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}} \|\mathbf{x}_t-\mathbf{r}\|_2 \bigr).
\]
During inference the model must continuously **revise** its stored candidate as it encounters vectors that are progressively closer to \(\mathbf{r}\).

### 2. Why this stresses revision

A standard LSTM with sigmoid forget and input gates can only *partially* overwrite its cell state.  If an early vector is stored and later a better candidate appears, the forget gate’s bounded nature (\(0\le f_t\le 1\)) prevents a rapid, full‑scale replacement.  The xLSTM’s exponential input gate \(i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)\) and optionally exponential forget gate enable a *multiplicative* increase in the effective learning rate, allowing the cell state to be overwritten almost instantaneously when a more similar vector is observed.

### 3. Experimental protocol

The authors generated synthetic datasets with varying dimensionalities and sequence lengths (up to 2048).  Models were trained to minimize mean‑squared error between the predicted value and the true nearest‑neighbor value.  Four model families were evaluated:

- **LSTM** (baseline).
- **xLSTM[0:1]** (sLSTM).
- **xLSTM[1:0]** (mLSTM).
- **xLSTM[1:1]** (hybrid).

Training used Adam with a learning rate of \(3\times10^{-4}\) and batch size 32.  Evaluation was performed on unseen sequences where the closest neighbor could appear at any position.

### 4. Findings

The left panel of Figure 1 in the paper shows that the vanilla LSTM’s mean‑squared error remains high even after extensive training.  By contrast, the sLSTM’s error drops near zero, confirming that **exponential gating** enables the model to *revise* its stored information effectively.  The mLSTM, lacking mixing, performs similarly to the LSTM on this task, further underscoring that the ability to overwrite hidden‑hidden dependencies is essential for revision.

## Multi‑Query Associative Recall and Memory Capacity

### 1. Task definition

The **Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR)** benchmark mimics a key‑value storage system.  For each time step \(t\), the model receives an input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) that is projected to a **key** \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and a **value** \(\mathbf{v}_t\).  After a series of such pairs, a query vector \(\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}\) is presented, and the model must retrieve the associated value:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{C}_{t+\tau}\,\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau},
\]
where \(\mathbf{C}\) is the internal memory matrix (for mLSTM) that aggregates outer products \(\mathbf{v}_k \mathbf{k}_k^{\!\top}\).

The difficulty is amplified by (i) increasing the number of key‑value pairs up to 256, and (ii) extending the context length to 2048, thereby demanding a **large storage capacity**.

### 2. Relevance to matrix memory

The mLSTM augments the scalar cell \(c_t\) to a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  Its update rule
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}
\]
is precisely the *covariance* or *outer‑product* rule used in associative memory literature.  This rule allows each key‑value pair to be stored in an *orthogonal* subspace, dramatically increasing the number of distinct items that can be remembered simultaneously.

### 3. Experimental design

Two‑block models were trained on MQAR with the same embedding size (1024) and optimizer settings as before.  The baseline set includes:

- **Transformer (Llama)**: uses self‑attention with quadratic complexity, which theoretically provides exponential memory capacity in the embedding dimension.
- **Mamba (SSM)**.
- **RWKV‑5/6 (RNN with linear attention)**.
- **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM) and **xLSTM[1:1]** (mixed).
- **xLSTM[0:1]** (pure sLSTM) for comparison.

Evaluation metric: *recall accuracy*—the proportion of queries for which the retrieved value matches the ground‑truth value within a small tolerance.

### 4. Observations

The right panel of Figure 1 shows that **xLSTM[1:1]** outperforms all non‑Transformer baselines across the entire range of key‑value pair counts.  Notably, the pure sLSTM (xLSTM[0:1]) does not suffer a capacity loss; instead it *augments* the matrix memory by providing a more expressive mixing of scalar cells across heads.  This synergy suggests that even when the primary storage mechanism is a matrix, having a scalar‑mixing component helps distribute information more uniformly, reducing interference between stored pairs.

Conversely, Transformers achieve the highest absolute recall when the embedding dimension is large, confirming the known exponential capacity of attention.  However, their quadratic time‑space cost makes them infeasible for very long contexts, whereas the mLSTM maintains linear complexity.

## Long Range Arena for Context‑Length Robustness

### 1. Benchmark overview

The **Long Range Arena (LRA)** is a collection of six tasks (ListOps, Retrieval, Text Classification, Pathfinder, Image‑based, and Path Retrieval) designed to test models on sequences up to 16 K tokens.  Each task stresses a different aspect of long‑range dependency handling: hierarchical composition, memory‑based retrieval, or spatial reasoning.

### 2. Expected behavior of xLSTM variants

- **sLSTM**: retains the recurrent hidden‑hidden pathways, guaranteeing linear time but with potential latency due to sequential processing.
- **mLSTM**: eliminates hidden‑hidden connections, enabling *parallel* computation across time steps (by scanning the matrix updates), yet preserving a powerful associative memory.
- Both variants incorporate **exponential gating**, which in theory should prevent gate saturation over long horizons and maintain stable gradients.

### 3. Evaluation protocol

All models were supplied with a fixed context length of 4096 during training, then evaluated across the full LRA spectrum.  Hyper‑parameters (learning rate, dropout, etc.) were kept identical to those used in the previous synthetic experiments.  Accuracy (or F1 where appropriate) was reported per task and averaged.

### 4. Results synthesis

Across all six LRA tasks, the xLSTM family consistently achieved the highest scores among recurrent and linear‑Transformer baselines, and approached the performance of state‑of‑the‑art Transformers that were heavily tuned for each task.  The **pre‑up‑projection** architecture for mLSTM proved especially beneficial on tasks that require massive capacity (e.g., Path Retrieval), whereas the **post‑up‑projection** design for sLSTM excelled on tasks emphasizing hierarchical composition (e.g., ListOps).  

These outcomes confirm that **exponential gating** stabilizes training over very long sequences, while **matrix memory** supplies the raw bandwidth needed to retain information across thousands of tokens.  The dual design—mixing scalar recurrence where fine‑grained control is needed and matrix‑based associative recall where bulk storage is required—provides a versatile toolkit for handling diverse long‑range dependencies.

## Why Transformers and SSMs Fail Without Mixing

Both Transformers and most SSMs operate primarily on **feed‑forward** transformations of token representations, with recurrence limited to *linear state updates* (SSMs) or self‑attention that aggregates all past tokens simultaneously.  While these mechanisms excel at capturing pairwise interactions, they lack **state‑tracking** in the sense required by formal‑language tasks:

1. **Absence of hidden‑hidden recurrent connections** – Without a recurrent hidden state that is updated *before* the next input is processed, the model cannot maintain a compact “memory flag” that evolves deterministically with each new symbol.  This is why the parity task, which requires a single bit of state, cannot be solved by a pure attention mechanism.

2. **Saturation of gating functions** – Transformers employ softmax attention, which normalizes across all tokens.  When a sequence grows, each individual contribution becomes diluted, preventing the network from sharply focusing on a single stored item.  In contrast, exponential gates in xLSTM can increase their magnitude arbitrarily, enabling a decisive overwrite when a more relevant token appears (as in Nearest‑Neighbor Search).

3. **Limited associative capacity in linear recurrences** – SSMs approximate the effect of convolutional kernels but do not embed a *covariance* style outer‑product update.  Consequently, they cannot store a large set of key‑value pairs with low interference, a deficiency that becomes evident on the MQAR benchmark.

Thus, the synthetic experiments collectively demonstrate that **memory mixing (hidden‑hidden connections)** and **exponential gating** are not merely cosmetic tweaks; they are fundamental mechanisms that endow recurrent models with the *algorithmic* capacity to emulate classical data structures (queues, associative memories) and deterministic automata—capabilities that pure attention‑based models lack.

--- 

### Summary

The synthetic benchmark suite serves a dual purpose: it validates each architectural innovation of xLSTM in isolation, and it provides a clear explanatory contrast to the limitations of Transformer‑based and SSM‑based language models.  Exponential gating dramatically improves revision speed, matrix memory expands storage capacity through an outer‑product associative rule, and the retention of hidden‑hidden mixing in the sLSTM supplies the essential state‑tracking ability for formal‑language tasks.  Together, these components enable xLSTM to scale to billions of parameters while preserving algorithmic expressiveness that rivals, and in several respects surpasses, the current state of the art.
