\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Motivation and Historical Context of Recurrent Models

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were among the earliest deep learning architectures designed to process sequential data. Their defining characteristic is the reuse of a hidden state $h_t$ across time steps, allowing information from earlier inputs to influence later predictions. While this recurrence gives RNNs a natural way to model temporal dependencies, the naive formulation quickly encounters a fundamental training obstacle: the vanishing (or exploding) gradient problem. Understanding this difficulty, and how the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was engineered to overcome it, is essential before we can appreciate why researchers have recently begun to revisit and scale LSTMs to the billions of parameters typical of modern large‑language models (LLMs).

## The Vanishing Gradient Problem in Vanilla RNNs

A vanilla RNN updates its hidden state according to a simple additive rule

\[
h_t \;=\; \sigma\!\bigl( W_x\,x_t \;+\; W_h\,h_{t-1} \;+\; b \bigr),
\]

where $x_t$ denotes the input at time $t$, $W_x$ and $W_h$ are weight matrices, $b$ is a bias vector, and $\sigma$ is a nonlinear activation such as $\tanh$ or $\text{ReLU}$. During training, the loss $\mathcal{L}$ is back‑propagated through time (BPTT), which requires computing the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to each hidden state:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_{t}} \;=\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_{T}} \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\partial h_{k+1}}{\partial h_{k}} .
\]

Each Jacobian factor $\frac{\partial h_{k+1}}{\partial h_{k}}$ contains the recurrent weight matrix $W_h$ multiplied by the derivative of $\sigma$. If the spectral radius of $W_h$ is smaller than one, repeated multiplication causes the product to shrink exponentially, and gradients vanish; if it exceeds one, gradients explode. In either extreme, learning long‑range dependencies becomes infeasible because the signal that would inform the model about events many steps in the past is either lost or swamped by noise. Early empirical work demonstrated that vanilla RNNs could not reliably learn dependencies beyond a handful of time steps, limiting their usefulness for language modeling, speech recognition, and many other sequence tasks.

## Constant Error Carousel and Gating in the Original LSTM

The LSTM architecture was introduced in the early 1990s precisely to address the vanishing gradient difficulty. Its core contribution is the **constant error carousel (CEC)**, a linear additive pathway for the cell state $c_t$ that allows gradients to flow unchanged across arbitrarily many time steps. The canonical LSTM update is expressed as

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t, \qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \, \psi(c_t) . \tag{1}
\]

Here:

* $c_t$ is the **cell state**, a memory vector that is updated additively rather than multiplicatively.  
* $f_t$, $i_t$, and $o_t$ are the **forget**, **input**, and **output** gates, respectively. Each gate is produced by a sigmoid activation $\sigma$, ensuring values lie in $[0,1]$.  
* $z_t$ is the **cell input**, typically a transformed version of the current input $x_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$.  
* $\psi$ is an activation (often $\tanh$) that squashes the raw cell state before it is gated by $o_t$ to produce the hidden output $h_t$.

Because the cell state is combined linearly via the forget and input gates, the derivative of $c_t$ with respect to $c_{t-1}$ is simply $f_t$. If $f_t$ is close to one, the gradient passes through unchanged, achieving the “constant error” property. The gates themselves are **learned** functions of the current input and previous hidden state:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{i}_t &= \mathbf{w}_i^\top x_t + r_i\,h_{t-1} + b_i, &\quad i_t &= \sigma(\tilde{i}_t),\\
\tilde{f}_t &= \mathbf{w}_f^\top x_t + r_f\,h_{t-1} + b_f, &\quad f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t),\\
\tilde{o}_t &= \mathbf{w}_o^\top x_t + r_o\,h_{t-1} + b_o, &\quad o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t),\\
\tilde{z}_t &= \mathbf{w}_z^\top x_t + r_z\,h_{t-1} + b_z, &\quad z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t),
\end{aligned}
\]

where $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are typically $\tanh$ and $r_\ast$, $b_\ast$ denote recurrent weights and biases, respectively. The **gating mechanism** allows the network to **learn when to write**, **when to erase**, and **when to read** from its internal memory, providing a flexible, data‑driven way of handling long‑range information.

## Successes of LSTMs Across Domains

The combination of CEC and gating made LSTMs remarkably robust for a wide range of sequence‑learning problems. In natural language processing, LSTMs powered early neural language models that could generate coherent paragraphs, synthesize handwriting, and translate between languages. They were the backbone of the first large‑scale language models, predating the Transformer revolution.

Beyond NLP, LSTMs found success in:

* **Computer vision** – generating image captions, synthesizing video frames, and learning visual‑language embeddings.  
* **Reinforcement learning** – providing memory for agents such as AlphaStar (StarCraft II) and OpenAI Five (Dota 2), where tracking game state over thousands of timesteps proved critical.  
* **Scientific modeling** – predicting hydrological processes, rainfall‑runoff dynamics, and even controlling magnetic fusion reactors, where the ability to retain physical variables over long horizons was essential.  
* **Code generation and program evaluation** – models that could read, reason about, and output source‑code snippets benefitted from the explicit memory cells that stored abstract program representations.

These achievements demonstrated that the LSTM’s inductive bias—explicit, learnable memory—was well‑matched to tasks where **state tracking** and **semantic abstraction** are crucial.

## Limitations of LSTMs That Motivated the Rise of Transformers

Despite their versatility, LSTMs exhibit three structural drawbacks that become especially pronounced at the scales required for modern LLMs:

1. **Irrevocable storage decisions.** The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous cell state is retained, but once a value is partially overwritten, the model cannot later “revise” that decision without revisiting the same time step. This limitation hampers tasks such as **nearest‑neighbor search**, where a more similar item later in the sequence should replace an earlier stored exemplar.

2. **Scalar (or low‑dimensional) memory bottleneck.** Classical LSTMs compress all information into a vector of modest size. When predicting **rare tokens** in large vocabularies, the limited capacity forces the model to compress many disparate facts into a small number of dimensions, leading to poorer performance on low‑frequency items.

3. **Sequential dependency (lack of parallelism).** The hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence $h_{t-1}\!\to\!h_t$ prevents simultaneous computation across time steps. Training and inference thus scale linearly with sequence length, whereas the self‑attention mechanism in Transformers enables **parallel** processing of all positions, drastically reducing wall‑clock time for long contexts.

These issues motivated the development of the **Transformer** architecture in 2017, whose self‑attention mechanism offers both **full parallelism** and an **unbounded receptive field** without the need for explicit gating. Transformers rapidly eclipsed LSTMs on benchmark language modeling tasks, especially when scaled to billions of parameters.

## Key Research Question: Scaling LSTMs to Billions of Parameters

The historical trajectory raises an intriguing counter‑question: *If we address the three LSTM limitations—reviseable storage, richer memory, and parallelism—can a recurrent model compete with Transformers when scaled to the same parameter budget?* The paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* proposes exactly this line of inquiry. By introducing **exponential gating** (allowing more aggressive and flexible updates) together with **novel memory structures**—the scalar‑mixing sLSTM and the matrix‑memory mLSTM—the authors construct **xLSTM blocks** that can be stacked residually, forming deep architectures comparable in size to contemporary LLMs.

The central hypothesis is straightforward:

> **If we endow LSTMs with mechanisms that eliminate irreversible storage, expand the representational capacity of the memory, and enable (partial) parallelization, then scaling them to billions of parameters should yield language‑model performance on par with, or even superior to, state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models.**

The remainder of the textbook will explore the concrete technical innovations—exponential gating, sLSTM’s memory mixing, and mLSTM’s covariance update rule—followed by empirical evidence showing that such **xLSTM** models indeed close the performance gap while retaining the linear‑time, constant‑memory advantages of recurrent architectures. This historical perspective underscores why revisiting LSTMs is not a nostalgic exercise but a purposeful effort to blend the best of recurrent inductive bias with modern scaling techniques.

# Fundamentals of the Classical LSTM  

The Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) network remains a cornerstone of recurrent neural‑network design.  Its longevity stems from a precise architectural solution to the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued early recurrent models.  In this chapter we derive, in full detail, the forward‑pass equations of the original LSTM, explain the role of each gate and parameter, and discuss why every component is essential.  The exposition follows the formulation given in the original LSTM papers and the canonical description reproduced in the xLSTM manuscript (Section 2.1).

---

## Notation and Basic Forward‑Pass Equations  

We consider a discrete‑time sequence of input vectors \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) indexed by the time step \(t\).  The LSTM maintains two internal state vectors at each step:

* the **cell state** \(\mathbf{c}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), which acts as a long‑term memory accumulator, and  
* the **hidden state** \(\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), which is the exposed output of the unit at time \(t\).

Both states share the same dimensionality \(d\), often called the hidden size.  The forward dynamics are defined by a set of affine transformations followed by element‑wise nonlinearities.  The core recurrence, sometimes called the “constant‑error carousel,” is

\[
\boxed{\mathbf{c}_{t}= \mathbf{f}_{t}\odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_{t}\odot \mathbf{z}_{t}} \tag{1}
\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication.  Equation (1) shows that the new cell state is an additive combination of a **retained** part (\(\mathbf{f}_{t}\odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1}\)) and a **newly written** part (\(\mathbf{i}_{t}\odot \mathbf{z}_{t}\)).  The functions that produce the three gate vectors \(\mathbf{i}_{t},\mathbf{f}_{t},\mathbf{o}_{t}\) and the candidate cell input \(\mathbf{z}_{t}\) are detailed next.

---

## Input, Forget, and Output Gates – Sigmoid Activation and Bias Terms  

Each gate is an element‑wise scalar in \([0,1]\) that modulates information flow.  They are obtained from an affine combination of the current input \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\) and the previous hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\), passed through the logistic sigmoid \(\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\).  The bias terms permit the network to learn useful default behaviours (e.g., a forget gate bias initialised positively encourages the network to retain information at the beginning of training).

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{t} &= \mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{t} \;+\; \mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_{i}, &
\mathbf{i}_{t} &= \sigma\!\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{t}\bigr),\\[4pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{t} &= \mathbf{W}_{f}\mathbf{x}_{t} \;+\; \mathbf{R}_{f}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_{f}, &
\mathbf{f}_{t} &= \sigma\!\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{t}\bigr),\\[4pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{t} &= \mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{x}_{t} \;+\; \mathbf{R}_{o}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_{o}, &
\mathbf{o}_{t} &= \sigma\!\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{t}\bigr).
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
\]

Here \(\mathbf{W}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{in}}}\) are input‑weight matrices, \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) are recurrent weight matrices that connect the previous hidden state to the current gate, and \(\mathbf{b}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) are bias vectors.  The three gates have distinct semantic functions:

* **Input gate \(\mathbf{i}_{t}\):** decides how much of the candidate information \(\mathbf{z}_{t}\) should be written into the cell.
* **Forget gate \(\mathbf{f}_{t}\):** decides how much of the previous cell content \(\mathbf{c}_{t-1}\) should be retained.  The forget gate was introduced by Gers et al. (2000) to give the network the ability to discard stale information.
* **Output gate \(\mathbf{o}_{t}\):** controls how much of the (potentially normalized) cell state is exposed as the hidden output \(\mathbf{h}_{t}\).

Because the sigmoid output is bounded, each gate can smoothly interpolate between full passage (value near 1) and complete blocking (value near 0).

---

## Cell Input Transformation and Activation Functions  

The candidate cell input \(\mathbf{z}_{t}\) is a provisional content vector that the network may write into the memory.  It is generated by a linear projection of the current input and the previous hidden state, followed by a pointwise nonlinearity \(\varphi\), typically the hyperbolic tangent:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t} &= \mathbf{W}_{z}\mathbf{x}_{t} \;+\; \mathbf{R}_{z}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_{z},\\
\mathbf{z}_{t} &= \varphi\!\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\bigr),\qquad 
\varphi(x)=\tanh(x).
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
\]

The tanh squashes the raw affine output to the interval \((-1,1)\), ensuring that the candidate contributions remain bounded and that the additive update in (1) does not cause uncontrolled growth of the cell state.

---

## Hidden‑State Computation and Output Gating  

Once the cell state \(\mathbf{c}_{t}\) has been updated, the hidden state is formed by first applying a cell‑state activation \(\psi\) (again normally \(\tanh\)) and then modulating the result with the output gate:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t} &= \psi\!\bigl(\mathbf{c}_{t}\bigr),\qquad \psi(x)=\tanh(x),\\
\mathbf{h}_{t} &= \mathbf{o}_{t}\odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}.
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
\]

The activation \(\psi\) normalizes the possibly unbounded cell values, while the output gate \(\mathbf{o}_{t}\) determines which dimensions of the normalized cell are allowed to influence downstream layers or the next time step.

The complete forward pass of a scalar‑cell LSTM (i.e., a single memory per hidden dimension) is thus captured by equations (1)–(4) together with the affine definitions (2) and (3).

---

## Vector‑Valued Cells and Recurrent Weight Matrices  

In early formulations the LSTM was described with a single scalar cell per hidden unit.  Modern implementations generalise this to a *vector* of cells, \(\mathbf{c}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), allowing all gates and the candidate input to be vectors of the same size.  This change introduces *recurrent weight matrices* \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\) that capture hidden‑to‑hidden interactions across the whole hidden dimension, rather than a set of independent scalar recurrences.  The matrix form makes the LSTM expressive enough to mix information across different hidden units at each step, a capability referred to as **memory mixing** in the xLSTM paper.

The vectorised formulation retains the same equations; only the weight shapes differ:

* \(\mathbf{W}_{*}\) – input‑to‑gate matrices (\(d\times d_{\text{in}}\));
* \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\) – hidden‑to‑gate matrices (\(d\times d\));
* \(\mathbf{b}_{*}\) – bias vectors (\(d\)).

When \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\) are identity matrices (or zero), the recurrent connections are removed, reducing the model to a shallow feed‑forward network applied at each time step.  Empirically, allowing full \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\) matrices improves performance because the network can learn richer temporal dependencies.

---

## Ablation Insights: Importance of Each Gate and Connection  

A comprehensive ablation study for the original LSTM was performed by Greff et al. (2015) (cited in the xLSTM manuscript).  The study systematically removed or altered components and measured the impact on benchmark tasks.  The key findings, directly relevant to the classical LSTM, are:

1. **All three gates are indispensable.**  Removing any gate (setting it permanently to 1) degrades performance dramatically, confirming that the input, forget, and output gates each serve a unique purpose.
2. **Input‑dependent gating matters.**  Gates that depend only on the previous hidden state (or are fixed) perform worse than those that also incorporate the current input \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\).  This underscores the importance of the affine terms \(\mathbf{W}_{*}\mathbf{x}_{t}\) in (2).
3. **Recurrent weight matrices increase expressivity.**  Models that restrict \(\mathbf{R}_{*}\) to diagonal or zero matrices lose the ability to mix information across hidden dimensions, leading to poorer results on tasks that require coordinated memory updates.
4. **Bias terms are crucial for stable learning.**  In particular, initializing the forget‑gate bias to a positive value (e.g., 1) encourages the network to retain information early in training, mitigating the risk of premature forgetting.
5. **Cell‑state activation \(\psi\) (tanh) stabilizes gradients.**  Without the nonlinearity, the additive update in (1) could cause the cell values to explode, breaking back‑propagation through time.

These ablations validate the intuition that the LSTM’s success hinges on a delicate balance: additive memory updates prevent vanishing gradients, while multiplicative gates afford fine‑grained control over what is stored, forgotten, and emitted.

---

### Summary  

The classical LSTM comprises a set of tightly coupled equations that together solve the vanishing‑gradient problem and endow the network with a persistent, selectively writable memory.  The cell state update (1) is additive, guaranteeing that gradients can flow backwards unchanged over long horizons.  Multiplicative gates (2) regulate the flow of information, each governed by sigmoid activations with learnable biases.  The candidate input (3) introduces new, bounded content, while the hidden output (4) exposes a gated, normalized view of the memory.  Extending the scalar cell to a vector with full recurrent weight matrices yields powerful hidden‑to‑hidden interactions, a design choice confirmed by ablation studies to be essential for high‑performing sequence models.  Understanding these fundamentals provides the necessary grounding to appreciate later extensions such as the exponential gating and matrix memories introduced in xLSTM.

# Identified Limitations of the Standard LSTM  

The Long Short‑Term Memory network (LSTM) has been a work‑horse of sequence modelling for more than three decades.  Its core mechanisms—the constant‑error carousel and the three sigmoid gates—solve the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued earlier recurrent neural networks.  Nevertheless, when LSTMs are pushed to the scale of today’s large language models (LLMs), three fundamental shortcomings become evident.  These limitations are not merely academic curiosities; they directly impair the ability of an LSTM‑based architecture to compete with modern Transformer‑based systems on language modelling tasks.  In this chapter we dissect each of these drawbacks, illustrate them with concrete synthetic experiments described in the xLSTM paper, and connect the observations to a broader body of literature on recurrent networks.

---

## Inability to Revise Storage Decisions  

### The storage‑revision problem  

The original LSTM update for the cell state  

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t
\tag{1}
\]

allows the network to either retain the previous memory (through the forget gate \(f_t\)) or to write a new contribution (through the input gate \(i_t\)).  Once a value has been written into the scalar cell, the only way to “overwrite” it later is to let the forget gate decay the old content and then add a fresh input.  This design is inherently **unidirectional**: a later, more informative observation cannot retroactively replace an earlier stored value without first erasing the whole cell.

### Nearest‑Neighbor Search as a diagnostic task  

The paper uses a synthetic *Nearest‑Neighbor Search* problem to expose this weakness.  A reference vector \(\mathbf{r}\) is provided at the beginning of the sequence.  The model then receives a long stream of candidate vectors \(\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T\}\) and, at the final time step, must output the label attached to the **most similar** candidate (according to Euclidean distance).  An optimal algorithm would keep the best candidate seen so far and replace it whenever a closer one appears.  

When a standard LSTM processes the stream, its scalar cell can store only a single aggregated quantity.  If a more similar vector appears later, the network must first forget the previous value (via a small \(f_t\)) and then write the new value (via a large \(i_t\)).  Because the forget gate is bounded by a sigmoid, the decay is gradual, and the network exhibits a high mean‑squared error even after extensive training.  This inability to *revise* a stored decision is highlighted in the left panel of Figure 1 of the paper.

### How exponential gating mitigates the issue  

The xLSTM introduces **exponential gating**: the input gate \(i_t\) (and optionally the forget gate \(f_t\)) are computed as \(\exp(\tilde{i}_t)\) instead of \(\sigma(\tilde{i}_t)\).  Exponential activations can produce values far greater than one, allowing the network to *immediately* amplify a new, more relevant contribution while simultaneously scaling down the influence of older information.  The authors further stabilize these potentially large numbers by maintaining a normalizer state \(n_t\) and a stabilizer state \(m_t\) (see Equations (2)–(4) in the paper).  Empirically, the exponential‑gated sLSTM (the scalar variant) dramatically reduces the error on the nearest‑neighbor task, demonstrating that revisable storage is now feasible.

---

## Limited Scalar Memory Capacity and Its Effect on Rare‑Token Prediction  

### Scalar versus matrix memory  

In the classic LSTM each cell holds a **single scalar** \(c_t \in \mathbb{R}\).  Even when many cells are stacked into a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\), each component remains a one‑dimensional bucket of information.  This design forces the model to compress all contextual cues into a low‑dimensional representation, which is adequate for many tasks but becomes a bottleneck for language modelling where the distribution of tokens is heavily skewed: a tiny fraction of tokens (the “rare” ones) appear only a handful of times in the training corpus.

### Rare‑token prediction experiment  

The authors evaluate this limitation on the **Wikitext‑103** benchmark, partitioning tokens by their training frequency.  Perplexity (the standard language‑modeling loss) is measured separately for frequent and rare token groups.  Figure 2 (right panel) of the paper shows a clear disparity: the baseline LSTM’s perplexity skyrockets on the rare‑token subset, whereas a Transformer‑based model maintains a relatively flat curve because its attention mechanism can retrieve fine‑grained token‑level details from many heads.

The intuition is that a scalar cell cannot simultaneously preserve detailed statistics about *all* token types.  When a rare token is encountered, its embedding must be merged into the existing scalar state, potentially overwriting information useful for later predictions of other rare tokens.  Consequently, the model’s ability to recall the correct distribution for low‑frequency words deteriorates.

### Matrix memory of mLSTM as a remedy  

To address the capacity ceiling, the xLSTM introduces **mLSTM**, whose memory is a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\).  The update rule follows a **covariance (outer‑product) accumulation**:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top},
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and \(\mathbf{v}_t\) are respectively a *key* and a *value* vector derived from the current input.  This rule is essentially a fast‑weight or associative memory mechanism: each key‑value pair contributes a rank‑one update to the memory matrix, and the forget gate serves as a decay factor.  The matrix can store a **large set of associations** in a distributed fashion, dramatically increasing the effective storage bandwidth compared with scalar cells.

When evaluated on the same rare‑token partitions, the mLSTM‑based xLSTM narrows the perplexity gap: it can retain enough distinct information about infrequent words to make accurate predictions, while still benefiting from the recurrent inductive bias of the constant‑error carousel.

---

## Sequential Hidden‑Hidden Connections → Lack of Parallelism  

### The hidden‑hidden recurrence  

Beyond the cell state recurrence, canonical LSTMs also contain hidden‑to‑hidden connections: the current hidden state \(h_t\) is computed from the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\) via the recurrent weights \(r_z, r_i, r_f, r_o\).  This **memory‑mixing** operation couples each time step to its predecessor, enforcing a strictly sequential computation order.  Even when the cell state update (the constant‑error carousel) is linear in time, the hidden‑hidden pathway introduces a second, non‑parallelizable dependency.

### Consequences for large‑scale training  

Training a Transformer benefits from massive parallelism: self‑attention can be evaluated for all positions simultaneously, and the quadratic cost can be reduced with efficient kernels.  An LSTM, by contrast, must perform a *scan* over the sequence—each step waiting for the hidden state of the previous step.  This serial dependency limits GPU utilisation, inflates wall‑clock time, and makes it difficult to scale to the billions of tokens and parameters typical of modern LLMs.

### Parallelizable mLSTM and the trade‑off  

The mLSTM deliberately **removes hidden‑hidden connections**.  Its recurrence resides solely in the matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\) and the normalizer \(\mathbf{n}_t\), both of which are **linear scans** that can be expressed as prefix‑sum operations.  Because no hidden‑to‑hidden weight matrix is involved, the entire forward pass can be reformulated as a set of parallel matrix multiplications, akin to the way FlashAttention rewrites the attention recurrence.  The paper notes that this design makes mLSTM “fully parallelizable,” and empirical timing results show that the wall‑clock overhead is modest despite the \(d\times d\) matrix updates.

In contrast, the scalar sLSTM retains hidden‑hidden connections to preserve *memory mixing* across multiple heads, which is essential for solving state‑tracking formal‑language tasks (e.g., parity or nested‑bracket languages).  The authors acknowledge that sLSTM therefore remains sequential, but they provide a highly tuned CUDA kernel that reduces the slowdown to less than a factor of two relative to the parallel mLSTM.

---

## Empirical Evidence from Prior Literature  

The three limitations described above have been observed repeatedly in the broader RNN literature:

1. **Irrevocable memory updates** – Early works on associative memories (e.g., Hopfield networks, bidirectional associative memories) highlighted the need for *fast overwrite* capabilities, which later motivated fast‑weight programmers and exponential gate designs.

2. **Scalar bottleneck** – Studies on *capacity limits of recurrent networks* (e.g., Greff et al., 2015) demonstrated that a fixed‑size hidden vector struggles to encode long‑range dependencies when the information load grows, motivating augmentations such as external memory matrices (Neural Turing Machines, Differentiable Neural Computers).

3. **Sequential bottleneck** – The computational inefficiency of RNNs was precisely the problem that motivated the Transformer’s self‑attention (Vaswani et al., 2017).  Subsequent work on *linear attention* and *state‑space models* (e.g., S4, Mamba) focuses on achieving **linear time** and **parallelizable** recurrences, echoing the design choice in mLSTM to drop hidden‑hidden mixing.

Together, these prior observations converge on the same three axes of improvement that the xLSTM paper formalizes: (i) revisable storage via exponential gating, (ii) richer memory structures via matrix (fast‑weight) updates, and (iii) parallelism by eliminating hidden‑hidden recurrence.  The systematic experimental suite—nearest‑neighbor search, rare‑token prediction, formal‑language state tracking, and long‑range arena—provides concrete evidence that each modification directly addresses its corresponding limitation.

---  

In summary, while the original LSTM introduced groundbreaking mechanisms that solved the vanishing‑gradient crisis, its **scalar memory**, **non‑revisable gating**, and **sequential hidden‑hidden connections** become severe bottlenecks when the model is scaled to the billions of parameters required for modern language modelling.  The xLSTM architecture directly tackles these three constraints, paving the way for recurrent networks to compete with Transformers and state‑space models on large‑scale language tasks.

**Exponential Gating: Theory and Stabilization**

The classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) cell relies on *sigmoid* activations for its input, forget, and output gates.  While sigmoid gates are bounded between 0 and 1, this boundedness hampers two important capabilities that modern language models demand.  First, a sigmoid‑gated cell cannot *revise* a storage decision once it has been made: if a large value was written to the cell early in a sequence, the forget gate can only decay it multiplicatively, never replace it with a larger contribution without a proportional increase in the input gate.  Second, the scalar nature of the LSTM cell state limits the amount of information that can be stored, especially for rare tokens that require precise, high‑dimensional encoding.

The **exponential gate** resolves both shortcomings.  By allowing the gate value to grow without an a‑priori upper bound, the model can *overwrite* a previously stored entry with a much larger one, and it can amplify the contribution of a rare token through a larger input gate.  However, naïvely using the exponential function $\exp(\cdot)$ creates numerical instability: exponentials quickly overflow to infinity, and gradients can explode.  The xLSTM framework therefore pairs exponential gating with two auxiliary states—a **normalizer** $n_t$ and a **stabilizer** $m_t$—that keep the forward and backward passes well‑behaved.

Below we develop the theory behind exponential gating, derive the normalizer and stabilizer updates, and sketch why these modifications preserve the original learning dynamics while enabling the aforementioned capabilities.

---

### Motivation for Exponential Gates

Consider the scalar LSTM update  
\[
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t,
\qquad
f_t,i_t \in (0,1),
\]
where $f_t$ (forget) and $i_t$ (input) are sigmoid‑activated.  Because $i_t\le 1$, the magnitude of any newly written content $z_t$ cannot exceed the raw input value; similarly, $f_t$ can only attenuate the existing memory.  When a sequence contains a *better* value later (e.g. a nearer neighbour in a search task), the cell is forced to *gradually* forget the old content, which slows learning and limits accuracy.

If we replace the sigmoid with an exponential,
\[
i_t = \exp (\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp (\tilde{f}_t) \quad\text{or}\quad \sigma(\tilde{f}_t),
\]
the gate can become arbitrarily large.  In the forward pass a large $i_t$ can dominate the additive term, instantly overwriting $c_{t-1}$.  Likewise a large $f_t$ can *boost* the retained memory rather than merely preserving it, granting the cell the flexibility to *revise* stored information in a single step.

This flexibility is precisely what is needed for (i) **revision of storage decisions** (the nearest‑neighbor experiment) and (ii) **enhanced capacity** (rare‑token prediction).  Yet, without safeguards, $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ can become astronomically large, causing overflow in floating‑point arithmetic and destroying gradient flow.  The normalizer and stabilizer states are introduced to tame these explosions.

---

### Mathematical Form of Exponential Activation

In the *sLSTM* variant, the gate pre‑activations $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ are computed exactly as in a traditional LSTM:
\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_{i} \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_{i},
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{f}\,\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_{f} \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_{f},
\]
but the *gate values* become
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\;\; \text{or}\;\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]
The exponential gate is thus a simple re‑parameterization of the sigmoid gate: the logarithm of $i_t$ is exactly the original pre‑activation $\tilde{i}_t$, and similarly for the exponential forget gate.  This re‑parameterization does not alter the functional form of the cell update; it merely expands the admissible range of gate values.

For the *mLSTM* (matrix‑memory) variant, the same exponential activation is used for both input and forget gates, while the output gate remains sigmoid:
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t) \;\; \text{or}\;\; \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t).
\]

---

### Normaliser State \(n_t\) and Its Role in Scaling Hidden Output

Because exponential gates can increase without bound, the raw cell state $c_t$ can also grow without bound, rendering the hidden output $h_t$ unsuitable for downstream layers.  The *sLSTM* introduces a *normalizer* $n_t$ that tracks the cumulative “mass” contributed by the gates:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
n_t &= f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t .
\end{aligned}}
\]
Intuitively, $n_t$ is the denominator of a weighted average: the cell state $c_t$ accumulates scaled inputs $i_t z_t$, while $n_t$ accumulates the same scaling factors without the input vectors.  The hidden state is then formed by dividing the cell state by the normalizer:
\[
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad
h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t .
\]
This division ensures that, regardless of how large the gates become, the hidden representation remains *scale‑invariant*: a uniformly large scaling of both numerator and denominator cancels out.  Consequently, the network can employ unbounded gates for rapid revision while preserving a numerically stable hidden activation.

In the *mLSTM* the normalizer is vector‑valued:
\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{k}_t,
\]
where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the *key* vector derived from the input.  The hidden state is normalized by the scalar
\[
\max\!\bigl\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\bigr\},
\]
with $\mathbf{q}_t$ the query vector.  This prevents division by numbers close to zero and caps the denominator at 1, ensuring a well‑conditioned hidden output.

---

### Stabilizer State \(m_t\) and Log‑Space Re‑Parameterization

Even with a normalizer, intermediate values $i_t$ and $f_t$ may overflow before being combined with $n_t$ or $c_t$.  The *stabilizer* state $m_t$ stores, in log‑space, the **maximum** cumulative log‑gate observed so far:
\[
\boxed{
m_t = \max\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr).
}
\]
The recursion tracks the largest exponent that has contributed to the cell, akin to a running maximum of log‑weights.  Using $m_t$, the gates are *renormalized*:
\[
\begin{aligned}
i'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log i_t - m_t\bigr) 
      = \exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\bigr), \\
f'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Both $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ lie in the interval $(0,1]$ by construction because the subtraction of the maximal log‑value guarantees that the exponent is non‑positive.  Importantly, the *product* $f'_t i'_t$ equals the original $f_t i_t$ up to a constant factor that cancels when multiplied across time steps.  Hence the *relative* contribution of each gate to the cell state is unchanged.

The stabilizer therefore re‑expresses the exponential gates in a **log‑space** representation, where addition replaces multiplication.  This mirrors the classic technique of performing computations in log‑space to avoid overflow in probabilistic models.  In the forward pass, the actual gate values used are the stabilized $i'_t$ and $f'_t$, while the *raw* exponentials are retained implicitly in $m_t$ for gradient computation.

---

### Proof Sketch That Stabilized Gates Preserve Gradients

We outline why substituting $(i_t,f_t)$ with $(i'_t,f'_t)$ does not alter the loss gradient with respect to any parameter $\theta$ that influences the pre‑activations $\tilde{i}_t$ or $\tilde{f}_t$.

1. **Forward Equivalence.**  By construction,
   \[
   i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}, \qquad
   f_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t}\ \text{or}\ \sigma(\tilde{f}_t).
   \]
   The stabilizer defines
   \[
   i'_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t - m_t}, \qquad
   f'_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1} - m_t}.
   \]
   Multiplying $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ yields
   \[
   i'_t f'_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t + \tilde{f}_t - m_t + m_{t-1} - m_t}
            = e^{\tilde{i}_t + \tilde{f}_t } e^{m_{t-1} - 2m_t}.
   \]
   The factor $e^{m_{t-1} - 2m_t}$ is a *global* scalar that is *identical* for all terms that share the same time index because $m_t$ is defined as the maximum of the two arguments.  When the cell update
   \[
   c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t
   \]
   is unrolled, the global scalar cancels across the additive terms, leaving exactly the same $c_t$ as obtained with the raw gates.

2. **Derivative Flow.**  The derivative of $i'_t$ w.r.t. $\tilde{i}_t$ is
   \[
   \frac{\partial i'_t}{\partial \tilde{i}_t}
   = i'_t
   = e^{\tilde{i}_t - m_t}.
   \]
   The derivative of $m_t$ w.r.t. $\tilde{i}_t$ is either $0$ (if $m_t$ came from $\log f_t + m_{t-1}$) or $1$ (if $m_t = \log i_t$).  In either case, the chain rule yields
   \[
   \frac{\partial i'_t}{\partial \tilde{i}_t}
   = i'_t - i'_t \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{m_t = \log i_t\}},
   \]
   which matches the derivative of the original $i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}$ up to the same global scaling factor that appeared in the forward pass.  Because the loss is linear in $c_t$, these scaling factors cancel when summing over time, leaving the *total* gradient $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta$ unchanged.

3. **Inductive Argument.**  Assuming the gradient equivalence holds up to step $t-1$, the recurrence for $c_t$ is linear in $i'_t$ and $f'_t$.  The stabilizer only introduces a *constant* multiplicative factor that is independent of the downstream loss (it does not depend on $z_t$ or $c_{t-1}$).  Hence the gradient contributions from step $t$ retain the same proportion as in the un‑stabilized case.

Thus, the stabilizer re‑parameterization is *gradient‑preserving*: training dynamics are identical, while the forward pass remains numerically safe.

---

### Practical Considerations

#### Initialization

Since exponential gates can explode, careful initialization of the pre‑activations is crucial.  In practice the biases $b_i$ and $b_f$ are set to small negative values (e.g. $-1$) so that $\exp(\tilde{i}_0)$ and $\exp(\tilde{f}_0)$ start near 1, mimicking the behavior of a conventional LSTM at the start of training.  The weight matrices are initialized with standard schemes (e.g., Xavier or Kaiming), and LayerNorm is applied before key/value projections in the mLSTM to enforce zero‑mean inputs, which improves the stability of the covariance update.

#### Overflow Handling

Even with the stabilizer, a single time step could produce a gate with $\tilde{i}_t$ far larger than typical floating‑point ranges.  The max‑operation in $m_t$ prevents this by *clipping* the log‑gate: the exponential is never computed directly; instead $i'_t$ is formed by subtracting $m_t$, guaranteeing that the exponent never exceeds zero.  This log‑space trick eliminates overflow without sacrificing the expressive power of the exponential gate.

#### Implementation Tips

* **Scan‑based computation.**  The recursions for $c_t$, $n_t$, and $m_t$ are simple *prefix scans*.  On GPUs these can be implemented efficiently using thrust‑like primitives or custom CUDA kernels, yielding a modest overhead compared to the original LSTM.
* **Mixed‑precision.**  When training with half‑precision (FP16), the stabilizer becomes even more important because the dynamic range is reduced.  The log‑space representation fits naturally in FP16, and the division by $n_t$ (or the max‑norm denominator in mLSTM) guards against underflow.
* **Choice of forget gate.**  The paper reports empirical equivalence between sigmoid and exponential forget gates; however, exponential forget gates often lead to faster convergence when the task requires aggressive memory revision.

---

In summary, **exponential gating** augments the traditional LSTM with the ability to instantly revise stored information and to allocate greater capacity to rare events.  The accompanying **normalizer** guarantees that hidden activations stay bounded, while the **stabilizer** re‑expresses gate values in log‑space, eliminating overflow and preserving gradients.  Together, these mechanisms constitute the core theoretical contribution of the xLSTM family and enable the empirical successes reported throughout the paper.

# sLSTM: Scalar Memory with Memory Mixing  

In this chapter we examine the **scalar Long Short‑Term Memory** variant, denoted **sLSTM**, which enriches the classic LSTM with *exponential gating* and a *normalizer state*.  The sLSTM retains a single scalar cell per head but introduces a new mechanism for mixing information across multiple cells and across multiple heads.  We present the complete forward equations, discuss the design choices for the forget gate, describe how several memory cells can be coupled through recurrent mixing matrices, and explain how heads are processed independently while still allowing intra‑head mixing.  Finally we compare sLSTM to the original multi‑cell LSTM and give practical notes on implementation and CUDA acceleration.

---

## sLSTM Forward Equations  

The core of the sLSTM is a scalar cell state \(c_t\) that is updated in the same additive fashion as the original LSTM, but the gates that modulate the update are allowed to be **exponential** rather than strictly sigmoid.  In addition, a *normalizer* \(n_t\) is kept alongside the cell so that the hidden state can be expressed as a *normalized* cell value.  The full forward pass for a single head at time step \(t\) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t                                            &&\text{(cell state)}\\[4pt]
n_t &= f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t                                                &&\text{(normalizer state)}\\[4pt]
h_t &= o_t \, \tilde{h}_t, \qquad
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}                                                 &&\text{(hidden state)}\\[6pt]
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \qquad
\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_z \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_z   &&\text{(cell input)}\\[4pt]
i_t &= \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i   &&\text{(input gate)}\\[4pt]
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \;\;\text{or}\;\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_f \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_f   &&\text{(forget gate)}\\[4pt]
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad
\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_o \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_o   &&\text{(output gate)} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

All symbols have the same meaning as in the classic LSTM: \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is the input vector, \(h_{t-1}\) the previous hidden state, \(\mathbf{w}_\ast\) are input‑to‑gate weight vectors, \(r_\ast\) are hidden‑to‑gate scalar recurrent weights, and \(b_\ast\) are biases.  The activation \(\varphi\) (commonly \(\tanh\)) squashes the raw cell input, while \(\psi\) in the classic formulation is replaced here by the explicit division by the normalizer \(n_t\).  The normalizer accumulates the total “gate mass’’ that has been contributed up to time \(t\); dividing by it guarantees that \(\tilde{h}_t\) remains numerically stable even when gates become very large due to the exponential non‑linearity.

### Stabilisation of Exponential Gates  

Because \(\exp(\cdot)\) can produce extremely large values, the sLSTM introduces a *stabiliser state* \(m_t\) that rescales the gates without changing any gradients:

\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t\bigr) ,\\
i'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log i_t - m_t\bigr),\\
f'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

Replacing \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) by their stabilized counterparts \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) in (1) does not affect the final output or the loss gradients (see Appendix C of the original paper).  The stabiliser can be interpreted as a running log‑scale that keeps the product of gates in a numerically safe range.

---

## Exponential versus Sigmoid Forget Gate  

The forget gate in the original LSTM is strictly sigmoid, constraining it to the interval \((0,1)\).  In sLSTM the forget gate may either be sigmoid **or** exponential:

* **Sigmoid forget gate** retains the classic behaviour: the gate can only attenuate the previous cell state (\(0 < f_t < 1\)), thereby ensuring a monotonic decay of past information unless the input gate adds new content.

* **Exponential forget gate** permits values larger than one.  When \(\exp(\tilde{f}_t) > 1\), the term \(f_t c_{t-1}\) *amplifies* the previously stored scalar.  This capability is essential for *revising* storage decisions: if a later input reveals that an earlier entry should have been stored with higher magnitude, the exponential forget gate can boost the old value before the new contribution arrives.  Empirically, the authors show that exponential forgetting dramatically improves tasks that require iterative refinement, such as the Nearest‑Neighbor Search experiment.

Both options are available because in some settings a bounded forget gate remains desirable for stability, whereas in others the extra expressive power of an unbounded gate outweighs the risk of runaway values—yet the stabiliser defined in (2) mitigates that risk.

---

## Multiple Memory Cells and Recurrent Mixing  

Just as the conventional LSTM may contain a vector of cell states \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), the sLSTM can be extended to **multiple scalar cells** per head.  Let us collect the scalar states into a column vector \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) and similarly define \(\mathbf{n}_t\) for the normalizers.  The recurrence then becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t ,\\
\mathbf{n}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication and each gate vector is obtained from a **recurrent mixing matrix** that connects the previous hidden state to each gate:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_i \, \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_i ,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_f \, \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_f ,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_o \, \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_o ,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_z \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_z \, \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_z .
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

The matrices \(\mathbf{R}_\ast \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) are the **memory‑mixing matrices**.  They allow each scalar cell to be influenced, directly or indirectly, by the hidden state of all other cells at the previous time step.  This coupling creates a richer dynamics than the independent scalar cells of the classic LSTM, enabling the network to *track* composite state information that would otherwise require a larger hidden dimension.

When exponential gating is used, the same stabiliser trick from (2) is applied element‑wise to each entry of \(\mathbf{i}_t\) and \(\mathbf{f}_t\).

---

## Head‑wise Processing and Intra‑Head Mixing  

Modern large‑scale language models frequently employ **multiple attention heads** to capture diverse subspaces of the representation.  The sLSTM adopts a similar idea: each *head* maintains its own set of scalar cells and its own normalizer, but **mixing occurs only within a head**, not across heads.  Formally, suppose we have \(H\) heads, each with \(d\) scalar cells.  For head \(h\) we denote the vectors by \(\mathbf{c}^{(h)}_t, \mathbf{n}^{(h)}_t\) etc.  The forward recurrence for each head follows (3)–(4) with head‑specific mixing matrices \(\mathbf{R}^{(h)}_\ast\).

* **No cross‑head mixing:** The hidden state entering the next layer is the concatenation of the per‑head hidden vectors,
  \[
  \mathbf{h}_t = \operatorname{concat}\bigl( \mathbf{o}^{(1)}_t \odot \frac{\mathbf{c}^{(1)}_t}{\mathbf{n}^{(1)}_t},
  \dots,
  \mathbf{o}^{(H)}_t \odot \frac{\mathbf{c}^{(H)}_t}{\mathbf{n}^{(H)}_t} \bigr) .
  \]
  Because each head processes its own scalar memory independently, the parameter count grows linearly with \(H\) rather than quadratically.

* **Intra‑head mixing:** Within a head, the mixing matrices \(\mathbf{R}^{(h)}_\ast\) enable *memory mixing across the \(d\) cells*.  This is the key difference to the original multi‑cell LSTM, where mixing is achieved only through a *single* recurrent weight matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that couples the hidden vector to the gate *vectors*.  In sLSTM the mixing is explicit at the gate level, which together with exponential gating yields a more flexible revision of stored information.

The head‑wise design also makes it straightforward to attach different numbers of cells to each head, or to share mixing matrices across heads for a parameter‑efficient variant.

---

## Comparison to the Original Multi‑Cell LSTM  

| Aspect | Classic Multi‑Cell LSTM | sLSTM (scalar) |
|--------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Cell type** | Vector of scalars \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) with element‑wise gating. | Same, but each scalar is *normalized* by a companion normalizer \(\mathbf{n}_t\). |
| **Gating non‑linearity** | All gates sigmoid (bounded \([0,1]\)). | Input gate always exponential; forget gate can be sigmoid *or* exponential, giving unbounded dynamics. |
| **Revision ability** | Forget gate can only decay; cannot increase a stored value after it has been written. | Exponential forget can *amplify* previous cell values, enabling revision of earlier storage decisions. |
| **Memory mixing** | Implicit via the hidden‑to‑gate matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). No explicit mixing of cell states themselves. | Explicit *recurrent mixing matrices* \(\mathbf{R}_\ast\) that directly couple cells at the gate level, and *head‑wise* mixing confined within each head. |
| **Stability mechanisms** | Implicit through bounded sigmoid gates. | Additional stabilizer state \(m_t\) and division by the normalizer keep values in a safe range despite exponentials. |
| **Parallelizability** | Sequential due to hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence (standard RNN). | Still sequential because of hidden‑to‑hidden connections, but the authors provide a custom CUDA kernel that reduces overhead to ≈2× the cost of the parallelizable mLSTM variant. |

Thus sLSTM can be seen as a *middle ground*: it preserves the strong sequential modeling capacity of an RNN (thanks to hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence) while gaining the ability to **revise past storage** and to **mix information across cells** in a more expressive way than the classic LSTM.

---

## Implementation Notes and CUDA Acceleration  

The sLSTM recurrence (1)–(2) is a **scan** (prefix‑sum) operation: each time step depends on the previous cell and normalizer.  Naïve implementations therefore run in \(O(T)\) time with a constant factor that can be high because of the exponential and stabilizer calculations.  The authors address this in two ways:

1. **Custom CUDA kernel** – By mapping the scan onto a parallel reduction pattern (similar to segmented scans), the kernel keeps the intermediate states (\(c_t, n_t, m_t\)) in GPU registers, reducing memory traffic.  The kernel also fuses the linear projections for gates and the element‑wise exponentials, avoiding separate kernel launches.

2. **Mixed‑precision support** – All matrix‑vector products for the gate pre‑activations are performed in FP16 (or BF16) while the stabilizer and division steps are kept in FP32 to retain numerical fidelity.  This mirrors the mixed‑precision training used for large Transformers and yields a modest memory savings.

Although sLSTM cannot be *fully* parallelized across time (the hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence forbids that), the optimized kernel makes the per‑step cost comparable to the *parallel* mLSTM variant, which uses a matrix memory but no hidden‑to‑hidden mixing.  Empirically the authors report that the sLSTM kernel is **less than two times slower** than the mLSTM kernel on modern GPUs, a negligible overhead when the overall model size is in the billions of parameters.

When integrating sLSTM into an xLSTM block (the residual module used throughout the paper), the forward pass follows a *post up‑projection* pattern: the scalar hidden vector is first processed by the sLSTM, then linearly expanded to a high‑dimensional space before the residual addition.  This ordering preserves the information‑mixing benefits of the scalar memory while still benefitting from the higher‑dimensional representation used by downstream layers.

--- 

**In summary**, the sLSTM augments the classic scalar LSTM cell with exponential gating, a normalizer that yields a well‑behaved hidden representation, and explicit mixing matrices that enable rich inter‑cell dynamics within each head.  These modifications give the model the ability to *revise* previously stored values, a property that is crucial for tasks requiring state tracking, while retaining the sequential processing advantages that have historically made LSTMs successful for a wide range of sequence modeling problems.  The careful engineering of stabilisation and CUDA acceleration ensures that the added expressivity does not come at prohibitive computational cost, making sLSTM a practical building block for the larger xLSTM architecture.

**mLSTM: Matrix Memory with Covariance Updates**

The mLSTM component of the xLSTM family abandons the traditional scalar cell state of the classic Long Short‑Term Memory and replaces it by a full matrix memory.  This seemingly simple change brings together three long‑standing ideas—associative memory, fast‑weight programming, and covariance learning—into a recurrent module that can be trained at the scale of modern large language models while remaining fully parallelizable.

---

### From a scalar cell to a \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) memory matrix  

In the original LSTM the hidden dynamics are governed by a single scalar (or, in later variants, a vector) cell state \(c_t\).  The update rule  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1}+ i_t\,z_t
\]

adds a new contribution \(z_t\) that is gated by an input gate \(i_t\) and attenuated by a forget gate \(f_t\).  While this additive form solves the vanishing‑gradient problem, it forces all information at time \(t\) to be compressed into a one‑dimensional quantity (or, at best, a vector of dimension equal to the hidden size).  Consequently the capacity to store distinct pieces of information—particularly when many items must be remembered simultaneously—is limited.

mLSTM addresses this bottleneck by promoting the cell state to a square matrix  

\[
\mathbf{C}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]

where \(d\) is the hidden dimension.  Instead of a scalar “value”, the memory now stores a bilinear relationship between two vectors that we will call a **key** \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and a **value** \(\mathbf{v}_t\).  The matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\) can be interpreted as a weighted superposition of outer products \(\mathbf{v}_\tau\mathbf{k}_\tau^{\!\top}\) for all past time steps \(\tau\le t\).  In this way the memory becomes an associative store that can retrieve a value when presented with a query that resembles the original key.

---

### Key–value formulation and query‑based retrieval  

At each time step the input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is first mapped, by learned linear projections, to three distinct vectors:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{q}_t &= \mathbf{W}_q\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q, \qquad &&\text{(query)}\\[4pt]
\mathbf{k}_t &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{W}_k\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k, \qquad &&\text{(key)}\\[4pt]
\mathbf{v}_t &= \mathbf{W}_v\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v, \qquad &&\text{(value)} .
\end{aligned}
\]

The scaling factor \(1/\sqrt{d}\) on the key matches the convention used in Transformer attention to keep inner products at a comparable magnitude.  The query \(\mathbf{q}_t\) will later be used to read from the matrix memory; the key–value pair \((\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t)\) constitutes the new piece of information that must be written.

The retrieval operation is a simple matrix‑vector product:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\displaystyle \max\!\Bigl\{\,\bigl|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t\bigr|,\;1\Bigr\}} .
\]

The numerator multiplies the query with the accumulated outer products, thereby producing a linear combination of stored values weighted by the similarity between the current query and each stored key.  The denominator normalises this combination; it prevents the hidden state from exploding when many keys have been accumulated.

---

### Covariance (outer‑product) update rule and its optimality  

The core of the mLSTM memory dynamics is the **covariance update**:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}.
\tag{1}
\]

If the forget gate \(f_t\) were fixed to \(1\) and the input gate \(i_t\) to \(1\), (1) would reduce to the classic outer‑product accumulation used in **Bidirectional Associative Memories** (BAMs).  The rule is called “covariance” because, under the assumption that the keys are zero‑mean (enforced by a layer‑norm before the projections), the outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}\) coincides with the empirical cross‑covariance between values and keys.

Dayan & Abbott (1991) showed that this update is **optimal for maximal separability** of binary patterns stored in a linear associative map: the signal‑to‑noise ratio of a retrieved pattern grows proportionally to the number of stored pairs, provided the keys are orthogonal on average.  In other words, the covariance rule yields the highest possible retrieval fidelity given the linear memory budget.

Later works on **Fast‑Weight Programmers** recognised the same update as a differentiable analogue of Hebbian learning: a fast weight matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\) is generated on the fly from the current input and then used to transform subsequent inputs.  By embedding this rule inside a recurrent architecture, mLSTM inherits the rapid‑adaptation property of fast‑weights while remaining trainable by back‑propagation.

---

### Exponential input/forget gates as learning and decay rates  

Gating remains essential in mLSTM, but the activation functions differ from the vanilla LSTM.  The forget gate can be either sigmoid or exponential, and the input gate is **always exponential**:

\[
\begin{aligned}
i_t &= \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad &&\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t + b_i,\\[4pt]
f_t &= 
\begin{cases}
\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\\[2pt]
\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde{f}_t), 
\end{cases}
\qquad &&\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t + b_f .
\end{aligned}
\]

Because an exponential can produce arbitrarily large positive numbers, it directly scales the magnitude of the outer product in (1).  Interpreting \(i_t\) as a **learning rate** and \(f_t\) as a **decay factor** makes the update rule analogous to a continuous‑time differential equation:

\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}}{\mathrm{d}t}= i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top} - (1-f_t)\,\mathbf{C}.
\]

Thus the network learns how aggressively to write new key‑value pairs (via \(i_t\)) and how quickly older information should fade (via \(f_t\)).  The exponential form provides a larger dynamic range than a sigmoid, allowing the model to sharply switch between “store aggressively” and “ignore” depending on the context—an ability that proved crucial in the synthetic Nearest‑Neighbor Search experiments described in the paper.

To avoid numerical overflow, the same **stabilisation state** \(m_t\) used for the scalar sLSTM is employed: the raw gates are rescaled by subtracting the maximum accumulated log‑gate, guaranteeing that the effective gates \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) remain bounded while preserving exact forward‑pass values and gradients (see Equation (3) in the paper).

---

### Normaliser vector \(\mathbf{n}_t\) and safe division for the hidden output  

The denominator of the hidden‑state computation contains a **normaliser vector**:

\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t .
\tag{2}
\]

\(\mathbf{n}_t\) aggregates the keys that have been stored, weighted by the same gates that affect the matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\).  Its inner product with the current query, \(\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t\), estimates the total magnitude contributed by the stored keys.  Dividing by \(\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\}\) guarantees two important properties:

1. **Scale invariance** – If all keys are uniformly scaled, both numerator and denominator scale proportionally, leaving \(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t\) unchanged.  
2. **Numerical safety** – When the query is orthogonal to all accumulated keys, the inner product may be near zero; clipping at \(1\) prevents division by a tiny number that would otherwise amplify noise.

The final hidden state is obtained by an output gate (sigmoid) and an element‑wise Hadamard product:

\[
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \;\odot\; \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t, \qquad
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t),\;
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_o\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_o .
\]

---

### Parallel implementation and relationship to FlashAttention  

A major limitation of classic recurrent networks is their inherently sequential nature: each step depends on the hidden state of the previous step, preventing efficient GPU parallelism.  In the mLSTM, **memory mixing**—the hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence present in the scalar sLSTM—is deliberately omitted.  Consequently the only recurrence resides in the two linear recurrences (1) and (2), which are simple **scan (prefix‑sum)** operations over the sequence:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{C}_t &= \operatorname{scan}_{\!\!t}\!\bigl[f_t,\;i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}\bigr],\\
\mathbf{n}_t &= \operatorname{scan}_{\!\!t}\!\bigl[f_t,\;i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]

Modern deep‑learning frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, JAX) expose highly‑optimised parallel scan kernels that evaluate such recurrences in \(O(\log L)\) time for a sequence of length \(L\).  After the scans have produced \(\mathbf{C}_t\) and \(\mathbf{n}_t\) for all timesteps, the retrieval step (matrix‑vector product) can be performed independently for each position, which is exactly the kind of operation FlashAttention accelerates for Transformers: a batched matrix‑vector multiplication combined with a per‑token scaling factor.

Therefore the overall computational complexity of mLSTM is **linear in sequence length** (the scans) and **quadratic in hidden dimension** (due to the \(d\times d\) matrix).  The quadratic term is comparable to the cost of the self‑attention kernel in Transformers, yet because the matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\) is **shared across all tokens at a given time step**, the cost does not grow with the context length.  Empirically the paper reports that the matrix‑memory operations can be fully parallelised on GPUs with only a modest wall‑clock overhead relative to the highly‑optimised FlashAttention implementation.

---

### Summary  

The mLSTM extends the classic LSTM cell from a scalar to a full covariance matrix, turning the recurrent unit into a fast‑weight associative memory.  By casting the write operation as a gated outer‑product update, the model learns **how much** to write (input gate) and **how quickly** to forget (forget gate) in a manner that is both theoretically optimal for pattern separability and practically powerful for long‑range language modeling.  The normaliser vector guarantees stable hidden‑state magnitudes, while the removal of hidden‑to‑hidden mixing makes the entire recurrence amenable to parallel scans, bringing the runtime characteristics close to those of modern attention kernels such as FlashAttention.  This combination of rich storage capacity, principled gating, and efficient parallel execution is what enables the mLSTM to compete with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models in large‑scale language modeling experiments.

# xLSTM Block Design: Residual Backbones and Up‑Projection

In modern large‑scale language models, the raw recurrent unit—whether it is a classic LSTM, the newly proposed sLSTM, or the matrix‑memory mLSTM—does not operate in isolation.  It is embedded inside a **residual block** that governs how information flows from one layer to the next, how non‑linearities are applied, and how the dimensionality of the representation evolves along the depth of the network.  This chapter explains in detail how the two variants of the extended LSTM (sLSTM and mLSTM) are incorporated into contemporary residual backbones, why two distinct up‑projection strategies are required, and how the overall design is motivated by the geometric intuition of Cover’s theorem.  By the end of the chapter a reader should be able to (i) trace a complete forward pass through an xLSTM block, (ii) understand the role of pre‑LayerNorm and the two up‑projection patterns, and (iii) see how the block can be stacked to form large language models that are compatible with existing LLM infrastructures.

---

## 1. Residual Connection with Pre‑LayerNorm

The foundation of every xLSTM architecture is the **pre‑LayerNorm residual block**, a pattern that has become de‑facto standard in large language models such as GPT‑3, Llama, and many recent State‑Space models.  The block can be written as

\[
\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{x}+ \operatorname{Block}\bigl(\operatorname{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathbf{x}\) denotes the input hidden tensor of shape \((B,\,T,\,D)\) (batch size \(B\), sequence length \(T\), hidden dimension \(D\)), \(\operatorname{LayerNorm}\) standardises the activations component‑wise, and \(\operatorname{Block}(\cdot)\) denotes the core computational module that contains the recurrent unit (sLSTM or mLSTM) together with any linear projections.

The reason for placing **LayerNorm before** the block (rather than after, as in the original Transformer) is twofold.  First, normalisation stabilises the statistics that enter the recurrent gates, which is especially important when those gates use **exponential activations** (Section 2).  Second, the pre‑normalisation makes the residual addition in (1) a simple identity mapping for the gradient, thereby improving training stability for very deep stacks—an essential property when we aim for billions of parameters.

Overall, the pre‑LayerNorm residual connection guarantees that the forward and backward information streams can each travel through the network **linearly** (via the skip connection) while the non‑linear transformation inside the block is free to reshape the representation arbitrarily.  This pattern is the scaffold atop which the two up‑projection strategies are built.

---

## 2. Post Up‑Projection Block (Used for sLSTM)

The **scalar‑memory sLSTM** retains the classic additive update of the cell state, but it augments the gate dynamics with exponential activations and a dedicated normaliser state \(n_t\).  Because the cell state is a **scalar** (or a low‑dimensional vector when multiple cells are used), the raw hidden output \(\tilde{h}_t=c_t/n_t\) lives in a relatively low‑dimensional space.  If we were to feed this directly back into the residual backbone, the network would have limited capacity to **separate distinct historical contexts**—a problem that becomes acute when modeling long textual passages.

To overcome this, the sLSTM is wrapped in a **post up‑projection block**.  The forward flow can be described step by step:

1. **Layer Normalisation**  
   \(\mathbf{u}= \operatorname{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\).

2. **Linear Down‑Projection** (optional)  
   In practice, the hidden dimension \(D\) may be reduced to a smaller internal dimension \(d_{\text{int}}\) (e.g. \(D/4\)) via a linear map  
   \(\mathbf{u}_{\downarrow}= \mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\mathbf{u}\).  
   This reduces the computational load of the recurrent core.

3. **sLSTM Core**  
   The down‑projected tensor is fed to the sLSTM recurrence (Eq. (4)–(9) of the paper).  Because the sLSTM yields a **scalar‑valued hidden state** per cell, the result after the recurrence has shape \((B,\,T,\,d_{\text{cells}})\), where \(d_{\text{cells}}\) is the number of parallel scalar cells.  The hidden state is then multiplied element‑wise with the output gate \(o_t\) to obtain \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{sLSTM}}\).

4. **Linear **_Post_** Up‑Projection**  
   The sLSTM output is **lifted** back to the original hidden dimension via  
   \[
   \mathbf{h}_{\uparrow}= \mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\mathbf{h}_{\text{sLSTM}}.
   \tag{2}
   \]  
   This matrix \(\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d_{\text{cells}}}\) implements a learned linear expansion.  The term “post” reflects that the expansion occurs **after** the recurrent computation and after the nonlinear gating has already acted on the low‑dimensional memory.

5. **Non‑linear Activation**  
   A pointwise non‑linearity (typically GELU or ReLU) is applied to \(\mathbf{h}_{\uparrow}\):
   \[
   \mathbf{z}= \operatorname{GELU}(\mathbf{h}_{\uparrow}).
   \]

6. **Residual Addition**  
   Finally, the block output is added back to the original input:
   \[
   \mathbf{y}= \mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{z}.
   \]

The essential intuition behind this design is that the **high‑dimensional expansion** performed in step 4 creates a richer feature space in which the low‑dimensional, highly compressed memory can be **linearly separated** from other contexts.  This matches Cover’s theorem, which asserts that a non‑linear mapping into a sufficiently high‑dimensional space makes classes more likely to become linearly separable.  In our case, the non‑linearity is already present in the sLSTM gates; the additional linear up‑projection simply provides the dimensionality needed for linear separation after the recurrent computation.

---

## 3. Pre Up‑Projection Block (Used for mLSTM)

The **matrix‑memory mLSTM** already operates on a high‑dimensional internal representation: its cell state \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) stores a full covariance matrix, and the hidden state is obtained by an inner product \(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t\) followed by normalisation.  Consequently, the raw output of the mLSTM core already lives in the target hidden dimension (or a close approximation thereof).  Introducing a **post** up‑projection would be redundant and would waste parameters.

Instead, the mLSTM is embedded in a **pre up‑projection block**.  The computation proceeds as follows:

1. **Layer Normalisation**  
   \(\mathbf{u}= \operatorname{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\).

2. **Linear **_Pre_** Up‑Projection**  
   The normalised tensor is first projected **up** to a larger intermediate dimension \(d_{\text{int}}\) (often larger than the final hidden dimension):
   \[
   \mathbf{u}_{\uparrow}= \mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\mathbf{u},
   \quad \mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{int}}\times D}.
   \tag{3}
   \]  
   This expansion is performed **before** the recurrent core, giving the mLSTM a larger space in which to construct its key \(\mathbf{k}_t\), value \(\mathbf{v}_t\), and query \(\mathbf{q}_t\) vectors (Eq. (13)–(16) of the paper).  Because the update rule \(\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\) involves outer products of these vectors, a larger embedding dimension directly translates into a richer covariance memory.

3. **mLSTM Core**  
   The up‑projected tensor \(\mathbf{u}_{\uparrow}\) is split into three linear heads to produce \(\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t,\) and \(\mathbf{q}_t\).  The gates \(i_t, f_t, o_t\) are computed exclusively from \(\mathbf{u}_{\uparrow}\) (no recurrent hidden‑to‑gate connections are needed because the mLSTM abandons hidden‑hidden mixing).  The forward equations (13)–(19) of the paper are applied, resulting in an output hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{mLSTM}}\) that already has dimension \(D\).

4. **Non‑linear Activation**  
   As with the sLSTM block, we apply a pointwise activation:
   \[
   \mathbf{z}= \operatorname{GELU}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{mLSTM}}).
   \]

5. **Residual Addition**  
   The final block output is \(\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{z}\).

The **pre‑projection** therefore acts as a “capacity booster” for the matrix memory: by feeding a higher‑dimensional representation into the mLSTM, the outer‑product storage can capture more nuanced associations between keys and values.  Since the mLSTM’s recurrence is fully **parallelizable** (no hidden‑hidden connections), this extra linear work does not introduce a sequential bottleneck; it can be executed efficiently on modern GPUs using batched matrix multiplications.

---

## 4. Non‑Linear Summarisation of Past Information

Both residual block designs share a common functional goal: **summarise the entire past sequence** up to time \(t\) in a way that makes the hidden state useful for predicting the next token.  The summarisation is achieved by the recurrent core (sLSTM or mLSTM), which aggregates information across time through the gated additive or covariance updates.  However, the raw recurrent output is still a linear combination of past hidden states—only **non‑linear gating** (sigmoid, exponential, and the squashing function \(\psi\)) has been applied.

To make the representation **highly discriminative**, the block injects an **additional non‑linearity** after the up‑projection (GELU).  This step is crucial for two reasons:

1. **Cover’s Theorem in Practice**  
   The theorem states that a non‑linear mapping into a higher‑dimensional space raises the probability that data points belonging to different classes become linearly separable.  In language modelling, “classes” correspond to distinct contextual histories (e.g., “the dog that chased the cat” vs. “the cat that chased the dog”).  The combination of (i) a non‑linear recurrent update, (ii) a linear projection that expands dimensionality, and (iii) a pointwise non‑linearity implements precisely the mapping advocated by Cover.

2. **Mitigation of Saturation**  
   Gates with exponential activations can produce very large or very small numbers, potentially driving the hidden state toward saturation.  The post‑GELU activation re‑centres the distribution, improving gradient flow in subsequent residual layers.

Thus the block can be viewed as a **two‑stage non‑linear encoder**: first, a recurrent encoder that aggregates the sequence, second, a feed‑forward encoder that expands and reshapes the aggregated signal before it is added back to the residual path.

---

## 5. Diagrammatic Walk‑Through of an xLSTM Block

Below we describe the data flow for each variant in a textual diagram; readers may sketch the corresponding computational graph for clarity.

### 5.1 sLSTM Block (Post‑Projection)

```
input x  ──► LayerNorm ──► LinearDown (optional) ──► sLSTM core
                                                    │
                                    ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
                                    │  scalar hidden h_t (c_t/n_t)│
                                    └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                                                 ▼
                                        LinearUp (W↑)
                                                 ▼
                                             GELU
                                                 ▼
                                          residual add
                                             (x + …)
```

*Key points*: the recurrent core outputs a low‑dimensional vector; the linear up‑projection (W↑) lifts it back; the GELU introduces non‑linearity; the residual addition yields the block output.

### 5.2 mLSTM Block (Pre‑Projection)

```
input x  ──► LayerNorm ──► LinearUp (W↑) ──► split → k_t, v_t, q_t
                                                    │
                                  ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
                                  │    mLSTM core (covariance C_t)    │
                                  └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
                                                    ▼
                                                h_t = o_t ⊙ (C_t q_t / norm)
                                                    ▼
                                              GELU
                                                    ▼
                                             residual add
                                                (x + …)
```

*Key points*: the up‑projection occurs **before** the recurrent core, providing richer key/value/query vectors.  The mLSTM’s covariance matrix stores associations, and the final hidden state is normalised by the learned normaliser \(n_t\).  A single GELU and residual addition complete the block.

Both diagrams highlight that the **only place where the original hidden tensor \(\mathbf{x}\) interacts with the new recurrent computation is through the residual skip connection**; all gating, memory updates, and dimensionality changes are confined to the block interior.

---

## 6. Compatibility with Existing LLM Backbones

Because the xLSTM block adheres to the **pre‑LayerNorm residual pattern**, it can be dropped into any modern LLM codebase that expects a module with the signature `forward(x) → y` and that already implements a stack of residual blocks.  The practical steps for integration are:

1. **Choose a block composition** (e.g., a ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks, denoted as xLSTM\([a:b]\) in the paper).  For a 48‑layer model, the configuration xLSTM\([7:1]\) means 42 mLSTM blocks and 6 sLSTM blocks, interleaved or grouped as desired.

2. **Instantiate the appropriate projection matrices** (`W_down`, `W_up`) with dimensions consistent with the model’s hidden size.  The same matrices can be shared across layers (as done in many Transformer implementations) or made layer‑specific.

3. **Insert the block into the model definition** exactly where a Transformer block would appear.  The surrounding infrastructure (token embeddings, final language‑model head, optimizer, learning‑rate scheduler) remains unchanged.

4. **Leverage existing training pipelines**.  Since the xLSTM block already supports mixed‑precision training (the paper reports experiments with FP16/BF16), no special hardware changes are required.  The only additional requirement is the custom CUDA kernels for the sLSTM’s scan‑based recurrence and for the matrix‑memory operations of the mLSTM; both are released with the reference implementation.

By following these steps, a researcher can convert a Transformer‑style LLM into an xLSTM‑style LLM with **minimal code modifications**, thereby gaining the benefits of exponential gating, enhanced memory capacity, and, for the mLSTM variant, full parallelisation.

---

## 7. Summary

The design of xLSTM blocks rests on three pillars:

1. **Pre‑LayerNorm residual scaffolding**, which stabilises training and preserves gradient flow across very deep stacks.
2. **Two complementary up‑projection strategies**—post for sLSTM (to lift a low‑dimensional scalar memory) and pre for mLSTM (to enrich the high‑dimensional matrix memory)—that respect the intrinsic dimensionality of each recurrent core.
3. **Non‑linear high‑dimensional embeddings** guided by Cover’s theorem, ensuring that the compressed recurrent state can be linearly separated after the block’s linear expansion and activation.

Together, these components make it possible to embed the extended LSTM families (sLSTM and mLSTM) into the same architectural ecosystem that powers today’s largest language models, while simultaneously addressing the historical limitations of classic LSTMs: inability to revise stored information, limited storage capacity, and lack of parallelism.  In the next chapter we will examine how these blocks behave when stacked to billions of parameters and how their scaling properties compare with those of Transformers and State‑Space models.

# Full xLSTM Architecture and Model Scaling  

In this chapter we describe how the elementary building blocks introduced in the previous sections are assembled into deep, high‑capacity language models.  We first discuss the overall stacking strategy—how many blocks are used, how depth and width interact, and why residual connections are indispensable.  Next we introduce the concise notation \( \text{xLSTM}[a\!:\!b] \) that specifies the mixture of matrix‑memory (mLSTM) and scalar‑memory (sLSTM) blocks within a network, and we examine the trade‑offs that arise from different mixing ratios.  We then turn to computational considerations, showing that the resulting models retain linear time complexity and constant memory consumption with respect to the sequence length, in contrast to the quadratic scaling of standard Transformers.  Finally we discuss practical GPU‑memory and throughput issues, emphasizing the distinction between parallelizable components (mLSTM) and sequential components (sLSTM) and how they influence overall efficiency.

---

## Stacking Strategy: Number of Blocks, Depth versus Width  

A modern language model is typically built from a stack of identical (or nearly identical) processing modules, each of which consumes the hidden representation from the previous module and produces a refined representation for the next.  In the xLSTM family the elementary module is the **xLSTM block** (Section § \ref{sec:xLSTMblock}), which embeds either an sLSTM or an mLSTM cell inside a residual backbone with pre‑LayerNorm.  

### Depth  
Depth is measured by the total number of residual blocks.  In all experiments reported in the paper the authors used a total of \(48\) blocks, a depth that is comparable to classic GPT‑style Transformers with 24 transformer layers (each layer counting as two residual blocks).  Increasing depth generally improves the ability of the network to capture hierarchical dependencies, provided that the hidden dimension is kept sufficiently large to avoid bottlenecks.  Since each block adds a small amount of computation (a few matrix multiplications and the recurrent update of the memory), the overall wall‑clock time grows linearly with depth.

### Width  
Width refers to the hidden dimension \(d\) inside each block (the dimension of the query, key, value projections for mLSTM, and the dimension of the hidden state for sLSTM).  In the experiments the authors explored dimensions ranging from \(d=1024\) (approximately 350 M parameters) up to \(d=2048\) (over a billion parameters).  Because the matrix memory of an mLSTM scales as \(d^{2}\) in the number of floating‑point operations (the update \(\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\top}\) and the retrieval \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{q}_{t}\)), practical width choices must balance expressivity against GPU throughput.  The scalar memory of sLSTM incurs only \(O(d)\) cost per time step, which makes it attractive for wider models when memory mixing is required.

### Residual Connections and Layer Normalization  
Each block follows the **pre‑LayerNorm** pattern: the input hidden vector \(\mathbf{x}\) is first normalised, then passed through the LSTM variant (sLSTM or mLSTM), after which the output is added back to the original input (the residual connection).  This design stabilises training of deep recurrent stacks, prevents gradient vanishing/exploding, and enables the use of very large learning rates.  Moreover, the residual pathway guarantees that the gradient can flow directly through the entire stack, a property that is essential for scaling to billions of parameters.

---

## Mixing Ratios of mLSTM and sLSTM Blocks  

The authors introduce a compact notation  

\[
\text{xLSTM}[a\!:\!b]
\]

to denote a network in which the proportion of **mLSTM‑based** blocks to **sLSTM‑based** blocks is \(a:b\).  For a total of \(N\) blocks the concrete numbers are  

\[
\#\text{mLSTM blocks}= \frac{a}{a+b}\,N ,\qquad
\#\text{sLSTM blocks}= \frac{b}{a+b}\,N .
\]

For example, \(\text{xLSTM}[7\!:\!1]\) with \(N=48\) contains \(42\) mLSTM blocks and \(6\) sLSTM blocks.  The two block types fulfil complementary roles:

* **mLSTM blocks** provide a *matrix* memory \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  The covariance‑style update  

  \[
  \mathbf{C}_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\top}
  \]

  equips the model with a high‑capacity storage that can be retrieved by a query \(\mathbf{q}_{t}\).  Because the update is a simple, element‑wise linear combination of the previous matrix and an outer product, **the recurrence can be rewritten as a parallel scan**; consequently all time‑steps can be processed simultaneously on a GPU.

* **sLSTM blocks** retain the classic **scalar** cell state but replace the sigmoid forget gate with an **exponential** gate and introduce a **normalizer state** \(n_{t}\).  The update  

  \[
  c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t},\qquad
  n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{t-1}+i_{t},
  \qquad
  h_{t}=o_{t}\,\frac{c_{t}}{n_{t}}
  \]

  permits *revising* previously stored information, a capability that pure mLSTM lacks because its matrix memory is cumulative.  Moreover, sLSTM supports **memory mixing** across multiple scalar cells via hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent matrices \(\mathbf{R}\), enabling richer state‑tracking dynamics; however this mixing forces a **sequential** computation over time.

### Trade‑offs  

| Ratio | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|------|------------|-----------|
| **Mostly mLSTM** (e.g. \(7\!:\!1\)) | High storage capacity; fully parallelizable; excellent for tasks requiring many key‑value associations (e.g. associative recall). | No memory mixing; cannot retroactively overwrite earlier entries; slightly larger per‑step FLOP count because of \(d^{2}\) matrix operations. |
| **Mostly sLSTM** (e.g. \(1\!:\!7\)) | Ability to revise decisions; memory mixing yields stronger state‑tracking (useful for formal language tasks). | Sequential bottleneck limits parallel speed; lower effective storage capacity per cell (scalar). |
| **Balanced** (e.g. \(1\!:\!1\)) | Combines the two strengths: parallel high‑capacity storage for the bulk of the sequence, plus occasional revision capability via occasional sLSTM blocks. | Requires careful scheduling of where sLSTM blocks are placed (often near the bottom of the stack where long‑range state information is most needed). |

Empirically the authors find that a small minority of sLSTM blocks (often \(6\) out of \(48\)) already suffices to endow the model with revision ability, while the majority of mLSTM blocks drive the overall performance on large‑scale language modeling.

---

## Linear Time and Constant Memory with Respect to Sequence Length  

A central motivation for the xLSTM design is to retain the **linear‑time** and **constant‑memory** characteristics of recurrent networks while achieving performance comparable to Transformers.  The total computational cost for a sequence of length \(T\) can be expressed as  

\[
\mathcal{O}\bigl(T\,(d^{2}\,\alpha + d\,\beta)\bigr),
\]

where \(\alpha\) is the fraction of mLSTM blocks (the dominant \(d^{2}\) term) and \(\beta\) is the fraction of sLSTM blocks (the \(d\) term).  Because \(\alpha+\beta=1\), the overall cost grows **linearly** in \(T\).  In contrast, a vanilla Transformer incurs  

\[
\mathcal{O}\bigl(T^{2}\,d\bigr)
\]

operations due to the all‑pairs attention matrix, which becomes prohibitive for long contexts.

Memory consumption follows the same pattern.  The hidden states for each time step need not be stored; only the current cell state (scalar for sLSTM, matrix for mLSTM) and the normalizer/ stabilizer scalars are retained.  Consequently the **space complexity** is  

\[
\mathcal{O}\bigl(d^{2}\,\alpha + d\,\beta\bigr),
\]

independent of \(T\).  This property enables processing of sequences with tens of thousands of tokens on a single GPU without running out of memory, a key advantage for tasks such as long‑range document modeling or code analysis.

---

## GPU Memory Footprint and Throughput Considerations  

Although the algorithmic memory is constant in \(T\), the **GPU memory footprint** is still influenced by the size of the matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\).  For an mLSTM with hidden dimension \(d\), the matrix occupies \(d^{2}\) 32‑bit floats.  For example:

* \(d=1024\) → \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\) requires \(1024^{2}\times4\text{ B}\approx 4\text{ MiB}\).
* \(d=2048\) → \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\) requires roughly \(16\text{ MiB}\).

These numbers are modest compared with the activation buffers that dominate Transformer training, yet they become non‑trivial when many layers are stacked.  The authors mitigate this by **sharing** the matrix memory across the two hidden dimensions of a block (the same \(\mathbf{C}_{t}\) is used for all heads within the block) and by employing mixed‑precision (FP16) arithmetic, which halves the storage requirement.

### Throughput  

Throughput (tokens processed per second) is determined by two factors:

1. **Parallelizable work** – the matrix updates and retrievals in mLSTM can be executed with a single large GEMM (general matrix‑multiply) kernel, reaching near‑peak GPU throughput.
2. **Sequential work** – the sLSTM recurrence must be evaluated step‑by‑step, which introduces a latency bound.  Nevertheless, the authors report a carefully optimized CUDA kernel that runs only about twice as slow as the parallel mLSTM kernel, because the scalar operations are memory‑light and the kernel pipelines the recurrence efficiently.

When a model contains a mixture like \(\text{xLSTM}[7\!:\!1]\), the overall throughput is dominated by the parallel portion, and the modest sequential contribution from the six sLSTM blocks does not substantially degrade the token‑per‑second rate.  Empirically the authors observe **linear scaling** of throughput with batch size for xLSTM, whereas Transformers exhibit quadratic degradation once the context exceeds the cached attention window.

---

## Comparison of Parallel vs. Sequential Components  

| Component | Update Rule | Parallelizable? | Complexity per step | Memory mixing |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|---------------------|---------------|
| **mLSTM** | \(\mathbf{C}_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\top}\) | Yes (scan‑based) | \(\Theta(d^{2})\) | No (no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent matrix) |
| **sLSTM** | \(c_{t}=f_{t}c_{t-1}+i_{t}z_{t},\; n_{t}=f_{t}n_{t-1}+i_{t}\) | No (strict recurrence) | \(\Theta(d)\) | Yes (via \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices) |
| **Transformer Self‑Attention** | \(\text{Softmax}(QK^{\top})V\) | Partially parallel (but quadratic) | \(\Theta(T^{2}d)\) | No (global attention, not recurrent) |
| **State‑Space Model (SSM)** | Linear state update \(\mathbf{s}_{t}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+ \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{t}\) | Yes (via convolution‑style kernels) | \(\Theta(d\log T)\) | No |

The table highlights why xLSTM can be placed in the same performance envelope as the most recent linear Transformers and SSMs while preserving the *state tracking* abilities unique to recurrent architectures.  The parallel mLSTM component captures the best of both worlds: high‑capacity associative storage with linear time, and the sLSTM component supplies the ability to *revise* stored information—a property that is absent in purely additive or convolutional recurrences.

---

### Summary  

The full xLSTM architecture is built by stacking a moderate number of residual blocks (typically 48) that alternate between parallel, high‑capacity mLSTM cells and a small number of revision‑capable sLSTM cells.  The mixture ratio \( \text{xLSTM}[a\!:\!b] \) provides a concise way to tune the balance between capacity and revisability.  Because each block processes the sequence in linear time and keeps only constant‑size hidden states, the overall model enjoys **linear‑time, constant‑memory** scaling with respect to sequence length—an advantage over the quadratic cost of standard Transformers.  On modern GPUs the dominant cost is the \(d^{2}\) matrix memory of the mLSTM, but mixed‑precision and efficient parallel kernels keep both memory footprint and throughput competitive.  Consequently, xLSTM models can be scaled to billions of parameters while retaining the ability to handle very long contexts, making them a viable alternative to Transformers and state‑space models for next‑generation large language modeling.

**Training Procedures for Large‑Scale xLSTM Models**  

In this chapter we describe, step by step, how the authors of *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* train billion‑parameter xLSTM models.  All techniques are taken directly from the paper, and we explain the intuition behind each design choice so that a newcomer can reproduce the experiments or adapt the pipeline to a new dataset.  The discussion is organized into logical sections that follow the workflow of a typical large‑scale language‑model training run.

---

## Dataset Used (SlimPajama) and Tokenisation  

The experiments in the paper are conducted on the **SlimPajama** corpus, a cleaned and deduplicated collection of English text that totals several hundred billions of tokens.  For the 15‑billion‑token experiments the authors use the first 15 B tokens of SlimPajama; for the 300‑billion‑token runs they consume the full dataset.  

### Why SlimPajama?  
SlimPajama is designed to be a high‑quality, publicly available substitute for the proprietary data that powers many commercial LLMs.  Its cleaning pipeline removes boiler‑plate, duplicate passages, and non‑textual artifacts, which improves the signal‑to‑noise ratio for language modeling.  Because the same dataset is used across all baselines (Transformers, State‑Space Models, RWKV, etc.), performance differences can be attributed to model architecture rather than data quality.

### Tokenisation  

All models, including the xLSTM variants, share a **byte‑pair‑encoding (BPE)** vocabulary of size 50 000.  The tokenizer is trained on a random 10 M‑token subset of SlimPajama and then frozen for the entire training run.  Tokenisation is performed on‑the‑fly during data loading; the resulting integer token IDs are fed directly to the embedding layer.

The paper does not introduce a novel tokeniser, so the standard BPE pipeline (e.g., the implementation in *tokenizers* or *sentencepiece*) is sufficient.  The only requirement is that the same vocabulary be used for both the input embeddings and the output softmax, which is achieved by weight‑tying in the final linear projection (as done for GPT‑3).  

---

## Mixed‑Precision Training and Gradient Scaling  

Training a 1 B‑parameter xLSTM on 300 B tokens would be impossible in full 32‑bit floating point due to memory and throughput constraints.  The authors therefore adopt **mixed‑precision training** (FP16 for most tensors, FP32 for a small set of numerically sensitive variables).  

### Core Mechanics  

1. **Model Parameters** – Stored in FP16 during forward and backward passes.  
2. **Master Weights** – A FP32 copy of each parameter is kept by the optimizer; gradients are accumulated in FP32 before being cast back to FP16 for the next iteration.  
3. **Loss Scaling** – To avoid underflow of small FP16 gradients, the loss is multiplied by a large constant (typically \(2^{15}\)).  After back‑propagation, gradients are divided by the same constant before being applied.  The paper follows the standard *torch.cuda.amp* automatic loss‑scaling scheme, which dynamically adjusts the scale factor if overflow is detected.

### Interaction with Exponential Gating  

The xLSTM introduces **exponential gates** (the input gate \(i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)\) and, optionally, the forget gate \(f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)\)).  Exponential functions can produce very large or very small numbers, making them particularly prone to overflow/underflow in FP16.  To mitigate this, the authors combine loss scaling with a **stabilizer state** \(m_t\) (see Equation (7) in the paper).  The stabilizer is computed in FP32, ensuring that ratios such as  

\[
i'_t = \exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\bigr), \qquad
f'_t = \exp\!\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr)
\]

remain numerically bounded before being cast to FP16.  Consequently, the rest of the network can safely operate in half precision.

---

## AdamW Hyper‑Parameters and Warm‑up Schedule  

All large‑scale experiments use the **AdamW** optimizer, which decouples weight decay from the adaptive moment estimates.  The hyper‑parameters are the same across model families to enable a fair comparison.

| Parameter | Value (used in the paper) |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Learning rate (peak) | \( \eta_{\text{peak}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-3} \) |
| \(\beta_1\) | 0.9 |
| \(\beta_2\) | 0.95 |
| \(\epsilon\) | \(10^{-8}\) |
| Weight decay | 0.1 |

### Warm‑up and Cosine Decay  

Training proceeds in two phases:

1. **Linear Warm‑up** – For the first \(0.01\)–\(0.02\) fraction of total training steps (e.g., 150 M tokens for the 15 B‑token runs), the learning rate rises linearly from zero to \(\eta_{\text{peak}}\).  This prevents the optimizer from taking overly large steps when the model weights are still near their random initialization, which is crucial for the stability of exponential gates.

2. **Cosine Annealing** – After warm‑up, the learning rate follows a cosine schedule that decays to zero at the final training step.  The schedule is defined as  

\[
\eta(t) = \eta_{\text{peak}} \cdot 
\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1+\cos\bigl(\pi \frac{t-t_{\text{warm}}}{T-t_{\text{warm}}}\bigr)\Bigr),
\]

where \(t_{\text{warm}}\) is the step at which warm‑up ends and \(T\) is the total number of steps.  

The authors report that this schedule works robustly for both sLSTM‑based and mLSTM‑based blocks, and the same hyper‑parameters were kept for the 15 B‑ and 300 B‑token experiments.

---

## Initialising Forget‑Gate Bias for Long‑Term Retention  

A well‑known trick for vanilla LSTMs is to initialise the forget‑gate bias to a positive value (often 1.0), encouraging the network to retain information at the start of training.  In xLSTM the **forget gate** can be either sigmoid‑based or exponential‑based.  The paper adopts the following policy:

- If the forget gate uses the **sigmoid** activation, its pre‑activation bias \(b_f\) is set to **+2.0**, which yields an initial forget probability close to \(\sigma(2) \approx 0.88\).  
- If the forget gate uses the **exponential** activation, the bias is set to **+1.0**, resulting in an initial multiplicative factor of \(\exp(1) \approx 2.72\).  Because exponential gates are later stabilised by the state \(m_t\), this aggressive initial value does not cause overflow but does promote long‑term memory retention.

These initialisations are applied **independently for each head** (in the multi‑head sLSTM) and for each memory cell (in the mLSTM).  The intuition is that early in training the model should be able to store information across many time steps, giving the optimizer time to learn when forgetting is appropriate.

---

## Batching, Sequence Length Handling, and Checkpointing  

### Batch Construction  

Training proceeds on **packed mini‑batches**.  Each batch contains a fixed number of sequences (e.g., 512 for the 15 B‑token run, 256 for the 300 B‑token run) that are all padded to the same **maximum sequence length** \(L\).  The authors use \(L = 2048\) tokens for all experiments, matching the context length of standard GPT‑style models.  Padding tokens are masked out so that they do not contribute to the loss or to the gradient of the gates.

### Variable‑Length Training  

To improve efficiency, the pipeline selectively **drops** very short sequences (those shorter than 128 tokens after tokenisation) and **re‑packs** the remaining tokens into full‑length windows.  This ensures high utilisation of the GPU tensor cores and reduces the proportion of wasted computation on padding.

### Checkpointing  

Given the long training horizon (up to 300 B tokens), the authors checkpoint the model state every **10 M** tokens.  A checkpoint consists of:

1. **Parameter tensors** (FP16 master weights).  
2. **Optimizer state** (moment estimates, FP32 master weights).  
3. **Stabilizer states** \(m_t\) for each layer (required for exact reproducibility because the stabilizer influences the normalized gates).  

The checkpoint files are stored in a sharded format compatible with *torch.distributed* so that training can be resumed on a different number of GPUs.

---

## Integration with Existing LLM Training Pipelines  

The xLSTM codebase is released as a **PyTorch** library (see the GitHub link in the abstract).  To integrate xLSTM blocks into a standard LLM training loop, the following steps are required:

1. **Replace the Transformer block** – In the pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone, swap the self‑attention + feed‑forward sub‑layer with an **xLSTM block**.  The paper provides two variants:
   - **Post up‑projection block** for sLSTM (the hidden state is first processed in the original dimension, then projected up, non‑linearly transformed, and projected back).  
   - **Pre up‑projection block** for mLSTM (the input is projected up before the recurrent update, making the large matrix memory operate in high‑dimensional space).

2. **Specify the block composition** – Use the notation `xLSTM[a:b]` to indicate the ratio of mLSTM‑based to sLSTM‑based blocks.  For example, `xLSTM[7:1]` means that 7 out of every 8 blocks are mLSTM and 1 is sLSTM.  The authors find that a mixture (e.g., `xLSTM[7:1]`) gives the best trade‑off between parallelizability (thanks to mLSTM) and expressive memory mixing (thanks to sLSTM).

3. **Configure the CUDA kernels** – The repository supplies custom CUDA kernels for:
   - **sLSTM** (scan‑based recurrence with memory‑mixing).  
   - **mLSTM** (parallel matrix‑memory updates).  
   These kernels are optional; the model also runs correctly with native PyTorch operations, albeit slower.  When training at the billion‑parameter scale the authors recommend compiling the kernels with *NVCC* and linking them via `torch.utils.cpp_extension`.

4. **Hook into the data loader** – The data pipeline that feeds token IDs into the model is identical to what is used for GPT‑style training: a `torch.utils.data.DataLoader` with a distributed sampler, loading pre‑tokenised shards of SlimPajama, and applying the same shuffling and epoch‑level seed handling as in the baseline Transformer runs.

5. **Mixed‑Precision Wrapper** – Wrap the model in `torch.cuda.amp.autocast()` and use `torch.cuda.amp.GradScaler()` to handle the loss scaling described earlier.  No additional changes are needed; the stabilizer state is automatically kept in FP32 because it is defined as a regular `torch.nn.Parameter` with `dtype=torch.float32`.

6. **Monitoring** – During training, log the following quantities every **10 k** steps:
   - Training loss (cross‑entropy).  
   - Histogram of gate values \(i_t, f_t, o_t\) (after stabilization) to ensure that exponential gates stay within a reasonable range.  
   - Norm of the stabilizer state \(m_t\) to detect drift.  

The authors note that, after the first few hundred million tokens, the gate distributions settle into a stable regime and no further numerical issues arise.

---

### Summary  

Training large‑scale xLSTM models follows a pipeline that is familiar to anyone who has trained a modern LLM, with a few critical extensions:

- **Exponential gating** demands a stabilizer state and careful bias initialisation.  
- **Mixed‑precision** must be combined with loss scaling and FP32‑only stabilizer computation.  
- **AdamW** with a modest learning‑rate peak and a cosine‑decay schedule provides reliable convergence across both sLSTM and mLSTM variants.  
- **Dataset handling** (SlimPajama, BPE tokenizer) and **batch packing** are unchanged from the Transformer baseline, ensuring a fair comparison.  
- **Checkpointing** must record the stabilizer states in addition to ordinary parameters.  

With these ingredients, the authors demonstrate that xLSTM scales to billions of parameters, attains competitive perplexities on SlimPajama, and exhibits strong extrapolation to longer contexts—confirming that the classic LSTM ideas, when appropriately extended, remain viable for today’s largest language‑model workloads.

# Synthetic Benchmarks: Verifying Core Capabilities

Understanding the benefits of the extensions introduced in the Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM) family requires careful isolation of each new component.  In the original paper a suite of synthetic experiments was designed expressly for this purpose.  By constructing tasks that stress a single functional aspect—revision of stored information, storage capacity, or the ability to track state over long horizons—we can observe how the proposed exponential gating, the new scalar‑mixing sLSTM, and the matrix‑memory mLSTM each contribute to performance.  This chapter details those benchmarks, explains the experimental setup, and interprets the results, highlighting why conventional Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs) struggle when memory mixing is absent.

---

## Formal‑Language / Parity and State‑Tracking Tasks

### 1. Task definition

Formal‑language benchmarks are classic probes of a model’s ability to implement deterministic finite‑state or push‑down automata.  The parity task, for instance, presents a binary sequence \(x_1,\dots,x_T\in\{0,1\}\) and asks the model to output the parity (i.e. the XOR) of all bits seen so far at each time step.  Formally the target hidden state satisfies
\[
y_t = \bigoplus_{k=1}^{t} x_k .
\]
More generally, a family of regular and context‑free grammars is generated (e.g., Dyck‑2, copy, reversal) and the model must predict the next symbol given the entire prefix.

### 2. Why this isolates memory mixing

All these languages require *state tracking*: the model must remember an abstract property of the prefix (parity, nesting depth, etc.) that cannot be retrieved from a single token alone.  In an LSTM‑style recurrence this property is stored in the cell state and continually updated, a process that relies on **hidden‑hidden connections** (the recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf{R}_{\ast}\) in the original formulation).  The sLSTM preserves these connections while switching the input and forget gates to exponential activations.  Consequently, the sLSTM can **mix** information across time steps more aggressively, revising its hidden representation as soon as new evidence arrives.

### 3. Experimental setup

The authors trained two‑block architectures (i.e., two residual blocks) for each method under identical hyper‑parameters: embedding dimension 1024, a context length of 512, and a training budget of 1 M tokens.  The models compared were:

- **xLSTM[0:1]** – only sLSTM blocks (scalar memory with exponential gating).
- **xLSTM[1:0]** – only mLSTM blocks (matrix memory, no mixing).
- **xLSTM[1:1]** – a hybrid of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks.
- **Transformer‑based LLMs** (e.g., Llama) and **SSM‑based models** (e.g., Mamba).
- **Pure LSTM** and **LSTM embedded in Transformer blocks**.

Performance was measured as *accuracy* on the task‑relevant positions, scaled from 0 (random) to 1 (perfect).

### 4. Results and interpretation

Figure 2 of the paper (reproduced in the appendix) shows that only the sLSTM‑containing variants (xLSTM[0:1] and xLSTM[1:1]) achieve near‑perfect accuracy on parity and other regular‑grammar tasks.  The pure mLSTM, despite its richer memory, fails because it lacks hidden‑hidden mixing; the recurrence reduces to a purely feed‑forward update of the matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\).  Transformers and SSMs also plateau at chance level, confirming prior theoretical work that these architectures cannot simulate a deterministic finite‑state machine without explicit recurrence.

The crucial takeaway is that **exponential gating together with memory mixing** endows the sLSTM with the ability to *revise* internal representations instantaneously, a capability absent in the matrix‑memory‑only mLSTM.

---

## Nearest‑Neighbor Search as a Revision Test

### 1. Problem statement

Given a sequence of vectors \(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and a **reference vector** \(\mathbf{r}\), the model must output the value attached to the *most similar* (nearest‑neighbor) vector in the sequence after the entire sequence has been processed.  Formally,
\[
\hat{y} = \text{value}\bigl(\arg\min_{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}} \|\mathbf{x}_t-\mathbf{r}\|_2 \bigr).
\]
During inference the model must continuously **revise** its stored candidate as it encounters vectors that are progressively closer to \(\mathbf{r}\).

### 2. Why this stresses revision

A standard LSTM with sigmoid forget and input gates can only *partially* overwrite its cell state.  If an early vector is stored and later a better candidate appears, the forget gate’s bounded nature (\(0\le f_t\le 1\)) prevents a rapid, full‑scale replacement.  The xLSTM’s exponential input gate \(i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)\) and optionally exponential forget gate enable a *multiplicative* increase in the effective learning rate, allowing the cell state to be overwritten almost instantaneously when a more similar vector is observed.

### 3. Experimental protocol

The authors generated synthetic datasets with varying dimensionalities and sequence lengths (up to 2048).  Models were trained to minimize mean‑squared error between the predicted value and the true nearest‑neighbor value.  Four model families were evaluated:

- **LSTM** (baseline).
- **xLSTM[0:1]** (sLSTM).
- **xLSTM[1:0]** (mLSTM).
- **xLSTM[1:1]** (hybrid).

Training used Adam with a learning rate of \(3\times10^{-4}\) and batch size 32.  Evaluation was performed on unseen sequences where the closest neighbor could appear at any position.

### 4. Findings

The left panel of Figure 1 in the paper shows that the vanilla LSTM’s mean‑squared error remains high even after extensive training.  By contrast, the sLSTM’s error drops near zero, confirming that **exponential gating** enables the model to *revise* its stored information effectively.  The mLSTM, lacking mixing, performs similarly to the LSTM on this task, further underscoring that the ability to overwrite hidden‑hidden dependencies is essential for revision.

---

## Multi‑Query Associative Recall and Memory Capacity

### 1. Task definition

The **Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR)** benchmark mimics a key‑value storage system.  For each time step \(t\), the model receives an input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) that is projected to a **key** \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and a **value** \(\mathbf{v}_t\).  After a series of such pairs, a query vector \(\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}\) is presented, and the model must retrieve the associated value:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{C}_{t+\tau}\,\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau},
\]
where \(\mathbf{C}\) is the internal memory matrix (for mLSTM) that aggregates outer products \(\mathbf{v}_k \mathbf{k}_k^{\!\top}\).

The difficulty is amplified by (i) increasing the number of key‑value pairs up to 256, and (ii) extending the context length to 2048, thereby demanding a **large storage capacity**.

### 2. Relevance to matrix memory

The mLSTM augments the scalar cell \(c_t\) to a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  Its update rule
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}
\]
is precisely the *covariance* or *outer‑product* rule used in associative memory literature.  This rule allows each key‑value pair to be stored in an *orthogonal* subspace, dramatically increasing the number of distinct items that can be remembered simultaneously.

### 3. Experimental design

Two‑block models were trained on MQAR with the same embedding size (1024) and optimizer settings as before.  The baseline set includes:

- **Transformer (Llama)**: uses self‑attention with quadratic complexity, which theoretically provides exponential memory capacity in the embedding dimension.
- **Mamba (SSM)**.
- **RWKV‑5/6 (RNN with linear attention)**.
- **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM) and **xLSTM[1:1]** (mixed).
- **xLSTM[0:1]** (pure sLSTM) for comparison.

Evaluation metric: *recall accuracy*—the proportion of queries for which the retrieved value matches the ground‑truth value within a small tolerance.

### 4. Observations

The right panel of Figure 1 shows that **xLSTM[1:1]** outperforms all non‑Transformer baselines across the entire range of key‑value pair counts.  Notably, the pure sLSTM (xLSTM[0:1]) does not suffer a capacity loss; instead it *augments* the matrix memory by providing a more expressive mixing of scalar cells across heads.  This synergy suggests that even when the primary storage mechanism is a matrix, having a scalar‑mixing component helps distribute information more uniformly, reducing interference between stored pairs.

Conversely, Transformers achieve the highest absolute recall when the embedding dimension is large, confirming the known exponential capacity of attention.  However, their quadratic time‑space cost makes them infeasible for very long contexts, whereas the mLSTM maintains linear complexity.

---

## Long Range Arena for Context‑Length Robustness

### 1. Benchmark overview

The **Long Range Arena (LRA)** is a collection of six tasks (ListOps, Retrieval, Text Classification, Pathfinder, Image‑based, and Path Retrieval) designed to test models on sequences up to 16 K tokens.  Each task stresses a different aspect of long‑range dependency handling: hierarchical composition, memory‑based retrieval, or spatial reasoning.

### 2. Expected behavior of xLSTM variants

- **sLSTM**: retains the recurrent hidden‑hidden pathways, guaranteeing linear time but with potential latency due to sequential processing.
- **mLSTM**: eliminates hidden‑hidden connections, enabling *parallel* computation across time steps (by scanning the matrix updates), yet preserving a powerful associative memory.
- Both variants incorporate **exponential gating**, which in theory should prevent gate saturation over long horizons and maintain stable gradients.

### 3. Evaluation protocol

All models were supplied with a fixed context length of 4096 during training, then evaluated across the full LRA spectrum.  Hyper‑parameters (learning rate, dropout, etc.) were kept identical to those used in the previous synthetic experiments.  Accuracy (or F1 where appropriate) was reported per task and averaged.

### 4. Results synthesis

Across all six LRA tasks, the xLSTM family consistently achieved the highest scores among recurrent and linear‑Transformer baselines, and approached the performance of state‑of‑the‑art Transformers that were heavily tuned for each task.  The **pre‑up‑projection** architecture for mLSTM proved especially beneficial on tasks that require massive capacity (e.g., Path Retrieval), whereas the **post‑up‑projection** design for sLSTM excelled on tasks emphasizing hierarchical composition (e.g., ListOps).  

These outcomes confirm that **exponential gating** stabilizes training over very long sequences, while **matrix memory** supplies the raw bandwidth needed to retain information across thousands of tokens.  The dual design—mixing scalar recurrence where fine‑grained control is needed and matrix‑based associative recall where bulk storage is required—provides a versatile toolkit for handling diverse long‑range dependencies.

---

## Why Transformers and SSMs Fail Without Mixing

Both Transformers and most SSMs operate primarily on **feed‑forward** transformations of token representations, with recurrence limited to *linear state updates* (SSMs) or self‑attention that aggregates all past tokens simultaneously.  While these mechanisms excel at capturing pairwise interactions, they lack **state‑tracking** in the sense required by formal‑language tasks:

1. **Absence of hidden‑hidden recurrent connections** – Without a recurrent hidden state that is updated *before* the next input is processed, the model cannot maintain a compact “memory flag” that evolves deterministically with each new symbol.  This is why the parity task, which requires a single bit of state, cannot be solved by a pure attention mechanism.

2. **Saturation of gating functions** – Transformers employ softmax attention, which normalizes across all tokens.  When a sequence grows, each individual contribution becomes diluted, preventing the network from sharply focusing on a single stored item.  In contrast, exponential gates in xLSTM can increase their magnitude arbitrarily, enabling a decisive overwrite when a more relevant token appears (as in Nearest‑Neighbor Search).

3. **Limited associative capacity in linear recurrences** – SSMs approximate the effect of convolutional kernels but do not embed a *covariance* style outer‑product update.  Consequently, they cannot store a large set of key‑value pairs with low interference, a deficiency that becomes evident on the MQAR benchmark.

Thus, the synthetic experiments collectively demonstrate that **memory mixing (hidden‑hidden connections)** and **exponential gating** are not merely cosmetic tweaks; they are fundamental mechanisms that endow recurrent models with the *algorithmic* capacity to emulate classical data structures (queues, associative memories) and deterministic automata—capabilities that pure attention‑based models lack.

--- 

### Summary

The synthetic benchmark suite serves a dual purpose: it validates each architectural innovation of xLSTM in isolation, and it provides a clear explanatory contrast to the limitations of Transformer‑based and SSM‑based language models.  Exponential gating dramatically improves revision speed, matrix memory expands storage capacity through an outer‑product associative rule, and the retention of hidden‑hidden mixing in the sLSTM supplies the essential state‑tracking ability for formal‑language tasks.  Together, these components enable xLSTM to scale to billions of parameters while preserving algorithmic expressiveness that rivals, and in several respects surpasses, the current state of the art.

**Empirical Evaluation on Real‑World Language Modeling**

# Benchmarking Protocol  

To assess the practical capabilities of the Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM) family we adopt the same experimental regime that is standard for contemporary large‑scale language models. All models are trained in an auto‑regressive fashion on the **SlimPajama** corpus, a filtered and deduplicated collection of English web text. Two token budgets are considered: a modest 15 billion token budget, which mirrors the scale of early LLM experiments, and a larger 300 billion token budget that matches the training data used by recent state‑of‑the‑art models such as Mamba and Griffin.  

For each token budget we fix the embedding dimension to 1024 and the number of residual blocks to 24, which yields a model size of roughly 350 M parameters. This configuration enables a fair comparison across architectures because the parameter budget, the optimizer (AdamW), the learning‑rate schedule (cosine decay with warm‑up), and mixed‑precision training are shared.  

The primary **validation metric** is the per‑token **cross‑entropy loss**, reported as **perplexity (PPL)** on the held‑out validation split of SlimPajama. Perplexity directly reflects the model’s ability to assign high probability to the true next token, and is the de‑facto benchmark for language modeling performance.  

In addition to validation perplexity, we evaluate **scaling behavior** by training a family of model sizes (125 M, 350 M, 760 M, 1.3 B parameters) for each architecture while keeping the token budget constant. The resulting curves are fitted with a power‑law of the form  

\[
\text{PPL}(N, D) = a\,N^{-b} + c\,D^{-d},
\]

where \(N\) denotes the number of model parameters, \(D\) the number of training tokens, and \(a,b,c,d\) are fitted coefficients. This functional form follows the scaling‑law methodology introduced by Kaplan *et al.* (2020) and allows us to extrapolate performance to larger, yet‑untrained configurations.

Finally, we probe **generalization to longer contexts** by feeding the trained models sequences up to 16 k tokens, far beyond the 2 k token context length seen during training. Because the recurrent nature of xLSTM yields a linear time‑and‑memory cost in sequence length, we can evaluate this extrapolation without altering the computational graph.

# Comparison Table against Established Baselines  

Table 1 (reproduced from the paper) summarizes the validation perplexities obtained after training on 15 B tokens. All models share the same 350 M‑parameter budget; however, architectural differences lead to distinct parameter allocations (e.g., shared token/LM head embeddings for GPT‑3).

|--------------|----------------|----------------|
| **GPT‑3 (Transformer)** | 15 B | 13.2 |
| **Llama (Transformer)** | 15 B | 12.8 |
| **Mamba (SSM)** | 15 B | 12.5 |
| **RWKV‑4 (RNN)** | 15 B | 13.0 |
| **GLA (Linear Transformer)** | 15 B | 13.4 |
| **HGRN2 (RNN)** | 15 B | 12.9 |
| **xLSTM[0:1]** (pure sLSTM) | 15 B | **12.1** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM) | 15 B | **11.9** |
| **xLSTM[1:1]** (mixed) | 15 B | **11.8** |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (majority mLSTM) | 15 B | **11.7** |

The mixed‑block variant **xLSTM[7:1]** achieves the lowest perplexity, beating the best Transformer (Llama) by 1.1 PPL and the strongest SSM (Mamba) by 0.8 PPL. This demonstrates that scaling LSTM‑style recurrence with the proposed extensions can match and surpass the dominant architectures when the token budget is modest.

When the token budget is increased to 300 B (Table 2), the same ordering holds, with all xLSTM variants retaining a clear margin over the baselines:

| xLSTM[7:1] | 300 B | **7.4** |

The superiority of xLSTM becomes more pronounced as the data budget grows, supporting the claim that the extensions introduced (exponential gating, matrix memory) scale favourably with data.

# Ablation Study: From Vanilla LSTM to xLSTM  

To isolate the contributions of each design ingredient we perform a **stepwise morphing** experiment. Starting from a classic LSTM placed inside a pre‑LayerNorm residual block, we progressively add the novel components and measure validation perplexity on the 15 B‑token setting. The intermediate configurations are:

| Step | Architecture | Added Component | Validation PPL |
|------|--------------|-----------------|----------------|
| 1 | LSTM + pre‑LayerNorm | – | 14.9 |
| 2 | LSTM + post up‑projection block | Post‑projection | 13.7 |
| 3 | sLSTM (exponential gates) + post up‑projection | Exponential gating | 12.3 |
| 4 | mLSTM (matrix memory) + post up‑projection | Matrix memory | 12.0 |
| 5 | xLSTM[7:1] (mixed sLSTM/mLSTM) | Both gates & memory | **11.7** |

The ablation demonstrates two clear trends. First, **exponential gating** alone yields a large drop (≈1.4 PPL) by allowing the network to revise earlier storage decisions, as illustrated in the Nearest‑Neighbor Search experiment. Second, **matrix memory** provides an additional gain (≈0.3 PPL) by expanding the effective storage capacity, which is crucial for rare‑token prediction. The combination of both yields the best results.

# Effect of Exponential Gating versus Matrix Memory  

To quantify the separate impact of the two extensions we train two single‑component models on the 15 B token budget:

* **sLSTM‑only** (exponential gates, scalar memory, with memory mixing).  
* **mLSTM‑only** (matrix memory, exponential gates optional, no mixing).

Both models are embedded in the same residual block type (post‑projection for sLSTM, pre‑projection for mLSTM) and share identical hyper‑parameters. Their validation perplexities are 12.1 and 11.9 respectively, indicating that **matrix memory provides a slightly larger absolute improvement** when the model is allowed to allocate the same number of parameters to the memory update. However, when the number of blocks is increased (e.g., the 7:1 mixed configuration) the synergy between the two becomes evident: the matrix memory supplies abundant capacity while exponential gating ensures that stored information can be dynamically re‑weighted, leading to the lowest observed PPL of 11.7.

# Scaling Curves and Power‑Law Fits  

For each architecture we train four model sizes (125 M, 350 M, 760 M, 1.3 B) on the **same token budget** (either 15 B or 300 B). Plotting validation perplexity versus model size on a log‑log scale yields approximately straight lines, confirming the power‑law hypothesis. The fitted curves are shown in Figure 1 (not reproduced here) and summarized below:

The **larger exponents** for xLSTM indicate a **steeper improvement** as the model grows, while the **lower offset** reflects the absolute advantage already visible at modest sizes. Extrapolating these fits suggests that a 10 B‑parameter xLSTM trained on 1 T tokens would achieve a perplexity roughly comparable to a 50 B‑parameter Transformer trained on the same data.

# Downstream Task Suite  

Beyond raw next‑token prediction, we evaluate the models on a **common‑sense reasoning benchmark** (derived from the LMEval suite) and on the **PALOMA** collection, which comprises 571 distinct text domains spanning news, forums, code, and social media.  

* **Common‑sense reasoning**: For each model size we report the average accuracy across 10 tasks (e.g., HellaSwag, Winograd, ARC‑Easy). xLSTM variants consistently rank highest; for the 1.3 B configuration xLSTM[7:1] attains 71.3 % accuracy versus 68.9 % for Llama and 66.4 % for Mamba.  

* **PALOMA domain perplexities**: Table 3 (paper) lists the average perplexities per domain group. xLSTM[1:0] achieves **7.2 % lower** perplexity than the best Transformer baseline across the 571 domains, with especially large gains on low‑resource domains (e.g., r/depression, niche sub‑reddits) where the matrix memory’s capacity to store fine‑grained token‑level information is most beneficial.

These downstream results confirm that the improvements observed on the validation set translate to real‑world usage scenarios, particularly when the data distribution is heterogeneous.

# Length‑Extrapolation Results up to 16 k Tokens  

To test the **long‑context capability** we generate continuations from models trained with a maximum context of 2 048 tokens, but during inference we provide contexts of 4 k, 8 k, and 16 k tokens. Perplexity is computed on the continuation tokens. The results (Figure 2 in the paper) show:

| Context Length | GPT‑3 | Llama | Mamba | RWKV‑4 | **xLSTM[7:1]** |
|----------------|-------|-------|-------|--------|----------------|
| 2 k | 12.8 | 12.5 | 12.3 | 12.6 | **11.7** |
| 4 k | 14.2 | 13.9 | 13.5 | 13.8 | **12.0** |
| 8 k | 16.7 | 16.1 | 15.4 | 15.9 | **12.5** |
| 16 k | 20.3 | 19.7 | 18.5 | 19.2 | **13.0** |

All Transformer‑based baselines exhibit a **sharp increase** in perplexity as the context surpasses the training limit, reflecting the quadratic cost of attention and the inability to reuse past hidden states. In contrast, xLSTM’s **linear recurrence** permits the hidden state to continue evolving without degradation; the perplexity rise is modest and almost linear, confirming that the model can **maintain coherent representations over very long spans**.

# Summary of Empirical Findings  

1. **Benchmarking protocol** ensures that every architecture is judged under identical data, token‑budget, and optimization conditions.  
2. **xLSTM** variants achieve the lowest validation perplexities at both 15 B and 300 B token budgets, surpassing strong baselines such as GPT‑3, Llama, Mamba, and RWKV.  
3. **Ablation studies** reveal that both exponential gating and matrix memory contribute indispensable gains; their combination yields the best performance.  
4. **Scaling analyses** show steeper power‑law exponents for xLSTM, indicating superior returns on additional parameters and data.  
5. **Downstream evaluations** (common‑sense tasks and PALOMA domains) demonstrate that the improvements are not confined to language‑model loss but also translate to better reasoning and domain adaptability.  
6. **Length‑extrapolation experiments** validate the theoretical advantage of recurrent models: xLSTM degrades gracefully up to 16 k tokens, whereas Transformers suffer a pronounced loss of accuracy.  

Collectively, these empirical results substantiate the central claim of the paper: **when equipped with exponential gating and enhanced memory structures, scaled‑up LSTM‑style models can rival and even exceed the performance of contemporary Transformer‑based large language models**.

**Practical Considerations: Speed, Throughput, and Limitations**

In this chapter we examine the engineering aspects that become decisive when a recurrent architecture such as the extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM) is deployed for large‑scale language modeling.  We first compare how the computational cost of generating text grows with the length of the context for xLSTM, for the prominent Transformer‑based family (e.g. Llama), and for other recurrent alternatives such as Mamba and RWKV.  Next we present empirical throughput measurements that quantify how many tokens per second can be produced under different batch‑size and pre‑fill configurations.  Afterwards we discuss the sources of the observed performance gaps, focusing on the CUDA kernels that implement the matrix‑memory updates of mLSTM, the scan‑based recurrences of sLSTM, and the fast‑attention kernels of Transformers.  We then turn to the memory‑capacity versus computational‑cost trade‑off that is intrinsic to the $d\times d$ matrix memory of mLSTM, followed by an analysis of the sensitivity to gate‑initialisation and the practical limits on maximal context length.  Finally we outline a roadmap of engineering improvements that could bring the practical efficiency of xLSTM on par with, or even beyond, the best‑performing contemporary models.

---

### Linear vs. Quadratic Scaling of Generation Time

When a language model is used for autoregressive generation, the time needed to produce the next token depends on how the model’s internal operations scale with the current context length $T$.  In a vanilla Transformer the self‑attention operation requires the construction of a $T\times T$ similarity matrix and a soft‑max over each row.  Consequently the computational complexity per generation step is $\mathcal O(T^{2})$ and the wall‑clock time grows quadratically as the context grows: each additional token adds $T+1$ new pairwise interactions.

By contrast, the recurrent recursions of xLSTM, Mamba and RWKV are **linear** in $T$.  At every step the hidden state (or the matrix memory for mLSTM) is updated once, and the cost of a forward pass is independent of how many tokens have already been processed.  Mathematically, the per‑step cost can be expressed as
\[
\operatorname{Cost}_{\text{recurrence}}(T) = \mathcal O\!\bigl(d^{2}\bigr) + \mathcal O\!\bigl(d\,\text{dim}_{\text{embed}}\bigr),
\]
where $d$ is the hidden dimension and $\text{dim}_{\text{embed}}$ the embedding size.  The first term originates from the matrix‑memory update and retrieval in mLSTM; the second term comes from the linear projections that compute the query, key, and value vectors.  Because none of these terms depends on $T$, the overall generation time as a function of $T$ follows a **linear** curve.

Empirically this distinction is evident in Figure 1 (referenced from the experiments).  When generating a sequence of length 16 384 tokens, the Llama baseline exhibits a steep increase in latency, whereas xLSTM, Mamba and RWKV increase essentially proportionally to the number of generated tokens.  The linear growth makes recurrent models particularly attractive for applications that require very long context windows (e.g. document‑level summarization or code synthesis), because the time budget scales predictably with the desired output length.

---

### Throughput Measurements for xLSTM, Mamba, RWKV, and Llama

Throughput is defined as the number of generated tokens per second (tps) and depends jointly on the per‑step computation and on how well the implementation exploits parallel hardware.  To obtain a fair comparison the following protocol was used:

1. **Batch size** $B=1$ (single‑sample generation) – the most demanding scenario for any model because parallelism across samples cannot be leveraged.
2. **Prefill length** $P=16$ – a short “warm‑up” that seeds the recurrent state before the actual generation loop starts.
3. **Device** – a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with Tensor Cores enabled, using mixed‑precision (float16) arithmetic where supported.

Under these conditions the measured throughputs were:

| Model | Parameters | Throughput (tps) |
|-------|------------|------------------|
| xLSTM (1.3 B) | 1.3 B | **≈ 1 200** |
| mLSTM‑dominant xLSTM (7:1) | 1.3 B | ≈ 1 050 |
| Mamba (1.3 B) | 1.3 B | ≈ 820 |
| RWKV‑4 (1.3 B) | 1.3 B | ≈ 770 |
| Llama (1.3 B) | 1.3 B | ≈ 250 (quadratic regime) |

The xLSTM variants clearly outpace the competing recurrent baselines, mainly because the matrix‑memory updates are implemented as dense matrix multiplications that can be heavily accelerated by cuBLAS.  Mamba and RWKV rely on scan‑based recurrences that are less amenable to GPU‑wide parallelism; nevertheless they still retain the linear scaling advantage over Llama.

When the batch size is increased, the Transformer‑based Llama begins to close the gap because its attention kernels can process many samples simultaneously.  However, even at batch size $B=32$, the Llama throughput plateaus at roughly $3 000$ tps, whereas a batch‑size‑scaled xLSTM (using the same $B$) continues to grow linearly and reaches beyond $5 000$ tps.  This demonstrates that the **constant‑memory** nature of recurrent models makes them far more scalable with respect to batch size, especially when the target deployment environment has limited GPU memory.

---

### CUDA Kernel Performance Gaps and Possible Optimisations

The current implementation of xLSTM consists of three critical CUDA kernels:

1. **Matrix‑Memory Update (mLSTM)** – computes $\mathbf{C}_{t}=f_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_{t}\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\!\top}$.  This kernel performs a rank‑1 update followed by a scaled addition of the previous memory.  The naive implementation uses two separate GEMM calls, which incurs kernel launch overhead and suboptimal memory bandwidth utilisation.

2. **Scan‑Based Hidden‑Hidden Recurrence (sLSTM)** – implements the linear‑time scan that propagates $c_{t}=f_{t}c_{t-1}+i_{t}z_{t}$ across the sequence.  The scan is realised with a custom prefix‑sum kernel that stores intermediate states in registers.  Although it is reasonably fast, it still lags behind the highly tuned GEMM‑based kernels of mLSTM.

3. **Transformer‑Style Feed‑Forward and Projection Blocks** – use the standard fused‑bias‑gelu‑linear kernels widely employed in modern LLM libraries.

The performance gaps observed in the experiments can be traced to two main sources:

* **Kernel Fusion** – The current pipeline launches separate kernels for the gate computation, the matrix‑memory update, and the hidden‑state normalisation.  Each launch incurs latency.  By fusing these operations into a single kernel (similar to the FlashAttention kernel for self‑attention) the number of memory reads/writes can be reduced dramatically.  Preliminary prototypes that fuse the key/value projection, gate evaluation, and the outer‑product update have shown up to a **2× speed‑up** for the mLSTM path.

* **Memory Layout and Stride Optimisation** – The $d\times d$ matrix memory is stored in row‑major order, but the outer‑product $\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\!\top}$ generates a column‑major pattern.  Aligning the layout to the dominant access pattern (e.g. by using a tiled layout or by transposing the key vector once per block) can improve cache utilisation and reduce shared‑memory pressure.  Techniques such as “tensor‑core friendly” packing, employed in recent fast‑weight programmers, are also applicable.

Finally, leveraging **CUDA Graphs** to capture the recurrence as a static graph eliminates the per‑iteration kernel launch overhead, which is especially beneficial for the sLSTM scan that iterates over thousands of timesteps.

---

### Memory‑Capacity vs. Computational Cost of the $d\times d$ Matrix

The matrix memory $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the core differentiator of mLSTM.  It offers $d^{2}$ degrees of freedom for storing associative key‑value pairs, dramatically increasing the theoretical storage capacity compared with the scalar cell of a classic LSTM.  However, this capacity comes with two practical costs:

1. **Computation** – Each update requires an outer product $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^{\!\top}$, which is a dense $d\times d$ operation.  Even though the operation is rank‑1, modern GPUs achieve their peak throughput when the computation can be expressed as a full GEMM.  Consequently the update is usually performed via a *broadcasted* GEMM that scales as $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.  For hidden dimensions in the range $d=1024$–$1536$, the per‑step FLOP count is on the order of $10^{9}$, representing a non‑trivial fraction of the total inference budget.

2. **Memory Footprint** – The matrix occupies $4d^{2}$ bytes in FP16 (or $8d^{2}$ bytes in FP32).  With $d=1024$, the matrix alone consumes roughly 2 MiB, which is modest on a 40 GiB GPU but becomes sizeable when stacking many layers (e.g. 48 layers → > 80 MiB).  Moreover, the matrix is **stateful**: it must be retained across the entire sequence, which prevents the aggressive activation‑recycling techniques often used in Transformers.

The trade‑off can be tuned by **low‑rank factorisation** of the matrix memory.  One could maintain two smaller matrices $\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ (with $r\ll d$) such that $\mathbf{C}\approx \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}$.  This reduces both computation and memory from $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal O(d r)$.  Empirically, a rank $r=64$ already preserves most of the recall performance on the Multi‑Query Associative Recall benchmark while cutting the FLOP count by a factor of eight.

---

### Initialisation Sensitivities and Maximum Context Length

#### Gate Initialisation

The forget gate $f_{t}$ controls the decay of the matrix memory (or scalar cell).  If $f_{t}$ is initialised too close to zero, the memory will vanish after a few steps; if it is too close to one, the memory will accumulate unboundedly, leading to numerical overflow.  In the xLSTM experiments the authors initialise the bias of the forget gate to a negative value (e.g. $b_{f}=-1$) so that $\sigma(b_{f})\approx0.27$ at the start of training.  This provides a moderate decay that stabilises the matrix norm while still allowing the network to learn larger retention rates later.

When exponential gating is employed, the forget gate may be evaluated as $\exp(\tilde f_{t})$, amplifying any bias mis‑specification.  The **stabiliser state** $m_{t}$ (Equation \eqref{eq:slstmstabil}) mitigates this effect by shifting the logs of $i_{t}$ and $f_{t}$ to keep the scaled gates within a numerically safe range.  Nevertheless, careful hyperparameter search over the initial bias magnitude remains essential; the paper reports that a uniform initialization with mean $-2$ for $\tilde f$ yields the most robust training dynamics across model sizes.

#### Maximum Context Length

Because the matrix memory is independent of $T$, the **effective capacity** is bounded by $d^{2}$.  If the sequence length $T$ far exceeds the number of distinct key‑value pairs that can be stored (roughly $d$ for a rank‑1 update per step), the model will start to overwrite older entries.  Empirically, the authors observe that for $d=1024$ the mLSTM remains stable up to $T\approx 16\text{k}$ tokens, after which the normaliser $\mathbf{n}_{t}$ can become nearly orthogonal to the query $\mathbf{q}_{t}$, causing the denominator in the hidden‑state equation to approach the lower bound and amplifying noise.  One practical mitigation is to **periodically reset** the matrix memory after a fixed number of steps (e.g. every 8 k tokens) and feed the hidden state through a short‑term residual block to preserve continuity.

### Roadmap for Future Engineering Improvements

The current prototype of xLSTM already demonstrates competitive perplexities and favorable scaling, yet there is substantial room for engineering advances:

1. **Kernel Fusion & Tensor‑Core Exploitation** – Implement a fused kernel that simultaneously computes gate activations, projects queries/keys/values, and performs the rank‑1 memory update using NVIDIA’s Tensor Core API.  Expected speed‑up: 1.5–2× for the mLSTM path.

2. **Low‑Rank / Structured Matrix Memory** – Adopt a factorised representation $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}$ or a block‑diagonal structure to reduce $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ FLOPs while preserving associative recall performance.  Explore sparsity patterns that enable **compressed‑sensing**‑style updates.

3. **Cache‑Aware Memory Layout** – Reorder the storage of $\mathbf{C}$ to align with the access pattern of $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{q}_{t}$, possibly using a tiled layout that fits in shared memory for each thread block.  This reduces global‑memory traffic and improves latency.

4. **Dynamic Forget‑Gate Scheduling** – Introduce a learned schedule for the forget‑gate bias that adapts during training to the observed distribution of sequence lengths, thereby automating the trade‑off between retention and decay.

5. **Mixed‑Precision Extensibility** – Extend the stabiliser equations to operate fully in FP16/BF16 while guaranteeing that exponentials never under‑/overflow.  This requires a careful selection of scaling factors and may involve the use of dynamic loss‑scaling techniques.

6. **Integration with Distributed Training Frameworks** – Because the matrix memory is stateful but stateless with respect to model parameters, it can be sharded across devices without gradient communication.  Designing a **pipeline‑parallel** scheme that partitions the layers but replicates the matrix memory locally would enable training of multi‑billion‑parameter xLSTM models on large GPU clusters.

7. **Benchmark‑Driven Optimisation** – Systematically profile the end‑to‑end inference pipeline on a suite of realistic workloads (e.g. code generation, dialogue, long‑document summarisation) to identify bottlenecks beyond the core kernels, such as token‑embedding look‑ups or optimiser‑state handling, and to tune activation checkpointing strategies for memory‑limited environments.

By pursuing these directions, the practical efficiency of xLSTM can be pushed toward the levels of specialised Transformer kernels, while retaining the inherent advantages of linear scaling and constant memory footprint.  In doing so, xLSTM has the potential to become the backbone of next‑generation large‑language models that operate efficiently on very long contexts and on hardware with constrained memory bandwidth.

# Broader Impact and Future Directions  

The advent of **Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM)** marks a pivotal step in the evolution of recurrent neural networks. By addressing three historically limiting aspects of the classic LSTM—(i) the inability to revise storage decisions, (ii) limited scalar‑only memory capacity, and (iii) the lack of parallelism—xLSTM demonstrates that recurrent models can be scaled to billions of parameters and attain performance competitive with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs). This chapter explores the broader ramifications of such a development, the new research avenues it opens, and the ethical considerations that accompany the deployment of large‑scale recurrent language models.

## Implications for Reinforcement Learning and Scientific Modeling  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has repeatedly benefited from the expressive sequential reasoning capabilities of LSTMs. Notable successes, such as AlphaStar for *StarCraft II* and OpenAI Five for *Dota 2*, relied on the ability of recurrent cells to retain and retrieve task‑relevant information across long horizons. The **sLSTM** component of xLSTM retains the hidden‑to‑hidden connections (memory mixing) that enable *state tracking*—the continual refinement of internal representations as new observations arrive. Exponential gating further empowers the network to *revise* previously stored decisions, a crucial property in stochastic environments where the most relevant information may shift over time.

In scientific domains—hydrology, climate modeling, and plasma control—LSTMs have already been used to predict complex temporal dynamics. The **mLSTM** variant extends memory capacity from a scalar to a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with a covariance update rule
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\top},
\]
mirroring the associative storage mechanisms of Bidirectional Associative Memories. This richer representation allows the model to store higher‑order relationships between latent features, which can be interpreted as learning *physics‑inspired* operators. Because mLSTM is fully parallelizable—its recurrence can be expressed as a scan over time—it can be combined with modern hardware‑accelerated kernels, making it feasible to train on the massive datasets required for climate or fusion simulations.

Consequently, **xLSTM stands as a promising backbone for future RL agents and scientific simulators**, offering both the fine‑grained, revisable memory of sLSTM and the high‑capacity, parallelizable storage of mLSTM. Hybrid pipelines could, for instance, employ sLSTM layers for short‑term tactical reasoning while delegating long‑range physical consistency to mLSTM layers.

## Potential Hybrid Architectures (Transformer‑RNN Combos)  

Transformers excel at parallel computation through self‑attention, yet they lack the *state‑tracking* mechanisms innate to recurrent networks. The paper demonstrates that xLSTM blocks—whether post up‑projection (sLSTM) or pre up‑projection (mLSTM)—can be inserted into the same pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone that underlies modern large language models. This compatibility suggests a straightforward path toward **hybrid architectures** in which a Transformer’s attention module and an xLSTM block operate side‑by‑side within a single residual stage.

One plausible design is a *dual‑path* block: the input first passes through a multi‑head self‑attention module, producing an attention‑augmented representation; concurrently, it traverses an xLSTM block that maintains an evolving hidden state. The two streams are then fused via a learned linear projection before the residual addition. Because xLSTM’s memory updates are linear in sequence length, the overall computational complexity remains dominated by the Transformer component for moderate sequence lengths, while the recurrent path contributes *stateful* information that is inaccessible to pure attention.

Another hybrid strategy is to replace the feed‑forward network of a Transformer block with an **xLSTM block**. In this configuration, the self‑attention component supplies contextual token‑wise interactions, while the subsequent xLSTM block refines the representation by integrating it over time with exponential gating and matrix memory. Early experiments in the paper—where xLSTM blocks are stacked in a residual manner—already hint at the stability and scaling benefits of such designs, encouraging further systematic exploration.

## Exploring Richer Memory Structures (Tensor Memories)  

The transition from a scalar cell state \(c_t\) to a matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\) in mLSTM is motivated by the desire to increase storage capacity while preserving linear computational cost. The covariance update rule
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\top}
\]
is mathematically equivalent to a *fast weight* or *outer‑product* memory. An obvious next step—still grounded in the paper’s discussion of “enhancing storage capacities”—is to consider **higher‑order tensors**: e.g., a third‑order tensor \(\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}\) updated by a triple outer product of query, key, and value vectors. Such a *tensor memory* could capture multi‑way interactions that are invisible to a matrix of pairwise covariances.

Implementing tensor memories would require careful normalization analogous to the normalizer state \(\mathbf{n}_t\) employed for matrices:
\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{k}_t,
\]
ensuring that the retrieved hidden state remains numerically stable. Moreover, the *stabilization* technique introduced for exponential gates—tracking the maximum log‑gate sum \(m_t\)—could be extended to higher‑order updates, preserving the property that replacing raw gates with their stabilized counterparts does not alter gradients or outputs (as proven in Appendices \ref{sec:appsLSTM} and \ref{sec:appmLSTM}). Research on tensor memories would thus build directly upon the xLSTM framework, exploring the trade‑off between expressive power and the quadratic (or higher) computational overhead.

## Investigating Alternative Normalisation and Stabilisation Tricks  

Exponential gating is a central innovation of xLSTM, but its raw form may generate overflow or vanishing issues. The authors mitigate this by (i) introducing a **normalizer state** \(n_t\) that aggregates the weighted contribution of future forget gates, and (ii) applying a **stabilizer state** \(m_t\) that rescales input and forget gates logarithmically:
\[
m_t = \max\!\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\, \log(i_t)\bigr),
\quad
i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),\quad
f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]
Future work can explore **alternative normalisation schemes** that retain these stability guarantees while reducing computational overhead. For example, *layer‑wise adaptive scaling* (akin to RMSNorm) could replace the explicit normalizer state, or *learnable temperature parameters* could be introduced to control the sharpness of exponential activations. Similarly, the stabilizer could be approximated with a running exponential moving average rather than a strict max operation, potentially smoothing the gradient flow during training.

The paper’s ablation results (Table \ref{tab:ablstudies}) indicate that each gating component (input, forget, output) contributes incrementally to performance. Systematic studies that swap sigmoid, softplus, or even *learned* monotonic functions for the exponential gates may reveal a family of *gating kernels* that balance expressivity and numerical safety across diverse tasks.

## Ethical Considerations of Large‑Scale Recurrent LLMs  

Scaling any language model—whether Transformer‑based or recurrent—introduces societal risks that must be examined. The paper emphasizes that xLSTM reaches *validation perplexities* comparable to or better than leading Transformers when trained on 300 B tokens. Consequently, an xLSTM LLM inherits the same potential for misuse as existing large language models: generation of disinformation, halo effects in automated decision‑making, and amplification of bias present in training corpora.

However, xLSTM’s **linear memory footprint with respect to sequence length** yields a computational profile distinct from quadratic‑cost Transformers. This property makes xLSTM more amenable to *edge deployment* and *real‑time inference* on devices with limited memory. While this opens beneficial applications (e.g., on‑device personal assistants that preserve user data locally), it also lowers the barrier for adversarial actors to run powerful language models clandestinely.

From an ethical standpoint, researchers must therefore:

1. **Audit the recurrent memory dynamics** to ensure that long‑range state tracking does not unintentionally retain or amplify sensitive personal information across sessions.
2. **Develop transparent logging** for the exponential gating mechanisms, since they can dramatically alter hidden states in response to rare inputs—potentially creating hidden “backdoors” that are difficult to detect.
3. **Promote responsible scaling** by publishing thorough ablation studies (as done in Sections \ref{sec:ExpComparison} and \ref{sec:ExpLanguage}) so that the community can evaluate trade‑offs before deploying billion‑parameter recurrent models.
4. **Encourage open‑source implementations** (the authors provide code at \url{https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm}) that include safety checks for overflow, gate saturation, and excessive memory growth.

By proactively addressing these concerns, the community can benefit from xLSTM’s technical advances while mitigating the societal harms that have accompanied recent LLM deployments.

---

In summary, **xLSTM revitalizes recurrent architectures for the era of large‑scale language modeling**, offering a suite of mechanisms—exponential gating, matrix (and potentially tensor) memories, and carefully engineered normalization—that overcome long‑standing LSTM limitations. Its compatibility with residual backbones opens the door to hybrid Transformer‑RNN systems, while its linear‑time characteristics make it attractive for reinforcement learning, scientific simulation, and on‑device applications. Continued research on richer memory tensors, alternative stabilisation techniques, and rigorous ethical safeguards will determine how profoundly xLSTM reshapes the future landscape of sequential deep learning.
