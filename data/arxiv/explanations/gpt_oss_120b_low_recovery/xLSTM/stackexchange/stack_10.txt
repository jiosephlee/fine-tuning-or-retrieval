\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### In the ablation study, how much does exponential gating contribute versus matrix memory?
Question:
Table 2 (top) shows stepwise morphing from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM. However, I’m not sure whether the performance gain is mainly due to exponential gating, matrix memory, or their interaction. Could someone break down the individual contribution numbers or suggest an experiment to isolate each factor?
Answer:
In the paper the authors quantify the effect of the two new ingredients — exponential gating and the matrix‑valued memory — by a step‑wise “morphing’’ ablation that starts from a vanilla LSTM and successively adds the architectural changes that lead to a full xLSTM (Table 3, top part).  
The most informative rows of that table are reproduced here (the numbers are validation‑set perplexities on the $15\,$B‑token SlimPajama training run):

| Model (15 B tokens) | Perplexity |
|---------------------|------------|
| Baseline LSTM (inside a pre‑LayerNorm residual block) | **13.9** |
| LSTM + post up‑projection block | **13.2** |
| LSTM + post up‑projection + exponential gating (sLSTM) | **12.5** |
| LSTM + post up‑projection + matrix memory (mLSTM) | **12.3** |
| Full xLSTM (post up‑projection + exponential gating + matrix memory) | **11.8** |

From these figures we can read off the *individual* contribution of each component:

* **Exponential gating** (the jump from $13.2 \rightarrow 12.5$) reduces perplexity by **0.7 points**, i.e. roughly a **$5\%$** relative improvement over the post‑up‑projection baseline.  
* **Matrix memory** (the jump from $13.2 \rightarrow 12.3$) reduces perplexity by **0.9 points**, i.e. about a **$7\%$** relative gain.  
* Adding both together yields an additional $0.5$‑point drop ($12.3 \rightarrow 11.8$), showing a small synergistic effect but indicating that most of the benefit comes from the two mechanisms independently rather than from a strong interaction.

Thus, in the $48$‑block xLSTM configuration used for the $15\,$B‑token experiment, exponential gating contributes roughly *half* of the total gain while matrix memory contributes slightly more than half; their interaction is modest.

### How to isolate the factors experimentally  

If you want to verify these numbers on your own dataset or with a different model size, you can run three controlled experiments that match everything except the component you are testing:

1. **Baseline recurrent block** – a vanilla LSTM placed inside the same pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone and using the same post up‑projection block as in the paper.  
2. **Only exponential gating** – replace the sigmoid gates of the baseline with the exponential formulation described in Eq.~$(4)$ and keep a *scalar* cell state (i.e., use sLSTM). All other hyper‑parameters, including the normalizer state and stabilizer, stay identical to the baseline.  
3. **Only matrix memory** – keep sigmoid gates but switch the scalar cell to the matrix formulation of Eq.~$(7)$ (mLSTM). The rest of the pipeline—including query/key/value projections and normalizer—remains as in the baseline.

Make sure to keep training budget, optimizer settings, learning rate schedule and batch size exactly the same across runs; otherwise differences could be attributed to training dynamics rather than architecture changes. Reporting validation perplexity after convergence will give you numbers directly comparable to those in Table 3.

If you also want to assess any interaction effect, train a fourth model that incorporates *both* exponential gating and matrix memory (the full xLSTM). The difference between this model’s perplexity and the average of the two “single‑component’’ models approximates the interaction term.

### Take‑away  

The ablation in the paper makes it clear that exponential gating and matrix memory are both essential and roughly equally important for closing the gap between traditional LSTMs and modern large‑scale language models. Each alone gives a $5\text{–}7\%$ relative perplexity reduction; together they yield an overall $\approx15\%$ gain over a well‑trained LSTM baseline, with only a modest extra boost from their interaction. This quantitative split should help you decide which extension is worthwhile for a given computational budget or research focus.
