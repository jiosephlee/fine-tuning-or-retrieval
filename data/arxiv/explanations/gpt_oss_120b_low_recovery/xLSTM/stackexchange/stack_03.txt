\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How does the stabilizer state \(m_t\) prevent overflow in exponential gates?
Question:
Equation 4 defines a stabilizer state \(m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))\) and then rescales the gates to \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\). I am confused about why this max operation works and how it guarantees that the exponentials stay within a numerically stable range. Is this equivalent to subtracting a running log‑max across time steps?
Answer:
In the sLSTM the input‑ and forget‑gates are allowed to be **exponential**:  

$$
i_t = \exp (\tilde i_t),\qquad 
f_t = \exp (\tilde f_t)\;\;(\text{or }\sigma(\tilde f_t)).
$$

Because the exponentials act on an affine transformation of the inputs, the raw values of $i_t$ and $f_t$ can become astronomically large (or extremely small) as the recurrence proceeds.  If we were to use these raw numbers directly in  

$$
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1}+ i_t\,z_t ,\qquad
n_t = f_t\,n_{t-1}+ i_t ,
$$

the products $f_tc_{t-1}$ or $i_tz_t$ would quickly overflow the finite‑precision floating‑point format, and the gradients would become either zero (under‑flow) or NaN (over‑flow).  

The paper solves this problem by **tracking a single scalar “stabilizer” $m_t$** that records the dominant magnitude of the product of gates up to time $t$.  The update rule (Eq. 4) is  

$$
m_t = \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \; \log i_t\bigr).
$$

Interpret this as follows.  
The term $\log f_t + m_{t-1}$ is exactly the logarithm of the quantity that would multiply the previous stabilizer state when we propagate a factor through a forget gate:
$$
\exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1}) = f_t \,\exp(m_{t-1}).
$$
The second argument, $\log i_t$, is the logarithm of the fresh contribution from the input gate at the current step.  
Taking the **maximum** picks the larger of the two logarithmic magnitudes, i.e. the dominant scale among all contributions that have been accumulated so far.  Consequently $m_t$ is a running log‑*max* of all factors that appear in the recurrence.

Once $m_t$ is known we **renormalise** the gates:

$$
i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t)=\frac{i_t}{e^{m_t}},\qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t)=
      \frac{f_t\,e^{m_{t-1}}}{e^{m_t}} .
$$

Both $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ are now guaranteed to lie in $[0,1]$.  The proof is immediate from the definition of $m_t$:

* if $m_t = \log i_t$ then $\log i_t - m_t =0$ ⇒ $i'_t=1$, and because  
  $\log f_t + m_{t-1}\le m_t$ we have $\log f_t + m_{t-1}-m_t \le 0$ ⇒ $f'_t\le 1$;
* if $m_t = \log f_t + m_{t-1}$ then analogously $f'_t=1$ and $i'_t\le 1$.

Thus **the stabilizer reduces every exponential gate to a number that can never exceed one**, eliminating any risk of overflow while preserving their *relative* strengths.  Importantly, substituting $i'_t,f'_t$ for $i_t,f_t$ does **not** change the overall computation:

$$
c_t = f'_t\,c_{t-1}+ i'_t\,z_t,
\qquad
n_t = f'_t\,n_{t-1}+ i'_t ,
$$

because every term has been divided by exactly the same factor $e^{m_t}$; that common factor cancels out when we later form ratios such as  

$$
\tilde h _t = \frac{c_t}{n_t},
$$

which is precisely what appears in Eq. (2).  Therefore the forward‑pass outputs and all gradients are unchanged – only their numerical representation is stabilised.

Conceptually this is equivalent to maintaining a **running log‑maximum** across time steps and subtracting it from each newly computed log‑gate before exponentiating.  The idea mirrors standard tricks used in softmax or attention implementations (subtracting a max before exponentiation), but here it is applied recursively over a sequence so that *every* intermediate product stays within a safe dynamic range.

In short, the max operation in Eq. 4 chooses the dominant logarithmic scale at each step, and by normalising both gates with that scale we keep all exponentials bounded in $[0,1]$.  This prevents overflow/under‑flow without altering any mathematical property of the recurrent update.
