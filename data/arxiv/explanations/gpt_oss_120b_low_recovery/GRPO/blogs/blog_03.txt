\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Teaching LLMs to Think Step‑by‑Step: Instruction Tuning and Chain‑of‑Thought for Math  

Large language models have become surprisingly good at solving math problems, but getting a 7 B parameter model to rival 70 B peers is still a challenge. The new **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7 B** shows how far we can go with a well‑designed instruction‑tuning stage that explicitly teaches the model to reason *step‑by‑step*. In this post we walk through the data used, the training pipeline, and the resulting performance on classic benchmarks such as **MATH** and **GSM8K**—all without any external toolkits or massive model sizes.

---

## 1. Why “step‑by‑step” matters  

Mathematical reasoning is inherently sequential: you must unpack a problem, perform intermediate calculations, and finally write the answer. Earlier LLMs were trained mainly on next‑token prediction, which encourages them to produce plausible‑looking text but often skips the logical chain.  

Two ideas have proven effective at injecting explicit reasoning:

| Idea | What it adds | Example |
|------|--------------|---------|
| **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)** | Natural‑language reasoning steps (e.g., “First compute … then …”) | Solving a quadratic: expand, apply formula, simplify. |
| **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)** | Generate executable code (usually Python) that does the heavy lifting | Write a `sympy` script to evaluate an integral. |
| **Tool‑Integrated Reasoning** | Mix natural language and code, allowing the model to call a Python interpreter during inference | Explain a combinatorial argument, then run `math.comb` for the final number. |

When a model sees thousands of examples that follow any of these patterns, it learns to *structure* its own outputs in the same way.

---

## 2. Curating a multilingual instruction dataset  

The authors built a **776 K** example instruction set that covers both English and Chinese mathematics. The collection process can be broken down into three components:

1. **English sources**  
   * **GSM8K & MATH** – original problems were re‑annotated with detailed CoT and PoT solutions.  
   * **MathInstruct & Lila‑OOD** – publicly released instruction data already formatted as stepwise reasoning.  
   * Topics span algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry.

2. **Chinese sources**  
   * Over **76 K‑12 sub‑topics** (linear equations, sequences, geometry…) sourced from local curricula and competition archives.  
   * Each problem received both CoT explanations *and* Python‐based tool solutions where appropriate.

3. **Formatting**  
   Every entry follows a consistent “instruction → response” schema: the prompt contains the problem statement; the response begins with a short natural language plan, optionally interleaves code blocks marked with ```python```, and ends with the final answer.

The key insight is that **quality beats quantity**: a few hundred thousand well‑structured examples are enough to teach a 7 B model to generate coherent reasoning traces.

---

## 3. From base model to DeepSeekMath‑Instruct  

DeepSeekMath starts from **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B)**—a model already familiar with code syntax because it was pre‑trained on large code corpora. This coding background turns out to be crucial for PoT style training.

### Training details

| Phase | Data | Tokens | Learning rate | Steps |
|------|------|--------|----------------|-------|
| **SFT (Supervised Fine‑Tuning)** | 776 K curated math instructions | ≈ 2 B tokens (4 K context per batch) | 5 × 10⁻⁵ (constant) | 500 steps |
| **Batching** | Randomly concatenated examples until hitting 4 K tokens | – | – | – |
| **Optimizer** | AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95) with weight decay 0.1 | – | – | – |

A tiny number of steps may sound odd—however each step processes millions of tokens because of the large batch size (256 sequences). The short training schedule prevents overfitting while still allowing the model to internalize the reasoning patterns.

## 4. Evaluation: How far does stepwise instruction take us?  

### Benchmarks considered
* **GSM8K** – grade school arithmetic (≈8 K problems).  
* **MATH** – competition‐level algebra/geometry/calculus (≈12 K problems).  
* **SAT Math**, **OCW Courses**, **MMLU‑STEM**, plus Chinese datasets (**CMATH**, **MGSM‑zh**, Gaokao benchmarks).

All evaluations are done in a *zero‑shot* setting: we simply prepend the problem statement and let the model generate its solution using either CoT or PoT prompting.

### Results snapshot  

| Model (7 B) | GSM8K (CoT) | MATH (CoT) | MATH (Tool) |
|------------|------------|-----------|-------------|
| Baseline DeepSeek‑Coder 7 B | 71.4 % | 28.3 % | 32.5 % |
| **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7 B** | **82.9 %** → **88.2 % after RL** | **46.8 %** → **51.7 % after RL** | ~60 % (tool‐integrated) |

Key takeaways:

* With pure instruction tuning (no RL), DeepSeekMath‑Instruct already surpasses all open‐source 7–34 B models on both GSM8K and MATH.
* Adding reinforcement learning (GRPO, described later) nudges GSM8K up to an impressive **88.2 %**, putting it on par with many proprietary 70 B models.
* On MATH—a notoriously hard benchmark—the model reaches **51.7 %**, making it the first open‐source 7 B model to cross the 50 % barrier.
* Chinese benchmarks see similar gains; multilingual pretraining plus bilingual instruction data yields strong performance on CMATH and Gaokao tasks.

### Why does this work?  

1. **Code first, math second** – Starting from a coder model gives immediate proficiency in generating correct syntax for PoT solutions; math fine‐tuning merely teaches how to *use* that syntax properly.
2. **Multilingual exposure** – The corpus includes both English and Chinese web pages; consequently the model learns commonsense math vocabulary in both languages.
3. **Explicit reasoning templates** – The model never has to “discover” how to write out steps; they are part of every training example.

## 5. A peek at reinforcement learning: Group Relative PPO (GRPO)  

While instruction tuning already yields strong numbers, the authors further boost performance with a lightweight RL variant called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.

### How GRPO differs from classic PPO

* PPO maintains an extra *critic* network that estimates a value function for each token—a memory hog for large language models.
* GRPO drops this critic entirely: for each question it samples a *group* of outputs (e.g., 64 answers), computes rewards using a trained reward model, then treats the group’s average reward as a baseline.
* Advantages are calculated *relative* to this baseline, which aligns naturally with pairwise reward models that are trained on comparisons between answers.

The result is an RL algorithm that uses far less GPU memory while still giving solid gains—enough to push GSM8K from 82.9 % to 88.2 % and MATH from 46.8 % to 51.7 %.

### Training loop in practice

1. Sample 64 completions for each question from the current policy.
2. Score each completion with the reward model (trained on correct/incorrect labels from SFT data).
3. Normalize scores within each group → compute advantages.
4. Perform one gradient update per batch using the GRPO objective.
5. Periodically refresh the reward model with newly generated data (“iterative RL”).

All of this runs on a single GPU for a modest number of steps because the policy already starts from a strong baseline.

## 6. What we can learn for our own projects  

1️⃣ ***Curate focused instruction data.*  
   Even a few hundred thousand well‑structured examples can dramatically improve reasoning ability—no need for billions of tokens if they’re high quality.*

2️⃣ ***Leverage code pretraining.*  
   Starting from a coder model simplifies program­-of-thought fine‑tuning; you get correct syntax “for free”.*

3️⃣ ***Make your training multilingual.*  
   Including non‑English math pages not only improves Chinese benchmarks but also enriches overall mathematical vocabulary.*

4️⃣ ***Consider lightweight RL.*  
   If you already have an instruction-tuned model, adding GRPO or an equivalent group‐based method can give you an extra few points without massive compute.*

5️⃣ ***Tool integration is optional but powerful.*  
   The same model can be used in pure CoT mode or with Python tool use; just switch prompting style at inference time.*

---

## 7. Where we still fall short  

DeepSeekMath‑Instruct shines on quantitative reasoning but still lags behind closed models on geometry visualisation and formal theorem proving—likely due to data selection bias toward textual problem statements rather than diagrammatic content.

Also, unlike GPT‑4, DeepSeekMath does not show strong few-shot improvements; its performance is almost identical in zero- vs few-shot settings.

Future work could involve:

* Adding diagram or LaTeX rendering data for geometry.
* Exploring more sophisticated sampling strategies during RL (e.g., tree search) to improve top-1 correctness rather than just “majority” gains.
* Scaling up the reward model’s process supervision for finer stepwise feedback.

---

## 8. TL;DR  

- DeepSeekMath‑Instruct turns a generic coder LLM into a math problem solver by teaching it three reasoning styles: chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning.
- A curated bilingual dataset of ~776 K examples is enough to lift a 7 B model past every open-source competitor and into the territory of much larger proprietary models on benchmarks like GSM8K (+6 points) and MATH (+5 points).
- Adding a memory‑efficient RL stage (GRPO) pushes scores even higher while keeping compute modest.
- The recipe—code pretraining → high-quality stepwise instruction → lightweight RL—is replicable for anyone looking to build stronger mathematical or logical reasoning abilities into modestly sized LLMs.

If you’re interested in trying this yourself, keep an eye out for the released DeepSeekMath corpus and tooling; they provide an excellent starting point for any research team aiming to make small models think like mathematicians—one step at a time.
