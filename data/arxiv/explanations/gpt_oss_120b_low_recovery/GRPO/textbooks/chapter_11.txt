\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 11: Future Directions in Math‑Focused LLMs
# Enhancing Geometry and Formal Proof Performance

The experimental results presented in the DeepSeekMath study reveal a clear disparity between the model’s strong quantitative reasoning abilities and its comparatively weaker performance on geometry and formal theorem‑proving tasks. This gap stems from two intertwined causes: (1) the data‑selection pipeline, while highly effective at harvesting large quantities of algebraic and arithmetic content from the Common Crawl, contains far fewer examples of geometric reasoning or rigorous formal proof structures; (2) the instruction‑tuning and reinforcement‑learning stages primarily rely on chain‑of‑thought or program‑of‑thought formats that excel for step‑by‑step numeric calculations but do not explicitly teach the spatial intuition and diagrammatic reasoning required for geometry, nor the symbolic manipulation patterns needed for interactive theorem provers such as Isabelle.

Addressing this limitation first requires a targeted enrichment of the pre‑training corpus. One promising avenue is to augment the DeepSeekMath Corpus with dedicated geometry‑focused web domains (e.g., geometry‑specific forums, textbooks, and problem repositories) and with formal‑proof datasets beyond the miniF2F benchmark, such as the full Isabelle Archive of Formal Proofs or the Lean mathematical library. The same iterative classifier‑based pipeline described in the paper can be retrained with seed examples that contain geometric diagrams described in LaTeX/TikZ or explicit Euclidean constructions, thereby expanding the classifier’s recall for geometry‑rich pages. Similarly, a seed set of formal proof excerpts can be used to train a specialized classifier that captures the signature of proof‑assistant code and formal statements, ensuring that the resulting corpus contains higher‑quality proof‑level material.

Beyond data, the training objectives themselves could be adapted. The current instruction‑tuning data includes chain‑of‑thought, program‑of‑thought, and tool‑integrated reasoning formats, but does not explicitly model the multi‑modal nature of geometry (e.g., parsing a textual description of a diagram, generating a diagram, then reasoning about it). Incorporating multimodal instruction examples that pair textual problem statements with synthetic diagram tokens, or even with external rendering tools, would encourage the model to learn representations that capture spatial relationships. For formal proof, fine‑tuning on “proof‑sketch‑to‑full‑proof” pipelines—where a model first produces a high‑level sketch and then, aided by a proof assistant, fills in the low‑level details—could bridge the gap between informal reasoning and formal verification.

Finally, evaluation should be expanded to include more stringent geometry benchmarks (e.g., geometry subsets of the AoPS database) and larger formal‑proof suites, allowing researchers to measure progress quantitatively and to iterate on data and objectives in a closed loop.

# Advanced Sampling & Tree‑Search for RL Exploration

Reinforcement learning in the DeepSeekMath framework relies on sampling multiple candidate outputs for each question and ranking them with a learned reward model. The paper demonstrates that increasing the number of samples per question (e.g., 64 outputs in GRPO) improves the in‑domain MATH score, yet the sampling remains a naïve nucleus or temperature‑based decoding. A more sophisticated exploration strategy could yield higher‑quality candidate sets while keeping computational cost manageable.

Tree‑search methods, such as the Monte‑Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) used in game‑playing agents, have been recently applied to language‑model‑based reasoning. In a tree‑search setting, each node represents a partial reasoning step (e.g., a CoT token or a program line), and the search algorithm expands promising nodes guided by a value estimate (here, the reward model). By maintaining a frontier of partially constructed solutions, the algorithm can allocate more computation to ambiguous regions of the reasoning space, effectively performing a look‑ahead that naïve sampling lacks. Integrating tree‑search with GRPO would replace the flat group of $G$ samples with a hierarchical set of partial trajectories, each evaluated with process‑level rewards (see the next section). The advantage estimator $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ would then be derived from the relative scores of sibling nodes, preserving the group‑relative nature of GRPO while exploiting a structured exploration space.

Practically, this integration could be realized by iterating over “rollouts”: for each question, run a limited‑depth tree search that generates a small number of complete solutions (leaf nodes). The reward model scores each leaf, and the relative advantage of each intermediate token is computed from the sibling leaf scores. Because tree‑search can prune unpromising branches early, the overall number of completed samples needed to achieve a high‑quality set may be lower than the 64 samples used in the current GRPO implementation, reducing the wall‑clock time of each RL update.

# Robust RL Algorithms Against Noisy Reward Signals (Weak‑to‑Strong Alignment)

A central observation of the DeepSeekMath work is that the reward model is trained on comparisons derived from a rule‑based correctness signal (e.g., exact answer match). Consequently, the reward model inherits the noise and bias of those rule‑based labels. When the reward model is imperfect, the advantage estimates $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ may mislead the policy update, especially for nuanced reasoning steps where the rule signal provides no gradient.

To mitigate this, the authors propose a “weak‑to‑strong” alignment perspective: start with a weak, possibly noisy reward signal and gradually evolve it into a stronger, more reliable guide. One concrete implementation is to fuse the rule‑based reward with model‑based uncertainty estimates. For each sampled output, the reward model can also output a confidence score (e.g., the variance of a Bayesian ensemble or the entropy of a softmax over comparison logits). The advantage can then be weighted by this confidence, reducing the influence of low‑certainty scores. Formally, let $c_i$ be the confidence for output $o_i$; a confidence‑aware advantage could be defined as
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\text{conf}} = c_i \cdot \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{r})}.
\]
Outputs with high variance in the reward model therefore receive diminished gradient magnitude, protecting the policy from being driven by noisy feedback.

Another strategy is to incorporate an auxiliary self‑consistency loss, as demonstrated by the paper’s self‑consistency experiments (sampling 64 outputs and voting). By jointly optimizing the policy to maximize the reward while also encouraging consensus among high‑reward samples, the algorithm implicitly regularizes against outlier high‑reward but low‑quality responses.

Finally, iterating the reward model with a replay buffer—mixing new high‑reward samples with a subset of historical data—helps stabilize training. This replay mechanism, already used in the iterative GRPO algorithm, can be extended to prioritize samples where the reward model’s prediction changed significantly between iterations, focusing learning on regions where the reward signal is most volatile.

# Better Reward‑Model Uncertainty Handling and Process Rewards

The DeepSeekMath paper distinguishes between outcome supervision (reward only at the final token) and process supervision (reward after each reasoning step). Process supervision is shown to yield superior performance when combined with GRPO, because it supplies a finer‑grained training signal aligned with the incremental nature of chain‑of‑thought reasoning.

To further enhance process rewards, two complementary ideas arise directly from the paper’s analysis:

1. **Explicit Uncertainty Propagation** – For each intermediate step, the process reward model can produce not just a scalar score but also an uncertainty measure (e.g., predictive variance). When computing the cumulative advantage for token $t$, the algorithm can discount contributions from steps with high uncertainty, effectively focusing learning on well‑understood portions of the reasoning chain. This yields a weighted sum:
   \[
   \hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{j: \text{index}(j) \ge t} \frac{\widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}}{1 + \sigma_i^{\text{index}(j)}},
   \]
   where $\sigma_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ denotes the uncertainty of the $j$‑th step.

2. **Hierarchical Process Rewards** – Geometry and formal proof tasks naturally decompose into sub‑goals (e.g., “construct auxiliary line”, “apply theorem X”). A hierarchical reward model could assign separate scores to each sub‑goal, allowing the policy to learn which sub‑goals are most critical. During GRPO updates, the advantage for tokens belonging to a particular sub‑goal would be computed relative to the average score of all outputs that achieve the same sub‑goal, preserving the group‑relative advantage formulation while capturing the structured nature of the task.

Both approaches retain the simplicity of GRPO—no extra value network is needed—while enriching the signal that guides the policy’s token‑level updates.

# Scaling to Larger Models and Multilingual Extensions

The DeepSeekMath experiments demonstrate that a 7 B‑parameter model, when pre‑trained on a high‑quality 120 B‑token math corpus and fine‑tuned with GRPO, can rival many larger closed‑source systems. Nevertheless, the authors acknowledge that model scale still influences few‑shot capability and the ability to internalize more complex reasoning patterns, especially in geometry and theorem‑proving.

Scaling the approach to larger architectures (e.g., 30 B, 70 B) is conceptually straightforward: the same data‑selection pipeline, instruction‑tuning recipes, and GRPO algorithm can be applied without modification. However, larger models increase the memory footprint of PPO‑style critics dramatically; the primary motivation for GRPO—removing the critic—becomes even more critical at those scales. In addition, larger models often benefit from longer context windows, enabling them to ingest richer problem statements (e.g., full problem PDFs, multi‑part geometry diagrams). Extending the tokenizer to handle additional mathematical symbols and diagram‑encoding tokens would allow the model to ingest and generate richer representations.

Multilingual extensions have already shown promise: the DeepSeekMath Corpus contains substantial Chinese content, and the resulting model improves on Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and Gaokao‑MathCloze. Future work can broaden this multilinguality by adding high‑quality mathematical web pages in other languages (e.g., Russian, French, Arabic) using the same fastText‑based classifier, potentially leveraging language‑specific seed corpora. Moreover, the reward model itself should be multilingual, either by training separate language‑specific critics or by using a unified multilingual architecture that shares parameters across languages but retains language‑aware embeddings. This would permit GRPO to be applied uniformly across all language subsets, ensuring that the reinforcement signal respects cultural and notation differences in mathematical expression.

Finally, as model size grows, the interplay between pre‑training corpus size and parameter count becomes a fertile research direction. The DeepSeekMath results suggest that data quality can compensate for parameter count, yet it remains open how the marginal benefit of additional math tokens scales with model size. Systematic ablations—training a 30 B model on 60 B, 120 B, and 240 B math tokens—could reveal whether the “data‑dominant” regime persists or whether diminishing returns appear, thereby guiding the allocation of compute resources in future large‑scale math‑focused LLM projects.

# Concluding Remarks

The DeepSeekMath paper establishes a solid foundation for open‑source mathematical language models: a scalable web‑mining pipeline, a code‑first pre‑training strategy, and an efficient RL algorithm (GRPO) that sidesteps the memory overhead of traditional PPO. The future research directions outlined above grow directly from the paper’s findings. By enriching geometry and formal‑proof data, integrating advanced tree‑search exploration, designing reward models that explicitly handle uncertainty, and scaling both model size and multilingual coverage, the community can push the envelope of mathematical reasoning in LLMs even further—bringing open‑source systems ever closer to the capabilities of proprietary giants such as GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra.
