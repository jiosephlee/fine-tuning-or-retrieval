\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 9: # Unified Paradigm for Fine‑Tuning and RL Methods  

Mathematical reasoning with large language models (LLMs) has progressed rapidly, yet the community still employs a bewildering variety of fine‑tuning and reinforcement‑learning (RL) techniques.  In the DeepSeekMath work, the authors discovered a clean way to view **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)**, **Rejection‑Sampling Fine‑Tuning (RFT)**, **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**, **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, and their own **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** under a single gradient‑based formulation.  This chapter develops that formulation, explains how each method fits into it, and discusses the empirical findings that arise from the unified view.  The goal is to give the reader an intuitive yet rigorous understanding of why certain design choices—particularly the handling of data, reward signals, and algorithmic processing—lead to measurable improvements in mathematical problem solving.

---

## 1. General Gradient Formulation and Its Three Fundamental Components  

All fine‑tuning and RL methods ultimately adjust the parameters θ of a language model by **maximizing a scalar objective** that can be expressed as an expectation over data and a per‑token gradient coefficient.  The authors of DeepSeekMath write this compactly as  

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} 
\underbrace{GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{\mathrm{rf}})}_{\text{gradient coefficient}}
\;\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t}\bigr)
\right]}
\tag{1}
\]

where  

* **\(q\)** denotes an input question (e.g., a math problem).  
* **\(o\)** denotes a model‑generated output (a solution or proof).  
* **\(|o|\)** is the number of tokens in that output.  
* **\(\pi_{\theta}\)** is the current policy (the LLM with parameters θ).  
* **\(\mathcal{D}\)** is a *data source* that supplies pairs \((q,o)\).  
* **\(\pi_{\mathrm{rf}}\)** is a *reward function* (either a deterministic rule or a learned reward model).  
* **\(GC_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)\)** is the *gradient coefficient* produced by a particular *algorithm* \(\mathcal{A}\) (e.g., SFT, PPO, GRPO).

Equation (1) makes explicit **three independent components** that fully determine any fine‑tuning or RL method:

1. **Data Source \(\mathcal{D}\)** – where the \((q,o)\) pairs come from.  This may be a static human‑annotated dataset (offline) or samples generated on‑the‑fly by the current policy (online).  

2. **Reward Function \(\pi_{\mathrm{rf}}\)** – the scalar signal that judges the quality of an output.  It can be a **rule‑based** indicator (e.g., “answer is correct”) or a **model‑based** learned reward model.  

3. **Algorithm \(\mathcal{A}\)** – the recipe that transforms the raw reward into the per‑token coefficient \(GC_{\mathcal{A}}\).  Different algorithms decide whether to use the reward directly, to subtract a baseline, to clip ratios, etc.

Understanding a method is therefore equivalent to specifying these three items.  The unified view also clarifies why seemingly disparate approaches can be interpreted as variations on the same underlying gradient.

---

## 2. Classification of Methods Along the Three Axes  

### 2.1 Offline vs. Online Data  

* **Offline data** (used by SFT, RFT, DPO) is drawn once from a *fixed* distribution, typically the outputs of a *pre‑existing* model (often the SFT model).  The dataset does not change during training.  

* **Online data** (used by Online RFT, PPO, GRPO) is produced *dynamically* by sampling from the *current* policy at each training step.  As the policy improves, the data distribution shifts, providing a constantly refreshed supervisory signal.

The authors empirically compare **offline RFT** to **online RFT** (Figure 5 in the paper).  Early in training, both behave similarly because the policy has not moved far from the original SFT model, but later the online version gains a clear advantage—demonstrating that fresh, policy‑driven samples become increasingly informative as training proceeds.

### 2.2 Rule‑Based vs. Model‑Based Reward  

* **Rule‑based rewards** are binary or scalar functions that directly check a property of the final answer, such as exact match with the ground‑truth solution.  In the DeepSeekMath experiments, this corresponds to the “correct‑answer” check used in most outcome‑supervision settings.

* **Model‑based rewards** are learned reward models \(r_{\varphi}\) that assign a continuous score to an entire output (or to intermediate steps).  The reward model is trained on pairwise human preferences, thus capturing more nuanced notions of quality (e.g., logical coherence, brevity).

The authors show that **outcome‑supervision** (using only the final answer) works, but **process‑supervision**—where a reward model evaluates each reasoning step—provides a richer gradient signal, leading to higher performance (GRPO + PS > GRPO + OS).

### 2.3 Algorithmic Variants  

| Method | Gradient Coefficient \(GC_{\mathcal{A}}\) | Baseline / Normalisation | KL Regularisation |
|--------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **SFT** | \(1\) (i.e., pure MLE) | none | none |
| **RFT** | \(1\) for outputs that satisfy the rule, \(0\) otherwise | none | none |
| **DPO** | \(\sigma\bigl(r(o^{+})-r(o^{-})\bigr)\) where \(o^{+}\) is preferred | none | none |
| **PPO** | \(\displaystyle\min\!\Bigl[\rho_{t} A_{t},\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t})A_{t}\Bigr]\) with \(\rho_{t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\) | value‑function baseline \(V_{\psi}\) (learned critic) | per‑token KL penalty added to reward (Eq. 2) |
| **GRPO** | Same PPO clipping, but **\(A_t\) is a *group‑relative* advantage** computed from the mean‑centered rewards of a *set* of samples on the same question | *no learned critic*; baseline = group average reward | KL divergence added **directly** to the loss (unbiased estimator) |

The key distinction for **GRPO** is that the *baseline* is not a separate neural critic but the **average reward of a sampled group**.  This eliminates the memory‑heavy value network required by PPO while still providing variance reduction.

## 3. Experimental Comparison of Representative Pairs  

### 3.1 Online RFT vs. Offline RFT  

In the DeepSeekMath study, both methods start from the same SFT model and use the same rule‑based correctness reward.  The **offline** version builds a static pool of “good” and “bad” samples once, then fine‑tunes on them for many epochs.  The **online** version repeatedly samples fresh outputs from the *current* policy, filters them by the rule, and fine‑tunes immediately.

Empirically (see Figure 5), the **online** method overtakes the offline baseline after roughly the midway point of training.  The intuition is that as the policy drifts away from the initial SFT distribution, offline samples become stale and no longer reflect the model’s present weaknesses; online sampling continues to surface the *hardest* mistakes, which drives further improvement.

### 3.2 GRPO vs. PPO  

Both algorithms share the same clipped surrogate objective (the min‑operator in Eq. 1).  The divergence lies in **advantage estimation** and **KL handling**:

* **PPO** computes an advantage \(A_t = \hat{Q}_t - V_{\psi}(s_t)\) where \(\hat{Q}_t\) is the discounted return and \(V_{\psi}\) is a separate value network.  This introduces a *large memory footprint* because the value network must be stored and updated alongside the policy.

* **GRPO** replaces the learned critic with a *group relative* baseline: for a question \(q\) it samples \(G\) outputs \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\), scores them with the reward model, and defines  

  \[
  \hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})},
  \]

  i.e., the (standardised) deviation of each sample’s reward from the group mean.  This baseline is **free** (no extra parameters) and automatically adapts to the current difficulty of the question.

* Moreover, PPO adds a *per‑token* KL penalty to the reward (Eq. 2), which complicates the advantage computation; GRPO instead adds a *global* KL divergence term between the new policy and a fixed reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) directly to the loss, using an unbiased estimator that guarantees positivity.

The authors report that **GRPO achieves the same or higher gains on GSM8K (82.9 % → 88.2 %) and MATH (46.8 % → 51.7 %)** while using considerably less GPU memory than PPO.  The reduction in resources makes it feasible to run RL on 7 B‑scale models, which otherwise would be prohibitive with a full PPO pipeline.

## 4. Why GRPO’s Gradient Coefficients Are More Effective  

The gradient coefficient in GRPO captures **relative quality** among a set of candidate outputs for the same question.  This has two important consequences:

1. **Fine‑grained discrimination** – Because the advantage is proportional to the *difference* between an individual reward and the group mean, outputs that are *slightly* better than average receive a positive push, while those *slightly* worse are pulled back.  In PPO, the advantage is dominated by the (noisy) value network estimate, which can be inaccurate for LLMs where the reward is usually only defined at the final token.

2. **Inherent variance reduction** – Subtracting the group mean is mathematically equivalent to using a baseline that is *optimal* for minimizing the variance of the Monte‑Carlo estimator of the policy gradient (the classic REINFORCE variance reduction trick).  Since the baseline is computed *exactly* from the sampled group, there is no learning error involved, and the variance of the gradient is dramatically lowered.

These properties explain the empirical observation that **GRPO outperforms PPO even though it lacks a learned critic**.  The authors explicitly note that “the group relative way that GRPO leverages to calculate the advantages aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models, as reward models are typically trained on datasets of comparisons between outputs on the same question.”

---  

## 5. Iterative RL: Benefits and Diminishing Returns  

GRPO, like any RL algorithm, can be applied **iteratively**: after a first RL phase, the policy becomes the new reference model, the reward model is refreshed with fresh preference data, and a second RL phase begins.  The DeepSeekMath paper presents Algorithm 1 (Iterative GRPO) which alternates between:

1. **Exploration** – sample groups of outputs from the current policy.  
2. **Reward‑model update** – train a new reward model on these samples (using a replay buffer to retain 10 % of older data).  
3. **Policy update** – run GRPO with the updated reward model.

Empirically (Figure 7), the **first iteration yields a large jump** in performance (e.g., GSM8K from 82.9 % to 88.2 %).  A **second iteration provides a smaller, yet still noticeable, boost**, after which gains taper off.  This pattern is typical of RL: the most obvious mistakes are eliminated quickly, leaving only subtle deficiencies that require more sophisticated data or a stronger reward model to address.

The authors also discuss **diminishing returns** in terms of “the policy may over‑fit to the current reward model’s quirks” if iterations continue unchecked.  Consequently, they suggest **monitoring the variance of rewards** and possibly expanding the data source (e.g., incorporating out‑of‑distribution prompts) to keep the training signal informative.

## 6. Synthesis: What the Unified Paradigm Reveals  

Viewing all fine‑tuning and RL approaches as instances of Eq. (1) clarifies several design principles:

* **Data freshness matters**: Online sampling supplies increasingly challenging examples as the model improves, which is why online RFT outperforms its offline counterpart.

* **Baseline quality is critical**: A well‑chosen baseline (group average in GRPO) can replace a learned value network, saving memory and improving gradient stability.

* **Reward granularity matters**: Process‑supervision (step‑wise rewards) yields stronger gradients than outcome‑only supervision because the per‑token advantages become more informative.

* **Iterative refinement is worthwhile but limited**: Updating both policy and reward model in a loop yields substantial early gains; later iterations require richer data or more expressive reward functions to sustain improvement.

By systematically dissecting each method along the three axes—**data source**, **reward function**, and **algorithm**—researchers can deliberately select the combination that best matches their computational budget and performance goals.  For open‑source mathematical LLMs at the 7 B scale, the DeepSeekMath study demonstrates that **GRPO with online data and a model‑based reward** delivers a sweet spot: it pushes the model past the 50 % mark on the competition‑level **MATH** benchmark while staying within modest GPU memory limits.

---  

### Key Takeaways  

1. **All fine‑tuning / RL methods are gradient‑based**; they differ only in the three components of Eq. (1).  
2. **Online data** and **model‑based rewards** consistently outperform their offline / rule‑based counterparts on mathematical reasoning tasks.  
3. **GRPO’s group‑relative baseline** provides a mathematically optimal, parameter‑free variance reducer, making it more effective than PPO for LLMs.  
4. **Iterative RL** yields the biggest improvements in the first round; later rounds need richer data or stronger rewards to avoid plateauing.  

Armed with this unified paradigm, future work can explore novel combinations—e.g., **online process‑supervised GRPO** with multilingual web data—to keep advancing the frontier of mathematical reasoning in open‑source language models.
