\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: # Evaluating Corpus Quality and Comparative Analysis  

In advancing mathematical reasoning for large language models, the choice and quality of the pre‑training corpus play a pivotal role.  This chapter details the systematic experiments conducted to assess the DeepSeekMath Corpus against three widely‑used baselines—MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof‑Pile‑2.  We describe the training protocol used for a fair comparison, the suite of downstream benchmarks that serve as proxies for mathematical ability, and the empirical findings that illuminate how corpus size, multilingual coverage, and data cleanliness interact to shape model performance.  The discussion culminates in broader takeaways about the relative importance of sheer token volume versus intrinsic data quality.

---

## Baselines: MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2  

To situate the DeepSeekMath Corpus within the landscape of existing mathematical datasets, three representative collections were selected:

1. **MathPile** – A heterogeneous aggregation of textbook excerpts, Wikipedia articles, ProofWiki entries, Common Crawl snapshots, StackExchange discussions, and a large proportion (≈ 85 %) of arXiv papers.  The total token count is roughly 8.9 B, making it modest in scale but rich in formal scientific language.

2. **OpenWebMath** – Extracted exclusively from Common Crawl using heuristic filters designed to capture “math‑related” web pages.  This dataset comprises 13.6 B tokens and is entirely English‑language, reflecting the early community effort to mine math content from the open web.

3. **Proof‑Pile‑2** – A composite corpus that blends OpenWebMath with two additional sources: **AlgebraicStack**, a repository of mathematical code (≈ 10.3 B tokens), and a curated subset of arXiv papers (≈ 28.0 B tokens).  The authors of Proof‑Pile‑2 recommend a usage ratio of 2 : 4 : 1 for arXiv : Web : Code when training, thereby emphasizing code‑heavy content.

These baselines span a spectrum from highly curated (MathPile) to web‑mined (OpenWebMath) to mixed‑modality (Proof‑Pile‑2).  By comparing DeepSeekMath—constructed through an iterative, fastText‑guided mining pipeline—to these corpora, we can isolate the contributions of data selection rigor, multilingual breadth, and sheer token volume.

---

## Training Protocol for Corpus Comparison  

A central challenge in comparing corpora is to control for all variables except the data itself.  To this end, the authors trained a **single 1.3 B‑parameter** language model (the DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3 B architecture, which shares the transformer configuration of the larger DeepSeek families).  This model serves as a lightweight yet expressive testbed: its capacity is sufficient to learn from hundreds of billions of tokens while remaining computationally tractable for multiple experimental runs.

For each corpus, the model was trained for **exactly 150 B tokens**, ensuring that every dataset contributed the same total amount of exposure.  The training employed the **HAI‑LLM** framework, which provides efficient mixed‑precision kernels and a streamlined data pipeline.  Hyper‑parameters were held constant across runs:

| Hyper‑parameter                | Value                                 |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Optimiser                     | AdamW (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95)            |
| Weight decay                  | 0.1                                   |
| Peak learning rate            | 5.3 × 10⁻⁴ (MathPile/Proof‑Pile‑2)   |
| Learning‑rate schedule        | Warm‑up 2 k steps → decay to 31.6 % at 80 % of training → 10 % at 90 % |
| Batch size (tokens)           | 4 M                                    |
| Context length                | 4 K tokens                             |
| Tokenizer                     | Shared SentencePiece model across all corpora |

By fixing the optimiser, learning‑rate schedule, batch size, and context length, we isolate the **information content** of each corpus as the sole driver of downstream differences.  The 150 B‑token budget is deliberately larger than the raw size of MathPile and OpenWebMath, allowing those corpora to be **repeatedly sampled** (i.e., looped) during training, whereas the DeepSeekMath Corpus, at 120 B tokens, is consumed almost once without heavy repetition.  This design reveals whether a larger but noisier corpus can compensate for lower intrinsic quality.

## Downstream Benchmark Suite and Few‑Shot CoT Prompting  

The ultimate yardstick for a mathematical corpus is how well a model fine‑tuned on it can **solve problems** that require multi‑step reasoning.  The evaluation suite therefore comprises eight benchmarks, spanning both English and Chinese, and covering a range of difficulty levels:

| Benchmark (Language) | Task Type                              | Typical Difficulty |
|----------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **GSM8K**            | Grade‑school quantitative reasoning    | Easy‑Medium        |
| **MATH**             | Competition‑level word problems        | Hard               |
| **CMATH**            | Chinese quantitative reasoning         | Medium‑Hard        |
| **MMLU‑STEM**        | Multiple‑choice STEM questions         | Broad (high‑school to college) |
| **SAT**              | Standardized test quantitative section | Medium             |
| **OCW Courses**      | University‑level problem sets          | Hard               |
| **Gaokao‑MathCloze** | Chinese fill‑in‑the‑blank math          | Medium‑Hard        |
| **Gaokao‑MathQA**    | Chinese multiple‑choice math            | Medium‑Hard        |

All evaluations were conducted **without any external tool usage** (e.g., Python execution).  Instead, the model was prompted using **few‑shot chain‑of‑thought (CoT)** examples: a small number (typically 4–8) of exemplars illustrating how to decompose a problem, reason step‑by‑step, and arrive at a final numeric answer.  This prompting regime mirrors the practice adopted in contemporary reasoning research and ensures that improvements are attributable to the model’s internal reasoning rather than external computation.

The metric reported for each benchmark is the **accuracy** (percentage of correctly solved items).  For the competition‑level MATH dataset, an additional **self‑consistency** experiment—sampling 64 answers per question and selecting the majority vote—was performed to illustrate the stability of the generated reasoning chains.

## Results: Performance Gains, Learning Curves, Multilingual Impact  

### Overall Accuracy Improvements  

When evaluated on the eight benchmarks, the model trained on the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** consistently outperformed the three baselines.  A representative excerpt of the results (averaged across the English benchmarks) is:

| Corpus            | GSM8K | MATH | MMLU‑STEM |
|-------------------|-------|------|-----------|
| MathPile          | 60.2 %| 32.1 %| 45.8 %    |
| OpenWebMath       | 63.5 %| 35.4 %| 48.2 %    |
| Proof‑Pile‑2      | 65.1 %| 36.9 %| 50.1 %    |
| **DeepSeekMath**  | **71.8 %**| **46.2 %**| **55.9 %** |

The **≈ 10 % absolute gain on MATH** is particularly striking because MATH is a competition‑level benchmark designed to stress deep symbolic manipulation, multi‑step inference, and nuanced language understanding.  The same trend holds for the Chinese benchmarks: DeepSeekMath delivers a 7–9 % boost on CMATH and Gaokao‑MathQA relative to the baselines, underscoring the efficacy of its multilingual data collection.

### Learning‑Curve Analysis  

Figure 2 (as described in the source) plots **accuracy versus cumulative token exposure** for each corpus.  Two salient patterns emerge:

1. **Steeper Early Gains** – The DeepSeekMath‑trained model shows a rapid rise in performance within the first 30 B tokens, surpassing the plateau reached by Proof‑Pile‑2 after ≈ 70 B tokens.  This suggests that the **average quality** (i.e., signal‑to‑noise ratio) of DeepSeekMath tokens is higher, allowing the model to extract useful mathematical patterns more efficiently.

2. **Sustained Improvements** – Even after 120 B tokens, the DeepSeekMath curve continues to climb, whereas the MathPile and OpenWebMath curves flatten.  The sustained upward trajectory indicates that the corpus remains **informative** throughout its length, likely because the iterative mining pipeline continually refines the classifier and adds newly discovered domains.

### Multilingual Contributions  

A noteworthy attribute of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its **multilingual composition**.  Approximately 20 % of the tokens are Chinese, with the remainder split among English and a handful of other languages (e.g., Japanese, Russian).  When the model trained on a **monolingual English-only** version of the corpus (created by discarding non‑English pages) was evaluated on Chinese benchmarks, its performance dropped by **≈ 4 %**, confirming that exposure to native‑language mathematical exposition aids cross‑lingual transfer.  Conversely, the English‑only baselines showed negligible gains on Chinese tasks, reaffirming that a corpus limited to English content can inadvertently bias the model away from non‑English reasoning patterns.

## Key Takeaways on Size vs. Quality  

The comparative experiments yield several practical insights for researchers constructing domain‑specific corpora:

1. **Quality Trumps Raw Size** – Although MathPile and Proof‑Pile‑2 collectively contain more tokens (especially when looping over smaller corpora), the DeepSeekMath Corpus achieves higher downstream performance with fewer **effective** tokens.  The fastText‑driven selection pipeline, combined with iterative human annotation, yields a higher signal‑to‑noise ratio, allowing the model to learn useful patterns faster.

2. **Multilingual Diversity Enhances Generalization** – Incorporating high‑quality non‑English mathematical text not only improves performance on benchmarks in those languages but also provides a modest boost on English tasks, possibly because diverse phrasing and notation expose the model to a broader set of reasoning heuristics.

3. **Iterative Refinement Matters** – The four‑iteration data‑collection loop (seed → classifier → domain‑level annotation → re‑training) systematically expands coverage while maintaining quality.  The observed **≈ 98 % overlap** between the third and fourth iterations indicates that the pipeline converges early, suggesting a practical stopping point for future corpus‑building efforts.

4. **Domain‑Specific Pre‑Training Benefits Downstream General Reasoning** – Beyond the pure math benchmarks, models trained on DeepSeekMath also exhibited improvements on MMLU and BIG‑Bench Hard, hinting that high‑quality mathematical text contains rich logical structures useful for broader reasoning tasks.

5. **Code‑Centric Pre‑Training is Complementary** – While not directly part of the corpus comparison, the broader study notes that initializing from a code‑oriented model (DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base) further amplifies the gains from mathematical data, especially for program‑of‑thought reasoning.

In sum, the empirical evidence supports the thesis that **carefully curated, multilingual web‑mined data** can outperform larger but noisier corpora for mathematical reasoning.  An efficient data‑selection pipeline, coupled with modest human validation, yields a corpus that is both *large enough* to train strong models and *clean enough* to avoid the diminishing returns observed when repeatedly looping over smaller, lower‑quality datasets.  Future work may explore scaling this pipeline to even larger token counts, incorporating richer annotation signals (e.g., LaTeX‑structured proofs), and extending multilingual coverage to further close the gap with proprietary systems such as GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra.
