\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 10: **Practical Lessons and Recommendations**

# Impact of Code Pre‑Training on Math Reasoning (Two‑Stage vs. Mixed Training)

One of the central questions investigated in the DeepSeekMath study is whether exposure to source‑code data can improve a language model’s ability to perform mathematical reasoning.  The authors examine this hypothesis by comparing several training schedules for a 1.3 B‑parameter base model (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B).  All experiments share a common final stage of **math‑only** training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus, but differ in the way code data are introduced.

## Two‑Stage Training

In the two‑stage regime the model first undergoes a prolonged phase of **code‑only** pre‑training (400 B tokens) before receiving a second phase of **math‑only** training (150 B tokens).  A control condition replaces the code phase with an equivalent amount of **general‑language** tokens to isolate the effect of code itself.  The results, reported in Tables X‑Y of the paper, show a clear advantage for the code‑first schedule:

- **Program‑aided reasoning** (e.g., solving GSM8K and MATH problems by generating Python scripts) improves dramatically after the code phase, even before any math‑specific exposure.  The model learns to manipulate the Python API, to call libraries such as `math` and `sympy`, and to emit syntactically correct programs.
- **Subsequent math‑only training** builds on this foundation, yielding further gains on both tool‑assisted and tool‑free tasks.  The model that experienced code pre‑training achieves the highest accuracy on the competition‑level MATH benchmark after the full 550 B‑token curriculum.

Crucially, the control condition (general‑language first) lags behind the code‑first schedule on the same downstream metrics, indicating that the benefit is not simply due to additional pre‑training steps but stems from the **structural nature** of code—its precise syntax, explicit control flow, and deterministic semantics—which appears to prime the model for step‑by‑step logical manipulation.

## Mixed (One‑Stage) Training

The authors also explore a **single‑stage** curriculum that interleaves code and math tokens (400 B code + 150 B math, shuffled at the token level).  This design attempts to avoid the “catastrophic forgetting” observed when the model switches abruptly from one domain to another.  The mixed schedule yields nuanced outcomes:

- **Program‑aided reasoning** benefits from the mixed exposure almost as much as the two‑stage schedule, because the model still sees abundant code examples throughout training.
- **Pure mathematical reasoning (without tools)**, however, does not improve as markedly.  The authors speculate that a 1.3 B‑parameter model lacks the capacity to simultaneously absorb the distinct statistical patterns of programming languages and natural‑language mathematical exposition.  Consequently, the model’s attention is divided, and the fine‑grained reasoning patterns required for chain‑of‑thought solutions receive less reinforcement.

These findings collectively suggest a **practical recipe** for future work: when resources permit, allocate a substantial **pure code pre‑training phase** before any math‑specific exposure, especially for models in the 1–2 B‑parameter range.  For larger models (≥ 7 B parameters) the mixed schedule may become more viable, as the additional capacity can accommodate both domains without severe interference.

# Why arXiv Papers Offered Little Benefit in This Setting

A widespread practice in contemporary mathematical language‑model research is to harvest LaTeX source files from arXiv, under the intuition that research papers contain high‑quality mathematical text.  DeepSeekMath’s ablation experiments directly challenge this assumption.

## Experimental Setup

Two distinct arXiv‑centric corpora are examined:

1. **MathPile** – an 8.9 B‑token mix where > 85 % of the tokens are arXiv LaTeX files, with the remainder drawn from textbooks and web sources.
2. **ArXiv‑RedPajama** – a cleaner 28.0 B‑token collection obtained by stripping LaTeX preambles, comments, macros, and bibliography entries.

Both corpora are used **exclusively** for math‑only training of either the 1.3 B‑parameter DeepSeek‑LLM or the 7 B‑parameter DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5.  Training budgets (150 B tokens for the 1.3 B model, 40 B tokens for the 7 B model) match those applied to the DeepSeekMath Corpus in the main experiments.

## Observations

Across a suite of benchmarks—including **quantitative reasoning** (GSM8K, MATH), **multiple‑choice STEM** (MMLU‑STEM), and **formal theorem proving** (miniF2F)—the arXiv‑only models consistently **under‑perform** or at best match a baseline trained on generic web text.  Specific patterns include:

- **No significant lift** in answer accuracy on GSM8K or MATH relative to a model that has never seen arXiv data.
- **Slight degradations** on multiple‑choice tasks, possibly due to over‑fitting to the narrow distribution of academic prose.
- **Weak performance** on informal‑to‑formal proof generation, despite the presence of rigorous formal statements in the source papers.

These results indicate that **raw arXiv LaTeX** does not translate into useful training signal for the downstream tasks targeted by DeepSeekMath.  Several plausible explanations are offered in the paper:

1. **Domain mismatch** – Benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH consist of elementary‑ to college‑level problems phrased in everyday language, whereas arXiv papers are written for a specialist audience using dense notation and abstract exposition.
2. **Noise from LaTeX preprocessing** – Even after aggressive cleaning, remnants of formatting commands or macros can corrupt tokenization, leading the model to allocate capacity to non‑semantic symbols.
3. **Redundancy with existing web data** – The DeepSeekMath Corpus already captures a broad swath of publicly available mathematical explanations (tutorials, forum discussions, educational sites).  Adding arXiv may yield diminishing returns because the model already learns the core mathematical patterns from web sources.

The authors caution that these conclusions are **scale‑dependent**; larger models (≫ 50 B parameters) may be able to leverage the formal depth of arXiv content more effectively.  Nonetheless, for the 1–7 B regime studied, the empirical evidence advises against expending large token budgets on arXiv alone.

# Tips for Building High‑Quality Math Corpora from Web Data

The DeepSeekMath pipeline demonstrates that **public web crawls** can be transformed into a massive, multilingual math corpus with minimal manual effort.  The authors distill several actionable recommendations for practitioners.

## 1. Seed‑Driven Classifier Bootstrapping

- **Start with a high‑precision seed** such as OpenWebMath, which contains manually curated math webpages.  Randomly sample an equal number of positive and negative examples (e.g., 500 k each) to train a lightweight **fastText** classifier.
- **Model hyper‑parameters** that proved effective: 256‑dimensional word vectors, learning rate 0.1, max n‑gram length 3, minimum word count 3, and 3 training epochs.  These settings balance speed and discriminative power for large‑scale web filtering.

## 2. Iterative Expansion with Domain‑Level Mining

- After the first recall pass, **group pages by domain** (base URL) and compute the fraction of pages already selected.  Domains where > 10 % of pages have been harvested are strong candidates for further exploration.
- **Manually annotate** a small set of URLs within these candidate domains (e.g., the `/questions` path of MathOverflow).  Adding these URLs to the positive set dramatically improves recall for subsequent iterations.

## 3. Deduplication and Near‑Deduplication

- Apply **URL‑based deduplication** to remove exact duplicates, then a **content‑based near‑deduplication** (e.g., MinHash or sha‑256 shingling) to collapse near‑identical pages.  This step reduced the raw Common Crawl from billions of pages to **≈ 40 B** unique HTML documents, a tractable set for large‑scale inference.

## 4. Scoring, Ranking, and Token‑Budget Selection

- Run the fastText model over the deduplicated pool and **rank pages by predicted probability**.  Conduct **pre‑training pilots** on truncated token budgets (e.g., the top 40 B, 80 B, 120 B tokens) to empirically locate the point of diminishing returns.  In DeepSeekMath’s case, the **120 B‑token** cutoff captured ~ 98 % of the useful content.

## 5. De‑contamination from Evaluation Benchmarks

- To avoid **benchmark leakage**, perform exact 10‑gram matching against all evaluation sets (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.).  If a 10‑gram appears, excise the containing segment; for shorter benchmark strings (3–9 grams), also apply exact matching.  This simple heuristic proved sufficient to eliminate inadvertent exposure to test data.

## 6. Multilingual Inclusion

- Although the seed set is English‑centric, the web‑based pipeline naturally discovers **Chinese** math pages (e.g., from zhihu, cn‑math forums).  The resulting corpus exhibits a **balanced multilingual distribution**, which directly correlates with improved performance on Chinese benchmarks (MGSM‑zh, CMATH).  Practitioners should therefore **avoid language‑filtering** unless a monolingual focus is explicitly required.

By following these steps, researchers can assemble a **high‑quality, scalable** math corpus without relying on proprietary datasets, dramatically expanding the accessible training signal for open‑source LLMs.

# Scalability Considerations: Token Count vs. Model Size

The DeepSeekMath experiments illuminate how **data scale** interacts with **model capacity** in the context of mathematical reasoning.

## Empirical Scaling Laws Observed

1. **Token‑Dominated Gains for Small Models**  
   - The **1.3 B‑parameter** DeepSeek‑LLM trained on 120 B math tokens (≈ 90 ×  its parameter count) attains performance on par with the **540 B‑parameter** closed‑source Minerva, a model that was trained on far fewer math‑specific tokens relative to its size.  
   - This confirms the authors’ claim that “the number of parameters is not the only key factor”—high‑quality, domain‑focused data can **compress** the amount of computation needed to reach a target accuracy.

2. **Diminishing Returns Beyond a Certain Token Budget**  
   - When the authors extended the corpus beyond 120 B tokens, the proportion of **newly discovered** high‑quality pages fell to < 2 % (the fourth iteration collected only 2 % of previously unseen content).  Consequently, validation accuracy plateaued, indicating a **data ceiling** for the current classifier and seed design.

3. **Code‑Pretraining Synergy Scales with Model Size**  
   - The code‑first two‑stage schedule yields the biggest relative improvement for the 1.3 B model.  For the **7 B** DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5, which already possesses strong code representations, the marginal benefit of a separate code phase shrinks, suggesting that **larger models inherently capture programming patterns** during generic pre‑training.

## Practical Recommendations for Scaling

- **Prioritize Data Quality Over Pure Volume**:  When scaling token counts, ensure the **precision of the classifier** remains high; otherwise, adding more low‑signal pages merely inflates compute cost without performance gains.
- **Align Data Budget with Model Capacity**: For models ≤ 2 B parameters, aim for a **token‑to‑parameter ratio** of at least 50–100× to achieve competitive reasoning.  For 7 B‑plus models, a ratio of 10–20× may suffice, allowing resources to be redirected toward **instruction tuning** and **RL refinement**.
- **Monitor Multilingual Balance**: As models grow, the **intrinsic multilingual capacity** expands.  Maintaining a balanced language mix in the math corpus prevents performance drop‑offs on non‑English benchmarks.
- **Iterative Evaluation During Corpus Growth**: Use a **small validation suite** (e.g., a few hundred GSM8K and MATH samples) after each token‑budget increment to detect early saturation, saving downstream training cycles.

In sum, the DeepSeekMath project demonstrates that **thoughtful data engineering**—particularly a pipeline that can harvest, rank, and de‑duplicate massive web resources—combined with **strategic pre‑training schedules** (code first, math later) yields outsized gains for modest‑size LLMs.  Researchers aiming to replicate or extend these results should allocate their compute budget first to building a high‑precision math corpus, then to a brief code‑pre‑training phase, and finally to targeted instruction‑tuning and reinforcement‑learning refinements.
