\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: # Building a Large‑Scale Math Corpus from the Web  

In order to endow a language model with strong mathematical reasoning abilities, the authors of *DeepSeekMath* first tackled the most fundamental prerequisite for any such system: a high‑quality, massive collection of mathematical text.  This chapter explains, in detail, how the 120 billion‑token **DeepSeekMath Corpus** was assembled from publicly available web data.  We follow the exact pipeline described in the paper, emphasizing the intuition behind each design decision, the concrete engineering steps, and the empirical observations that guided the process.  The discussion is organized around the key components of the pipeline: seed‑corpus selection and fastText classifier training, iterative mining with domain‑level expansion, URL‑based deduplication, the human‑annotation loop, benchmark de‑contamination, and multilingual coverage.

---

## Seed Corpus Selection and FastText Classifier Training  

The first step of any large‑scale data‑mining effort is to obtain a **seed corpus** that exemplifies the desired content.  The DeepSeekMath team chose **OpenWebMath** as this seed.  OpenWebMath is a curated collection of web pages that have already been identified as high‑quality mathematical sources (e.g., forum discussions, lecture notes, and problem sets).  By starting from a trusted set, the authors ensured that the subsequent classifier would learn a reliable notion of “mathematical text”.

With the seed in hand, the authors trained a **fastText** model, a linear classifier that works directly on character n‑grams and is especially efficient for web‑scale data.  Training proceeded as follows:

1. **Positive examples** – 500 000 pages were randomly sampled from OpenWebMath.  
2. **Negative examples** – 500 000 pages were drawn from the broader Common Crawl dump, without any pre‑filtering, to represent generic web content.  
3. **Model configuration** – The fastText classifier used a 256‑dimensional embedding vector, a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum n‑gram length of 3, a minimum word frequency of 3, and was trained for three epochs.  

The classifier thus learned to assign a **score** to any web page, reflecting how “mathematics‑like” its textual content is.  Because fastText operates on bag‑of‑ngrams, it can capture domain‑specific terminology (e.g., “integral”, “Galois”, “sympy”) while remaining computationally cheap enough to apply to billions of pages.

## Iterative Mining and Domain‑Level Expansion  

A single pass of the classifier over the entire Common Crawl would inevitably miss many relevant pages, primarily because the initial seed does not capture the full diversity of mathematical websites.  To remedy this, the authors devised an **iterative mining loop** that progressively broadens the notion of “mathematical domain”.

### First Iteration  
After training the fastText model, the team applied it to a **deduplicated** version of Common Crawl (see the next section).  All pages were scored, then ranked in descending order of their fastText score.  Only the top‑scoring pages were retained, and the total token budget was examined by pre‑training on several candidate sizes: 40 B, 80 B, 120 B, and 160 B tokens.  For the first round, the authors chose the **top 40 B tokens** as a conservative starting point.

### Domain‑Level Expansion  
After the first pass, many mathematical sites remained undiscovered because the classifier had been trained on a relatively narrow vocabulary.  To expand coverage, the authors performed the following analysis:

1. **Domain partitioning** – The entire Common Crawl was split into *domains*, where a domain corresponds to all pages sharing the same base URL (e.g., all pages under `mathoverflow.net`).  
2. **Coverage estimation** – For each domain, they computed the fraction of its pages that had been selected in the first iteration.  Domains with **>10 %** coverage were flagged as “math‑related”.  
3. **Manual URL annotation** – Human curators inspected the flagged domains and annotated the specific URL prefixes that actually host mathematical content (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/`).  

Pages reachable via these newly annotated prefixes, but absent from the first‑iteration set, were then **added to the seed corpus**.  By enlarging the positive training set with these newly discovered examples, the fastText model could be **re‑trained** to recognize a broader spectrum of math‑related language.  This process was repeated for **four iterations**, each time retrieving more pages, updating the classifier, and expanding the seed.

The iterative nature of the pipeline ensures a **self‑reinforcing loop**: each round’s newly discovered pages improve the classifier, which in turn uncovers even more pages in the next round.  Empirically, after the fourth iteration the authors observed that **98 %** of the final 120 B tokens had already been collected in the third iteration, indicating diminishing returns and prompting them to stop.

---

## URL‑Based Deduplication and Near‑Deduplication  

When mining billions of web pages, a large fraction of the raw data consists of duplicate or near‑duplicate content (e.g., mirrored copies of the same lecture notes, boilerplate text, or identical forum threads).  Training on such redundancy would waste computation and could bias the model toward over‑represented phrasing.

The DeepSeekMath pipeline therefore applied two complementary techniques:

1. **URL‑based deduplication** – Pages sharing the exact same URL were collapsed, removing exact copies that often appear because the same page is crawled at different times.  
2. **Near‑deduplication** – For pages with different URLs but highly overlapping text, the authors employed a similarity‑based filter (the precise algorithm is not detailed in the paper, but typical approaches involve MinHash or shingling).  This step reduced the raw Common Crawl set from **≈40 B HTML pages** to a cleaner subset, dramatically cutting the storage and compute burden while preserving linguistic diversity.

By performing deduplication **before** classifier scoring, the fastText model evaluated a far smaller, higher‑quality candidate pool, and the subsequent token budget calculations (40 B, 80 B, etc.) reflected truly distinct content.

---

## Human Annotation Loop and Classifier Re‑Training  

Even a well‑trained fastText model can make systematic errors, especially when encountering unconventional mathematical text (e.g., LaTeX fragments, multilingual snippets, or code‑heavy explanations).  To mitigate these errors, the authors introduced a **human‑in‑the‑loop** stage:

1. After each mining iteration, the set of newly retrieved pages was **sampled** and presented to annotators.  
2. Annotators **validated** whether each page indeed contained substantive mathematical material.  They also identified and corrected mislabeled negatives that the classifier had erroneously promoted.  
3. Validated pages were **added back** into the positive training set for the next fastText re‑training round.  

This feedback loop served two purposes.  First, it **improved precision** by eliminating noisy pages that would otherwise dilute the corpus.  Second, it **expanded the classifier’s lexical coverage**, because annotators often flagged pages containing less common terminology (e.g., non‑English symbols or domain‑specific jargon).  The resulting classifier became progressively better at distinguishing math‑rich content across languages and formats.

---

## De‑Contamination to Avoid Benchmark Leakage  

A critical concern for any model‑training pipeline that aims to evaluate on public benchmarks is **benchmark contamination**: inadvertently training on the exact problems (or their solutions) that appear in evaluation sets.  Such leakage would inflate reported scores without reflecting genuine reasoning ability.

The DeepSeekMath team adopted a stringent de‑contamination protocol:

- They compiled **all evaluation benchmarks** used downstream (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval).  
- For each benchmark, they extracted every possible **10‑gram substring** (a contiguous sequence of ten tokens).  
- Any web page in the corpus containing a matching 10‑gram was **removed entirely**.  
- For shorter benchmark texts (3‑gram to 9‑gram), they performed exact matching as well, ensuring that even brief, distinctive phrasing could not slip through.

This approach guarantees that **no exact question‑answer pair** from the test suites appears in the training data, preserving the integrity of subsequent evaluations.  The authors note that this de‑contamination step is applied *after* the human‑annotation and re‑training phases, because the removal of contaminated pages does not affect the classifier’s learning of genuine mathematical language.

## Multilingual Content and Token Statistics  

Unlike many prior math‑pre‑training corpora that focus almost exclusively on English (e.g., MathPile, which is 85 % arXiv papers), the DeepSeekMath Corpus deliberately includes **multilingual material**, primarily English and Chinese.  This multilinguality emerged naturally from the **domain‑level expansion** step: many high‑traffic math forums and educational sites exist in Chinese, and the fastText classifier—trained on both English and Chinese seed pages—learned to recognize them.

Empirically, the final corpus comprises **120 billion tokens** distributed as follows (approximate numbers extracted from the paper’s description):

- **English** – the majority of tokens, reflecting the abundance of English‑language mathematical sites on the public web.  
- **Chinese** – a substantial fraction, sufficient to improve performance on Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and Gaokao‑MathCloze.  

The authors observed that training on this multilingual corpus not only boosts performance on Chinese benchmarks but also **does not harm** English benchmark scores, indicating that the model can jointly learn mathematical reasoning across languages.  Moreover, the multilingual nature of the data likely contributes to the model’s robustness on out‑of‑domain tasks, as it has seen a richer variety of notation, problem styles, and pedagogical explanations.

---

## Summary of the Pipeline’s Effectiveness  

By combining a **high‑precision seed**, an **efficient fastText classifier**, **iterative domain expansion**, **rigorous deduplication**, **human validation**, and **benchmark de‑contamination**, the DeepSeekMath team produced a **120 B‑token** math‑focused corpus that is:

- **Larger** than existing public math corpora (≈7 × the size of Minerva’s web math pages and ≈9 × OpenWebMath).  
- **Higher‑quality**, as evidenced by downstream experiments where models trained on this corpus outperform those trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath, or Proof‑Pile‑2 at comparable token budgets.  
- **Multilingual**, providing balanced English–Chinese coverage and yielding gains on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks.  

The resulting **DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B** model, pre‑trained on this corpus, achieves performance on GSM8K and the competition‑level MATH benchmark that rivals or exceeds far larger closed‑source models, thereby demonstrating that **data quality and scale—particularly from publicly available web sources—can compensate for model size** in the domain of mathematical reasoning.
