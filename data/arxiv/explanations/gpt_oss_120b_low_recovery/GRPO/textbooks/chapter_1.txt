\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: **Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models**

Mathematical reasoning lies at the heart of many scientific and engineering tasks, yet it remains one of the most demanding capabilities for modern large language models (LLMs).  Unlike ordinary natural‑language generation, solving a mathematics problem requires the model to maintain a precise internal representation of symbols, follow multi‑step logical chains, and often manipulate abstract objects such as functions, sets, or geometric figures.  Small mistakes in any intermediate step can render the entire solution invalid, which makes the evaluation of mathematical reasoning particularly unforgiving.  In this chapter we set the stage for the DeepSeekMath project by first examining why existing open‑source models fall short, then surveying the benchmark ecosystem that drives progress, and finally describing the overarching goals and system architecture that guide the development of DeepSeekMath.

---

### Limitations of Existing Open‑Source Models

Open‑source LLMs have democratized access to powerful language technology, but when it comes to high‑level mathematics they still trail far behind the most advanced closed models such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra.  The primary shortcomings can be grouped into three inter‑related categories:

1. **Insufficient Exposure to Structured Mathematical Data**  
   Many publicly released models are trained predominantly on general‑purpose web text, code repositories, or synthetic datasets.  While such corpora contain occasional equations, they rarely provide the dense, step‑by‑step derivations needed for deep reasoning.  Consequently, the models develop a superficial “pattern‑matching” ability—recognizing that a problem resembles a familiar template—without learning the underlying algebraic or logical manipulations.

2. **Lack of Domain‑Specific Pre‑Training**  
   The most successful closed‑source models incorporate massive amounts of mathematical content harvested from the public web, scientific archives, and question‑answer forums.  Open‑source counterparts typically lack a dedicated mathematics pre‑training phase; they rely on a single, monolithic training pass over a heterogeneous dataset.  This one‑size‑fits‑all approach limits the model’s capacity to internalize the rigorous syntax and semantics of mathematical language.

3. **Limited Reinforcement‑Learning Fine‑Tuning**  
   Recent research demonstrates that reinforcement learning from human preferences or from automated reward models (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO) can markedly improve reasoning and problem‑solving.  However, most open‑source models are released after a modest supervised fine‑tuning stage and do not benefit from subsequent RL refinement.  The omission of this step leaves a performance gap that is especially evident on competition‑level benchmarks.

Together, these factors produce a pronounced performance gap: on the MATH benchmark—a competition‑level suite of problems requiring multi‑step reasoning—open‑source 7‑billion‑parameter models typically achieve single‑digit accuracies, whereas the best closed‑source systems surpass the 50 % mark.  Bridging this gap demands a concerted effort to (i) gather a massive, high‑quality mathematical corpus, (ii) perform dedicated pre‑training on that corpus, and (iii) apply efficient reinforcement‑learning algorithms that can further refine reasoning without prohibitive computational cost.

---

### Benchmark Landscape

To assess progress in mathematical reasoning, the community has converged on a set of standardized evaluation suites.  Each benchmark emphasizes a different facet of mathematics—ranging from elementary arithmetic to advanced olympiad‑level problem solving—and thereby provides a multidimensional view of model capabilities.  Below we outline the most influential benchmarks used in the DeepSeekMath study.

1. **GSM8K** – A collection of 8,000 grade‑school arithmetic and algebra problems, each accompanied by a detailed chain‑of‑thought solution.  GSM8K is widely regarded as the entry point for quantitative reasoning; success on this set indicates a model can reliably perform step‑wise calculations and simple algebraic manipulations.

2. **MATH** – A competition‑level dataset containing over 12,000 problems drawn from mathematics contests such as the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME) and the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO).  Problems typically require multiple reasoning steps, clever insight, and occasionally geometric or combinatorial arguments.  The benchmark is measured in a “self‑contained” setting: the model must output a complete textual solution without external tools.

3. **CMATH** – The Chinese counterpart of GSM8K, containing grade‑school problems written in Mandarin.  CMATH allows researchers to evaluate multilingual reasoning and highlights the importance of non‑English training data.

4. **SAT Math** – A subset of the SAT quantitative section, emphasizing high‑school level algebra, geometry, and data analysis.  This benchmark stresses time‑constrained reasoning and the ability to handle multiple‑choice formats.

5. **MMLU‑STEM** – The STEM portion of the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark, comprising multiple‑choice questions across physics, chemistry, biology, and mathematics.  Though not a pure math benchmark, performance on the math subset serves as an indicator of broader scientific reasoning.

6. **MiniF2F** – A formal mathematics benchmark that asks models to translate informal problem statements into formal proofs in the Isabelle proof assistant.  Success here signals a model’s capacity for theorem‑proving and integration with formal verification tools.

7. **Chinese Benchmarks (Gaokao‑MathCloze, Gaokao‑MathQA, MGSM‑zh)** – Designed around the Chinese national college‑entrance examinations, these datasets test advanced high‑school algebra, calculus, and geometry in Mandarin, and they often require a mix of textual reasoning and symbolic computation.

Evaluating across this diverse suite is crucial because a model that excels on GSM8K may still falter on MATH’s deeper problems, or on Chinese benchmarks if its pre‑training data is monolingual.  The DeepSeekMath project therefore adopts a unified evaluation protocol that reports both **step‑by‑step (chain‑of‑thought)** performance—where the model must generate a readable solution—and **tool‑integrated (program‑of‑thought)** performance—where the model may emit executable Python code to aid calculation.

### Goals of DeepSeekMath: Performance, Openness, and Scalability

The DeepSeekMath initiative was launched to address the three limitations identified above while adhering to a set of aspirational goals:

1. **Close the Accuracy Gap on Competitive Benchmarks**  
   The primary quantitative target was to surpass the 50 % accuracy threshold on the competition‑level MATH benchmark **without** resorting to external toolkits, voting ensembles, or post‑processing tricks.  Achieving this would place an open‑source model in the same performance tier as proprietary systems such as Gemini‑Ultra and GPT‑4.

2. **Maintain Full Openness and Reproducibility**  
   Unlike closed models whose training recipes, data sources, and hyper‑parameters remain proprietary, DeepSeekMath commits to publishing the full data selection pipeline, model checkpoints, and training scripts.  This openness enables the research community to replicate results, scrutinize data contamination, and extend the work to new domains.

3. **Demonstrate Scalability with Modest Compute**  
   By exploiting a carefully engineered data selection mechanism and an efficient reinforcement‑learning algorithm (Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO), DeepSeekMath shows that a 7‑billion‑parameter model can match or exceed the performance of much larger closed‑source counterparts.  The success of this approach suggests that scaling up model size is not the sole path to stronger reasoning; high‑quality data and algorithmic efficiency can compensate for limited parameter budgets.

4. **Promote Multilingual Mathematical Reasoning**  
   The DeepSeekMath corpus deliberately integrates Chinese web pages alongside English sources, yielding a multilingual token distribution.  Empirical results reveal that multilingual pre‑training improves performance on both English and Chinese benchmarks, underscoring the importance of diverse language exposure.

5. **Provide a Unified Paradigm for Reinforcement Learning in LLMs**  
   The project not only introduces GRPO—a memory‑efficient variant of PPO that eliminates the need for a separate critic—but also situates it within a broader theoretical framework that unifies supervised fine‑tuning, rejection‑sampling fine‑tuning, direct preference optimization, and various RL methods.  By exposing the common structure of these techniques, DeepSeekMath aims to guide future research on more effective RL for LLMs.

Collectively, these goals shape a research agenda that balances ambitious performance targets with a commitment to transparency and resource‑conscious methodology.

### Overview of the DeepSeekMath System Architecture

The DeepSeekMath pipeline consists of three major stages, each designed to progressively endow the model with stronger mathematical reasoning capabilities.  The stages are loosely sequential but also interact iteratively through data feedback loops.

1. **Mathematical Pre‑Training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus**  
   The foundation of the system is a massive, 120‑billion‑token corpus harvested from the Common Crawl.  The corpus is built via an iterative data‑selection pipeline that starts from a seed set (OpenWebMath), trains a fastText classifier to recognize mathematics‑rich pages, and refines the classifier through multiple rounds of manual annotation and domain‑level analysis.  After four iterations the pipeline yields 35.5 million high‑quality web pages, spanning English and Chinese content.  The model—initially the code‑oriented DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B parameters)—continues pre‑training on this corpus for 500 billion tokens, interleaving a modest proportion of natural‑language and code data to preserve coding abilities.

2. **Mathematical Instruction Tuning (Supervised Fine‑Tuning)**  
   Following pre‑training, the model undergoes supervised fine‑tuning on 776 k carefully curated instruction examples.  Each example pairs a problem statement (in either English or Chinese) with a solution expressed in one of three formats:
   - **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)**: step‑by‑step textual reasoning.  
   - **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)**: a runnable Python script that computes the answer using libraries such as `math` or `sympy`.  
   - **Tool‑Integrated Reasoning (ToRA)**: a hybrid that mixes natural‑language explanation with occasional tool calls.  
   This stage yields the **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B** model, which already surpasses all open‑source 7‑B models on a wide range of benchmarks.

3. **Reinforcement Learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
   To push performance beyond the ceiling of supervised fine‑tuning, DeepSeekMath applies an RL refinement step.  GRPO differs from classic PPO in two key ways:
   - It **removes the critic** (value‑function network) and instead uses the average reward of a group of sampled outputs as a baseline, drastically reducing memory consumption.
   - It **regularizes directly with the KL divergence** between the current policy and a reference (the previous iteration of the model), rather than embedding the KL penalty into the per‑token reward.
   
   During training, each question is answered by sampling **64** responses from the current policy.  A learned reward model scores each response (either outcome‑level or step‑level).  Advantages are computed relative to the group mean, and the policy parameters are updated to increase the probability of higher‑advantage tokens while staying close to the reference.  This process is iterated, periodically refreshing the reward model with newly sampled data.  The final product, **DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B**, achieves **88.2 %** accuracy on GSM8K and **51.7 %** on MATH—setting a new open‑source record.

The entire architecture can be visualized as a **data‑centric loop**: high‑quality web data fuels pre‑training, which creates a stronger base model; the base model then generates richer instruction data, which further refines the model through supervised and reinforcement learning; the refined model, in turn, can be used to harvest even better data for future iterations.  This virtuous cycle embodies the core philosophy of DeepSeekMath: **quality data + efficient algorithms = powerful reasoning, even at modest scale**.

--- 

In summary, mathematical reasoning poses a unique set of challenges for language models, and existing open‑source systems have not yet met the demanding standards set by competitive benchmarks.  By constructing a massive, multilingual mathematics corpus, applying a two‑phase fine‑tuning regimen (supervised instruction followed by GRPO‑based reinforcement learning), and rigorously evaluating across a broad benchmark landscape, DeepSeekMath attains performance that rivals much larger proprietary models while preserving openness and reproducibility.  The architecture and methodological insights presented in this chapter provide a solid foundation for anyone seeking to understand or extend state‑of‑the‑art mathematical reasoning in large language models.
