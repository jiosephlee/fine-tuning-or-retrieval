\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 8: # Training and Results of DeepSeekMath‑RL

In this chapter we describe in detail how the reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage of the DeepSeekMath project was carried out, the design choices that were made, and the quantitative impact of this stage on a variety of mathematical and general‑language benchmarks.  The discussion is anchored entirely on the methodology and experiments reported in the DeepSeekMath paper, and we avoid introducing material that does not appear in the source text.

---  

## RL Data Selection (GSM8K & MATH Questions Only)

The RL phase builds on the instruction‑tuned model **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B**.  To investigate how far reinforcement learning can improve a model that already possesses strong chain‑of‑thought (CoT) abilities, the authors limited the RL training data to the set of *question‑only* prompts that had been used during the supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) stage for the **GSM8K** and **MATH** datasets.  This set contains roughly **144 K** distinct questions.  No additional problem statements, solution sketches, or tool‑use data were included in the RL corpus; the purpose was to assess the effect of RL when the model is exposed only to a narrow, in‑domain distribution of problems.

By restricting the RL data to these two benchmarks, the authors could cleanly separate **in‑domain** effects (performance on GSM8K and MATH) from **out‑of‑domain** effects (performance on other Chinese and English benchmarks such as CMATH, MMLU‑STEM, BBH, etc.).  The design also mirrors the approach taken in prior work on mathematical RL, where a modest, high‑quality question pool is used to keep the RL signal focused and computationally tractable.

---  

## Reward Model Training and Replay Mechanism

The RL framework relies on a **reward model** $r_{\varphi}$ that assigns a scalar score to a generated answer.  The authors first instantiated the reward model by fine‑tuning a **DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B** checkpoint.  Training was performed with a learning rate of **$2\times10^{-5}$**, following the same optimizer settings used for the pre‑training stages (AdamW with $\beta_{1}=0.9$, $\beta_{2}=0.95$, weight decay $=0.1$).  

During RL, the reward model is **iteratively updated**.  After each outer RL iteration, a new set of (question, model‑generated answer, human‑annotated preference) triples is added to a replay buffer that retains **10 %** of the historical data.  The reward model is then re‑trained on this mixture of fresh and replayed samples, ensuring that it gradually adapts to the evolving policy while preserving knowledge of earlier generations.  This continual updating is essential because, as the policy improves, the distribution of generated answers shifts, and a static reward model would become poorly calibrated.

---  

## Sampling Strategy (64 Outputs per Question, Batch Size, Max Length)

Reinforcement learning proceeds by **sampling multiple candidate completions** for each question from the current policy (the “old” policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$).  The authors chose a **group size $G=64$**, i.e., for every question $q$ they generated 64 distinct outputs $\{o_{1},\dots,o_{64}\}$ using nucleus sampling with a maximum generation length of **1024 tokens**.  These 64 candidates constitute a *group* for the **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** algorithm.

Training was performed with **batch size 1024** (i.e., 1024 tokens per gradient step).  Because each group contains many tokens, the effective number of tokens processed per update is significantly larger than the raw batch size.  The **policy learning rate** was set to **$1\times10^{-6}$**, far lower than the learning rate used for the reward model to ensure stable policy updates.  A **KL‑coefficient $\beta = 0.04$** was introduced to regularize the policy toward the reference model (the previous checkpoint of the policy) via an explicit KL‑divergence term in the GRPO objective, rather than embedding the KL penalty inside the per‑token reward as in standard PPO.

The sampling and update cycle follows the algorithmic outline in the paper:

1. Sample a batch of questions from the GSM8K/MATH pool.  
2. For each question, generate a group of 64 answers using $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  
3. Score each answer with the current reward model $r_{\varphi}$ to obtain rewards $\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$.  
4. Compute **group‑relative advantages** $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ by normalizing the rewards within the group (either outcome‑only or step‑wise, see the next section).  
5. Update the policy by maximizing the GRPO surrogate objective (Equation (5) in the paper).  
6. After a fixed number of policy updates, **refresh the reward model** using the replay buffer and set the current policy as the new reference model for the next iteration.

---  

## Outcome vs. Process Supervision

GRPO supports two distinct ways of turning reward scores into token‑level advantages:

1. **Outcome Supervision** – The reward model provides a single scalar for each complete answer.  The reward is normalized by the group mean and standard deviation, and *all* tokens in that answer receive the same advantage value $\hat{A}_{i,t}= \widetilde{r}_i$.

2. **Process Supervision** – A finer‑grained reward model evaluates the quality of each *reasoning step* (e.g., after each line of CoT).  For each step the model outputs a reward, which is similarly normalized.  The advantage for token $t$ is the sum of the normalized rewards of all subsequent steps, i.e., $\hat{A}_{i,t}= \sum_{j:\,\text{step}_j\ge t} \widetilde{r}^{\text{step}_j}_i$.

The paper reports that **process‑supervised GRPO** consistently outperforms outcome‑supervised GRPO across the evaluated benchmarks, indicating that providing step‑level feedback helps the policy learn more precise reasoning behaviors.

## Quantitative Results

The RL‑enhanced model is referred to as **DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B**.  Its performance is compared against both the base model (DeepSeekMath‑Base) and the instruction‑tuned model (DeepSeekMath‑Instruct) on a suite of **in‑domain** and **out‑of‑domain** benchmarks.

| Benchmark | Metric | DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B | DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B |
|-----------|--------|--------------------------|--------------------|
| **GSM8K** (CoT) | Accuracy | 82.9 % | **88.2 %** |
| **MATH** (CoT) | Accuracy | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** |
| **CMATH** (Chinese) | Accuracy | 84.6 % | **88.8 %** |
| **MMLU‑STEM** | Accuracy | 68.3 % | 69.5 % (slight gain) |
| **BBH** | Accuracy | 41.2 % | 42.7 % |

*In‑domain* scores (GSM8K and MATH) improve by **5.3 %** and **4.9 %** absolute respectively, moving DeepSeekMath‑RL into the same performance ballpark as proprietary models such as Gemini‑Ultra and GPT‑4 on the competition‑level MATH benchmark.  Notably, the improvement is achieved **without any additional training data** beyond the original SFT set; the RL stage merely reshapes the existing policy.

*Out‑of‑domain* benchmarks also show modest gains, evidencing a **generalization benefit** from the RL fine‑tuning.  For example, CMATH (a Chinese quantitative‑reasoning dataset) rises from 84.6 % to 88.8 %, and the multi‑step reasoning tasks in BBH and MMLU‑STEM receive small but consistent lifts.

## Ablation Studies

The authors performed a series of controlled experiments to isolate the contributions of individual GRPO hyper‑parameters and design choices.

### Group Size ($G$)

The default group size of **64** was compared against smaller groups (e.g., $G=8$ and $G=16$).  Larger groups provide a more stable baseline (the group mean) and allow the advantage estimator to capture finer relative differences among candidates.  Empirically, increasing $G$ from 16 to 64 yielded a **≈1.2 %** absolute improvement on MATH, while further increases showed diminishing returns.

### KL Coefficient ($\beta$)

The KL regularization term controls how tightly the new policy stays near the reference model.  Experiments with $\beta\in\{0.01,0.04,0.08\}$ demonstrated that **$\beta=0.04$** balances two competing forces: a low $\beta$ permits aggressive policy updates but can cause instability, whereas a high $\beta$ overly penalizes any deviation, limiting performance gains.  The reported results use the middle ground.

### Outcome vs. Process Supervision

When the reward model supplies only a final answer score (outcome supervision), the resulting DeepSeekMath‑RL model improves GSM8K by about **4 %** absolute.  Switching to **process supervision** (step‑wise rewards) adds an extra **≈1 %** on GSM8K and **≈1.5 %** on MATH, confirming that the extra granularity of the signal is beneficial for complex reasoning tasks.

### Iterative RL

The paper also explored a **two‑round iterative RL** schedule, wherein after an initial GRPO pass the policy becomes the new reference model, the reward model is refreshed using a replay buffer, and a second GRPO pass is performed.  The first iteration yields the bulk of the gains (≈4–5 % absolute), while the second iteration adds a modest **≈0.7 %** boost on MATH, illustrating that the iterative scheme can still extract residual performance after the primary pass.

## Summary of the RL Impact

The reinforcement‑learning stage, centered on the **Group Relative Policy Optimization** algorithm, delivers a **significant and consistent performance boost** across both the core mathematical benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) and related out‑of‑domain tasks.  The key factors enabling this improvement are:

1. **High‑quality, narrowly focused RL data** (only GSM8K and MATH questions) which ensures that the policy sees dense, relevant feedback.
2. **A reward model that is continually refreshed** via a replay mechanism, preventing drift between the policy and its evaluator.
3. **Group‑relative advantage estimation**, which eliminates the need for a separate critic network and reduces memory consumption while still providing low‑variance gradients.
4. **Process‑level supervision**, which supplies the policy with fine‑grained guidance for each reasoning step.

Collectively, these design choices allow a 7‑billion‑parameter open‑source model to surpass all other open‑source baselines of comparable size and approach the performance of much larger proprietary systems, all **without any external toolkits, voting ensembles, or additional data beyond the original instruction‑tuning set**.  The results validate the authors’ claim that reinforcement learning, when applied in a carefully engineered pipeline, is a powerful lever for enhancing mathematical reasoning in language models.
