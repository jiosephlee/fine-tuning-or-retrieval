\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText classifier distinguish mathematical web pages from general pages in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline?
Question:
The paper mentions training a fastText model on 500k positive and 500k negative examples, then using it to recall math pages from Common Crawl. I’m unclear on:
1. What features does fastText use (e.g., n‑grams, character‑grams) to capture mathematical content?
2. How is the decision threshold chosen when ranking pages by the model’s scores?
3. Does the classifier consider LaTeX fragments or code snippets, and how are they tokenized for fastText?
Understanding these details would help me replicate the data selection process.
Answer:
The paper only gives a short “implementation‑detail” paragraph for the fastText‑based page filter, but from that paragraph we can infer exactly what the authors did and why it works for spotting mathematical web pages.

**What the fastText model sees**

FastText is a linear text‑classifier that represents a document as the sum (or average) of the embeddings of its *word* n‑grams.  
When the authors train it they set

* **vector dimension = 256** – each n‑gram is mapped to a 256‑dimensional vector.  
* **max word n‑gram = 3** – every contiguous sequence of 1, 2 or 3 words that appears in the document becomes a feature. (FastText also builds character‑gram features internally, but the paper does not mention changing the default; the default character‑ngram range (3‑6) is kept, so the model also sees sub‑word patterns.)  
* **min word count = 3** – a word (or n‑gram) must appear at least three times in the whole training corpus to be kept, which removes noisy rare tokens such as random identifiers or one‑off URLs.  

Thus the feature space consists of **bag‑of‑word‑n‑grams (up to trigrams) plus the built‑in character‑gram features**. This combination is well‑suited to mathematical pages because many of the cues that make a page “math‑related” are lexical rather than syntactic: the presence of words like *theorem*, *proof*, *integral*, *lemma*, *corollary*, or even domain‑specific phrases such as *Euler’s formula*; and the character‑grams capture LaTeX‑style markup (e.g. “\(\backslash\)frac”, “\(\backslash\)sum”, “\(\backslash\)int”) that would otherwise be split into unknown tokens.

**Training data and label balance**

The authors draw **500 k positive examples** from the OpenWebMath seed corpus and **500 k negative examples** from a random sample of Common Crawl pages. The two‑class training set is therefore perfectly balanced, which means the learned linear hyper‑plane directly reflects the log‑odds of a page being math‑related. No extra weighting or cost‑sensitive loss is mentioned.

**Scoring and the decision threshold**

FastText outputs a **raw class score** (the dot product between the summed n‑gram vector of a document and the weight vector of the positive class). The authors use this score only for **ranking**: after scoring every deduplicated page they keep the “top‑ranking” ones. The exact cutoff (e.g. keep the top N pages or keep everything above a fixed probability) is not listed, but the description “only preserve the top‑ranking ones” implies that they sort all pages by the positive‑class log‑probability and select a fixed token budget (40 B tokens in the first iteration). In practice you would pick a threshold that yields the desired total token count; the classifier itself does not need a calibrated probability, just a monotonic score.

**Handling LaTeX and code snippets**

Because fastText works on raw token strings, any LaTeX fragments that appear in the HTML source become part of the token stream. The model does **not** perform any special parsing of `<math>` tags or separate the code from the surrounding text. However, the character‑gram features (default 3‑6 char grams) automatically pick up the back‑slash and brace patterns that are characteristic of LaTeX (`\frac`, `\sum`, `{}`, etc.). Likewise, short code snippets (e.g. Python `def`, `for i in range`) are captured by word‑n‑grams and by character‑grams. Consequently the classifier can distinguish a page that mainly contains plain prose from one that is heavy in mathematical markup or programming examples, even though it never sees a full parse tree.

**Why this works for replication**

If you want to reproduce the pipeline, the steps are:

1. **Prepare the training set** – 500 k positive pages (any high‑quality math web corpus) and 500 k negative pages (random Common Crawl).  
2. **Tokenisation** – feed the raw extracted text (HTML stripped of tags, but keep LaTeX delimiters) directly to fastText; the library will internally generate word‑n‑grams up to length 3 and character‑grams 3‑6.  
3. **Train** – `fasttext supervised -dim 256 -lr 0.1 -epoch 3 -wordNgrams 3 -minCount 3`.  
4. **Score the whole crawl** – run `predict-prob` (or `predict` and keep the raw score) on each deduplicated page.  
5. **Rank and cut** – sort pages by the positive‑class score and keep the top fraction that yields the desired token budget (e.g. 40 B tokens for the first iteration).  

In short, the classifier relies on **bag‑of‑word‑trigrams plus sub‑word character n‑grams**, which naturally encode the frequent mathematical terminology and LaTeX syntax that differentiate math pages from general web content. The decision threshold is not a fixed probability but a rank‑based cut that matches the token budget required for the downstream pre‑training. No extra LaTeX‑specific tokeniser is needed; the built‑in character‑gram features are sufficient to capture the markup. This is why the authors were able to retrieve high‑quality mathematical web pages at scale using a very lightweight model.
