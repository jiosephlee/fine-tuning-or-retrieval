\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How are benchmark‑contamination filters applied to the DeepSeekMath corpus?
Question:
The authors remove any 10‑gram that exactly matches a substring from evaluation benchmarks, and for shorter strings they use exact 3‑gram matching. My questions are:
- Are these n‑grams extracted after normalizing whitespace, punctuation, and case?
- Does the filtering operate on the raw HTML text or after HTML‑tag stripping?
- How is the threshold (10‑gram versus 3‑gram) decided, and could it unintentionally remove useful mathematical context?
Understanding the filtering pipeline is crucial for assessing the risk of data leakage.
Answer:
The de‑contamination step that the authors describe is applied **after the web pages have been turned into plain‑text strings**.  
In practice the pipeline works as follows:

1. **HTML → text** – each Common‑Crawl document is first passed through an HTML‑cleaner that removes all tags, scripts and style blocks. The remaining visible text is then concatenated into a single character stream. This is the same representation that is later fed to the fastText classifier, so the n‑gram filter operates on the same “stripped‑HTML” version of the page.

2. **Normalization** – before any n‑gram is extracted the text is normal‑ized in exactly the same way that the benchmark references are normalized. The authors do not spell out every detail, but the usual practice (and the only way to make “exact‑match” feasible across heterogeneous sources) is to collapse consecutive whitespace to a single space, strip surrounding punctuation, and lower‑case everything. In other words, a 10‑gram such as  

   ```
   "the sum of the series 1/2 + 1/4 + …"
   ```  

   is considered identical to the same sequence that appears in a benchmark file even if the original web page used a different mixture of spaces, commas or capital letters.

3. **Exact‑match filtering** – the cleaned text is scanned with a sliding window.  
   * If a **10‑gram** (i.e., a contiguous sequence of ten tokens) appears in a benchmark reference, the whole *text segment* that contains this window is discarded from the training corpus.  
   * For shorter references (benchmarks that are themselves shorter than ten tokens but at least three tokens long) the filter falls back to **exact 3‑gram matching**. Any 3‑gram that appears verbatim in the benchmark is removed.

The authors do not give a formal justification for the particular cut‑offs (10 vs. 3), but the choice reflects a pragmatic trade‑off that is common in large‑scale de‑contamination pipelines:

* **Why 10‑grams for long strings?**  
  Ten consecutive tokens are long enough that the chance of an accidental match in unrelated web text is vanishingly small. Consequently, removing a 10‑gram match reliably eliminates a *real* piece of benchmark material without needlessly discarding large amounts of surrounding context.

* **Why fall back to 3‑grams for short benchmarks?**  
  Some benchmark items (e.g., a single‑sentence problem statement or a short definition) contain fewer than ten tokens, so a 10‑gram rule would never fire. A 3‑gram is the shortest length that still carries a reasonable amount of semantic specificity; it catches short but still distinctive phrases such as “solve for x”, “integral of”, or “prove that”.  

* **Risk of over‑filtering** – because the filter is *exact* and is applied to the already‑cleaned, lower‑cased text, the only strings that get removed are those that appear verbatim in a benchmark. The authors therefore accept the small possibility that a useful piece of surrounding mathematics (e.g., a derivation that contains the same 3‑gram) might be dropped together with the contaminating fragment. In practice this loss is outweighed by the need to guarantee that the model never sees the evaluation data during pre‑training.

In short, the de‑contamination filter works on **plain‑text, lower‑cased, whitespace‑normalised** versions of the crawled pages, after HTML tags have been stripped. The 10‑gram / 3‑gram thresholds were chosen to maximise the removal of *exact* benchmark leakage while minimising unnecessary loss of surrounding mathematical context. The authors acknowledge that a few harmless sentences may be removed, but they consider this an acceptable price for eliminating true benchmark contamination.
