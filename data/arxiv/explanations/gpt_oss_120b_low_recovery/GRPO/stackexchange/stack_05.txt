\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does reinforcement learning improve Maj@K but not Pass@K on mathematical benchmarks?
Question:
Figure 9 shows that after GRPO training the Maj@K (majority vote) accuracy rises while Pass@K (any correct sample) stays flat. The authors hypothesize that RL makes the output distribution more robust, pushing the correct answer higher in the top‑K. Could this be due to:
- The reward model only distinguishing correct vs. incorrect answers (binary), thus reinforcing already‑good answers rather than creating new solution strategies?
- The use of a fixed group size (64 samples) limiting diversity of generated solutions?
I would like a deeper explanation of the mechanism, perhaps with an analysis of probability mass shifts across the output distribution.
Answer:
The fact that **Maj@K** (the *majority‑vote* accuracy over a fixed group of \(K\) samples) goes up after GRPO while **Pass@K** (the *any‑correct* accuracy) stays flat is a direct consequence of what the reinforcement‑learning signal is actually doing to the model’s output distribution.

### What the paper measures  

In the RL stage the authors train a reward model that only tells *“this whole solution is correct / incorrect”* (a binary outcome).  
For each question they draw a **group** of \(G=64\) responses from the current policy, evaluate each with the reward model, and compute a *group‑relative* advantage:

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_{1..G})}{\operatorname{std}(r_{1..G})}\; .
\]

Because the advantage is *relative* to the other samples in the same group, the update **pushes the probability mass of the better‑scoring responses upward** and **pulls the mass of the worse‑scoring responses downward**. The KL‑penalty term keeps the overall distribution from drifting too far from the reference (the SFT model), but it does not prevent the relative re‑ranking of the samples inside the group.

### Why Maj@K improves

Maj@K looks at the *most frequent answer* among the \(K\) samples and asks whether that answer is correct. After GRPO training the distribution of correct answers becomes more **concentrated**: a larger fraction of the 64 samples now place the correct answer near the top of the ranking, and consequently the same answer tends to appear more often in the group. Even if the absolute probability of the correct answer (the marginal \(p_{\theta}(\text{correct}\mid q)\)) does not change dramatically, the *relative* ordering inside the group improves, and the majority vote flips from a wrong answer to the right one. Hence Maj@K goes up.

### Why Pass@K stays flat

Pass@K only cares whether **any** of the \(K\) samples is correct. As long as there is *at least one* correct answer in the group, Pass@K is counted as a success. The binary reward already ensures that the policy puts some probability on correct answers (otherwise the reward would be zero and the gradient would vanish). GRPO’s relative advantage therefore does not need to create *new* correct samples—it merely reshapes the distribution of existing ones. If the pre‑RL model already had, say, a 30 % chance that at least one of the 64 samples is correct, the RL update cannot push this probability much higher because the KL regularizer limits how much total probability mass can be moved toward the correct answer. The net effect is that the **tail** of the distribution (the rare events that produce a correct answer) changes only slightly, leaving Pass@K essentially unchanged.

### Intuitive picture of the probability‑mass shift  

Imagine the probability mass over all possible completions for a given question as a bar chart. Before RL, the correct answer sits on a modest bar, while many other distractor answers occupy the surrounding bars. After GRPO:

* The bar of the *best* correct answer is raised (the model assigns higher token‑wise probabilities to the steps that lead to that answer).
* The bars of the *worst* answers in the same group are lowered, because their relative advantage is negative.
* The total area under the curve is kept roughly constant by the KL penalty, so the increase in the correct bar is compensated by a slight reduction spread over many incorrect bars.

Maj@K cares about the **height of the tallest bar** (the majority), so it benefits from the lifted correct bar. Pass@K cares about the **presence of any non‑zero bar** for the correct answer, which already existed before RL and does not gain much from merely reshaping the heights.

### How the two hypotheses in the question fit

1. **Binary reward model** – because the reward only distinguishes *correct vs. incorrect* at the whole‑output level, the gradient is *coarse*: it rewards any output that is already correct, but it does not provide a signal that would guide the model to discover *new* solution strategies. Consequently the RL stage mostly **reinforces** the solutions that were already being generated, moving them higher in the ranking (Maj@K) rather than creating additional correct samples (Pass@K).

2. **Fixed group size of 64** – the group size determines how many relative comparisons the algorithm can make per question. With \(G=64\) the model can already achieve a reliable estimate of the mean and variance of the rewards, which is enough to push the top‑ranked correct answer upward. However, increasing \(G\) would not necessarily raise Pass@K either, because the KL regularizer still caps the total probability that can be transferred to the correct mode. The limitation is therefore not the group size per se, but the **type of supervision** (outcome only, binary) combined with the KL constraint.

### Take‑away  

GRPO’s reinforcement learning makes the model’s distribution **more peaked** around the already‑correct solutions, which is exactly what Maj@K measures. Since Pass@K only needs *any* correct sample, and the binary reward does not generate new correct modes, Pass@K remains flat. To obtain a genuine increase in Pass@K one would need either (i) a richer reward signal that rewards intermediate reasoning steps (process supervision) and/or (ii) a reward that can distinguish *better* correct solutions from *just* correct ones, thereby encouraging the model to explore additional correct answer modes rather than simply re‑ranking the existing ones. This interpretation is fully consistent with the analysis and discussion presented in Section 5.2 of the DeepSeekMath paper.
