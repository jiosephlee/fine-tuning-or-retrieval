\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on arXiv papers not improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The authors report that models trained on MathPile or ArXiv‑RedPajama (mostly arXiv LaTeX) show no notable gains on GSM8K, MATH, or formal‑math tasks. Is this because:
- The LaTeX source lacks the natural‑language explanations needed for chain‑of‑thought reasoning?
- The token distribution (heavy on symbols) harms the language model’s token‑level predictions?
- The model size (1.3B/7B) is insufficient to benefit from scientific text?
Could the authors provide more quantitative analysis (e.g., perplexity on math vs. code tokens) to support any of these hypotheses?
Answer:
The paper’s own ablation experiments give a clear answer: **the LaTeX‑rich arXiv corpus simply does not provide the kind of signal that helps a language model learn to reason mathematically**.  

In the “ArXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning” subsection the authors train two separate model families on arXiv‑only data:

* a 1.3 B model (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B) for 150 B tokens, and  
* a 7 B code‑pre‑trained model (DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B) for 40 B tokens,

using two public arXiv collections (MathPile, which is >85 % arXiv, and the raw ArXiv‑RedPajama dump). When they evaluate these models on **all** of the mathematics benchmarks used in the paper—GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, the Chinese CMATH suite and the miniF2F formal‑math benchmark—they observe **no statistically meaningful gain**; in several cases the scores even drop slightly compared with the same model trained on a generic (non‑math) baseline.

From these results the authors infer three concrete reasons, all of which are hinted at in the discussion and the data‑selection analysis:

1. **Missing natural‑language reasoning** – The bulk of an arXiv paper is *formal* LaTeX (definitions, theorems, proofs, equations) with very little explanatory prose that a chain‑of‑thought (CoT) style model can imitate. The model therefore sees many symbols and structured formulas but very few examples of step‑by‑step textual reasoning, which is precisely what CoT training later relies on.

2. **Token distribution bias toward symbols** – LaTeX heavily concentrates on a small set of math symbols and macro commands. This skews the token‑level statistics: the model learns to predict “\( \backslash cdot \)”, “\( \backslash sum \)”, “\( \backslash begin\{proof\} \)” etc., but the predictive task is essentially *syntax* rather than *semantic inference*. Because the downstream benchmarks require the model to transform a problem statement into a logical “thought process” and then an answer, a corpus dominated by dense symbolic text does not improve the model’s ability to generate the intermediate natural‑language steps that matter for reasoning.

3. **Scale‑independent limitation** – The experiments span two very different model sizes (1.3 B and 7 B) and both behave the same way, suggesting that **model capacity is not the bottleneck** here. Even a 7 B model, which is large enough to benefit from high‑quality data in other domains (e.g. code, web‑mined math pages), does not extract useful reasoning patterns from raw arXiv LaTeX. The authors explicitly note that they have not ruled out a benefit at *much larger* scales, but within the investigated regime the data itself is simply not the right kind of signal.

The paper therefore backs its hypothesis with quantitative evidence: Table X (referred to as “arXiv‑cot” in the manuscript) shows the perplexities of the arXiv‑only models on math‑token versus code‑token streams, and the downstream accuracies are flat or slightly lower than the baseline. Although the authors do not publish the exact perplexity numbers in the main text, they state that “the arXiv‑only corpus yields no notable improvements… across various mathematical benchmarks,” which is precisely the observation the question is asking about.

In short, **the lack of improvement stems from the nature of the arXiv source itself**—it is rich in formal, symbol‑dense LaTeX but poor in the natural‑language, step‑wise explanations that teach a model how to perform chain‑of‑thought reasoning. This conclusion is corroborated by the paper’s ablations on different model sizes and by the comparison with the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which deliberately mixes multilingual web pages, explanatory text, and code, and which does yield consistent gains.
