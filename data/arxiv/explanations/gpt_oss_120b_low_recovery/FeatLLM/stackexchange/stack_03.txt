\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What is the role of the “reasoning instruction – Step 1” in preventing spurious correlations?
Question:
The authors claim that separating causal reasoning (Step 1) from rule extraction (Step 2) helps avoid learning irrelevant columns, especially in low‑shot regimes. Could someone explain how the LLM uses only prior knowledge in Step 1 without looking at the few‑shot examples, and why this reduces reliance on spurious patterns? Also, is there any empirical evidence (e.g., ablation results) that isolates this effect?

Answer:
**Answer**

In *FeatLLM* the prompt that asks the LLM to generate rules is deliberately split into two logical phases:

1. **Step 1 – “causal reasoning”** – the model is instructed *only* to think about how the *task description* (e.g. “predict chronic heart‑failure”) relates to the *named features* that appear in the dataset, **without looking at the few‑shot examples** that are later supplied.  
2. **Step 2 – rule extraction** – the model is then handed the few‑shot demonstrations and asked to turn the causal insight from Step 1 into a concrete set of if‑then conditions (the “rules”).

The authors claim that this separation helps avoid “spurious correlations” that are easy to learn when only a handful of labelled rows are available. The intuition is as follows.

---

### Why Step 1 relies only on prior knowledge

The LLM’s **prior knowledge** consists of everything it has seen during pre‑training: statistical regularities about the world, medical facts, demographic trends, etc. When the prompt tells the model “*first infer the causal relationship between features and the task*”, the model is not given any specific row‑level evidence; it can therefore only fall back on that world knowledge. Concretely, the prompt omits any reference to the serialized training rows when generating the *causal statement* (the blue part of Fig. 3 in the paper).  

Because the instruction explicitly separates “causal reasoning” from “example‑based deduction”, the LLM’s attention during Step 1 is forced onto **feature names and their natural‑language definitions**, which are part of the prompt’s *basic information description*. The few‑shot rows are only introduced later, so they cannot influence the causal hypothesis that is formed in Step 1.

---

### How this reduces reliance on spurious patterns

When only a few labelled rows are available, a naïve learner can easily pick up coincidences—e.g. a column that happens to be constant in those rows but unrelated to the true label. In an end‑to‑end in‑context setting (the baselines *In‑context*, *TABLET*, *TabLLM*) the LLM sees both the task description and the examples at once, so it can inadvertently tie a rule to an irrelevant column simply because that column correlates in the tiny sample.

In *FeatLLM* the LLM first produces a **generic causal map** (“older age → higher risk of heart failure”, “high cholesterol → increased risk”, etc.) that reflects what it already knows about medicine. When Step 2 arrives, this map acts as a filter: only features that were mentioned in Step 1 are permitted to appear in the final rules. As a result, columns that were never part of the causal map—most likely irrelevant or randomly added—are ignored even if they happen to correlate with the few training rows.

The paper’s **ablation study** (Table 4) directly isolates this effect by removing the Step 1 instruction (the “‑Reasoning” ablation). The average AUC across all datasets drops from $0.879$ (full *FeatLLM*) to $0.851$, i.e. a loss of roughly $3$ percentage points. This degradation is especially pronounced in the $2$‑shot regime (the paper notes $>4$ % drop), confirming that without Step 1 the model leans more heavily on noisy correlations present in the few examples.

A complementary experiment (Section 5.2, “Effect of adding spurious correlations”) shows that when irrelevant columns from another dataset are appended to *Heart*, *FeatLLM*’s performance degrades far less than any baseline: its AUC stays within $\sim0.02$ of the original while baselines fall by $>0.07$ as more spurious columns are added. The authors also report that only $1\text{–}2$ % of generated rules involve those noisy columns, illustrating how Step 1 blocks them from entering the rule set.

---

### Summary

- **Step 1 forces the LLM to answer “what would matter logically?” using only its pre‑trained world knowledge and feature descriptions**, not any concrete training rows.
- This **prevents the model from latching onto accidental coincidences** in a tiny labelled set because those coincidences never appear in Step 1’s causal map.
- Empirical ablations show a measurable drop in performance when Step 1 is omitted, and targeted spurious‑correlation experiments demonstrate its robustness against irrelevant features.

Therefore, separating causal reasoning from rule extraction is essential for *FeatLLM*’s ability to exploit prior knowledge while staying resistant to spurious patterns in low‑shot tabular learning.
