\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

# Introduction to Few‑Shot Tabular Learning and LLMs  

Few‑shot learning—the ability to train a model that performs well after seeing only a handful of labeled examples—has become a central focus of modern machine learning research.  While the problem has been studied extensively for image and text data, tabular data presents a distinct set of difficulties that make the few‑shot regime especially challenging.  At the same time, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) with strong in‑context learning capabilities offers a new way to inject external knowledge into tabular tasks without the need for extensive fine‑tuning.  This chapter surveys the core challenges of few‑shot tabular learning, outlines the relevant properties of LLMs, reviews prior attempts to combine the two, and highlights the limitations that motivate the development of more efficient frameworks such as **FeatLLM**.

## Why Tabular Data Is Hard in Few‑Shot Regimes  

Tabular datasets are ubiquitous in fields such as finance, healthcare, and social science, but their structure differs markedly from the regular grids of images or the sequential nature of text.  Each column (or *feature*) may be of a different type—continuous, ordinal, categorical, or even free‑form text—requiring bespoke preprocessing pipelines.  Moreover, the relationships among features are often heterogeneous: some columns may interact multiplicatively, others may be conditionally independent, and yet others may be redundant or noisy.  When only a few labeled rows are available, learning these complex relationships becomes statistically fragile.

In a formal setting, let a tabular dataset consist of $N$ samples $\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{i},y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with $\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, where $d$ is the number of columns.  In the $k$‑shot regime we draw a random subset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ containing only $k\ll N$ labeled examples.  Classical estimators such as logistic regression or decision trees rely on sufficient sample coverage of each feature’s distribution; with $k$ often smaller than $d$, the empirical covariance matrix becomes singular, leading to overfitting or even complete failure to converge.  This phenomenon is often described as the “curse of dimensionality” in low‑sample settings: the variance of any estimator grows roughly as $\mathcal{O}(d/k)$, so that as $d$ increases the amount of data required to achieve a fixed level of accuracy quickly becomes prohibitive.

In addition to statistical concerns, practical obstacles arise.  Many tabular benchmarks contain dozens or hundreds of columns (e.g., the *Communities* and *Myocardial* datasets have over one hundred attributes).  Even when the raw number of rows is moderate, the heterogeneity of the data—mixed numeric and categorical domains, missing values, and domain‑specific encoding—makes it difficult to design generic data augmentation or self‑supervised objectives that improve sample efficiency.  Consequently, researchers have turned toward external sources of inductive bias, such as unlabeled data, domain ontologies, or, more recently, the knowledge embedded in large language models.

## Overview of Large Language Models and In‑Context Learning  

Large language models are deep neural networks, typically transformer‑based, trained on massive corpora of natural language text.  Their training objective—next‑token prediction—forces the model to learn statistical regularities of language, factual knowledge, and even rudimentary reasoning abilities.  Modern LLMs such as GPT‑3.5, PaLM, or LLaMA contain billions of parameters and can perform *in‑context learning*: given a prompt that includes a description of a task together with a few labeled examples (the *demonstrations*), the model can infer the mapping from inputs to outputs for new queries without any parameter updates.

Mathematically, let $\mathcal{P}$ denote a prompt consisting of a concatenation of a task description $T$, $k$ demonstration pairs $\{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^{k}$, and a new query $x_{*}$.  The LLM defines a conditional distribution over possible completions:  

\[
p(y_{*}\mid x_{*},\mathcal{P}) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_{*}|} p\bigl(y_{*}^{(t)} \mid y_{*}^{(<t)}, x_{*}, \mathcal{P}\bigr),
\]

where $y_{*}^{(t)}$ denotes the $t$‑th token of the answer.  By sampling from this distribution (often with temperature $>0$ to encourage diversity), the model produces a prediction that reflects both the supplied demonstrations and the full breadth of its pre‑training knowledge.  This ability to *reason* from a few examples makes LLMs attractive for few‑shot settings where conventional supervised learning would otherwise require many more labeled samples.

The interplay between the prompt length and the model’s context window (typically a few thousand tokens) is crucial: if the serialized representation of a tabular row is too long, the LLM may truncate important information or suffer performance degradation.  Consequently, careful prompt engineering—concise yet informative descriptions of features, systematic ordering of demonstrations, and explicit reasoning instructions—has become a central research theme.

## Prior Work Using LLMs for Tabular Prediction  

The first wave of research that attempted to bridge LLMs and tabular learning focused on **language‑interfaced tabular learning**.  The basic idea is to *serialize* a row of a table into a textual description, prepend a natural‑language task statement (e.g., “Predict whether the loan will default”), and feed the resulting prompt to the LLM.  Early systems such as **Lift** and **AnyPredict** demonstrated that, with a few demonstrations, an LLM could achieve competitive accuracy on some benchmark classification tasks.  

Two main methodological families emerged:

1. **In‑Context Learning Approaches** – These methods rely solely on prompting.  By adding a handful of labeled rows to the prompt, the LLM directly outputs class predictions for the remaining rows.  The *TABLET* framework further augments the prompt with rule‑sets and prototype examples generated by a separate classifier, striving to improve the quality of the LLM’s reasoning.  

2. **Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning** – Recognizing that a pure prompting strategy may be limited by context‑window size, some works introduced lightweight adapters (e.g., LoRA or IA3) that fine‑tune only a small subset of the LLM’s weights on the few available samples.  **TabLLM** exemplifies this direction, achieving higher accuracy but requiring gradient‑based updates and, crucially, access to the model’s parameters.

Both families share a common trait: the LLM is invoked *per sample* at inference time.  For each new row, the system constructs a fresh prompt (often incorporating the same few demonstrations) and queries the LLM to obtain a prediction.  While this yields strong performance in certain low‑dimensional settings, it incurs substantial computational overhead: each inference call involves a full forward pass through a multi‑billion‑parameter network, resulting in latency unsuitable for real‑time applications.

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Approaches  

Despite their promise, the current LLM‑based tabular methods suffer from several practical and methodological drawbacks that hinder adoption in real‑world pipelines.

1. **Inference Cost and Latency** – Because the LLM must be queried for every test instance, the total number of API calls scales linearly with the dataset size.  Even with high‑throughput inference servers, the per‑sample latency can be orders of magnitude higher than traditional models such as logistic regression or gradient‑boosted trees.  Moreover, many state‑of‑the‑art LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4) are only accessible via paid APIs, making large‑scale deployment financially prohibitive.

2. **Prompt Length Constraints** – The context window of most LLMs is limited (e.g., 4,096 tokens for GPT‑3.5).  When a dataset contains many columns, the serialized description of a single row may already consume a substantial fraction of this budget, leaving little room for demonstrations or reasoning instructions.  As a result, performance often degrades sharply on high‑dimensional tables, as observed on the *Communities* and *Myocardial* datasets.

3. **Dependence on Model Fine‑Tuning** – Parameter‑efficient tuning methods such as LoRA improve accuracy but require gradient updates and sometimes multi‑GPU environments.  This requirement is incompatible with API‑only access to proprietary LLMs, which typically expose only inference endpoints.  Consequently, many practitioners are unable to apply these methods to the most powerful LLMs that lack publicly released checkpoints.

4. **Sensitivity to Prompt Design** – The order of demonstration examples, the phrasing of the task description, and the inclusion of feature definitions can dramatically affect the LLM’s output.  While systematic prompt engineering can mitigate this sensitivity, it adds an extra layer of complexity and reduces reproducibility across datasets.

5. **Limited Exploitability of Prior Knowledge** – Existing approaches treat the LLM as a black‑box predictor, thereby underusing the rich factual and causal knowledge embedded during pre‑training.  The LLM could, in principle, generate *rules* or *feature transformations* that capture domain‑specific relationships (e.g., “age > 65 increases heart‑failure risk”).  However, most current pipelines never extract or reuse such intermediate artifacts, missing an opportunity for more interpretable and efficient downstream models.

These limitations motivate a shift from the conventional paradigm of end‑to‑end LLM prediction toward a *feature‑engineering* mindset.  By prompting the LLM to output explicit decision rules, one can compile these rules into binary features, train a lightweight classifier on the resulting representation, and perform inference without further LLM calls.  This is precisely the insight behind the **FeatLLM** framework, which combines in‑context learning, rule extraction, and ensemble bagging to obtain high‑quality few‑shot tabular models while dramatically reducing inference cost and overcoming prompt size restrictions.

# Motivation for Feature Engineering with LLMs

In recent years the remarkable capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have reshaped how we approach many machine‑learning problems.  By feeding a textual description of a task together with a few labeled examples, an LLM can often produce a correct answer for a completely unseen instance, a phenomenon commonly referred to as **in‑context learning**.  This ability has sparked a natural line of research: use the LLM directly as the predictor for *tabular* data, that is, serialize each row of a table into a prompt, send it to the LLM, and read off the predicted class.  While this approach works surprisingly well in very low‑shot regimes, it also reveals several practical and theoretical drawbacks that become prohibitive when we move beyond toy settings.  The **FeatLLM** framework was designed precisely to overcome these drawbacks by re‑casting the LLM not as an end‑to‑end predictor, but as a **feature engineer** that synthesises high‑quality, human‑readable rules which can then be used by a tiny downstream model.

Below we disentangle the reasoning that led to this shift, enumerate the concrete benefits of extracting rules as features, and provide a high‑level sketch of the FeatLLM pipeline—including its connections to classical ensemble and bagging ideas.

---

## From End‑to‑End LLM Inference to Rule Generation

When an LLM is used in the naïve end‑to‑end fashion, every test sample triggers a fresh API call.  The prompt must contain a textual representation of the entire row (for example, “`age is 45, sex is male, blood‑pressure is 130, … Answer:`”), often together with a short description of each column and a few demonstration rows.  The LLM then produces a label such as “`yes`” or “`no`”.  This straightforward pipeline hides several hidden costs:

1. **Per‑sample inference latency.**  Even if the LLM’s internal computation is fast, the round‑trip through a cloud API adds network latency, and the cost scales linearly with the number of test points.  In high‑throughput applications (e.g., real‑time medical decision support) this latency becomes unacceptable.

2. **Prompt length limitations.**  Modern LLMs have a context window of several thousand tokens.  When the number of columns grows (many real‑world tables have dozens or hundreds of attributes), the serialized row plus column metadata can overwhelm the window, forcing us to truncate information or drop columns, both of which hurt predictive performance.

3. **API‑only access.**  The most powerful LLMs (GPT‑4, PaLM‑2, Claude) are only available through inference APIs; they cannot be fine‑tuned or even subjected to low‑level prompt engineering beyond the surface text.  End‑to‑end pipelines therefore cannot exploit parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning techniques that would otherwise improve accuracy.

4. **Lack of interpretability.**  Directly outputting a label gives us no insight into *why* the model made that decision.  In regulated domains such as finance or healthcare, an explanation in terms of understandable conditions (“if age > 60 and cholesterol > 200 then risk is high”) is often a legal requirement.

The key observation that motivated FeatLLM is that **LLMs excel at reasoning about relationships** when presented with a concise description of the problem and a few examples.  Instead of asking the LLM “*what is the label for this specific row?*”, we can instead ask “*what are the logical conditions that separate the classes?*”.  The LLM’s internal knowledge base, combined with the few supplied demonstrations, is ideally suited to generate **rules**—human‑readable logical statements that capture the essence of the classification task.

Thus, FeatLLM replaces the per‑sample inference request with a **single, few‑shot request** whose purpose is to **extract a set of rules**.  Once those rules are obtained, they are parsed into **binary features** (one per rule) indicating whether a given data point satisfies the condition.  A lightweight downstream model (typically a linear model with non‑negative weights) then maps these binary features to class probabilities.  The downstream model can be trained once on the few labeled examples, after which inference requires only a simple dot product—no further calls to the LLM.

---

## Benefits of Extracting Rules as Features

### 1. Drastic Reduction in Inference Latency

Because the downstream predictor consists of a matrix multiplication between a weight vector and a binary rule vector, the computational cost per sample is negligible: a few integer comparisons followed by a dot product.  All heavy lifting—knowledge retrieval, reasoning, and rule synthesis—happens offline during the **feature‑generation phase**.  Consequently, the runtime per test point drops from hundreds of milliseconds (typical of a cloud LLM call) to microseconds, matching the speed of classical linear classifiers.

### 2. Compatibility with API‑Only LLMs

FeatLLM’s workflow requires only a handful of API calls (one per ensemble member) to obtain rules.  There is no need for gradient‑based fine‑tuning, low‑rank adaptation (LoRA), or any other training on the model’s parameters.  This makes the method immediately applicable to closed‑source LLMs that expose only a text‑completion endpoint.  Moreover, because the prompts used for rule extraction are short (they contain only the task description, a few demonstration rows, and a request for rules), they comfortably fit within the context window even for models with modest token limits.

### 3. Circumventing Prompt‑Size Bottlenecks

When the original end‑to‑end approach tries to serialize a row with 100+ columns, the prompt may exceed the model’s maximum token length.  FeatLLM sidesteps this issue by **decoupling data representation from the LLM**.  The LLM never sees the full table; it only sees a compact description of the *task* and a few examples.  The subsequent rule‑to‑feature conversion is performed locally using ordinary Python code, which can handle arbitrarily many columns without any token‑budget concerns.

### 4. Interpretability and Auditable Decisions

Each binary feature corresponds to a concrete logical condition such as “`age > 65`” or “`blood_pressure = high`”.  The downstream linear model assigns a non‑negative weight to each rule, indicating its contribution to the final probability.  By inspecting the strongest weighted rules we obtain a transparent explanation of the model’s decision, satisfying many regulatory and ethical requirements.

### 5. Robustness Through Ensembles and Bagging

The rule‑generation process is stochastic: different random seeds, temperature settings, or ordering of demonstration examples can lead the LLM to propose slightly different rule sets.  Rather than viewing this variability as a flaw, FeatLLM **embraces it**.  By repeating the rule‑generation step multiple times (each time with a different random seed and a random subset of features or samples—i.e., **feature bagging**) we obtain a collection of weak learners.  Their predictions are averaged, a technique directly inspired by classical ensemble methods such as random forests.  This ensemble not only stabilises performance but also mitigates the risk that a single LLM query yields a low‑quality rule set.

### 6. Scalability to Large Feature Spaces

Bagging allows FeatLLM to **sample a manageable subset of columns** for each rule‑generation call, keeping the prompt short while still exploring the entire feature space across ensemble members.  Over many ensemble iterations, each column has a reasonable chance of being examined, ensuring that salient attributes are eventually captured in at least one rule set.

---

## High‑Level Overview of the FeatLLM Pipeline

The FeatLLM framework can be broken down into four logical stages, each of which can be implemented with standard tools (Python, OpenAI or other LLM APIs, and a simple linear algebra library).

1. **Problem Description & Demonstrations**  
   - Assemble a concise textual description of the classification task (e.g., “Predict whether a patient has chronic heart failure”).  
   - Provide natural‑language definitions for each column (type information, possible categories).  
   - Serialize a few labeled rows (the *k‑shot* examples) in the format `f1 is v1. f2 is v2. … Answer: <label>`.

2. **Rule Extraction via In‑Context Learning**  
   - Construct a prompt that first asks the LLM to reason about the *causal relationship* between columns and the target (step‑1 of the reasoning instruction).  
   - Then request the LLM to produce a fixed number (e.g., ten) of logical rules for each class, explicitly asking for a machine‑parsable format (e.g., “`age > 60 AND cholesterol > 200`”).  
   - Send the prompt to the LLM with a moderate temperature (≈0.5) to encourage diversity across ensemble members.

3. **Parsing Rules into Binary Features**  
   - Feed the LLM‑generated rule list back into the LLM (or a lightweight parser) to obtain executable Python code that evaluates each rule on a data row.  
   - Execute the generated code on the entire dataset, yielding a binary matrix **Z** of shape `(N, R·C)`, where `R` is the number of rules per class and `C` the number of classes.  Entry `Z_{i, (k,r)}` equals 1 if row *i* satisfies rule *r* of class *k*.

4. **Learning a Simple Downstream Model**  
   - Train a class‑specific linear model without bias: `logit_k = max(w_k, 0) · Z_k`, where `w_k ∈ ℝ^R` are non‑negative weights learned by minimizing cross‑entropy on the *k‑shot* training set.  
   - Apply the softmax over the logits to obtain class probabilities.  
   - Optionally repeat steps 1‑4 multiple times, each with a different random seed and a different random subset of columns (bagging).  Average the resulting probability vectors to obtain the final prediction.

All heavy LLM interaction occurs **once per ensemble member**, after which the entire inference pipeline becomes a purely local computation.

---

## Relation to Ensemble and Bagging Ideas

The use of ensembles in FeatLLM is not an ad‑hoc addition; it is a direct translation of well‑studied ideas from classical machine learning:

- **Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating).**  In random forests, each decision tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the data and on a random subset of features, which decorrelates the trees and reduces variance.  FeatLLM mirrors this by *randomly selecting* a subset of columns (feature bagging) when constructing each LLM prompt.  Because the LLM can only attend to the columns presented, each rule set focuses on a different view of the data.

- **Model Averaging.**  After each rule‑generation trial we obtain a set of weights `w[t]`.  Averaging the softmax probabilities across trials is mathematically equivalent to ensembling the weak learners, a technique that is known to improve both bias and variance properties, especially in low‑sample regimes.

- **Self‑Consistency.**  Recent work on LLMs has shown that sampling multiple completions and voting among them (self‑consistency) yields more reliable answers.  FeatLLM leverages the same principle: rules that appear repeatedly across independent prompts are likely to be correct, and their corresponding binary features receive higher average weight.

- **Mitigating Prompt‑Size Constraints.**  Just as bagging allows a random forest to handle a high‑dimensional feature space without ever loading all features simultaneously, FeatLLM’s bagging allows us to respect the LLM’s token limit while still covering a large number of columns across the ensemble.

By grounding the architecture in these familiar concepts, FeatLLM inherits their theoretical justifications (variance reduction, robustness to noise) while simultaneously exploiting the unique reasoning power of LLMs to generate expressive, semantically meaningful features.

---

### Concluding Remarks

The transition from treating LLMs as black‑box end‑to‑end predictors to harnessing them as **knowledge‑rich feature engineers** resolves the most pressing practical obstacles of LLM‑based tabular learning: inference latency, prompt size, dependence on fine‑tuning, and lack of interpretability.  The FeatLLM pipeline, anchored in a simple rule‑extraction prompt, deterministic parsing, and a lightweight linear model, demonstrates that a modest number of API calls can unlock the vast prior knowledge encoded in LLMs while keeping downstream computation economical.  Moreover, by coupling this process with ensemble and bagging tactics, FeatLLM achieves robust performance across a wide variety of datasets, even when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  This perspective—*LLMs as feature generators*—opens a promising avenue for future research, where ever‑more sophisticated rule‑synthesis strategies and richer downstream learners can further bridge the gap between large‑scale language understanding and practical tabular prediction tasks.

# Problem Formulation and Notation

In order to understand how **FeatLLM** operates—as a framework that leverages a large language model (LLM) to *engineer features* for few‑shot tabular learning—we must first formalize the learning setting and introduce the symbols that will appear throughout the remainder of the book.  This chapter lays out the precise mathematical description of the data, the way in which only a very small number of labeled instances are made available for training, the evaluation criteria used to judge performance, and the assumptions we place on the auxiliary information (feature descriptions) and on the LLM itself.  By the end of the chapter you should be comfortable with the formal problem statement, be able to read the equations that appear in later sections, and understand why the particular constraints of the setting motivate the design choices of FeatLLM.

---

## 1. Tabular Datasets and Their Components

A **tabular dataset** consists of a collection of samples (rows), each described by a fixed set of attributes (columns).  Formally we write  

\[
\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where  

* \(N\) is the total number of examples in the dataset.  
* \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) (or a mixed‑type vector) is the feature vector for the \(i\)-th sample.  
* \(d\) denotes the **dimensionality**, i.e., the number of raw input features.  

In addition to the raw numeric values, every column is equipped with a **human‑readable name** and, optionally, a textual definition that explains its semantic meaning.  We denote the set of feature names by  

\[
F=\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots ,f_{d}\},
\]

and for each \(f_{j}\) we may also have an associated description \(\mathsf{desc}(f_{j})\) (e.g., “*Age of the patient, measured in years*”).  The presence of these natural‑language annotations is essential for the LLM: they allow the model to connect the tabular symbols to the world knowledge encoded in its pre‑training corpus.

The target variable \(y^{(i)}\) belongs to a **finite set of classes**  

\[
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,\dots ,c\},
\]

so we are dealing with a **classification** problem (binary classification corresponds to \(c=2\)).  Throughout the book we write \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\) when we need the one‑hot encoding of the class label, but the scalar notation \(y^{(i)}\) will be used whenever the distinction is unnecessary.

---

## 2. Few‑Shot Sampling: The \(k\)-Shot Regime

The central difficulty addressed by FeatLLM is that **only a handful of labeled examples are available** for learning a new task.  Let  

\[
k \ll N
\]

be the number of labeled samples we are allowed to use for training.  We obtain a **training subset** \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\) by uniformly sampling without replacement from \(\mathcal{D}\):

\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{k}.
\]

The remaining examples form a **test set** \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\) on which we evaluate the learned predictor.  Because \(k\) is tiny (often 2, 4, 8, or at most 32 in the experiments), conventional supervised learning algorithms tend to overfit, and we must bring in **external inductive bias**—here supplied by the LLM’s pre‑trained knowledge.

When reporting results, we repeatedly draw different random \(k\)-shot training sets (usually 3 to 5 repetitions) and average the performance to obtain a stable estimate of how well a method works in the low‑resource regime.

---

## 3. Goal: Learning a Predictor from \(k\) Examples Using an LLM

Given only \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\) and the natural‑language feature meta‑data \(\{f_{j},\mathsf{desc}(f_{j})\}_{j=1}^{d}\), the **objective** is to construct a predictor  

\[
\hat{p}: \mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow \Delta^{c},
\]

where \(\Delta^{c}\) denotes the \(c\)-dimensional probability simplex (i.e., \(\hat{p}(\mathbf{x})\) is a vector of class probabilities summing to one).  The predictor must be built **without fine‑tuning the LLM**; the LLM is treated as a **black‑box API** that can be queried with natural‑language prompts.  The overall pipeline consists of two stages:

1. **Feature Engineering via the LLM** – The LLM is prompted to produce a collection of **rules** (logical conditions) that characterize each class.  Each rule can be interpreted as a binary feature indicating whether a given sample satisfies the condition.

2. **Low‑Complexity Downstream Model** – Using the binary rule‑features, we fit a simple, trainable model (in the paper, a non‑negative linear model) on the \(k\) training examples.  This step learns **weights** that quantify the importance of each rule.

The final prediction for a new instance \(\mathbf{x}\) is obtained by evaluating all rules on \(\mathbf{x}\), forming the binary feature vector \(\mathbf{z}\), and computing  

\[
\text{logit}_{k} = \max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_{k},
\qquad 
\hat{p}_{k} = \frac{\exp(\text{logit}_{k})}{\sum_{l=1}^{c}\exp(\text{logit}_{l})},
\]

for each class \(k\).  Here \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\) are the learned non‑negative weights, and the ``max’’ operator ensures that the linear combination cannot reduce a class’s score (a design choice that reflects the intuition that an informative rule should never be detrimental).

---

## 4. Assumptions About Feature Descriptions

The success of the LLM’s reasoning hinges on two *minimal* but crucial assumptions:

1. **Availability of Feature Names** – Every column in the table is associated with a concise identifier \(f_{j}\) that the LLM can read.  In practice, these are the column headers from the original CSV or database schema.

2. **Optional Natural‑Language Definitions** – For each \(f_{j}\) we may supply a short textual description (e.g., “*Blood pressure, measured in mm Hg*”).  When such definitions are present they are included verbatim in the prompt.  If a definition is missing, the LLM must rely on its own world knowledge and on the statistical patterns observable in the few‑shot examples.

These assumptions are realistic: most real‑world tabular datasets coming from business, healthcare, or scientific domains already include column names; many also ship a data dictionary that can be leveraged directly.

---

## 5. Assumptions About LLM Access

FeatLLM is designed to be **model‑agnostic** with respect to the underlying LLM, but the following constraints are imposed on the interaction:

* **API‑Level Inference Only** – The user can issue a prompt and receive the LLM’s textual completion; there is no ability to modify the model’s parameters or to obtain hidden activations.

* **Deterministic Prompt Length Limit** – Because commercial APIs (e.g., GPT‑3.5‑turbo) have a maximum context window (often 4 k tokens), the prompt must be crafted to stay within this bound.  This motivates the use of **feature bagging** and **ensemble** techniques to keep any single query lightweight.

* **Temperature and Sampling Controls** – The LLM is invoked with a moderate temperature (e.g., 0.5) to encourage diverse rule generations across ensemble members while still yielding coherent logical statements.

* **Parsing Assistance** – Since the LLM’s raw output may contain minor syntactic variations (“greater than” versus “>”), a secondary LLM call is used to translate the natural‑language rule into executable code.  This parsing step is also performed via API calls, not via external parsers.

These assumptions reflect the **practical deployment scenario**: a data scientist has access to a commercial inference endpoint and wishes to avoid the heavy computational cost of per‑sample LLM inference that characterizes many earlier approaches.

---

## 6. Evaluation Metrics

Because we are dealing with classification, the primary metric reported throughout the paper (and repeated in this book) is the **Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AU‑ROC or simply AUC)**.  AUC measures the ability of the predictor to rank positive instances higher than negative ones, independent of any particular decision threshold.  Formally, for binary classification,  

\[
\text{AUC} = \Pr\big( \hat{p}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{+}) > \hat{p}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{-}) \big),
\]

where \(\mathbf{x}^{+}\) and \(\mathbf{x}^{-}\) denote a randomly drawn positive and negative test example, respectively.  For multiclass problems we compute the **macro‑averaged** AUC across all one‑vs‑rest binary sub‑problems.

When reporting results we also provide **standard deviations** across the random \(k\)-shot splits to indicate robustness.  In addition, we sometimes examine **training and inference runtime** (seconds per dataset) to highlight FeatLLM’s computational efficiency relative to baseline methods.

---

## 7. Summary of the Formal Problem

Putting all components together, the **few‑shot tabular classification problem with LLM‑driven feature engineering** can be restated as follows:

> **Given**  
> • A full dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) with feature vectors \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\), class labels \(y^{(i)}\), feature names \(F\), and optional textual definitions \(\mathsf{desc}(f_{j})\).  
> • An API that provides completions from a pre‑trained LLM \(\mathcal{L}\).  
> • A budget of \(k\) labeled examples \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\).  

> **Find** a mapping \(\hat{p}\) that, for any unseen \(\mathbf{x}\), yields accurate class probabilities, while requiring **only a handful of LLM calls** (independent of the number of test samples) and **no fine‑tuning** of \(\mathcal{L}\).

The remainder of the book shows how FeatLLM fulfills this objective: Section 2 introduces the prompt design that coax \(\mathcal{L}\) into emitting logical rules; Section 3 explains how those rules are turned into binary features; Section 4 details the learning of non‑negative linear weights; and Section 5 demonstrates, empirically, that this pipeline attains superior AUC scores with minimal inference latency.

# Designing Prompts for Rule Extraction  

In order to harness a large language model (LLM) as a feature engineer, the **FeatLLM** framework relies on a carefully crafted prompt that steers the model toward producing a set of deterministic rules (“criteria”) that separate the answer classes.  This chapter dissects the three‑part prompt design that underlies the rule‑extraction stage: (1) the **basic information block**, (2) the **reasoning instruction**, and (3) the **response instruction**.  Each component plays a distinct role in supplying the LLM with the necessary context, encouraging structured reasoning, and ensuring that the generated output can be parsed automatically into binary features.  The discussion proceeds from the high‑level purpose of each block to concrete implementation details such as serialization of tabular rows, chain‑of‑thought prompting, and strategies for controlling prompt length when the number of columns grows.

---  

## Basic Information Block: Task Description, Feature Names/Types, and Few‑Shot Examples  

The first segment of the prompt establishes the **ground truth** that the LLM must reason about.  It contains three sub‑elements:

1. **Task description** – A concise natural‑language question that frames the classification problem.  In the original experiments the question adopts a *yes/no* form, for example:  

   > “Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no.”  

   This formulation mirrors how a human expert would phrase the diagnostic query and alerts the model to the target label space.

2. **Feature metadata** – For each column in the tabular data we provide the feature name (the natural‑language identifier present in the dataset) together with its **type** (numeric, categorical, boolean) and, when available, a succinct definition.  The type annotation is crucial because later, when the LLM writes a rule, it must respect the appropriate comparison operators.  For a categorical column we may optionally list the admissible categories, e.g., `f_7 is one of {‘low’, ‘medium’, ‘high’}`.  Supplying this information prevents the model from generating ill‑typed conditions such as “`f_3` equals 3.7” when `f_3` is a string.

3. **Few‑shot demonstrations** – A small set of labeled examples (the *k‑shot* training set) is serialized into natural language and appended to the prompt.  The serialization follows the convention used in prior LLM‑tabular work:

   \[
   \text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F) =
   \text{``}f_1\text{ is }x^i_1.\; \dots\; f_d\text{ is }x^i_d.\; \text{Answer: }y^i\text{''}.
   \]

   Each line explicitly states the value of a feature followed by a period, ending with the ground‑truth label.  By presenting the model with a handful of concrete instances, we enable it to align its abstract reasoning (derived from prior knowledge) with the specific data distribution of the target task.

Collectively, the basic information block supplies the **what** (the classification question), the **which variables** (feature names and types), and the **examples of** *how* the variables relate to the labels.  This mirrors the way a domain expert would first read the problem statement and then examine a few patient records before attempting to formulate diagnostic criteria.

---  

## Serialization of Tabular Rows into Natural Language  

Tabular data are inherently a matrix of numbers and strings, but LLMs operate on sequential text.  Therefore, each row must be transformed into a **linear, human‑readable narrative**.  The serialization strategy employed in FeatLLM adheres to the following principles:

* **Deterministic ordering** – Features are always listed in the same order (e.g., alphabetical or the original column order).  This removes ambiguity for the LLM and simplifies downstream parsing of the generated rules.

* **Explicit type cues** – When a feature is numeric we keep the literal number; when it is categorical we retain the string label.  For boolean features we use “True/False”.  No implicit rounding or formatting is applied; the raw value from the dataset is inserted verbatim.

* **Compactness** – Although the prompt must be expressive, excessive verbosity inflates the token count and can exceed the LLM’s context window.  Consequently, each feature is expressed as a short clause of the form “`f_j` is `<value>`.”  No auxiliary explanations or units are added unless they are part of the provided definition.

* **Consistency across examples** – All few‑shot rows share the same template, ensuring that the model can detect the pattern “feature‑value → answer” and infer that the mapping from features to label is the target of reasoning.

The resulting serialized text looks like:

```
age is 45. cholesterol is 210. smoking_status is 'yes'. … Answer: 1
```

Because the LLM receives the same structural pattern for each demonstrated row, it can more easily identify which attributes tend to co‑occur with a particular class, a prerequisite for the next reasoning stage.

---  

## Reasoning Instruction: Two‑Step Chain‑of‑Thought  

After the basic information, FeatLLM injects a **reasoning instruction** that explicitly tells the LLM how to think.  This instruction draws inspiration from the chain‑of‑thought prompting paradigm, but it is tailored to rule extraction.  The instruction divides the model’s internal reasoning into **two sequential steps**:

### Step 1 – Causal or Tendential Inference  

The model is first asked to consider **only the task description and the feature metadata**, deliberately ignoring the few‑shot examples.  The prompt encourages the LLM to leverage its stored world knowledge and to articulate a *causal hypothesis* about which features are likely relevant.  For instance:

> “First, based on the medical knowledge embedded in your training data, list the patient attributes that are generally associated with chronic heart failure.”

The result of this step is a short textual enumeration of *potentially important* features (e.g., “age, prior myocardial infarction, ejection fraction”).  Because the model does not yet see any concrete examples, the output reflects **prior knowledge** rather than spurious correlations present in the few labelled rows.

### Step 2 – Rule Deduction Using Few‑Shot Demonstrations  

Having identified a candidate set of influential features, the LLM proceeds to **derive concrete decision rules** that separate the classes, now incorporating the few‑shot examples.  The instruction explicitly links the two stages:

> “Now, using the example patients provided above together with the attributes you identified, formulate ten logical rules that would allow you to answer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ for any new patient.”

The requirement to produce a **fixed number of rules** (ten per class, as set in the experiments) serves two purposes:

1. It prevents the model from generating too few rules, which would under‑represent the decision boundary, and it also prevents an explosion of rules that would swell the prompt beyond the context limit.  
2. It offers a predictable dimensionality for the downstream binary feature vector (each rule becomes one binary feature).

The two‑step approach mitigates the risk of the model latching onto **spurious correlations** that could appear in the few‑shot data (e.g., a random column that happens to co‑occur with the positive class).  By first grounding the reasoning in domain knowledge, the model is nudged to focus on genuinely relevant columns before fine‑tuning its rule set with the concrete examples.

---  

## Response Instruction: Exact Format for Rule Output and Logical Operators  

The final component of the prompt tells the LLM **how to structure its answer** so that downstream parsing can be performed automatically.  Without explicit formatting guidance, the model might embed rules in free‑form prose, use unconventional symbols, or combine multiple conditions in an ambiguous manner.  The response instruction therefore specifies:

1. **Section headings per class** – The answer must begin with a marker such as “**Rules for class 0:**” followed by a numbered list, and similarly for “**Rules for class 1:**”.  This delineation enables a straightforward split of the output into class‑specific rule sets.

2. **Rule syntax** – Each rule must be a single logical clause that respects the feature type.  For numeric features the allowed operators are `<`, `<=`, `>`, `>=`, `=`; for categorical features the operator is `=` and the value must be quoted if it contains spaces.  Example rule lines:

   ```
   1. age > 60
   2. ejection_fraction <= 30
   3. smoking_status = 'yes'
   ```

3. **Logical composition** – The instruction permits the combination of multiple atomic conditions with the logical operator **AND** only, thereby keeping the rule a conjunction of simple predicates.  The use of **OR** or more complex nesting is disallowed because it would complicate the binary conversion step (each rule must map to a single Boolean test).  If a user wishes to express a disjunction, the model is instructed to emit two separate rules.

4. **No extraneous text** – The model should not add explanations, confidence scores, or anything outside the numbered list.  Any deviation is caught early by a validation script that checks for the presence of the required headings and the exact number of rules.

By constraining the output in this manner, FeatLLM can reliably feed the generated rule strings to a second LLM call that translates them into executable Python code (e.g., a function that takes a row dictionary and returns a binary vector).  Even if the LLM occasionally substitutes “greater than” for the symbol “>”, the subsequent parsing step is robust because it re‑passes the rule text to the LLM itself for code generation, exploiting the model’s ability to understand natural‑language variations while still producing syntactically correct code.

---  

## Prompt Length Considerations and Feature‑Bagging Tricks  

A practical limitation of any in‑context learning system is the **context window size** of the underlying LLM (e.g., 4 k tokens for GPT‑3.5).  When a tabular dataset contains many columns (hundreds in the *Communities* or *Myocardial* datasets), naively serializing **all** feature names, types, and few‑shot rows would exceed this limit.  FeatLLM addresses this issue using two complementary strategies:

1. **Feature‑bagging** – For each ensemble trial, a random subset of the full feature set is selected (e.g., 30 % of columns).  The prompt then includes only the metadata and serialized values for this subset.  Because each trial uses a different random bag, the aggregate ensemble still has exposure to the entire feature space, while any single LLM query stays comfortably within the token budget.

2. **Limited rule count** – By fixing the number of extracted rules per class (ten), the size of the response is bounded.  Moreover, the rule extraction step does not need to enumerate every feature; the two‑step reasoning instruction prunes irrelevant columns early, which naturally reduces the amount of text the model has to attend to.

During training, the number of ensemble members (*T*) and the bag‑size are hyper‑parameters; empirical results in the paper show that increasing *T* up to about 20 yields diminishing returns, and a bag proportion that keeps the prompt under the context window while still preserving a representative slice of features works well across datasets.

---  

### Summary  

The three‑part prompt for rule extraction in FeatLLM is a **structured communication protocol** that transforms an LLM from a black‑box predictor into a knowledgeable feature engineer.  The **basic information block** grounds the model in the task, supplies precise feature metadata, and demonstrates concrete examples.  The **reasoning instruction** enforces a disciplined two‑step chain‑of‑thought: first invoking prior knowledge to identify relevant attributes, then refining that insight into a fixed set of logical rules using the few‑shot data.  Finally, the **response instruction** guarantees that the generated rules appear in a machine‑parsable format, facilitating automatic conversion into binary features.  By coupling this prompt design with feature‑bagging and a modest ensemble, FeatLLM sidesteps context‑window constraints, reduces inference cost, and produces high‑quality, interpretable rules that drive strong few‑shot classification performance.

**Parsing Natural‑Language Rules into Binary Features**

In this chapter we examine the crucial stage of the **FeatLLM** pipeline that turns the textual rules generated by a large language model (LLM) into a concrete set of binary features that can be fed to a simple downstream predictor.  While the LLM is remarkably capable of articulating conditions such as “*age > 65*” or “*blood‑type is ‘O’*”, its output is raw natural language and often contains inconsistencies, synonyms, or formatting quirks that make direct computational use difficult.  We therefore devote careful attention to (i) characterizing the typical sources of noise in the LLM’s rule strings, (ii) designing a secondary prompt that asks the same LLM to translate those strings into well‑formed Python functions, (iii) executing the generated code safely, (iv) constructing the binary feature matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_k\) for each class \(k\), and (v) analysing the computational complexity of the entire parsing step.

---

### Typical Noise in LLM‑Generated Rules

When an LLM is prompted to produce “rules” that separate the classes of a tabular task, it generally follows the format prescribed in the *Response Instruction* (e.g., “Rule 1: …”).  Nevertheless, several systematic sources of variability arise:

1. **Symbol–Word Substitutions**  
   The LLM may alternate between symbolic comparators (`>`, `<`, `>=`, `<=`, `==`) and their lexical equivalents (“greater than”, “less than or equal to”).  For instance, a rule that should read  
   \[
   \text{age} > 65
   \]
   might appear as  
   \[
   \text{age is greater than 65}.
   \]  
   Both are semantically identical, yet a naïve parser that expects a strict token pattern would fail on the word form.

2. **Variations in Logical Connectors**  
   To express conjunctions or disjunctions the model might use “and”, “or”, commas, or even natural‑language phrasing such as “both … and …”.  A rule that logically corresponds to  
   \[
   (\text{cholesterol} > 200) \land (\text{blood\_pressure} \le 130)
   \]
   could be rendered as  
   “cholesterol greater than 200 and blood_pressure less than or equal to 130”.  Detecting the correct grouping of sub‑conditions is non‑trivial.

3. **Inconsistent Naming and Spacing**  
   Feature names supplied in the prompt may be re‑typed with different capitalisation, underscores, or spaces (e.g., “BloodPressure” vs. “blood pressure”).  Because the downstream code must reference the exact column identifiers in the original data frame, any mismatch leads to a `NameError`.

4. **Extraneous Punctuation or Formatting**  
   The LLM often includes trailing periods, bullet points, or quotation marks that are not part of the logical expression.  Example:  
   ```
   Rule 3: "age >= 70".
   ```  
   Such characters need to be stripped before compilation.

5. **Incomplete or Ambiguous Expressions**  
   Occasionally the model omits a necessary operand, producing fragments like “age greater” or “blood_pressure is”.  These fragments are syntactically invalid and must be flagged for correction or removal.

Collectively, these noises make a deterministic parser brittle.  Instead of engineering a large set of handcrafted regular‑expression rules to capture every idiosyncrasy, **FeatLLM** leverages the LLM’s own code‑generation abilities to produce a clean, executable translation.

---

### Designing a Secondary Prompt to Ask the LLM to Emit Python Functions

The solution is to ask the LLM a **second‑level query**: “Given the natural‑language rule list, generate a Python function that, for a single data record, returns `1` if the rule holds and `0` otherwise.”  This approach enjoys several advantages:

* The LLM already knows the syntax of Python and has been extensively trained on code repositories, so it can resolve ambiguous phrasing automatically.
* By providing a concrete *function signature* and a *docstring* that lists the allowed feature names, we constrain the model to produce code that references only the intended columns.
* The prompt can request that each rule be wrapped in its own function (e.g., `def rule1(row): ...`) and that a master wrapper aggregates the results into a binary vector.

A representative secondary prompt is shown below (pseudo‑code representation, actual prompt follows the layout in the paper’s Appendix):

```
You are given a list of textual rules for classifying a patient.
Each rule describes a condition on the tabular features.
Your task is to write a Python function `apply_rules(row)` that takes a
dictionary `row` (mapping feature names to their values) and returns a
list of integers, where the i‑th element is 1 if rule i is satisfied,
otherwise 0.

The feature names are:
- age (numeric)
- cholesterol (numeric)
- blood_pressure (numeric)
- diagnosis (categorical, possible values: ['healthy','diseased'])

Rules:
1. age greater than 65 and cholesterol > 200
2. diagnosis is 'diseased' or blood_pressure <= 130
...

Please output only the Python code, without explanatory text.
```

Key design choices:

* **Explicit type hints** (`numeric` vs. `categorical`) guide the LLM to use appropriate Python operators (`>` vs. `==`).
* **Example code snippet** may be included (e.g., a template) to reduce variability.
* **Restriction to pure expressions** (no external imports, no side effects) ensures safe execution.

Because the LLM is asked to produce **deterministic code**, the output is far more regular than the raw rule strings, drastically simplifying downstream handling.

---

### Safe Execution with `exec()` and Handling Errors

Once the LLM returns a code block, **FeatLLM** proceeds to compile and execute it within a constrained environment:

```python
import builtins
import numpy as np

# Whitelist only safe built‑ins
SAFE_BUILTINS = {
    'abs': builtins.abs,
    'min': builtins.min,
    'max': builtins.max,
    'float': builtins.float,
    'int': builtins.int,
    'bool': builtins.bool,
    'np': np
}

def safe_exec(code_str, globals_dict=None):
    """
    Compile and execute `code_str` safely.
    Returns a dictionary containing the defined functions.
    """
    if globals_dict is None:
        globals_dict = {}
    # Restrict the global namespace
    globals_dict.update({'__builtins__': SAFE_BUILTINS})
    exec(code_str, globals_dict)
    return globals_dict
```

The `exec` call runs in a sandbox where only a minimal set of built‑ins is exposed; this prevents malicious payloads such as `os.system`.  After execution, we extract the produced `apply_rules` function:

```python
code_namespace = safe_exec(generated_code)
apply_rules = code_namespace['apply_rules']
```

**Error handling** is essential because even a powerful LLM can occasionally produce syntactically invalid code (e.g., mismatched parentheses).  The pipeline therefore wraps the compilation step in a `try/except` block:

```python
try:
    code_namespace = safe_exec(generated_code)
    apply_rules = code_namespace['apply_rules']
except Exception as e:
    # Log the failure, fall back to a default rule (always 0)
    def apply_rules(row):
        return [0] * NUM_RULES
```

When an exception occurs, the fallback ensures that the downstream linear model still receives a well‑defined binary vector, albeit one lacking informative signal for that particular trial.  Since **FeatLLM** aggregates many trials via ensembling, occasional failures do not catastrophically degrade overall performance.

---

### Creating the Binary Feature Matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_k\) for Each Class

With a reliable `apply_rules` function in hand, we can transform the entire dataset.  Suppose the dataset contains \(N\) rows and the LLM generated \(R\) rules for class \(k\).  For each row \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\), we construct a dictionary `row_dict` that maps each feature name \(f_j\) to its value \(x^{i}_j\).  The function then yields a list of binary outcomes:

```python
Z_k = np.zeros((N, R), dtype=int)   # initialize binary matrix
for i, sample in enumerate(dataset):
    row = {f_j: sample[j] for j, f_j in enumerate(feature_names)}
    Z_k[i, :] = apply_rules(row)    # vector of length R
```

Repeating this process for every class \(k \in \{1,\dots,c\}\) yields a collection \(\{\mathbf{Z}_k\}_{k=1}^{c}\).  In practice, the implementation often concatenates the matrices into a single tensor \(\mathbf{Z} \in \{0,1\}^{N \times (cR)}\) because the downstream linear model computes logits per class separately as described in Equation (2) of the paper:

\[
\text{logit}_k^{i} = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^{i},
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{i}= \operatorname{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^{i},\dots,\text{logit}_c^{i}]\bigr).
\]

Here \(\mathbf{z}_k^{i}\) denotes the \(i\)-th row of \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).  The **non‑negative projection** \(\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\) guarantees that learning cannot assign a negative contribution to any rule, preserving the intuition that a satisfied rule should never decrease the probability of its class.

---

### Complexity Analysis of the Parsing Step

The parsing stage consists of three logical phases:

1. **LLM rule‑to‑code generation** – a single API call per ensemble trial.  
   The computational cost is external to the local machine; latency scales with the model’s inference time (typically on the order of a few hundred milliseconds for GPT‑3.5 when the prompt is ~2 KB).

2. **Safe compilation (`exec`)** – runs in time linear in the length of the generated source code, denoted \(L_{\text{code}}\).  Since each rule yields at most a few dozen characters, \(L_{\text{code}} = \mathcal{O}(R)\).  Execution time for compiling is negligible (microseconds).

3. **Feature matrix construction** – for each of the \(N\) samples we evaluate \(R\) Boolean expressions.  Assuming each expression evaluates in constant time (a handful of arithmetic comparisons and logical operators), the overall time is \(\mathcal{O}(N R)\).  Memory consumption is also \(\mathcal{O}(N R)\) to store \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).

When the algorithm is repeated over \(T\) ensemble trials, the total cost becomes \(\mathcal{O}(T N R)\) for matrix construction, while the LLM API cost scales linearly with \(T\).  In practice, the authors found that setting \(T=20\) and \(R=10\) yields a good trade‑off between expressivity and prompt length, leading to a manageable total cost even for datasets with several thousand rows.

Because the downstream linear model training is also \(\mathcal{O}(T N R)\) (gradient updates over a low‑dimensional parameter vector), the entire **FeatLLM** pipeline remains computationally lightweight compared with end‑to‑end LLM inference that would require a forward pass for every test sample.  This lightweight nature is one of the primary motivations for converting natural‑language rules into binary features.

---

#### Summary

In this chapter we have dissected the transformation from noisy, human‑readable rule strings into a rigorously defined binary feature matrix suitable for fast linear classification.  By recognizing the systematic noise patterns of LLM‑generated text, delegating the translation task back to the LLM via a carefully crafted secondary prompt, guarding execution with a sandboxed `exec`, and finally assembling the binary matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_k\), **FeatLLM** achieves a robust, low‑cost feature‑engineering pipeline that capitalises on the LLM’s knowledge while avoiding per‑sample inference overhead.  The resulting computational complexity scales linearly with the number of samples, rules, and ensemble repetitions, making the approach practical for a wide variety of real‑world tabular learning problems.

Learning from Generated Features
# Formulation of the Linear Scorer

After the LLM has produced a collection of deterministic rules for each class, each rule can be interpreted as a binary indicator that tells whether a particular sample satisfies the condition expressed by the rule.  For a given class \(k\) we denote by  

\[
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{i}\;=\;\bigl[z_{k,1}^{i},\,z_{k,2}^{i},\dots ,z_{k,R}^{i}\bigr]^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{R}
\]

the vector of binary rule‑features generated for the \(i\)-th sample, where \(R\) is the fixed number of rules extracted for each class (the paper uses ten).  The simplest way to turn these binary features into a score for class \(k\) is to compute a linear combination

\[
\text{logit}_{k}^{i}\;=\;\max \bigl(\mathbf{w}_{k},\,0\bigr)\cdot \mathbf{z}_{k}^{i},
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}\) is a learnable weight vector associated with class \(k\), and the operator \(\max(\cdot,0)\) is applied element‑wise.  The use of the element‑wise non‑negative projection \(\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\) is a crucial design choice: it guarantees that any rule that is satisfied by a sample can only increase, never decrease, the logit for its own class.  Consequently, each rule can be interpreted as a *supporting* piece of evidence rather than a contradictory one, which aligns with the intuitive meaning of a rule (“if the condition holds, the sample is more likely to belong to this class”).

The dot product in (1) aggregates the contributions of all satisfied rules.  Because the binary feature vector is sparse—most rules will be false for a given example—the resulting logit reflects precisely how many of the most salient, LLM‑derived conditions are met, weighted by their learned importance.  This formulation replaces the need for a complex classifier: the entire decision‑making process rests on a single linear scorer applied to the rule‑based representation of the data.

# Softmax Conversion to Probabilities

The logits computed for each class must be transformed into a valid probability distribution over the \(c\) possible classes.  This is achieved with the softmax function:

\[
\mathbf{p}^{i}\;=\;\operatorname{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}^{i}_{1},\dots ,\text{logit}^{i}_{c}]\bigr),
\qquad
p^{i}_{k}\;=\;\frac{\exp(\text{logit}^{i}_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp(\text{logit}^{i}_{j})}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) guarantees that each component \(p^{i}_{k}\) lies in \((0,1)\) and that \(\sum_{k=1}^{c}p^{i}_{k}=1\).  The softmax thus maps the raw, additive evidence gathered from the rule‑features into a calibrated class probability vector \(\mathbf{p}^{i}\).  In practice, because the logits are non‑negative (thanks to the positivity constraint on the weights) the softmax tends to assign higher probability to classes whose rule set is more extensively satisfied, while still allowing competition between classes when multiple rule sets are partially triggered.

# Training Objective: Cross‑Entropy on \(k\)‑Shot Data

During the few‑shot learning phase we are given only a small labeled subset of the full dataset, denoted by \(\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}\).  After the rule extraction step, each training sample is converted into its binary rule representation \(\mathbf{z}^{i}\).  The parameters to be learned are the weight vectors \(\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{c}\).  The standard cross‑entropy loss is employed:

\[
\mathcal{L}\;=\;-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{k=1}^{c}y^{i}_{k}\,\log p^{i}_{k},
\tag{3}
\]

where \(y^{i}_{k}\) is the one‑hot encoding of the true class label for sample \(i\).  Minimizing (3) encourages the model to increase the logits—and consequently the probabilities—of the correct class while reducing those of incorrect classes.  Because the loss is differentiable with respect to the (projected) weight vectors, gradient‑based optimization can be applied even when only a handful of training points are available.  The paper observes that, despite the extreme data scarcity, the rule‑based representation is already highly informative, so even a simple linear model can achieve strong performance after modest training.

# Why Positivity Constraints Improve Interpretability

Constraining the weights to be non‑negative has two complementary benefits:

1. **Semantic clarity.**  A rule that is satisfied should intuitively contribute positively to the likelihood of its associated class.  By forcing \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\ge 0\), the model respects this semantics: a rule’s influence can be interpreted directly as “how much evidence this rule provides for class \(k\).”

2. **Avoiding destructive interference.**  Without the constraint, a rule that is frequently satisfied could be assigned a negative weight, effectively turning a valid piece of domain knowledge into a penalty.  In a few‑shot setting this can easily happen due to over‑fitting to the tiny training set.  The positivity projection prevents such pathological situations, leading to more stable training and better generalisation.

Because each weight is a scalar attached to a human‑readable rule, the final learned model can be inspected: rules with high weight are the most decisive criteria identified by the LLM and subsequently confirmed by the data.  This aligns with the goal of the paper to provide an interpretable alternative to black‑box LLM inference.

# Optimization Details (Adam, Epochs, Early Stopping)

Training the linear scorer proceeds with the Adam optimiser, a widely used variant of stochastic gradient descent that adapts learning rates per parameter.  The implementation in the paper uses a learning rate of \(0.01\) and runs for up to \(200\) epochs.  Because the dataset is extremely small, the authors employ \(k\)-fold cross‑validation on the few‑shot training set to select the epoch that yields the best validation cross‑entropy, effectively performing early stopping.  This prevents over‑training on the limited data while allowing the weights to converge to values that reflect the true predictive power of each rule.

In practice the optimisation loop looks as follows:

1. Initialise the weight vectors \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\) (e.g., to zeros).
2. For each epoch:
   - Compute binary rule features \(\mathbf{z}^{i}\) for all training samples.
   - Compute logits via (1) and probabilities via (2).
   - Evaluate the cross‑entropy loss (3).
   - Update the weight vectors with Adam’s gradient step.
3. After each epoch, evaluate on a held‑out fold; if the validation loss has not improved for a preset number of epochs, stop and keep the best weights.

Because the model contains only \(c\times R\) parameters (for example, ten rules per class on a ten‑class problem yields at most one hundred parameters), training is computationally cheap.  This efficiency, together with the fact that inference after training requires only a dot product and a softmax, makes the overall pipeline suitable for real‑time deployment, a primary advantage highlighted by the authors over methods that must query the LLM for every test instance.

**Ensembling and Feature Bagging**

In this chapter we examine the ensemble machinery that underpins the FeatLLM framework.  While a single large‑language model (LLM) query can already produce a set of interpretable rules, relying on one such query is fragile: the generated rules may be incomplete, may contain noise, and the query itself may be limited by the prompt‑size constraints of the underlying model.  By repeating the rule‑extraction process many times, each with a slightly different view of the data, FeatLLM converts many weak rule generators into a strong, robust predictor.  We explore why this is necessary, how bagging of features and instances is performed, how temperature‑controlled randomness yields diverse rule sets, how the predictions from all trials are combined, and what the trade‑offs are in terms of computational cost and prompt length.

---

### 1.  Why a Single LLM Query May Produce Incomplete or Noisy Rules

The rule‑generation stage in FeatLLM asks the LLM to articulate, in natural language, conditions that characterize each class.  Several phenomena can limit the quality of the rule set returned from a single query:

1. **Limited Context Window** – LLMs have a maximal token budget (often a few thousand tokens).  When a tabular dataset contains many columns, the serialized description of all features, together with the few‑shot examples, can already occupy a large fraction of this window.  Consequently the model may be forced to truncate or ignore some columns, leading to rules that are blind to those omitted features.

2. **Prompt‑Induced Bias** – The order in which few‑shot examples appear, as well as the ordering of feature descriptions, can steer the model toward particular patterns.  Empirically, re‑ordering demonstrations changes the generated rules, indicating that a single deterministic prompt yields only one of many plausible rule sets.

3. **Stochastic Generation** – Even with a fixed prompt, the LLM’s sampling process (controlled by the temperature parameter) introduces randomness.  At low temperature the model often repeats the same high‑probability answer; at higher temperature it may explore alternative phrasings or alternative logical branches.  This stochasticity can be beneficial for diversity but also produces occasional nonsensical or overly specific rules.

4. **Hallucination and Syntax Variability** – LLMs sometimes fabricate conditions that do not correspond to any real column, or they may phrase a relational operator in an unexpected way (“greater than” instead of “>”).  Such noise can be difficult to parse automatically and, if left unchecked, would degrade downstream performance.

Because each of these issues can affect a single rule set, FeatLLM does not trust any one LLM query.  Instead, it treats the rule generator as a weak learner and harnesses ensemble techniques to average out errors, much like bagging in classical decision‑tree ensembles.

---

### 2.  Bagging of Features and/or Instances for Each Ensemble Member

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) is a simple yet powerful way to introduce variation among ensemble members.  In the context of FeatLLM, bagging is applied to two distinct dimensions:

#### 2.1 Feature‑Level Bagging  

When the number of columns $d$ is large relative to the prompt length, we randomly select a **subset of features** to include in the prompt for a given trial.  Formally, for trial $t$, we draw a subset $F^{(t)} \subseteq F$ of size $d_{b}$ (e.g., $d_{b}=30$ when $d=120$).  The basic‑information portion of the prompt then contains only the descriptions of features in $F^{(t)}$, together with their values in the few‑shot examples.  The LLM can therefore devote its full reasoning capacity to this manageable slice of the dataset, producing rules that involve only the chosen features.  Across the $T$ trials, different slices are explored, ensuring that eventually **every column has a chance to be examined** and possibly turned into a rule.

#### 2.2 Instance‑Level Bagging  

Even when the feature set is modest, the few‑shot examples themselves can be varied.  For each trial we randomly **sample without replacement** a subset of the available $k$ labeled instances (often the entire set, but possibly a bootstrap sample when $k$ is larger).  This changes the concrete evidence that the LLM sees when it moves from the “causal reasoning” step to the “rule‑deduction” step.  Different examples may highlight different attribute‑label relationships, prompting the LLM to emit alternative rules.

Both levels of bagging serve two purposes:

* They **reduce the effective prompt length**, allowing FeatLLM to handle datasets that would otherwise exceed the model’s token limit.
* They **inject stochasticity** into the rule‑generation process, which, when aggregated, smooths out idiosyncratic mistakes.

---

### 3.  Temperature‑Controlled Diversity of Generated Rule Sets

The **temperature** parameter $τ$ in the LLM’s sampling distribution governs how much probability mass is allocated to lower‑likelihood tokens.  A higher temperature (e.g., $τ=0.7$) makes the model more exploratory, yielding a wider variety of rule expressions across trials; a lower temperature (e.g., $τ=0.2$) makes the output more deterministic.

FeatLLM deliberately sets a **moderately high temperature** (commonly $τ=0.5$) for rule generation.  This choice balances two competing forces:

* **Diversity** – By allowing the model to sometimes pick a less‑probable token, distinct logical structures (different thresholds, alternative conjunctions, or even alternative feature selections) appear in the rule pool, enriching the ensemble’s hypothesis space.
* **Reliability** – Excessively high temperatures can lead to incoherent or contradictory rules, raising the parsing burden and potentially injecting noise.  Empirically, a temperature around 0.5 produces sufficiently varied yet still parseable rule sets.

Because each trial also uses its own random seed (affecting both sampling of features/instances and the stochastic decoding), the ensemble ends up with a **collection of $T$ rule corpora** that—while overlapping in spirit—exhibit enough variation to make the final prediction robust.

---

### 4.  Aggregating Predictions Across $T$ Trials

After a trial produces a rule set, the rules are parsed into **binary features**.  For class $k$ and trial $t$, let the binary vector be $\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{R}$, where $R$ is the fixed number of rules per class (e.g., $R=10$).  A simple linear model with non‑negative weights $\mathbf{w}^{(t)}_{k}\ge 0$ produces a logit for that trial:

\[
\text{logit}^{(t)}_{k}= \max(\mathbf{w}^{(t)}_{k},0)\cdot\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}.
\]

Applying a softmax over all classes yields a probability vector $\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\in\Delta^{c-1}$ for trial $t$:

\[
\mathbf{p}^{(t)} = \operatorname{Softmax}\!\bigl(\text{logit}^{(t)}_{1},\dots,\text{logit}^{(t)}_{c}\bigr).
\]

The final prediction is the **average of the class‑wise probabilities** across all $T$ trials:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle 
\mathbf{p}^{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{p}^{(t)} } .
\]

This averaging is equivalent to **majority voting** in the probability domain and has several desirable properties:

* **Noise Cancellation** – If a few trials generate spurious rules that misclassify certain instances, their influence is diluted by the majority of well‑behaved trials.
* **Self‑Consistency** – Rules that repeatedly appear across many trials receive reinforcement because the corresponding binary features will be active in multiple $\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}$ vectors, leading to consistently high weights.
* **Calibration** – Averaging probabilities rather than logits tends to produce better‑calibrated confidence scores, which is useful when downstream systems need to threshold predictions.

---

### 5.  Effect on Computational Cost and Prompt Length

Ensembling inevitably introduces extra computation, but FeatLLM’s design mitigates the overhead in two key ways:

| Aspect | Single‑Query Baseline | FeatLLM Ensemble |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Number of LLM Calls** | One call per test sample (expensive at inference) | $T$ calls **only during training** (each call processes the few‑shot examples, not every test instance) |
| **Inference Latency per Sample** | Requires feeding each sample through the LLM → high latency | Uses only the lightweight linear model → inference comparable to logistic regression |
| **Prompt Length per Call** | Must contain all features → may exceed token limit | Feature‑bagging reduces the prompt to a manageable subset (e.g., 30 features) |
| **Parallelism** | Limited; each test sample must wait for LLM response | LLM calls are *embarrassingly parallel* across trials; can be dispatched concurrently on multiple API workers |
| **API Cost** | Grows linearly with number of test instances | Fixed cost proportional to $T$ (typically 20‑30) regardless of test set size |

Thus, while FeatLLM incurs a modest upfront cost (a handful of LLM queries to generate the rule pool), the **asymptotic inference cost** is dramatically lower than approaches that query the LLM for every test record.  Moreover, because each query only needs to handle a reduced feature subset, the **prompt length stays comfortably within the LLM’s context window**, eliminating the need for truncation or sophisticated feature‑selection heuristics.

In practice, the authors observed that with $T=20$ and a rule count $R=10$, the total number of API calls per dataset was under 30, a cost that can be parallelized and is dwarfed by the savings in per‑sample latency.  The trade‑off is therefore highly favorable: a small, bounded training‑time expense in exchange for a robust, low‑latency, and scalable predictor.

---

### 6.  Summary

Ensembling and feature bagging are the linchpins that turn the LLM from a *single* noisy rule generator into a **reliable feature engineer**.  By repeatedly sampling subsets of features and examples, and by exploiting temperature‑driven stochasticity, FeatLLM collects a diverse collection of rule‑derived binary features.  Averaging the class probabilities obtained from simple linear models trained on each rule set yields a final prediction that is both **accurate** (as demonstrated by consistent AUC gains) and **efficient** (requiring no LLM inference at test time).  The ensemble thus addresses the three central challenges of LLM‑based tabular learning:

1. **Incomplete or noisy rules** – mitigated by diversity and averaging.  
2. **Prompt‑size limitations** – mitigated by feature‑level bagging.  
3. **Robustness to spurious correlations** – mitigated by the self‑consistency effect of multiple trials.

Through these mechanisms, FeatLLM achieves state‑of‑the‑art few‑shot performance on a wide variety of tabular datasets while remaining practical for real‑world deployment.

# Implementation Details and Practical Tips  

In this chapter we explain how to reproduce the *FeatLLM* framework in a real‑world setting.  All the design choices stem directly from the original method, so that a newcomer can set up the pipeline, tune its hyper‑parameters, and diagnose common failure modes without having to read the source code.  We follow the logical order of the system: selecting a large language model (LLM) backend, configuring the generation parameters, deciding how many ensembles and rules to generate, training the downstream linear model, and finally handling parsing problems that inevitably arise when the LLM’s textual output deviates from the expected format.

---

## Choosing an LLM Backend (GPT‑3.5, Alternatives)

*FeatLLM* treats the LLM purely as a **feature‑engineering oracle**.  The only interaction with the model occurs during a small number of inference calls (typically 20–30 per experiment).  Consequently, the choice of backend is guided by three practical considerations:

1. **API‑level access** – The method only requires the ability to send a prompt and receive a completion.  It does **not** need gradient access or fine‑tuning facilities, making any hosted LLM (OpenAI’s *gpt‑3.5‑turbo*, Azure OpenAI, Claude, or open‑source chat models exposed via an API) viable.  
2. **Reasoning competence** – The LLM must be able to follow the structured prompt, reason about causal relationships, and output logical conditions.  Empirically the authors observed that *GPT‑3.5‑turbo‑0613* produces the most reliable rule sets.  When substituting a different model, verify that it can understand chain‑of‑thought instructions and produce deterministic code‑like snippets (e.g., “if age > 60 and cholesterol = high → risk = 1”).  
3. **Cost and latency** – Since the number of queries is modest, even a relatively expensive model can be affordable.  However, an open‑source model such as *LLaMA‑2‑Chat* or *Mistral‑Instruct* may lower budget constraints while preserving performance, provided it has been trained on code data (which aids the later parsing step).

When switching to another backend, keep the prompt wording unchanged; only the model name and possible token limits differ.  If the new model’s context window is smaller, you may need to reduce the number of demonstration examples or employ more aggressive **feature bagging** (see “Number of ensembles and rules per class” below).

---

## Setting Temperature, Top‑p, and Max Tokens

The LLM generation hyper‑parameters directly affect **rule diversity** and **parsing stability**:

| Parameter | Recommended Value | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **temperature** | **0.5** | A moderate temperature encourages variability across ensemble runs (different rule sets) while still keeping outputs coherent enough for deterministic parsing.  Very low temperatures (≈0.0) produce identical rules in every trial, eroding the benefit of ensembling.  Very high temperatures (>0.9) increase the likelihood of nonsensical syntactic forms, worsening parsing failures. |
| **top‑p** (nucleus sampling) | **1.0** (default) | Keeping top‑p at 1.0 ensures the full probability mass is considered; the temperature already controls randomness.  Restricting top‑p can unintentionally bias the model toward high‑probability tokens that may not include the logical operators needed for rule formation. |
| **max tokens** | **≈ 1024** (or the model’s default) | The rule extraction prompt contains the task description, feature definitions, and a few‑shot serialization.  In most experiments, the entire completion (the rule list) comfortably fits within 500 tokens, leaving ample headroom.  If you encounter truncation warnings, increase the limit; if you are near the model’s context ceiling, reduce the number of few‑shot examples per prompt. |

These settings match the configuration used in the paper’s experiments (temperature = 0.5, top‑p = 1, default max tokens).  When using a model with a smaller context window (e.g., 4 k tokens), the **bagging** technique becomes essential: you randomly sample a subset of features for each ensemble run so that the prompt size stays under the limit.

---

## Number of Ensembles and Rules per Class – Default Values and Trade‑offs

Two hyper‑parameters dominate the computational budget and predictive performance:

1. **Ensembles (T)** – The number of independent rule‑generation trials.  Each trial draws a random subset of features (bagging) and a random ordering of the few‑shot demonstrations, then queries the LLM once.  The final prediction averages the softmax probabilities from all trials (Eq. (2) in the paper).  

2. **Rules per class (R)** – The fixed count of logical conditions that the LLM is instructed to output for each target class (the authors used 10).  

### Default Settings
* **T = 20** ensembles  
* **R = 10** rules per class  

These defaults yielded the best trade‑off across the 13 benchmark datasets: sufficient rule diversity to recover from occasional erroneous generations, yet a manageable feature dimensionality (the downstream linear model receives at most \(c \times R\) binary features, where \(c\) is the number of classes).

### Trade‑off Analysis  

| Scenario | Increase T | Decrease T | Increase R | Decrease R |
|----------|------------|------------|------------|------------|
| **More expressive rule set** | Improves robustness (self‑consistency) but adds API calls and modestly higher latency. | Faster inference; may suffer when some trials produce poor rules. | Potentially captures finer-grained patterns, but each new rule adds a binary feature that can over‑fit in low‑shot regimes. | Simpler model; easier to train, but may miss useful discriminative conditions. |
| **Prompt length constraints** | No impact (bagging already limits the number of features per prompt). | Same. | Larger R expands the LLM’s response, possibly hitting max‑token limits. | Keeps response short; safer for small context windows. |
| **Computational cost** | Linear in T (≈ T × API latency). | Linear reduction in cost. | Minor increase in downstream linear‑model training time (larger input dimensionality). | Faster training, less memory. |

Empirically, the authors observed diminishing returns after **T ≈ 20** (performance plateaued) and noted that setting **R = 10** balanced prompt size against the risk of over‑parameterizing the linear layer in a few‑shot setting.  When dealing with extremely high‑dimensional tables (hundreds of columns), you may wish to **increase T** while **reducing R** to stay within the LLM’s token budget.

---

## Training the Linear Layer (Learning Rate, Batch Size, k‑Fold CV)

After rule extraction, each sample is represented by a binary vector \(\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R\) for class \(k\).  The downstream model is a **bias‑free linear classifier** with non‑negative weights:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\bigr).
\]

Training proceeds by minimizing the cross‑entropy loss on the **k‑shot** labeled set.

### Hyper‑parameters  

| Parameter | Value Used in the Paper | Practical Guidance |
|-----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| **Learning rate** | **0.01** (Adam optimizer) | A relatively high learning rate works because the model is tiny (‑\(c \times R\) parameters).  If you experience divergence (loss exploding), halve the rate to 0.005 or use a learning‑rate scheduler that reduces after a few epochs. |
| **Batch size** | **All k samples (full‑batch)** | With k ≤ 32 the dataset is tiny, so there is no need for mini‑batching.  Full‑batch gradient descent stabilizes training and simplifies implementation. |
| **Number of epochs** | **200** (early stopped via k‑fold CV) | Over‑fitting is a risk in low‑shot regimes; therefore the authors use **k‑fold cross‑validation** (k = 2 or 4, ensuring each fold contains at least one example of every class) to select the epoch with the best validation loss.  Stop training once the validation loss ceases to improve for a patience of ~10 epochs. |
| **Regularization** | **None** (non‑negative projection suffices) | Since the weight vector is forced to be non‑negative, explicit L2 regularization is often unnecessary.  If you notice very large weight magnitudes, add a modest L2 penalty (e.g., \(10^{-4}\)). |

### Training Procedure  

1. **Construct the binary feature matrix** \(\mathbf{Z} \in \{0,1\}^{k \times (cR)}\) by concatenating the \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) across classes.  
2. **Initialize** \(\mathbf{w}_k\) with small positive values (e.g., Uniform\([0,0.1]\)).  
3. **Run Adam** for the prescribed number of epochs, projecting \(\mathbf{w}_k \gets \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\) after each gradient step.  
4. **k‑fold split** the k labeled points, compute validation loss per epoch, and pick the epoch achieving the lowest average validation loss across folds.  
5. **Store** the final \(\mathbf{w}_k\) for use in inference; repeat the whole training pipeline for each ensemble trial \(t\) to obtain \(\mathbf{w}[t]\).  

Because the linear model is tiny, the entire training loop finishes in a fraction of a second on a modern CPU or GPU.  The dominant runtime component remains the LLM API calls required for rule generation.

---

## Debugging Rule Parsing Failures

The LLM’s textual output can deviate from the strict “Rule = feature operator value” format, despite the response‑format instructions.  Common sources of failure include:

1. **Synactic drift** – Replacement of symbols (e.g., “>” becomes “greater than”) or insertion of extra whitespace.  
2. **Logical operator mixing** – The model may concatenate multiple conditions using “and/or” inside a single rule, which contradicts the parser’s expectation of a single comparison per rule.  
3. **Type mismatch** – A rule may compare a categorical feature with a numeric operator, producing code that crashes at runtime.  

### Recommended Debugging Workflow  

1. **Inspect Raw Completion** – Save the LLM’s raw text to a log file.  Spot patterns like “If age greater than 60” versus “age > 60”.  

2. **Normalize Operators** – Before feeding the rules to the parsing LLM, run a lightweight pre‑processor that maps common phrasing to canonical symbols:  
   ```python
   mapping = {
       "greater than": ">",
       "less than": "<",
       "equal to": "==",
       "not equal to": "!=",
       "at most": "<=",
       "at least": ">="
   }
   ```
   Apply these replacements globally.  

3. **Leverage the LLM for Code Generation** – The paper’s authors found that asking the LLM *again*—this time with a “code‑generation” prompt—produces robust Python snippets that evaluate the rules, even when the original wording is noisy.  The prompt includes:
   - The **function signature** (e.g., `def rule_age_gt_60(row):`),  
   - A description of each feature’s type,  
   - The raw rule text.  
   The LLM returns a small function body that can be executed with `exec()`.  This step greatly reduces parsing errors and avoids hand‑crafted regex brittleness.  

4. **Validate Generated Code** – After `exec()`, run the function on a few rows of the dataset.  If an exception is raised (e.g., `KeyError` for a missing column), print the offending rule and manually correct the mapping or discard the rule.  

5. **Automated Filtering** – The downstream linear model naturally down‑weights useless rules; however, extremely malformed rules (always false or causing runtime errors) should be removed early.  A simple heuristic: if a rule yields a constant vector (all zeros) on the *k‑shot* training set, discard it before training.  

6. **Iterate Temperature** – If parsing failures are frequent, lower the temperature slightly (e.g., 0.4).  A more deterministic LLM output tends to adhere more closely to the prescribed format, at the expense of rule diversity.  

By following this pipeline—pre‑process, LLM‑assisted code generation, validation—you can achieve a high success rate even with noisy completions.  The authors report that after this two‑stage parsing, the proportion of *useful* rules (AUROC > 0.5) exceeds 70 % before any weight tuning, confirming that the parser reliably extracts meaningful binary features.

---

### Summary Checklist  

- **Backend**: Choose a model with API access and code‑generation capability; *GPT‑3.5‑turbo‑0613* is the baseline.  
- **Generation Parameters**: Use temperature = 0.5, top‑p = 1, default max tokens; adjust only if you see truncation.  
- **Ensemble & Rules**: Start with T = 20 and R = 10; tune upward only if you have budget for more API calls or a larger context window.  
- **Linear Model Training**: Adam, lr = 0.01, full‑batch, 200 epochs with k‑fold early stopping; project weights to non‑negative.  
- **Parsing**: Apply operator normalisation, then ask the LLM to emit Python code; validate on a few rows, drop constant/erroneous features.  

With these practical guidelines you can faithfully reproduce *FeatLLM*’s impressive few‑shot performance while keeping inference latency low and debugging effort manageable.

# Experimental Evaluation

In this chapter we provide a comprehensive account of the empirical study that validates the proposed **FeatLLM** framework.  We describe the collection and salient properties of the thirteen tabular benchmarks, the families of baselines against which FeatLLM is compared, the evaluation protocol—including the few‑shot regime and the use of the area under the ROC curve (AUC) as the primary metric—and the main quantitative findings.  We also discuss the contexts in which FeatLLM delivers the most pronounced advantages, such as high‑dimensional tables and extremely limited labeled data.

---

## Dataset Collection and Characteristics

The experimental suite comprises **thirteen heterogeneous classification datasets** that span a broad spectrum of domains, sizes, and feature types.  All datasets are publicly available, and each provides natural‑language feature names and, where applicable, brief textual definitions of the attributes.  The collection can be grouped into three broad categories:

1. **Classic benchmark datasets** that have been widely used in the tabular learning literature:
   - **Adult** – predicting whether an individual’s annual income exceeds \$50 k.  
   - **Bank** – predicting subscription to a term deposit.  
   - **Blood** – predicting donor return.  
   - **Car** – predicting automobile quality.  
   - **Communities** – predicting crime rate in a region.  
   - **Credit‑g** – predicting good versus bad credit risk.  
   - **Diabetes** – predicting presence of diabetes.  
   - **Heart** – predicting coronary artery disease.  
   - **Myocardial** – predicting chronic heart failure.

2. **More recent or synthetic datasets** that were unlikely to have been seen during the pre‑training of the LLM used (GPT‑3.5‑turbo‑0613):
   - **Cultivars** – predicting soybean grain‑yield level.  
   - **NHANES** – predicting an individual's age group from health records.  
   - **Sequence‑type** – classifying numeric sequences as arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz.  
   - **Solution‑mix** – predicting whether the concentration of a four‑solution mixture exceeds 0.5.

The **size and dimensionality** of these tables vary substantially.  Some datasets (e.g., *Adult*, *Bank*) contain only a few dozen attributes, while others (*Communities*, *Myocardial*) exceed one hundred columns, thereby stressing the prompt‑length limitations of LLM‑centric approaches.  Table 1 in the original manuscript summarises the number of records, number of features, and the number of classes for each benchmark; this variety ensures that the experimental results are not tied to a single data regime.

All datasets are presented with **natural‑language feature names** (the set \(F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d\)) and, when available, textual definitions that the FeatLLM prompts can incorporate.  This aligns with the premise that LLMs can exploit such metadata to extract meaningful rules, as described in the method section.

---

## Baseline Categories

To contextualise FeatLLM’s performance, nine baseline methods are evaluated.  These fall into three principal families:

1. **Classical supervised tabular learners** that do not use any external knowledge:
   - **Logistic Regression (LogReg)** – a linear model with L2 regularisation.  
   - **XGBoost** – gradient‑boosted decision trees, a strong non‑linear baseline.  
   - **Random Forest** – an ensemble of decision trees with bagging.

2. **Semi‑supervised or pre‑training‑based methods** that leverage unlabeled data or synthetic pre‑training:
   - **SCARF** – a contrastive learning approach that learns representations from unlabeled tables.  
   - **STUNT** – a self‑training technique that augments few‑shot data with pseudo‑labels.  
   - **TabPFN** – a Bayesian neural network pre‑trained on a mixture of synthetic tabular distributions; inference is performed by feeding both training and test instances jointly.

3. **LLM‑centric approaches** that directly employ large language models for prediction:
   - **In‑context learning** – few‑shot examples are serialized into the prompt and the LLM outputs a class label for each test sample.  
   - **TABLET** – enriches the prompt with auxiliary hints (rule‑sets and prototypes) generated by an external classifier.  
   - **TabLLM** – fine‑tunes an LLM (via parameter‑efficient techniques such as IA3) on the few‑shot examples before inference.

All LLM‑based baselines use the same underlying language model (GPT‑3.5 for the in‑context baseline; T0 for TabLLM according to the original settings) to ensure a fair comparison of algorithmic ideas rather than model capacity.

---

## Evaluation Protocol

### Few‑Shot Regime

The central focus of the study is **low‑shot learning**, where only a tiny subset of labeled instances is available for training.  For each dataset, a random subset of \(k\) examples is drawn, with \(k\) taking values such as 2, 4, 8, or 16 depending on the experiment.  The remainder of the dataset is reserved for evaluation.  By varying \(k\), the authors assess how each method scales as the amount of labelled data grows.

### Repeated Random Splits

To reduce variance due to a particular train‑test split, the experiments are **repeated three times** with different random seeds for the selection of the few‑shot training set and the accompanying test set.  The reported numbers are the **mean and standard deviation of the AUC** (area under the receiver‑operating‑characteristic curve) across these repetitions.  AUC is chosen because it is insensitive to class‑balance variations and reflects the ranking quality of the predicted probabilities.

### Model Training and Hyper‑Parameters

- **FeatLLM**: Uses a temperature of 0.5 for LLM queries, extracts ten rules per class, and forms an ensemble of 20 independent models (each built with a different randomisation of demonstration order and feature bagging).  The downstream linear model is trained with Adam (learning rate 0.01) for 200 epochs, with early stopping guided by \(k\)-fold cross‑validation on the few‑shot set.
  
- **Classical baselines**: Hyper‑parameters are tuned via grid‑search combined with cross‑validation on the few‑shot data.

- **Semi‑supervised baselines**: Follow the implementations described in their original papers; they are provided with the same few‑shot labelled set but may also exploit additional unlabeled data where applicable.

- **LLM baselines**: Follow the prescribed prompting or fine‑tuning procedures (e.g., IA3 for TabLLM) and use the same temperature and sampling settings as FeatLLM when appropriate.

All methods are evaluated under identical computational settings (an A100 GPU for training, API calls for LLM inference) to ensure comparable runtime measurements.

---

## Main Results: Performance Gains and Statistical Significance

The aggregated results, displayed in Tables 1 and 2 of the original manuscript, reveal a **consistent superiority of FeatLLM** across the thirteen datasets.  Key observations include:

1. **Top‑ranked performance**: FeatLLM attains the highest average AUC on the majority of datasets.  In the few‑shot setting with only four labelled examples, its mean AUC is comparable to that of conventional baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, Random Forest) that are given **eight times more labelled data** (32 shots).  This demonstrates that the rule‑generation step effectively injects prior knowledge, compensating for the scarcity of supervised signals.

2. **Robustness to high dimensionality**: On datasets with large numbers of attributes—*Communities* (≈100 features) and *Myocardial* (also >100)—many LLM‑based baselines fail to produce predictions because the serialized prompt exceeds the model’s context window.  FeatLLM, by contrast, employs **feature bagging** and an ensemble of smaller prompts, thus maintaining viable performance even when the raw feature count is large.

3. **Statistical significance**: Across repeated splits, the standard deviations of FeatLLM’s AUC are modest (often < 0.02), indicating stable performance.  In pairwise comparisons with the strongest non‑LLM baseline (typically XGBoost), the **difference in mean AUC exceeds one standard error** on most datasets, suggesting statistical significance at the 95 % confidence level.

4. **Comparison with other LLM methods**: In‑context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM either under‑perform or outright collapse on high‑dimensional tables due to prompt‑length constraints.  Even when they succeed, their average gains over classical methods are modest.  FeatLLM’s design—extracting **compact binary rule features** and then applying a lightweight linear model—circumvents the need for per‑sample LLM inference, dramatically lowering both computational cost and error propagation.

5. **Effect of ensembling**: The ensemble of 20 rule‑generation trials yields a noticeable lift over a single FeatLLM instance (≈ 1–2 % AUC increase on average).  This confirms the value of stochasticity (temperature‑driven diversity, demonstration ordering, feature bagging) in producing complementary rule sets.

---

## Analysis of Where FeatLLM Shines

### High‑Dimensional Tables

When the number of columns approaches or exceeds the model’s context window, naïve serialization becomes infeasible for most LLM approaches.  FeatLLM’s **feature‑bagging strategy** selects a random subset of columns for each rule‑generation trial, thereby keeping the prompt within the allowed length.  Because the ensemble aggregates many such partial views, the final predictor benefits from a **coverage of the full feature space** without ever requiring the LLM to process the entire table at once.  Empirically, this yields **stable AUCs** on *Communities* and *Myocardial* where baselines either fail or suffer a sharp performance drop.

### Extreme Low‑Shot Scenarios

In the **2‑shot** regime, the scarcity of labelled examples threatens overfitting for any data‑driven learner.  FeatLLM mitigates this by anchoring rule extraction in the LLM’s **world knowledge** (Step‑1 of the reasoning instruction).  The LLM’s prior understanding of domain‑relevant relationships (e.g., “older age increases disease risk”) guides it to propose sensible conditions even when only two labelled rows are shown.  Ablation experiments (Table 3) demonstrate that removing the reasoning step or the feature‑description component leads to a pronounced drop in performance under 2‑shot conditions, underscoring the importance of leveraging prior knowledge.

### Robustness to Spurious Correlations

A controlled experiment on the **Heart** dataset injected irrelevant columns taken from the **Adult** dataset.  As the number of spurious columns increased, most baselines exhibited rapidly declining AUC, reflecting their tendency to latch onto accidental correlations present in the few‑shot examples.  FeatLLM’s AUC decreased only modestly, indicating that its rule‑generation process **filters out irrelevant features** during the initial causal reasoning stage.  The analysis showed that rules based on noisy columns constituted merely 1–2 % of the total rule set, confirming that the LLM preferentially reasons about features that align with the task description.

### Computational Efficiency

Because FeatLLM requires the LLM **only during the offline feature‑engineering phase** (a bounded set of 30 API calls for the standard configuration), its **inference latency** for new samples is comparable to that of classical linear models—orders of magnitude faster than per‑sample LLM inference required by the in‑context, TABLET, and TabLLM baselines.  Training time is also modest; the downstream linear model converges within a few hundred gradient steps on the few‑shot data, and the entire pipeline can be parallelised across the ensemble members.

---

### Summary

The experimental evaluation establishes **FeatLLM** as a highly effective few‑shot tabular learner.  By converting LLM reasoning into compact binary rule features and coupling these with a simple, trainable linear model, FeatLLM achieves **state‑of‑the‑art performance** across a diverse set of datasets while remaining **computationally tractable** and **robust to high dimensionality** and **spurious correlations**.  These results substantiate the central claim of the paper: large language models can be harnessed not as end‑to‑end predictors but as **feature engineers**, delivering superior data efficiency and practical applicability in real‑world tabular domains.

# Ablation Studies and Component Analysis

Understanding why a machine‑learning system works often requires more than just reporting its overall performance.  
In the **FeatLLM** framework, several novel choices are intertwined: a two‑step reasoning prompt, explicit natural‑language feature descriptions, extraction of a fixed set of logical rules, a lightweight linear model that learns non‑negative weights for those rules, and an ensemble that combines many such rule‑based learners using feature‑bagging.  
To verify that each of these ingredients truly contributes to the observed gains, the authors carried out a series of *ablation experiments*.  An ablation removes or modifies one component while keeping the rest of the pipeline fixed, allowing a clean comparison of the impact of that component.  
In this chapter we walk through each ablation, explain the motivation behind it, describe how it was performed, and interpret the resulting changes in performance across different few‑shot regimes (2‑shot, 4‑shot, 8‑shot, etc.).  The goal is to give the reader an intuitive sense of why each design decision matters and how they interact.

---

## Removing Weight Tuning vs. Simple Rule Count

### What the original design does
After the LLM has produced a set of binary rule‑based features \(\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R\) for each class \(k\), FeatLLM does **not** treat all rules as equally informative.  
Instead, it trains a *linear* model without bias:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\; \operatorname{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big),
\]

where \(\mathbf{w}_k\) are trainable, non‑negative weights learned by minimizing the cross‑entropy loss on the few labeled examples.  
The projection \(\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\) guarantees that a rule can only increase, never decrease, the logit for its class.  This step allows the system to **up‑weight** highly predictive rules and effectively discard noisy ones (weights near zero).

### The ablation
In the *‑Tuning* ablation the linear weights are omitted.  The logit for a class is simply the *sum* of its satisfied rules:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \sum_{r=1}^{R} z_{k,r}^i .
\]

No learning occurs; the model merely counts how many rules each sample satisfies.

### Why this matters
If the raw rule set already captures most of the signal, counting may be sufficient.  However, the LLM can generate *redundant* or *partially correct* rules, especially when the prompt contains many features or when the few‑shot examples are noisy.  A learned weighting scheme can differentiate:

- **Highly predictive rules** (e.g., “age > 65 AND cholesterol > 240”) receive a large weight.
- **Ambiguous or generic rules** (e.g., “gender is male”) receive a small weight.

By removing this step, we expect performance to degrade, particularly when more training shots are available, because the extra data make the weight estimation more reliable.

### Empirical findings
Across all 13 benchmark datasets, the *‑Tuning* ablation caused a **consistent drop** in area‑under‑the‑curve (AUC).  The magnitude of the drop grew with the number of shots: with 2 shots the loss was modest (≈ 0.5 % absolute AUC), while with 8 shots the loss reached up to 2 % absolute AUC on some datasets.  This pattern matches the intuition that richer supervision lets the linear model learn more accurate importance scores, amplifying the benefit of weight tuning.

---

## No Ensemble / No Bagging

### The original ensemble strategy
FeatLLM repeats the entire rule‑extraction pipeline \(T\) times (the default \(T=20\)).  For each repetition it:
1. Randomly *bags* a subset of the original columns (feature bagging) and/or a subset of the few‑shot examples,
2. Queries the LLM with a higher temperature (e.g., 0.5) to encourage diverse rule sets,
3. Trains a separate linear weight vector \(\mathbf{w}[t]\).

The final class probability for a test sample is the average of the softmax outputs from all \(T\) learners.  This process mirrors classic ensemble methods such as random forests: each weak learner may be imperfect, but their average reduces variance and mitigates systematic errors.

### The ablation
In the *‑Ensemble* ablation the pipeline is run **once** only, with no bagging and no averaging.  Consequently, the model relies on a single rule set and a single weight vector.

### Why this matters
Two failure modes are addressed by ensembling:

1. **Prompt‑size constraints** – When the original table has many columns, a single prompt may need to truncate or omit some features.  Bagging ensures each feature appears in at least some prompts, allowing the LLM to generate rules that involve the whole feature space across the ensemble.
2. **LLM stochasticity** – Even with a deterministic temperature, the LLM can produce different rule sets depending on the order of the few‑shot examples.  Averaging over many draws smooths out idiosyncratic, possibly incorrect rules.

### Empirical findings
Eliminating the ensemble reduced the average AUC by about **1–3 % absolute** across datasets, with the greatest penalties on the *high‑dimensional* datasets (e.g., *Communities* and *Myocardial*).  In those cases, a single prompt could not accommodate all columns; the bagging mechanism restored coverage in the full FeatLLM system, explaining the larger gap.

---

## Omitting Feature Descriptions

### What the description provides
Each column name \(f_j\) is accompanied by a short natural‑language definition (e.g., “*age*: integer representing years of the patient”).  The prompt also indicates the value type (numeric, categorical, binary).  This information serves two purposes:

1. **Disambiguation** – The LLM can correctly interpret “high”, “low”, “yes”, “no”, etc., according to the column’s type.
2. **Prior knowledge activation** – The description can trigger relevant world knowledge (“*cholesterol* is a risk factor for heart disease”).

### The ablation
The *‑Description* variant removes all feature definitions from the prompt, retaining only the column names and their serialized values.

### Why this matters
Without a definition, the LLM must infer the semantics of a column solely from its name and the few examples.  For ambiguous or domain‑specific names (e.g., “*G*” in a genetics dataset), the model may generate ill‑formed or mis‑typed rules (e.g., treating a categorical variable as numeric).  Moreover, the LLM loses the chance to inject relevant domain knowledge.

### Empirical findings
The omission caused a noticeable AUC decrease, especially in the **2‑shot** and **4‑shot** regimes where the few examples provide very limited statistical evidence.  Average losses of **0.8–1.5 %** were observed.  When more shots were available, the drop shrank because the data themselves began to dominate the LLM’s reasoning, but the gap never vanished entirely, underscoring the lasting value of explicit descriptions.

---

## Skipping the First Reasoning Step

### Two‑step reasoning prompt
FeatLLM’s prompt contains a *reasoning instruction* that splits the LLM’s thought process:

1. **Step 1 – Causal inference** – The LLM is asked to think about the *general relationship* between features and the task, without looking at the few‑shot examples.  This encourages the model to rely on its broad prior knowledge (e.g., “older age generally increases risk of heart disease”).
2. **Step 2 – Rule extraction** – Using the causal insight from Step 1 together with the concrete examples, the LLM then formulates explicit logical rules for each class.

The separation is designed to prevent the LLM from latching onto spurious correlations that only appear in the tiny demonstration set.

### The ablation
In the *‑Reasoning* version, Step 1 is **removed**; the LLM jumps straight to rule formulation using the examples alone.

### Why this matters
When only a few labeled rows are shown, random coincidences (e.g., “all training samples with label = 1 happen to have gender = female”) can mislead a model that does not first consider general domain knowledge.  By forcing the LLM to articulate a *prior* causal map, FeatLLM biases the rule‑generation toward features that are globally relevant.

### Empirical findings
The impact of dropping Step 1 is most severe in the **lowest‑shot** settings.  With 2 shots, the AUC loss averaged **≈ 2 %** absolute, indicating that many generated rule sets became dominated by dataset‑specific noise.  As the number of shots increased to 8, the loss shrank to about **0.5 %**, reflecting that sufficient examples can partially compensate for the missing prior reasoning.  Nevertheless, the presence of Step 1 consistently improved robustness across all datasets.

---

## Interpreting Results Across Shot Numbers

The ablation study reports performance for several *k‑shot* configurations (commonly 2, 4, and 8).  Examining how each component behaves as the amount of labeled data changes yields deeper insight:

| Component | Effect at 2‑shot | Effect at 4‑shot | Effect at 8‑shot |
|-----------|------------------|------------------|------------------|
| Weight tuning | Small improvement (≈ 0.5 % AUC) | Moderate (≈ 1 % AUC) | Larger (≈ 2 % AUC) |
| Ensemble / bagging | Very important (≈ 2–3 % AUC) | Important (≈ 1–2 % AUC) | Still helpful but smaller gain |
| Feature descriptions | Noticeable (≈ 1 % AUC) | Moderate (≈ 0.7 % AUC) | Minor (≈ 0.3 % AUC) |
| First reasoning step | Critical (≈ 2 % AUC) | Helpful (≈ 1 % AUC) | Slight benefit (≈ 0.5 % AUC) |

**Key takeaways:**

1. **Learning capacity grows with shots.**  The linear weight tuning becomes more valuable as more labeled examples are available, because the model can more reliably estimate each rule’s contribution.
2. **Prior knowledge matters most when data are scarce.**  Both feature descriptions and the causal reasoning step provide external information that compensates for the paucity of examples; their relative importance fades as the dataset grows.
3. **Ensembling is a universal stabilizer.**  Even with many shots, bagging mitigates the prompt‑size limitation and reduces variance induced by LLM randomness, so it remains beneficial across the board.

Overall, the ablation experiments confirm that the **FeatLLM** design is not an accidental collection of tricks; each piece addresses a concrete statistical challenge inherent to few‑shot tabular learning with large language models.  Removing any one of them degrades performance, and the magnitude of degradation aligns with theoretical expectations about bias‑variance trade‑offs, prior‑knowledge utilization, and model capacity.  This systematic analysis gives practitioners confidence that the full pipeline—reasoning prompt, feature descriptions, rule‑based binary features, learned non‑negative weights, and ensemble bagging—is a cohesive, well‑justified solution to the problem of data‑efficient tabular prediction.

# Robustness Analyses

In this chapter we examine the robustness of the **FeatLLM** framework when confronted with the kinds of difficulties that routinely arise in real‑world tabular learning: spurious correlations introduced by irrelevant columns, variability in the quality of the rules that the language model generates, and sensitivity to key hyper‑parameters such as the ensemble size and the number of rules extracted per class.  We also compare the computational cost of FeatLLM with alternative few‑shot methods and discuss the circumstances under which the approach may fail, together with strategies that can alleviate those failures.  All observations are drawn directly from the experimental results reported in the original paper.

---

## Synthetic Addition of Irrelevant Columns and Performance Impact

A central claim of FeatLLM is that, by asking the large language model (LLM) to reason about *causal* relations between features and the target label before it extracts concrete decision rules, the framework can ignore features that are unrelated to the task.  To evaluate this claim, the authors performed a controlled experiment on the **Heart** dataset, a binary classification problem that predicts coronary artery disease.  

The experiment proceeds as follows.  First, a clean version of the Heart dataset is taken as the baseline.  Then, columns from the **Adult** dataset—features that are known to be unrelated to heart disease (e.g., “hours‑per‑week”, “education‑num”)—are concatenated to the Heart data table.  The number of such irrelevant columns is increased stepwise, producing data matrices with progressively larger proportions of spurious features.  For each augmented version, all methods are trained with the same **8‑shot** labelled subset (i.e., eight randomly selected training examples, guaranteeing at least one example per class) and evaluated on a held‑out test set using the area under the receiver‑operating‑characteristic curve (AUROC).

The results, visualised in the paper’s Figure 7, reveal a stark contrast between FeatLLM and the baselines.  As the count of irrelevant columns grows, the AUROC of conventional models (Logistic Regression, XGBoost, Random Forest) and of other LLM‑based approaches (In‑context learning, TABLET, TabLLM) drops sharply, indicating that these methods readily pick up on spurious correlations when the feature space is polluted.  In contrast, FeatLLM’s performance degrades only marginally.  Quantitatively, even when dozens of noisy columns are added, the AUROC remains within a few percentage points of the clean‑data baseline.

Why does FeatLLM resist this degradation?  The two‑step reasoning instruction embedded in its prompts forces the LLM to first infer a *general* relationship between the task description and the set of features, using its world knowledge rather than the few exemplars.  Only after this high‑level causal reasoning does the model proceed to extract concrete rules, and it does so with a *fixed* budget of ten rules per class.  Consequently, the LLM tends to select conditions that involve only those features it deems relevant from its prior knowledge, effectively discarding the injected irrelevant columns.  The subsequent linear weighting stage further attenuates any residual noisy rules because the learned weights are constrained to be non‑negative and are trained on the few labelled examples; rules that never fire on the training data receive near‑zero weight.

A complementary analysis (described in the appendix of the paper) shows that when the added columns are *closely related* to the target domain (e.g., blood‑pressure‑related columns from another medical dataset), FeatLLM can occasionally be misled, extracting rules that inadvertently capture those spurious associations.  This observation underscores that the method’s robustness hinges on a clear semantic separation between relevant and irrelevant attributes.

---

## Rule Quality Metrics (AUROC of Individual Rules Before/After Tuning)

Beyond aggregate performance, the authors investigated the intrinsic informativeness of the binary features derived from the LLM‑generated rules.  Each rule yields a binary indicator – “1” if a sample satisfies the rule, “0” otherwise – and these indicators constitute the new feature space on which a simple linear model is trained.

To quantify rule quality, the authors computed the AUROC of each binary rule directly against the target label, *before* any weight tuning.  This per‑rule AUROC measures how well the rule alone separates the two classes.  The proportion of rules whose AUROC exceeds 0.5 (i.e., better than random guessing) is reported as “useful features”.  Table 9 of the paper shows that, across all 13 benchmark datasets, a substantial majority of rules are useful: the “Before tuning” column reports useful‑feature ratios ranging from roughly 0.6 to 0.9, indicating that the LLM is already producing highly relevant conditions merely from its prior knowledge and the few‑shot demonstrations.

After the linear model is trained on the few labelled examples, the learned non‑negative weights act as a filter, down‑weighting rules that are not predictive on the observed data.  The authors apply a threshold of 0.1 to the learned weights and recompute the proportion of remaining rules that retain AUROC > 0.5 (“After tuning”).  The ratios improve modestly or stay comparable, demonstrating that the training step successfully discards the few noisy rules that escaped the initial reasoning stage while preserving the informative ones.  For example, on the **Adult** dataset the useful‑feature ratio rises from 0.78 before tuning to 0.81 after tuning; on the more challenging **Cultivars** dataset it reaches 0.92 after tuning.

These measurements provide an interpretable validation that FeatLLM’s rule‑generation process yields genuinely discriminative features, rather than arbitrary textual artefacts.  Moreover, the simple linear weighting scheme proves sufficient to refine the raw rule set without overfitting, even in the extreme low‑shot regime.

---

## Effect of Ensemble Size and Number of Rules per Class

FeatLLM relies on an **ensemble of weak learners** to mitigate two practical issues: (1) the stochastic nature of LLM generation (different answers at different temperatures or demonstration orders) and (2) the limited prompt length when the original table has many columns.  Each ensemble member executes the full pipeline—prompt construction, rule extraction, parsing, binary feature creation, and linear weight learning—using a *bagged* subset of features or samples.  The final prediction is the average of the softmax probabilities from all members (Eq. 2 in the paper).

Two hyper‑parameters govern this ensemble:

1. **Number of ensembles (T)** – the number of independent LLM queries.
2. **Number of rules per class (R)** – the fixed budget of rules that each LLM call is asked to produce (set to 10 in the main experiments).

### Ensemble Size (T)

Figure 10 (Appendix) displays a curve of average AUROC versus the number of ensembles on several datasets.  Performance improves rapidly as T grows from 1 to roughly 10, reflecting the benefit of aggregating diverse rule sets.  Beyond about **20 ensembles**, the gain plateaus, indicating diminishing returns: additional members contribute largely redundant rules, while computational cost continues to increase linearly with T.  Consequently, the authors adopt T = 20 as the default, striking a balance between robustness and efficiency.

### Number of Rules per Class (R)

The authors also vary R from 5 to 15 while holding T fixed.  The results (Figure 9) show no statistically significant differences across this range for most datasets.  However, a modest sweet spot emerges at **R = 10**, which the authors adopt.  The rationale is twofold:

- **Prompt length**: each additional rule lengthens the LLM’s output, which, when combined with the serialized few‑shot examples and feature descriptions, may exceed the model’s context window for high‑dimensional tables.
- **Overfitting risk**: in the few‑shot regime, an overly large binary feature vector can cause the linear model to overfit the tiny training set, degrading generalisation.

Thus, the chosen defaults (T = 20, R = 10) provide a stable, high‑performing configuration across the heterogeneous benchmark suite, while keeping the total number of API calls (T × 1 = 20) modest.

---

## Training and Inference Time Comparison with Other Methods

A key practical advantage of FeatLLM is that, after the rule‑generation phase, **inference for new samples does not require any further LLM calls**.  Only the lightweight linear model—essentially a dot product between the binary rule vector and a learned weight vector—is evaluated.  The paper reports a detailed runtime analysis on the **Adult** dataset with a 16‑shot training set, executed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (all LLM calls are made via the OpenAI API, which is not GPU‑bound).

| Method               | Training Time* | Inference Time* |
|----------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| FeatLLM              | ≈ 30 seconds (30 API queries) | ≈ 0.02 seconds per batch |
| Logistic Regression  | ≈ 0.05 seconds | ≈ 0.01 seconds |
| XGBoost              | ≈ 0.12 seconds | ≈ 0.02 seconds |
| Random Forest        | ≈ 0.09 seconds | ≈ 0.03 seconds |
| In‑context Learning  | ≈ 0.04 seconds per sample (LLM call) | ≈ 0.04 seconds per sample |
| TABLET               | ≈ 0.05 seconds per sample (LLM call) | ≈ 0.05 seconds per sample |
| TabLLM (IA3‑tuned)   | ≈ 2 hours on 4 × A100 (model‑parallel fine‑tuning) | ≈ 0.06 seconds per sample (LLM call) |

\*Times are averaged over three runs; LLM latency refers to the round‑trip API time, which dominates the per‑sample cost for the LLM‑based baselines.

**Interpretation.**  

- **Training cost**: FeatLLM’s training cost is dominated by the 30 API calls needed to generate rule sets for the 20 ensemble members (each member sends a single query; the extra ten calls arise from the parsing‑code generation step).  This overhead is modest compared to the multi‑hour fine‑tuning required by TabLLM, and it can be parallelised across multiple API workers if desired.
  
- **Inference cost**: Once the binary rule vectors are pre‑computed, inference reduces to a few matrix multiplications—identical in complexity to traditional linear models.  Consequently, FeatLLM’s per‑sample latency matches or even slightly exceeds that of classical baselines, and is **orders of magnitude faster** than any method that must contact the LLM for every test example (In‑context, TABLET, TabLLM).  

This efficiency makes FeatLLM attractive for deployment scenarios where low latency and limited API usage are essential (e.g., clinical decision support or real‑time financial risk scoring).

---

## Discussion of Failure Modes and Mitigation Strategies

Although the empirical results demonstrate strong robustness, the authors acknowledge several situations where FeatLLM can struggle, as well as practical mitigations.

1. **Misleading Prior Knowledge**  
   - *Failure mode*: If the LLM’s pre‑training corpus contains incorrect or biased domain knowledge, the initial causal reasoning step may produce rules that are systematically wrong (e.g., assuming a risk factor that is debunked).  
   - *Mitigation*: Increase the number of ensembles and deliberately raise the temperature of the LLM during rule generation.  Diverse stochastic draws help surface alternative rule sets; the ensemble’s averaging can dilute systematic bias.  Additionally, incorporating domain‑specific feature descriptions (as the paper’s “description” ablation shows) can steer the LLM toward the correct semantics.

2. **Over‑reliance on a Small Set of Rules**  
   - *Failure mode*: Because the framework caps the rule count at ten per class, a particularly complex decision boundary may not be captured fully, leading to under‑fitting.  
   - *Mitigation*: Perform a modest increase of R (e.g., to 12) when the dataset has a very high number of informative features and the context window permits the longer prompt.  The authors’ hyper‑parameter study indicates that modest increases do not dramatically hurt runtime.

3. **Parsing Errors from Noisy LLM Output**  
   - *Failure mode*: The LLM may emit syntactic variants (“greater than” versus “>”) that break the simple text‑to‑code parser, resulting in missing or malformed binary features.  
   - *Mitigation*: The paper’s design already addresses this by *re‑prompting* the LLM to generate executable Python code that directly implements the rule logic.  This code‑generation step is itself robust to linguistic variation because the LLM has been trained on large code corpora.  In practice, a fallback “try‑except” wrapper around `exec()` can catch any runtime errors and skip malformed rules.

4. **Spurious Correlations from Semantically Related Distractors**  
   - *Failure mode*: When irrelevant columns are semantically close to the target domain (e.g., other medical measurements), the LLM’s causal reasoning may mistakenly treat them as relevant, producing rules that involve those distractors.  
   - *Mitigation*: Employ **feature bagging** more aggressively—sample a smaller subset of columns for each ensemble member—so that any given spurious column appears in only a minority of the rule sets.  The ensemble averaging then diminishes its impact.  Moreover, the linear weight learning will assign near‑zero weight to rules that never fire on the few labelled examples, automatically pruning them.

5. **Prompt Length Exceeded for Very High‑Dimensional Tables**  
   - *Failure mode*: Datasets such as **Communities** and **Myocardial** contain over a hundred attributes; serialising all feature names and descriptions may exceed the LLM’s context window.  
   - *Mitigation*: The authors explicitly integrate *bagging of features* into each ensemble iteration, randomly selecting a manageable subset of columns to include in the prompt.  This reduces the prompt size while preserving coverage across the full attribute set over many ensemble members.  The empirical results show that this strategy sustains performance even on the largest benchmarks.

Overall, the combination of **structured two‑step prompting**, **rule‑level bagging**, and **ensemble averaging** equips FeatLLM with multiple layers of defense against the typical robustness pitfalls encountered in few‑shot tabular learning.  When a particular failure mode is observed in practice, the corresponding mitigation—adjusting temperature, increasing ensemble count, or tightening the feature bagging ratio—can be applied without redesigning the entire system.

--- 

### Summary

The robustness analyses presented in the paper provide compelling evidence that FeatLLM can **screen out spurious correlations**, **produce high‑quality rule‑based features**, and **remain stable** across a range of hyper‑parameter settings.  Its **computational footprint** at inference time rivals that of classic linear models, while far outperforming other LLM‑centric approaches in latency.  By understanding the identified failure modes and applying the suggested mitigations—temperature tuning, more ensembles, tighter bagging, and careful feature description—practitioners can deploy FeatLLM confidently in settings where data are scarce, features are numerous, and real‑time predictions are required.

# Interpretability and Explainability

Understanding why a model makes a particular prediction is as important as achieving high predictive accuracy, especially in high‑stakes domains such as medicine and finance.  The **FeatLLM** framework introduces a transparent pipeline in which a large language model (LLM) first generates human‑readable rules and then a simple linear model learns to weight those rules.  Because every decision is ultimately reduced to a weighted sum of binary rule‑satisfaction features, the reasoning process can be inspected at multiple levels: the natural‑language rules themselves, the learned non‑negative weights that indicate rule importance, and the final class probabilities obtained from a soft‑max of the weighted logits.  This chapter explains how to interpret the outputs of FeatLLM, illustrates the process with concrete case studies, and situates the approach within the broader interpretability literature.

---

## Reading the Generated Natural‑Language Rules

The first step of FeatLLM is to prompt the LLM to produce **rules** that capture the conditions under which a sample belongs to a particular class.  The prompt is carefully structured into three parts:

1. **Basic Information Description** – the task description, feature names and definitions, and a few serialized training examples.  
2. **Reasoning Instruction** – a two‑step chain‑of‑thought cue that first asks the LLM to reason about causal relationships between features and the target, then to derive concrete rules using the few‑shot examples.  
3. **Response Instruction** – a strict template that tells the LLM how to format each rule (e.g., “If `age > 65` and `cholesterol = high` then class = `heart_failure`”).

Because the LLM is guided to produce a *fixed number of rules* (ten per class in the experiments), the output is a concise, enumerated list rather than a free‑form paragraph.  An example rule for a binary medical diagnosis might appear as:

```
Rule 1 (Heart Failure):
   (age > 65) AND (ejection_fraction < 30) AND (serum_creatinine > 1.2)
```

When reading such rules, a practitioner should:

- **Identify feature types** (numeric comparisons, categorical equality, or range checks).  The response instruction forces the LLM to respect the declared feature type, preventing type mismatches.
- **Check logical composition** – rules may contain conjunctions (`AND`) or disjunctions (`OR`).  The prompt explicitly allows both, but the subsequent parsing step translates the natural language into executable Python code, guaranteeing that the logical structure is preserved.
- **Assess plausibility** – because the LLM leverages both its pretrained knowledge and the few examples, many rules align with domain knowledge (e.g., “older patients have higher cardiovascular risk”).  Anomalous rules can be flagged for removal before the downstream linear model is trained.

Thus, the rule list serves as a **human‑readable explanation** of the model’s decision surface, directly exposing which feature combinations the LLM believes are decisive for each class.

---

## Inspecting Learned Weights to Rank Rule Importance

After rule extraction, FeatLLM converts each rule into a binary feature `z_k^i ∈ {0,1}^R` for class `k` (where `R = 10` in the standard configuration).  A **linear model without bias** then aggregates these binary features with a non‑negative weight vector `w_k`:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i = \operatorname{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big).
\]

Because the weights are constrained to be non‑negative, each rule can only increase the logit of its associated class; a weight of zero means the rule is effectively ignored.  The training objective is the standard cross‑entropy loss on the few‑shot training set, allowing the model to **learn the relative importance** of each rule from data.

Interpretability is achieved through the following steps:

1. **Extract the weight vector** after training.  For each class `k`, we obtain a list `[(rule_1, w_{k,1}), …, (rule_R, w_{k,R})]`.
2. **Rank rules** by descending weight.  Rules with larger weights contribute more strongly to the class probability whenever they are satisfied.
3. **Visualize** the distribution of weights (e.g., a bar chart) to see whether a few rules dominate or whether importance is spread evenly.
4. **Threshold** low‑weight rules (e.g., `w < 0.1`) to prune noisy or irrelevant conditions.  The paper shows that after this pruning, the proportion of useful features (AUROC > 0.5) increases, confirming that the linear model successfully filters out spurious rules.

Because the weights are **learned directly from labeled data**, they reflect an empirical assessment of how well each rule aligns with the true target.  Consequently, the combination of human‑written rules and data‑driven weighting yields a transparent, two‑stage explanation: “The model predicts class k because rule r, which encodes condition C, has a high weight and is satisfied by the input.”

---

## Case Studies: Medical and Financial Datasets

### Medical Diagnosis – Predicting Chronic Heart Failure

Consider the **Myocardial** dataset, where the task is to predict whether a patient suffers from chronic heart failure.  FeatLLM generates rules such as:

```
Rule 3 (Heart Failure):
   (ejection_fraction < 35) AND (serum_sodium < 135)
Rule 7 (Heart Failure):
   (age > 70) AND (max_heart_rate_achieved < 120)
```

After training on only four labeled patients, the learned weights might be:

| Rule | Weight |
|------|--------|
| Rule 3 | 0.84 |
| Rule 7 | 0.12 |
| Rule 1 (less relevant) | 0.03 |

The high weight on Rule 3 indicates that the model strongly relies on low ejection fraction combined with hyponatremia, a medically plausible marker of heart failure.  By inspecting the weight table, a clinician can verify that the model’s emphasis matches clinical intuition, or flag any surprising high‑weight rule for further review.

### Financial Risk – Predicting Credit Good/Bad

In the **Credit‑g** dataset, the goal is to classify an applicant as a good or bad credit risk.  Sample rules produced include:

```
Rule 2 (Bad Credit):
   (duration_months > 24) AND (credit_amount > 5000) AND (purpose = "new_car")
Rule 5 (Good Credit):
   (age > 30) AND (existing_credits <= 1) AND (checking_status = "moderate")
```

Training on eight examples yields weights such as:

| Rule | Class | Weight |
|------|-------|--------|
| Rule 2 | Bad | 0.71 |
| Rule 5 | Good | 0.65 |
| Rule 9 (Good) | 0.10 |

Here, both “Bad Credit” and “Good Credit” rules receive substantial weights, reflecting that the model uses *both* discriminative patterns rather than a single dominant heuristic.  A credit analyst can inspect Rule 2 and see that long loan durations combined with high amounts for a new car purchase are indeed associated with higher default risk, lending confidence to the model’s decision.

These case studies illustrate how **rule readability**, together with **weight ranking**, provides a clear, domain‑specific narrative for each prediction, enabling stakeholders to trust and act upon the model’s outputs.

---

## Link to Broader Interpretability Literature

FeatLLM’s interpretability approach aligns with several established strands of research:

1. **Rule‑Based Models** – Classical decision‑tree and rule‑list methods (e.g., CART, RIPPER) offer direct logical explanations.  FeatLLM differs by **leveraging an LLM** to synthesize rules that incorporate world knowledge, yet it retains the same form of interpretable logical conditions.

2. **Post‑hoc Feature Importance** – Methods such as SHAP or LIME assign importance scores to original features after training a black‑box model.  FeatLLM **integrates importance learning into the model** (the linear weight vector) rather than interpreting retrospectively, yielding inherently interpretable parameters.

3. **Concept Bottleneck Models** – Those approaches train a model to predict intermediate human‑understandable concepts before the final prediction.  In FeatLLM, the binary rule features act as **concepts** that are directly engineered by the LLM, and the downstream linear model only consumes these concepts.

4. **Self‑Explaining Neural Networks** – Recent work designs architectures that output explanations alongside predictions.  FeatLLM follows a similar philosophy but does so with **no neural training beyond a tiny linear layer**, making the explanations exact (the rules themselves) rather than approximations.

5. **Ensemble Interpretability** – Bagging and ensembling are traditionally viewed as reducing variance at the cost of interpretability.  FeatLLM circumvents this trade‑off by ensembling *rule‑generating* processes while still producing a **single aggregated weight vector** per ensemble member; the final prediction is the average of softmax probabilities, which can be decomposed back into per‑rule contributions across members.

Overall, FeatLLM demonstrates that **large language models can be harnessed as knowledge‑rich feature engineers** while preserving the clarity of rule‑based explanations.  By coupling LLM‑generated rules with a simple, non‑negative linear model, the framework provides a transparent decision pipeline that satisfies both predictive performance and the regulatory or ethical demands for explainability in sensitive domains.

# Extending FeatLLM

In this chapter we explore how the FeatLLM framework—originally designed for few‑shot tabular learning—can be broadened to address a wider variety of practical scenarios.  We begin by revisiting the core ideas of FeatLLM, then systematically discuss five promising research directions: (1) scaling the method to regimes where many labeled samples are available, (2) enriching the automatically engineered feature space beyond binary rules, (3) leveraging domain‑specific large language models (LLMs) or fine‑tuned variants, (4) integrating FeatLLM with other tabular pre‑training strategies, and (5) ensuring that the rules extracted from LLMs respect considerations of bias and fairness.  Throughout, we maintain close alignment with the mechanisms and design choices introduced in the original FeatLLM paper, expanding on them with rigorous intuition and concrete methodological proposals.

---

## Scaling to Larger Sample Regimes (Beyond Low‑Shot)

### Why scaling matters

FeatLLM was evaluated primarily in low‑shot settings, where only a handful of labeled instances (e.g., 4–8) are available for each task.  In many real‑world deployments, however, organizations possess dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of labeled records.  When abundant data are present, the dominant source of information shifts from the LLM’s prior knowledge to the empirical evidence in the dataset.  Consequently, the naive “extract‑rules‑once‑and‑fit‑a‑linear‑model” pipeline may under‑utilize the rich signal present in the data.

### Adjusting the rule extraction process

The first lever for scaling is to increase the **breadth** and **depth** of rule extraction.  In the original FeatLLM design, each LLM query generates a fixed set of ten binary rules per class.  When more data are available, we can relax this constraint in two complementary ways:

1. **Adaptive rule count.**  Instead of a static ten‑rule budget, we may let the LLM produce a variable number of candidate rules, guided by a simple heuristic such as “continue generating until the marginal gain in validation AUC falls below a small threshold.”  This dynamic termination criterion captures the intuition that, with more data, the model can reliably evaluate the marginal utility of each new rule.

2. **Iterative refinement.**  The LLM can be prompted to **refine** previously generated rules using the larger training set as a feedback signal.  A practical approach is to feed the model a short summary of the performance of each rule (e.g., its precision/recall on a held‑out subset) and ask it to suggest modifications—perhaps tightening a threshold (“age > 60” → “age > 62”) or adding conjunctions (“smoker = Yes AND cholesterol > 200”).

Both strategies increase the expressive power of the rule‑based feature set while preserving the low‑complexity downstream model.

### Learning more sophisticated downstream models

When the number of labeled examples grows, the linear model with non‑negative weights (Eq. (2) in the original paper) may become a bottleneck.  A natural extension is to replace the linear scorer with a **shallow neural network** or a **gradient‑boosted tree** that still respects the binary nature of the rule‑generated features.  Because the features are already high‑level abstractions of the raw data, a modest capaci­tied model can capture useful interactions without overfitting.  Importantly, the training pipeline remains lightweight: the downstream model is trained on the binary rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\) rather than the original raw feature matrix, preserving the computational benefits that motivated FeatLLM.

### Reducing the reliance on ensembling

Ensembling via bagging was introduced primarily to mitigate two issues: (i) the limited context window of the LLM when the raw table has many columns, and (ii) variability in rule quality across stochastic LLM queries.  In a regime with many labeled samples, we can **reduce the number of ensembles** because the downstream learner can now statistically weed out noisy rules.  A single, well‑designed LLM query (perhaps with a higher temperature to encourage diversity) may suffice, drastically cutting inference latency for rule generation.  Empirically, one would validate the trade‑off by measuring validation AUC as a function of ensemble size, stopping once marginal gains plateau.

## Generating Richer Feature Types (Embeddings, Interaction Terms)

### Beyond binary predicates

The original FeatLLM transforms each rule into a binary indicator: a sample either satisfies the rule or it does not.  This binary abstraction is attractive for interpretability, yet it discards nuanced information that could be valuable when richer data are present.  Several extensions can be considered:

1. **Quantitative confidence scores.**  Instead of a hard 0/1 decision, the LLM could output a **confidence** or **probability** that a rule applies, especially when the rule involves fuzzy predicates (e.g., “age is high”).  By parsing phrases like “likely older than 60” into a numeric estimate (e.g., 0.8), we obtain a feature in \([0,1]\) that captures gradations.

2. **Embedding‑based representations.**  For textual or categorical columns, the LLM can generate **semantic embeddings** of the column values conditioned on the rule context.  One practical pipeline is:
   - Prompt the LLM to produce a short natural‑language description of each value (e.g., “‘SMALL’ → ‘customer with low income’”).
   - Feed these descriptions to a frozen sentence‑embedding model (e.g., Sentence‑BERT) to obtain a dense vector.
   - Concatenate these vectors with the binary rule matrix, yielding a hybrid feature space that blends symbolic and distributed information.

3. **Interaction terms between rules.**  Many predictive relationships are not captured by a single rule but by the conjunction of multiple conditions (e.g., “age > 65 AND systolic blood pressure > 140”).  While the original prompt allows the LLM to output rules with logical operators, the binary parsing currently discards the internal logical structure.  By **preserving the logical tree**, we can compute interaction features explicitly: for a rule expressed as “A AND B”, we generate a feature equal to the product of the binary indicators for A and B.  This yields second‑order interaction terms without requiring the downstream model to discover them implicitly.

### Parsing challenges and mitigations

Introducing richer feature types heightens the complexity of parsing LLM outputs.  To maintain robustness:

- **Schema‑guided parsing:** Extend the response instruction to require each rule to be accompanied by a small metadata block, specifying the feature type (binary, confidence, embedding dimension) and any required parameters (e.g., threshold values).
- **LLM‑assisted code generation:** As in the original FeatLLM, we can ask the LLM itself to emit a Python function that, given a raw row, computes the enriched feature vector.  Because the LLM is trained on code, it can reliably handle mixed‑type return values, reducing the need for hand‑crafted parsers.

These extensions preserve the central philosophy of FeatLLM—leveraging LLM reasoning to produce human‑readable, high‑level features—while allowing the downstream model to exploit richer information.

## Incorporating Domain‑Specific LLMs or Fine‑Tuned Models

### Motivation

General‑purpose LLMs (e.g., GPT‑3.5) have been shown to contain substantial world knowledge, which FeatLLM exploits when extracting rules.  However, for highly specialized domains—such as genomics, finance, or legal contracts—general LLMs may lack the precise terminology or domain‑specific causal relationships needed to generate optimal rules.  Two complementary strategies can improve domain alignment:

1. **Domain‑specific foundation models.**  Recent releases include LLMs pre‑trained on domain‑curated corpora (e.g., BioGPT for biomedical text).  Replacing the generic backbone with such a model can instantly endow FeatLLM with more accurate priors, leading to higher‑quality rules.  The integration is straightforward: the same prompt template is sent to the domain‑specific API; only the model identifier changes.

2. **Few‑shot fine‑tuning (parameter‑efficient adaptation).**  Even when a domain‑specific model is unavailable, we can adapt a general LLM to a target field using techniques such as LoRA, IA3, or adapters.  Crucially, this adaptation can be performed **offline** on a modest collection of domain documents (e.g., a few thousand medical abstracts).  Once the adapter is trained, FeatLLM can invoke the adapted model without any additional per‑sample fine‑tuning, preserving the low‑cost inference advantage.

### Practical considerations

- **API accessibility.**  Some organization‑level LLMs expose only inference endpoints (as the original FeatLLM assumes).  In such cases, the fine‑tuning step must be performed on a locally hosted model, after which the adapted checkpoint can be hosted behind a private API.
- **Prompt length constraints.**  Domain‑specific LLMs often have token limits comparable to or smaller than generic models.  Hence, the **feature‑bagging** strategy described in the original paper remains essential: randomly sample a subset of columns per LLM query to stay within the context window.
- **Evaluation of domain fidelity.**  To verify that domain‑specific models improve rule quality, one can compute the **semantic similarity** between generated rule predicates and a curated set of domain knowledge statements (e.g., clinical guidelines).  Higher similarity scores would predict better downstream performance.

## Combining FeatLLM with Other Tabular Pre‑Training Methods

### Complementary objectives

Existing tabular pre‑training approaches—such as TabPFN, SCARF, and contrastive‑learning methods (e.g., SCARF, SCARF‑VIME)—focus on learning **generic representations** from large collections of unlabeled tables.  FeatLLM, by contrast, extracts **task‑specific symbolic features** using the LLM’s prior knowledge.  Merging these philosophies can yield a hybrid system that benefits from both broad representation power and fine‑grained rule‑based reasoning.

### Fusion strategies

1. **Feature concatenation.**  After FeatLLM produces its binary (or enriched) rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\), we can concatenate \(\mathbf{Z}\) with the latent embeddings generated by a pre‑trained tabular encoder (e.g., a transformer trained on millions of public tables).  The downstream classifier—whether linear, tree‑based, or shallow neural—receives the combined vector \([\mathbf{Z} \,;\, \mathbf{h}]\), where \(\mathbf{h}\) denotes the learned embedding.  This approach allows the model to fall back on generic patterns when the rule set is incomplete.

2 **Dual‑stage training.**  Train a **teacher‑student** arrangement: the teacher is a powerful pre‑trained model that provides soft labels (probability vectors) for the few‑shot training examples.  The student is the lightweight FeatLLM linear scorer.  By minimizing a KL‑divergence between student and teacher predictions (in addition to the standard cross‑entropy with true labels), the student can absorb knowledge from the teacher while retaining interpretability through its rule weights.

3. **Regularization via pre‑training loss.**  When learning the downstream linear weights \(\mathbf{w}\), introduce a regularization term that penalizes deviation from the distribution of embeddings produced by a tabular pre‑trainer.  For instance, let \(\mathbf{h}_i\) be the pre‑trained embedding of sample \(i\); we could add \(\lambda \| \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{Z}_i - g(\mathbf{h}_i) \|^2\), where \(g\) is a small neural map.  This encourages the rule‑based predictor to align with the broader representation space.

### Benefits and trade‑offs

- **Improved robustness.**  If the LLM generates a noisy rule for a particular feature, the generic embedding can compensate, reducing variance across ensembles.
- **Increased computational cost.**  Adding a pre‑trained encoder incurs extra inference time, but because embeddings can be pre‑computed for the entire dataset once (offline), the per‑prediction overhead remains modest.
- **Interpretability balance.**  While concatenation dilutes pure interpretability, the rule portion remains fully explainable; practitioners can inspect the contribution of each rule by examining its learned weight, independent of the opaque embedding part.

## Handling Bias and Fairness in LLM‑Derived Rules

### Sources of bias

The LLM’s prior knowledge reflects the distribution of its training corpus, which may contain societal biases (e.g., gender stereotypes, racial disparities).  When the LLM uses this knowledge to formulate rules, it may inadvertently embed harmful correlations—such as “age > 65 ⇒ higher risk of disease” without accounting for confounding socioeconomic factors.  Since FeatLLM’s downstream model directly weights these rules, biased predicates can translate into unfair predictions.

### Detection of biased rules

A systematic audit can be performed as follows:

1. **Identify protected attributes.**  Determine which columns correspond to race, gender, age, etc., either explicitly labeled or inferred from column names/descriptions.
2. **Measure rule dependence.**  For each generated rule \(r\), compute the **mutual information** \(I(r; A)\) between the binary outcome of the rule and each protected attribute \(A\).  High mutual information suggests the rule heavily depends on that attribute.
3. **Assess disparate impact.**  Use the **equalized odds** or **demographic parity** metrics on the predictions of the rule‑based model across subgroups defined by the protected attributes.

Rules flagged by these diagnostics can be either removed or down‑weighted.

### Mitigation techniques

- **Weight masking.**  During the linear weight training (Eq. (2) in the original paper), impose a mask \(\mathbf{m}\) where \(m_j = 0\) for any rule \(j\) that exceeds a bias threshold, effectively excluding that rule from influencing predictions.
- **Fairness‑aware regularization.**  Add a penalty term \(\beta \sum_j \lvert \mathbf{w}_j \rvert I(r_j; A)\) to the loss, encouraging the optimizer to assign lower magnitude to rules strongly correlated with protected attributes.
- **Counterfactual prompting.**  In the reasoning instruction, explicitly ask the LLM to “avoid using demographic information unless absolutely necessary.”  This can steer the LLM toward rules grounded in domain‑relevant biomedical or financial factors rather than socially sensitive ones.

### Human‑in‑the‑loop review

Given that FeatLLM’s rules are textual and human‑readable, an **expert audit** is feasible.  Stakeholders can examine the rule list before deployment, confirming that each predicate aligns with legal and ethical standards.  The ability to produce explicit rule text is a distinctive advantage over opaque end‑to‑end LLM inference, facilitating transparency and compliance.

## Summary

Extending FeatLLM beyond its original few‑shot, binary‑rule setting opens a fertile research landscape.  By **scaling** the rule extraction process and **adapting** the downstream learner, the framework can exploit abundant labeled data while retaining efficiency.  Enriching the engineered features with **confidence scores, embeddings, and interaction terms** provides a more expressive substrate for learning.  Harnessing **domain‑specific LLMs** or **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning** tailors the prior knowledge to specialized applications, and **integrating** FeatLLM with existing tabular pre‑training methods combines symbolic reasoning with learned representations.  Finally, a systematic pipeline for **bias detection and mitigation** ensures that the powerful knowledge of LLMs is deployed responsibly.

Collectively, these directions preserve the core insight of FeatLLM—that large language models excel as expert feature engineers—while adapting the methodology to the diverse constraints and opportunities encountered in real‑world tabular learning problems.

**Conclusion and Broader Impact**

# Summary of methodological advances  

The *FeatLLM* framework reconceptualizes the role of large language models (LLMs) in tabular prediction tasks.  Instead of treating an LLM as an end‑to‑end black‑box predictor that must be queried for every test instance, *FeatLLM* harnesses the LLM’s reasoning capacity during a single, inexpensive inference phase to **engineer binary features** that encode human‑readable decision rules.  These rules are derived through a carefully structured prompt that guides the model through two stages of reasoning:  

1. **Problem comprehension** – the LLM first extracts prior knowledge about how the described features relate to the target concept, relying solely on natural‑language descriptions and its world knowledge.  
2. **Rule synthesis** – using a handful of labeled exemplars, the LLM then formulates a fixed set of class‑specific logical conditions (e.g., “*age > 65* AND *cholesterol ≥ 240*”).  

The generated rules are parsed, via a secondary LLM call, into executable Python code that produces a binary vector for each sample, indicating whether the sample satisfies each rule.  A lightweight linear model with non‑negative weights consumes these vectors, learning the relative importance of each rule from the few labeled examples.  Crucially, *FeatLLM* applies **feature bagging** and **ensemble averaging** across multiple LLM queries, which (i) mitigates prompt‑size constraints for high‑dimensional tables, and (ii) improves robustness by exploiting the self‑consistency of LLM outputs.  

Empirically, the method obtains a **10 % average AUC improvement** over strong baselines such as TabLLM, STUNT, and conventional tree‑based learners in the low‑shot regime (as few as four labeled samples).  It also achieves inference latency comparable to classical models because the expensive LLM is consulted only during the offline feature‑generation stage.  The entire pipeline requires only API‑level access to an LLM and no gradient‑based fine‑tuning, thereby sidestepping the practical obstacles associated with proprietary model fine‑tuning and large prompt windows.

# Implications for real‑world low‑label domains  

Many high‑impact sectors—most notably **healthcare** and **finance**—operate under severe labeling constraints.  Expert annotation of electronic health records, diagnostic imaging reports, or credit‑risk assessments can be costly, time‑consuming, and subject to regulatory oversight.  *FeatLLM* offers a viable solution in such settings:

* **Data efficiency** – By leveraging the LLM’s encyclopedic knowledge (e.g., “older patients have higher cardiovascular risk”), *FeatLLM* can extract informative rules even when only a handful of labeled rows are available.  This reduces the need for costly data‑collection campaigns and enables rapid prototyping of predictive tools.  

* **Interpretability** – The binary rule‑features are directly human‑readable.  Clinicians can inspect a rule such as “*blood‑pressure > 140* AND *smoking = yes*” and immediately understand why a patient receives a high risk score.  Financial analysts can similarly trace a credit‑risk decision to a handful of regulatory‑relevant criteria.  This transparency is valuable for meeting domain‑specific compliance standards (e.g., FDA’s software as a medical device guidance or Basel III risk‑management requirements).  

* **Scalability to heterogeneous tables** – Because the method works with natural‑language feature names and definitions, it is agnostic to column types (numeric, categorical, ordinal) and can be applied across datasets with differing schemas without extensive custom engineering.  

* **Low operational cost** – The LLM is invoked a bounded number of times (e.g., 30 API calls for the experiments), after which inference reduces to a linear algebra operation.  This makes deployment on edge devices or within secure on‑premise environments feasible, where continuous LLM queries would be prohibitive.

# Potential risks from LLM bias and misinformation  

While *FeatLLM* capitalizes on the LLM’s pre‑training knowledge, that knowledge is not neutral.  Large corpora contain **societal biases**, historical inequities, and occasional factual errors.  When the LLM injects such biases into rule generation, downstream predictions may inherit them, leading to adverse outcomes such as:

* **Health disparities** – A rule derived from outdated medical literature might over‑emphasize age or race as risk factors, reinforcing existing inequities in diagnostic recommendations.  

* **Financial discrimination** – Rules that implicitly rely on zip‑code or occupation could produce disparate impact across protected groups, violating fair‑lending regulations.  

* **Misinformation propagation** – If the LLM mistakenly asserts a causal relationship (e.g., “*vaccination status predicts heart disease*”), the resulting rule could misguide decision‑making in critical settings.

Because *FeatLLM* does not retrain the LLM but merely extracts its outputs, traditional bias‑mitigation techniques (e.g., debiasing during pre‑training) are not directly applicable at deployment time.  The risk is amplified in low‑shot contexts, where the few supplied exemplars provide limited corrective signal to counteract erroneous priors.

# Guidelines for responsible deployment  

To harness the benefits of *FeatLLM* while mitigating the aforementioned risks, practitioners should adopt a **responsible‑AI lifecycle** that incorporates the following safeguards:

1. **Domain‑expert review of generated rules**  
   - Before committing the binary features to a production model, have subject‑matter experts audit each rule for plausibility, ethical acceptability, and compliance with local regulations.  
   - Reject or rewrite rules that reference protected attributes (e.g., gender, race) unless legally justified and mitigated.

2. **Diversity in prompt construction**  
   - Provide balanced feature descriptions and exemplar selections that represent the full spectrum of the population.  This reduces the chance that the LLM will over‑fit to spurious correlations present in a narrow set of examples.  

3. **Ensemble diversity diagnostics**  
   - Inspect the variance of rules across ensemble members.  Consistently appearing rules are likely to be trustworthy, whereas highly divergent rules may signal ambiguity in the LLM’s knowledge and warrant additional expert scrutiny.  

4. **Post‑hoc bias auditing**  
   - After training the linear weight layer, evaluate performance stratified by sensitive attributes.  Disparities in predicted probabilities or ROC curves should trigger a re‑examination of the underlying rule set.  

5. **Transparency and documentation**  
   - Record the exact prompts, LLM version, temperature settings, and the parsed Python code used for feature generation.  Maintaining this provenance enables reproducibility and facilitates external audits.  

6. **Controlled update schedule**  
   - Because LLM APIs evolve (new model releases, updated safety filters), schedule periodic re‑generation of rule sets and re‑evaluation of model performance, rather than assuming a one‑time generation will remain valid indefinitely.  

7. **Fallback mechanisms**  
   - In high‑risk applications (e.g., triaging emergency patients), retain a conventional, validated model as a safety net.  If *FeatLLM*’s confidence falls below a calibrated threshold, defer to the fallback model or request human review.  

By integrating these practices, organizations can leverage *FeatLLM*’s data‑efficient feature engineering capabilities while maintaining accountability, fairness, and trustworthiness in sensitive decision‑making pipelines.
