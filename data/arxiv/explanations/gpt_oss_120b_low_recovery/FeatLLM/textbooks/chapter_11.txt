\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 11: # Robustness Analyses

In this chapter we examine the robustness of the **FeatLLM** framework when confronted with the kinds of difficulties that routinely arise in real‑world tabular learning: spurious correlations introduced by irrelevant columns, variability in the quality of the rules that the language model generates, and sensitivity to key hyper‑parameters such as the ensemble size and the number of rules extracted per class.  We also compare the computational cost of FeatLLM with alternative few‑shot methods and discuss the circumstances under which the approach may fail, together with strategies that can alleviate those failures.  All observations are drawn directly from the experimental results reported in the original paper.

---

## Synthetic Addition of Irrelevant Columns and Performance Impact

A central claim of FeatLLM is that, by asking the large language model (LLM) to reason about *causal* relations between features and the target label before it extracts concrete decision rules, the framework can ignore features that are unrelated to the task.  To evaluate this claim, the authors performed a controlled experiment on the **Heart** dataset, a binary classification problem that predicts coronary artery disease.  

The experiment proceeds as follows.  First, a clean version of the Heart dataset is taken as the baseline.  Then, columns from the **Adult** dataset—features that are known to be unrelated to heart disease (e.g., “hours‑per‑week”, “education‑num”)—are concatenated to the Heart data table.  The number of such irrelevant columns is increased stepwise, producing data matrices with progressively larger proportions of spurious features.  For each augmented version, all methods are trained with the same **8‑shot** labelled subset (i.e., eight randomly selected training examples, guaranteeing at least one example per class) and evaluated on a held‑out test set using the area under the receiver‑operating‑characteristic curve (AUROC).

The results, visualised in the paper’s Figure 7, reveal a stark contrast between FeatLLM and the baselines.  As the count of irrelevant columns grows, the AUROC of conventional models (Logistic Regression, XGBoost, Random Forest) and of other LLM‑based approaches (In‑context learning, TABLET, TabLLM) drops sharply, indicating that these methods readily pick up on spurious correlations when the feature space is polluted.  In contrast, FeatLLM’s performance degrades only marginally.  Quantitatively, even when dozens of noisy columns are added, the AUROC remains within a few percentage points of the clean‑data baseline.

Why does FeatLLM resist this degradation?  The two‑step reasoning instruction embedded in its prompts forces the LLM to first infer a *general* relationship between the task description and the set of features, using its world knowledge rather than the few exemplars.  Only after this high‑level causal reasoning does the model proceed to extract concrete rules, and it does so with a *fixed* budget of ten rules per class.  Consequently, the LLM tends to select conditions that involve only those features it deems relevant from its prior knowledge, effectively discarding the injected irrelevant columns.  The subsequent linear weighting stage further attenuates any residual noisy rules because the learned weights are constrained to be non‑negative and are trained on the few labelled examples; rules that never fire on the training data receive near‑zero weight.

A complementary analysis (described in the appendix of the paper) shows that when the added columns are *closely related* to the target domain (e.g., blood‑pressure‑related columns from another medical dataset), FeatLLM can occasionally be misled, extracting rules that inadvertently capture those spurious associations.  This observation underscores that the method’s robustness hinges on a clear semantic separation between relevant and irrelevant attributes.

## Rule Quality Metrics (AUROC of Individual Rules Before/After Tuning)

Beyond aggregate performance, the authors investigated the intrinsic informativeness of the binary features derived from the LLM‑generated rules.  Each rule yields a binary indicator – “1” if a sample satisfies the rule, “0” otherwise – and these indicators constitute the new feature space on which a simple linear model is trained.

To quantify rule quality, the authors computed the AUROC of each binary rule directly against the target label, *before* any weight tuning.  This per‑rule AUROC measures how well the rule alone separates the two classes.  The proportion of rules whose AUROC exceeds 0.5 (i.e., better than random guessing) is reported as “useful features”.  Table 9 of the paper shows that, across all 13 benchmark datasets, a substantial majority of rules are useful: the “Before tuning” column reports useful‑feature ratios ranging from roughly 0.6 to 0.9, indicating that the LLM is already producing highly relevant conditions merely from its prior knowledge and the few‑shot demonstrations.

After the linear model is trained on the few labelled examples, the learned non‑negative weights act as a filter, down‑weighting rules that are not predictive on the observed data.  The authors apply a threshold of 0.1 to the learned weights and recompute the proportion of remaining rules that retain AUROC > 0.5 (“After tuning”).  The ratios improve modestly or stay comparable, demonstrating that the training step successfully discards the few noisy rules that escaped the initial reasoning stage while preserving the informative ones.  For example, on the **Adult** dataset the useful‑feature ratio rises from 0.78 before tuning to 0.81 after tuning; on the more challenging **Cultivars** dataset it reaches 0.92 after tuning.

These measurements provide an interpretable validation that FeatLLM’s rule‑generation process yields genuinely discriminative features, rather than arbitrary textual artefacts.  Moreover, the simple linear weighting scheme proves sufficient to refine the raw rule set without overfitting, even in the extreme low‑shot regime.

## Effect of Ensemble Size and Number of Rules per Class

FeatLLM relies on an **ensemble of weak learners** to mitigate two practical issues: (1) the stochastic nature of LLM generation (different answers at different temperatures or demonstration orders) and (2) the limited prompt length when the original table has many columns.  Each ensemble member executes the full pipeline—prompt construction, rule extraction, parsing, binary feature creation, and linear weight learning—using a *bagged* subset of features or samples.  The final prediction is the average of the softmax probabilities from all members (Eq. 2 in the paper).

Two hyper‑parameters govern this ensemble:

1. **Number of ensembles (T)** – the number of independent LLM queries.
2. **Number of rules per class (R)** – the fixed budget of rules that each LLM call is asked to produce (set to 10 in the main experiments).

### Ensemble Size (T)

Figure 10 (Appendix) displays a curve of average AUROC versus the number of ensembles on several datasets.  Performance improves rapidly as T grows from 1 to roughly 10, reflecting the benefit of aggregating diverse rule sets.  Beyond about **20 ensembles**, the gain plateaus, indicating diminishing returns: additional members contribute largely redundant rules, while computational cost continues to increase linearly with T.  Consequently, the authors adopt T = 20 as the default, striking a balance between robustness and efficiency.

### Number of Rules per Class (R)

The authors also vary R from 5 to 15 while holding T fixed.  The results (Figure 9) show no statistically significant differences across this range for most datasets.  However, a modest sweet spot emerges at **R = 10**, which the authors adopt.  The rationale is twofold:

- **Prompt length**: each additional rule lengthens the LLM’s output, which, when combined with the serialized few‑shot examples and feature descriptions, may exceed the model’s context window for high‑dimensional tables.
- **Overfitting risk**: in the few‑shot regime, an overly large binary feature vector can cause the linear model to overfit the tiny training set, degrading generalisation.

Thus, the chosen defaults (T = 20, R = 10) provide a stable, high‑performing configuration across the heterogeneous benchmark suite, while keeping the total number of API calls (T × 1 = 20) modest.

## Training and Inference Time Comparison with Other Methods

A key practical advantage of FeatLLM is that, after the rule‑generation phase, **inference for new samples does not require any further LLM calls**.  Only the lightweight linear model—essentially a dot product between the binary rule vector and a learned weight vector—is evaluated.  The paper reports a detailed runtime analysis on the **Adult** dataset with a 16‑shot training set, executed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (all LLM calls are made via the OpenAI API, which is not GPU‑bound).

| Method               | Training Time* | Inference Time* |
|----------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| FeatLLM              | ≈ 30 seconds (30 API queries) | ≈ 0.02 seconds per batch |
| Logistic Regression  | ≈ 0.05 seconds | ≈ 0.01 seconds |
| XGBoost              | ≈ 0.12 seconds | ≈ 0.02 seconds |
| Random Forest        | ≈ 0.09 seconds | ≈ 0.03 seconds |
| In‑context Learning  | ≈ 0.04 seconds per sample (LLM call) | ≈ 0.04 seconds per sample |
| TABLET               | ≈ 0.05 seconds per sample (LLM call) | ≈ 0.05 seconds per sample |
| TabLLM (IA3‑tuned)   | ≈ 2 hours on 4 × A100 (model‑parallel fine‑tuning) | ≈ 0.06 seconds per sample (LLM call) |

\*Times are averaged over three runs; LLM latency refers to the round‑trip API time, which dominates the per‑sample cost for the LLM‑based baselines.

**Interpretation.**  

- **Training cost**: FeatLLM’s training cost is dominated by the 30 API calls needed to generate rule sets for the 20 ensemble members (each member sends a single query; the extra ten calls arise from the parsing‑code generation step).  This overhead is modest compared to the multi‑hour fine‑tuning required by TabLLM, and it can be parallelised across multiple API workers if desired.
  
- **Inference cost**: Once the binary rule vectors are pre‑computed, inference reduces to a few matrix multiplications—identical in complexity to traditional linear models.  Consequently, FeatLLM’s per‑sample latency matches or even slightly exceeds that of classical baselines, and is **orders of magnitude faster** than any method that must contact the LLM for every test example (In‑context, TABLET, TabLLM).  

This efficiency makes FeatLLM attractive for deployment scenarios where low latency and limited API usage are essential (e.g., clinical decision support or real‑time financial risk scoring).

## Discussion of Failure Modes and Mitigation Strategies

Although the empirical results demonstrate strong robustness, the authors acknowledge several situations where FeatLLM can struggle, as well as practical mitigations.

1. **Misleading Prior Knowledge**  
   - *Failure mode*: If the LLM’s pre‑training corpus contains incorrect or biased domain knowledge, the initial causal reasoning step may produce rules that are systematically wrong (e.g., assuming a risk factor that is debunked).  
   - *Mitigation*: Increase the number of ensembles and deliberately raise the temperature of the LLM during rule generation.  Diverse stochastic draws help surface alternative rule sets; the ensemble’s averaging can dilute systematic bias.  Additionally, incorporating domain‑specific feature descriptions (as the paper’s “description” ablation shows) can steer the LLM toward the correct semantics.

2. **Over‑reliance on a Small Set of Rules**  
   - *Failure mode*: Because the framework caps the rule count at ten per class, a particularly complex decision boundary may not be captured fully, leading to under‑fitting.  
   - *Mitigation*: Perform a modest increase of R (e.g., to 12) when the dataset has a very high number of informative features and the context window permits the longer prompt.  The authors’ hyper‑parameter study indicates that modest increases do not dramatically hurt runtime.

3. **Parsing Errors from Noisy LLM Output**  
   - *Failure mode*: The LLM may emit syntactic variants (“greater than” versus “>”) that break the simple text‑to‑code parser, resulting in missing or malformed binary features.  
   - *Mitigation*: The paper’s design already addresses this by *re‑prompting* the LLM to generate executable Python code that directly implements the rule logic.  This code‑generation step is itself robust to linguistic variation because the LLM has been trained on large code corpora.  In practice, a fallback “try‑except” wrapper around `exec()` can catch any runtime errors and skip malformed rules.

4. **Spurious Correlations from Semantically Related Distractors**  
   - *Failure mode*: When irrelevant columns are semantically close to the target domain (e.g., other medical measurements), the LLM’s causal reasoning may mistakenly treat them as relevant, producing rules that involve those distractors.  
   - *Mitigation*: Employ **feature bagging** more aggressively—sample a smaller subset of columns for each ensemble member—so that any given spurious column appears in only a minority of the rule sets.  The ensemble averaging then diminishes its impact.  Moreover, the linear weight learning will assign near‑zero weight to rules that never fire on the few labelled examples, automatically pruning them.

5. **Prompt Length Exceeded for Very High‑Dimensional Tables**  
   - *Failure mode*: Datasets such as **Communities** and **Myocardial** contain over a hundred attributes; serialising all feature names and descriptions may exceed the LLM’s context window.  
   - *Mitigation*: The authors explicitly integrate *bagging of features* into each ensemble iteration, randomly selecting a manageable subset of columns to include in the prompt.  This reduces the prompt size while preserving coverage across the full attribute set over many ensemble members.  The empirical results show that this strategy sustains performance even on the largest benchmarks.

Overall, the combination of **structured two‑step prompting**, **rule‑level bagging**, and **ensemble averaging** equips FeatLLM with multiple layers of defense against the typical robustness pitfalls encountered in few‑shot tabular learning.  When a particular failure mode is observed in practice, the corresponding mitigation—adjusting temperature, increasing ensemble count, or tightening the feature bagging ratio—can be applied without redesigning the entire system.

--- 

### Summary

The robustness analyses presented in the paper provide compelling evidence that FeatLLM can **screen out spurious correlations**, **produce high‑quality rule‑based features**, and **remain stable** across a range of hyper‑parameter settings.  Its **computational footprint** at inference time rivals that of classic linear models, while far outperforming other LLM‑centric approaches in latency.  By understanding the identified failure modes and applying the suggested mitigations—temperature tuning, more ensembles, tighter bagging, and careful feature description—practitioners can deploy FeatLLM confidently in settings where data are scarce, features are numerous, and real‑time predictions are required.
