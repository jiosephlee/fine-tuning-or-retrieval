\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 13: # Extending FeatLLM

In this chapter we explore how the FeatLLM framework—originally designed for few‑shot tabular learning—can be broadened to address a wider variety of practical scenarios.  We begin by revisiting the core ideas of FeatLLM, then systematically discuss five promising research directions: (1) scaling the method to regimes where many labeled samples are available, (2) enriching the automatically engineered feature space beyond binary rules, (3) leveraging domain‑specific large language models (LLMs) or fine‑tuned variants, (4) integrating FeatLLM with other tabular pre‑training strategies, and (5) ensuring that the rules extracted from LLMs respect considerations of bias and fairness.  Throughout, we maintain close alignment with the mechanisms and design choices introduced in the original FeatLLM paper, expanding on them with rigorous intuition and concrete methodological proposals.

---

## Scaling to Larger Sample Regimes (Beyond Low‑Shot)

### Why scaling matters

FeatLLM was evaluated primarily in low‑shot settings, where only a handful of labeled instances (e.g., 4–8) are available for each task.  In many real‑world deployments, however, organizations possess dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of labeled records.  When abundant data are present, the dominant source of information shifts from the LLM’s prior knowledge to the empirical evidence in the dataset.  Consequently, the naive “extract‑rules‑once‑and‑fit‑a‑linear‑model” pipeline may under‑utilize the rich signal present in the data.

### Adjusting the rule extraction process

The first lever for scaling is to increase the **breadth** and **depth** of rule extraction.  In the original FeatLLM design, each LLM query generates a fixed set of ten binary rules per class.  When more data are available, we can relax this constraint in two complementary ways:

1. **Adaptive rule count.**  Instead of a static ten‑rule budget, we may let the LLM produce a variable number of candidate rules, guided by a simple heuristic such as “continue generating until the marginal gain in validation AUC falls below a small threshold.”  This dynamic termination criterion captures the intuition that, with more data, the model can reliably evaluate the marginal utility of each new rule.

2. **Iterative refinement.**  The LLM can be prompted to **refine** previously generated rules using the larger training set as a feedback signal.  A practical approach is to feed the model a short summary of the performance of each rule (e.g., its precision/recall on a held‑out subset) and ask it to suggest modifications—perhaps tightening a threshold (“age > 60” → “age > 62”) or adding conjunctions (“smoker = Yes AND cholesterol > 200”).

Both strategies increase the expressive power of the rule‑based feature set while preserving the low‑complexity downstream model.

### Learning more sophisticated downstream models

When the number of labeled examples grows, the linear model with non‑negative weights (Eq. (2) in the original paper) may become a bottleneck.  A natural extension is to replace the linear scorer with a **shallow neural network** or a **gradient‑boosted tree** that still respects the binary nature of the rule‑generated features.  Because the features are already high‑level abstractions of the raw data, a modest capaci­tied model can capture useful interactions without overfitting.  Importantly, the training pipeline remains lightweight: the downstream model is trained on the binary rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\) rather than the original raw feature matrix, preserving the computational benefits that motivated FeatLLM.

### Reducing the reliance on ensembling

Ensembling via bagging was introduced primarily to mitigate two issues: (i) the limited context window of the LLM when the raw table has many columns, and (ii) variability in rule quality across stochastic LLM queries.  In a regime with many labeled samples, we can **reduce the number of ensembles** because the downstream learner can now statistically weed out noisy rules.  A single, well‑designed LLM query (perhaps with a higher temperature to encourage diversity) may suffice, drastically cutting inference latency for rule generation.  Empirically, one would validate the trade‑off by measuring validation AUC as a function of ensemble size, stopping once marginal gains plateau.

## Generating Richer Feature Types (Embeddings, Interaction Terms)

### Beyond binary predicates

The original FeatLLM transforms each rule into a binary indicator: a sample either satisfies the rule or it does not.  This binary abstraction is attractive for interpretability, yet it discards nuanced information that could be valuable when richer data are present.  Several extensions can be considered:

1. **Quantitative confidence scores.**  Instead of a hard 0/1 decision, the LLM could output a **confidence** or **probability** that a rule applies, especially when the rule involves fuzzy predicates (e.g., “age is high”).  By parsing phrases like “likely older than 60” into a numeric estimate (e.g., 0.8), we obtain a feature in \([0,1]\) that captures gradations.

2. **Embedding‑based representations.**  For textual or categorical columns, the LLM can generate **semantic embeddings** of the column values conditioned on the rule context.  One practical pipeline is:
   - Prompt the LLM to produce a short natural‑language description of each value (e.g., “‘SMALL’ → ‘customer with low income’”).
   - Feed these descriptions to a frozen sentence‑embedding model (e.g., Sentence‑BERT) to obtain a dense vector.
   - Concatenate these vectors with the binary rule matrix, yielding a hybrid feature space that blends symbolic and distributed information.

3. **Interaction terms between rules.**  Many predictive relationships are not captured by a single rule but by the conjunction of multiple conditions (e.g., “age > 65 AND systolic blood pressure > 140”).  While the original prompt allows the LLM to output rules with logical operators, the binary parsing currently discards the internal logical structure.  By **preserving the logical tree**, we can compute interaction features explicitly: for a rule expressed as “A AND B”, we generate a feature equal to the product of the binary indicators for A and B.  This yields second‑order interaction terms without requiring the downstream model to discover them implicitly.

### Parsing challenges and mitigations

Introducing richer feature types heightens the complexity of parsing LLM outputs.  To maintain robustness:

- **Schema‑guided parsing:** Extend the response instruction to require each rule to be accompanied by a small metadata block, specifying the feature type (binary, confidence, embedding dimension) and any required parameters (e.g., threshold values).
- **LLM‑assisted code generation:** As in the original FeatLLM, we can ask the LLM itself to emit a Python function that, given a raw row, computes the enriched feature vector.  Because the LLM is trained on code, it can reliably handle mixed‑type return values, reducing the need for hand‑crafted parsers.

These extensions preserve the central philosophy of FeatLLM—leveraging LLM reasoning to produce human‑readable, high‑level features—while allowing the downstream model to exploit richer information.

## Incorporating Domain‑Specific LLMs or Fine‑Tuned Models

### Motivation

General‑purpose LLMs (e.g., GPT‑3.5) have been shown to contain substantial world knowledge, which FeatLLM exploits when extracting rules.  However, for highly specialized domains—such as genomics, finance, or legal contracts—general LLMs may lack the precise terminology or domain‑specific causal relationships needed to generate optimal rules.  Two complementary strategies can improve domain alignment:

1. **Domain‑specific foundation models.**  Recent releases include LLMs pre‑trained on domain‑curated corpora (e.g., BioGPT for biomedical text).  Replacing the generic backbone with such a model can instantly endow FeatLLM with more accurate priors, leading to higher‑quality rules.  The integration is straightforward: the same prompt template is sent to the domain‑specific API; only the model identifier changes.

2. **Few‑shot fine‑tuning (parameter‑efficient adaptation).**  Even when a domain‑specific model is unavailable, we can adapt a general LLM to a target field using techniques such as LoRA, IA3, or adapters.  Crucially, this adaptation can be performed **offline** on a modest collection of domain documents (e.g., a few thousand medical abstracts).  Once the adapter is trained, FeatLLM can invoke the adapted model without any additional per‑sample fine‑tuning, preserving the low‑cost inference advantage.

### Practical considerations

- **API accessibility.**  Some organization‑level LLMs expose only inference endpoints (as the original FeatLLM assumes).  In such cases, the fine‑tuning step must be performed on a locally hosted model, after which the adapted checkpoint can be hosted behind a private API.
- **Prompt length constraints.**  Domain‑specific LLMs often have token limits comparable to or smaller than generic models.  Hence, the **feature‑bagging** strategy described in the original paper remains essential: randomly sample a subset of columns per LLM query to stay within the context window.
- **Evaluation of domain fidelity.**  To verify that domain‑specific models improve rule quality, one can compute the **semantic similarity** between generated rule predicates and a curated set of domain knowledge statements (e.g., clinical guidelines).  Higher similarity scores would predict better downstream performance.

## Combining FeatLLM with Other Tabular Pre‑Training Methods

### Complementary objectives

Existing tabular pre‑training approaches—such as TabPFN, SCARF, and contrastive‑learning methods (e.g., SCARF, SCARF‑VIME)—focus on learning **generic representations** from large collections of unlabeled tables.  FeatLLM, by contrast, extracts **task‑specific symbolic features** using the LLM’s prior knowledge.  Merging these philosophies can yield a hybrid system that benefits from both broad representation power and fine‑grained rule‑based reasoning.

### Fusion strategies

1. **Feature concatenation.**  After FeatLLM produces its binary (or enriched) rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\), we can concatenate \(\mathbf{Z}\) with the latent embeddings generated by a pre‑trained tabular encoder (e.g., a transformer trained on millions of public tables).  The downstream classifier—whether linear, tree‑based, or shallow neural—receives the combined vector \([\mathbf{Z} \,;\, \mathbf{h}]\), where \(\mathbf{h}\) denotes the learned embedding.  This approach allows the model to fall back on generic patterns when the rule set is incomplete.

2 **Dual‑stage training.**  Train a **teacher‑student** arrangement: the teacher is a powerful pre‑trained model that provides soft labels (probability vectors) for the few‑shot training examples.  The student is the lightweight FeatLLM linear scorer.  By minimizing a KL‑divergence between student and teacher predictions (in addition to the standard cross‑entropy with true labels), the student can absorb knowledge from the teacher while retaining interpretability through its rule weights.

3. **Regularization via pre‑training loss.**  When learning the downstream linear weights \(\mathbf{w}\), introduce a regularization term that penalizes deviation from the distribution of embeddings produced by a tabular pre‑trainer.  For instance, let \(\mathbf{h}_i\) be the pre‑trained embedding of sample \(i\); we could add \(\lambda \| \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{Z}_i - g(\mathbf{h}_i) \|^2\), where \(g\) is a small neural map.  This encourages the rule‑based predictor to align with the broader representation space.

### Benefits and trade‑offs

- **Improved robustness.**  If the LLM generates a noisy rule for a particular feature, the generic embedding can compensate, reducing variance across ensembles.
- **Increased computational cost.**  Adding a pre‑trained encoder incurs extra inference time, but because embeddings can be pre‑computed for the entire dataset once (offline), the per‑prediction overhead remains modest.
- **Interpretability balance.**  While concatenation dilutes pure interpretability, the rule portion remains fully explainable; practitioners can inspect the contribution of each rule by examining its learned weight, independent of the opaque embedding part.

## Handling Bias and Fairness in LLM‑Derived Rules

### Sources of bias

The LLM’s prior knowledge reflects the distribution of its training corpus, which may contain societal biases (e.g., gender stereotypes, racial disparities).  When the LLM uses this knowledge to formulate rules, it may inadvertently embed harmful correlations—such as “age > 65 ⇒ higher risk of disease” without accounting for confounding socioeconomic factors.  Since FeatLLM’s downstream model directly weights these rules, biased predicates can translate into unfair predictions.

### Detection of biased rules

A systematic audit can be performed as follows:

1. **Identify protected attributes.**  Determine which columns correspond to race, gender, age, etc., either explicitly labeled or inferred from column names/descriptions.
2. **Measure rule dependence.**  For each generated rule \(r\), compute the **mutual information** \(I(r; A)\) between the binary outcome of the rule and each protected attribute \(A\).  High mutual information suggests the rule heavily depends on that attribute.
3. **Assess disparate impact.**  Use the **equalized odds** or **demographic parity** metrics on the predictions of the rule‑based model across subgroups defined by the protected attributes.

Rules flagged by these diagnostics can be either removed or down‑weighted.

### Mitigation techniques

- **Weight masking.**  During the linear weight training (Eq. (2) in the original paper), impose a mask \(\mathbf{m}\) where \(m_j = 0\) for any rule \(j\) that exceeds a bias threshold, effectively excluding that rule from influencing predictions.
- **Fairness‑aware regularization.**  Add a penalty term \(\beta \sum_j \lvert \mathbf{w}_j \rvert I(r_j; A)\) to the loss, encouraging the optimizer to assign lower magnitude to rules strongly correlated with protected attributes.
- **Counterfactual prompting.**  In the reasoning instruction, explicitly ask the LLM to “avoid using demographic information unless absolutely necessary.”  This can steer the LLM toward rules grounded in domain‑relevant biomedical or financial factors rather than socially sensitive ones.

### Human‑in‑the‑loop review

Given that FeatLLM’s rules are textual and human‑readable, an **expert audit** is feasible.  Stakeholders can examine the rule list before deployment, confirming that each predicate aligns with legal and ethical standards.  The ability to produce explicit rule text is a distinctive advantage over opaque end‑to‑end LLM inference, facilitating transparency and compliance.

## Summary

Extending FeatLLM beyond its original few‑shot, binary‑rule setting opens a fertile research landscape.  By **scaling** the rule extraction process and **adapting** the downstream learner, the framework can exploit abundant labeled data while retaining efficiency.  Enriching the engineered features with **confidence scores, embeddings, and interaction terms** provides a more expressive substrate for learning.  Harnessing **domain‑specific LLMs** or **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning** tailors the prior knowledge to specialized applications, and **integrating** FeatLLM with existing tabular pre‑training methods combines symbolic reasoning with learned representations.  Finally, a systematic pipeline for **bias detection and mitigation** ensures that the powerful knowledge of LLMs is deployed responsibly.

Collectively, these directions preserve the core insight of FeatLLM—that large language models excel as expert feature engineers—while adapting the methodology to the diverse constraints and opportunities encountered in real‑world tabular learning problems.
