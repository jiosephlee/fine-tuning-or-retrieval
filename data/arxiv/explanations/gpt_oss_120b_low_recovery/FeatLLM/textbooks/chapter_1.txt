\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Few‑Shot Tabular Learning and LLMs  

Few‑shot learning—the ability to train a model that performs well after seeing only a handful of labeled examples—has become a central focus of modern machine learning research.  While the problem has been studied extensively for image and text data, tabular data presents a distinct set of difficulties that make the few‑shot regime especially challenging.  At the same time, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) with strong in‑context learning capabilities offers a new way to inject external knowledge into tabular tasks without the need for extensive fine‑tuning.  This chapter surveys the core challenges of few‑shot tabular learning, outlines the relevant properties of LLMs, reviews prior attempts to combine the two, and highlights the limitations that motivate the development of more efficient frameworks such as **FeatLLM**.

## Why Tabular Data Is Hard in Few‑Shot Regimes  

Tabular datasets are ubiquitous in fields such as finance, healthcare, and social science, but their structure differs markedly from the regular grids of images or the sequential nature of text.  Each column (or *feature*) may be of a different type—continuous, ordinal, categorical, or even free‑form text—requiring bespoke preprocessing pipelines.  Moreover, the relationships among features are often heterogeneous: some columns may interact multiplicatively, others may be conditionally independent, and yet others may be redundant or noisy.  When only a few labeled rows are available, learning these complex relationships becomes statistically fragile.

In a formal setting, let a tabular dataset consist of $N$ samples $\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{i},y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with $\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, where $d$ is the number of columns.  In the $k$‑shot regime we draw a random subset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ containing only $k\ll N$ labeled examples.  Classical estimators such as logistic regression or decision trees rely on sufficient sample coverage of each feature’s distribution; with $k$ often smaller than $d$, the empirical covariance matrix becomes singular, leading to overfitting or even complete failure to converge.  This phenomenon is often described as the “curse of dimensionality” in low‑sample settings: the variance of any estimator grows roughly as $\mathcal{O}(d/k)$, so that as $d$ increases the amount of data required to achieve a fixed level of accuracy quickly becomes prohibitive.

In addition to statistical concerns, practical obstacles arise.  Many tabular benchmarks contain dozens or hundreds of columns (e.g., the *Communities* and *Myocardial* datasets have over one hundred attributes).  Even when the raw number of rows is moderate, the heterogeneity of the data—mixed numeric and categorical domains, missing values, and domain‑specific encoding—makes it difficult to design generic data augmentation or self‑supervised objectives that improve sample efficiency.  Consequently, researchers have turned toward external sources of inductive bias, such as unlabeled data, domain ontologies, or, more recently, the knowledge embedded in large language models.

## Overview of Large Language Models and In‑Context Learning  

Large language models are deep neural networks, typically transformer‑based, trained on massive corpora of natural language text.  Their training objective—next‑token prediction—forces the model to learn statistical regularities of language, factual knowledge, and even rudimentary reasoning abilities.  Modern LLMs such as GPT‑3.5, PaLM, or LLaMA contain billions of parameters and can perform *in‑context learning*: given a prompt that includes a description of a task together with a few labeled examples (the *demonstrations*), the model can infer the mapping from inputs to outputs for new queries without any parameter updates.

Mathematically, let $\mathcal{P}$ denote a prompt consisting of a concatenation of a task description $T$, $k$ demonstration pairs $\{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^{k}$, and a new query $x_{*}$.  The LLM defines a conditional distribution over possible completions:  

\[
p(y_{*}\mid x_{*},\mathcal{P}) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_{*}|} p\bigl(y_{*}^{(t)} \mid y_{*}^{(<t)}, x_{*}, \mathcal{P}\bigr),
\]

where $y_{*}^{(t)}$ denotes the $t$‑th token of the answer.  By sampling from this distribution (often with temperature $>0$ to encourage diversity), the model produces a prediction that reflects both the supplied demonstrations and the full breadth of its pre‑training knowledge.  This ability to *reason* from a few examples makes LLMs attractive for few‑shot settings where conventional supervised learning would otherwise require many more labeled samples.

The interplay between the prompt length and the model’s context window (typically a few thousand tokens) is crucial: if the serialized representation of a tabular row is too long, the LLM may truncate important information or suffer performance degradation.  Consequently, careful prompt engineering—concise yet informative descriptions of features, systematic ordering of demonstrations, and explicit reasoning instructions—has become a central research theme.

## Prior Work Using LLMs for Tabular Prediction  

The first wave of research that attempted to bridge LLMs and tabular learning focused on **language‑interfaced tabular learning**.  The basic idea is to *serialize* a row of a table into a textual description, prepend a natural‑language task statement (e.g., “Predict whether the loan will default”), and feed the resulting prompt to the LLM.  Early systems such as **Lift** and **AnyPredict** demonstrated that, with a few demonstrations, an LLM could achieve competitive accuracy on some benchmark classification tasks.  

Two main methodological families emerged:

1. **In‑Context Learning Approaches** – These methods rely solely on prompting.  By adding a handful of labeled rows to the prompt, the LLM directly outputs class predictions for the remaining rows.  The *TABLET* framework further augments the prompt with rule‑sets and prototype examples generated by a separate classifier, striving to improve the quality of the LLM’s reasoning.  

2. **Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning** – Recognizing that a pure prompting strategy may be limited by context‑window size, some works introduced lightweight adapters (e.g., LoRA or IA3) that fine‑tune only a small subset of the LLM’s weights on the few available samples.  **TabLLM** exemplifies this direction, achieving higher accuracy but requiring gradient‑based updates and, crucially, access to the model’s parameters.

Both families share a common trait: the LLM is invoked *per sample* at inference time.  For each new row, the system constructs a fresh prompt (often incorporating the same few demonstrations) and queries the LLM to obtain a prediction.  While this yields strong performance in certain low‑dimensional settings, it incurs substantial computational overhead: each inference call involves a full forward pass through a multi‑billion‑parameter network, resulting in latency unsuitable for real‑time applications.

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Approaches  

Despite their promise, the current LLM‑based tabular methods suffer from several practical and methodological drawbacks that hinder adoption in real‑world pipelines.

1. **Inference Cost and Latency** – Because the LLM must be queried for every test instance, the total number of API calls scales linearly with the dataset size.  Even with high‑throughput inference servers, the per‑sample latency can be orders of magnitude higher than traditional models such as logistic regression or gradient‑boosted trees.  Moreover, many state‑of‑the‑art LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4) are only accessible via paid APIs, making large‑scale deployment financially prohibitive.

2. **Prompt Length Constraints** – The context window of most LLMs is limited (e.g., 4,096 tokens for GPT‑3.5).  When a dataset contains many columns, the serialized description of a single row may already consume a substantial fraction of this budget, leaving little room for demonstrations or reasoning instructions.  As a result, performance often degrades sharply on high‑dimensional tables, as observed on the *Communities* and *Myocardial* datasets.

3. **Dependence on Model Fine‑Tuning** – Parameter‑efficient tuning methods such as LoRA improve accuracy but require gradient updates and sometimes multi‑GPU environments.  This requirement is incompatible with API‑only access to proprietary LLMs, which typically expose only inference endpoints.  Consequently, many practitioners are unable to apply these methods to the most powerful LLMs that lack publicly released checkpoints.

4. **Sensitivity to Prompt Design** – The order of demonstration examples, the phrasing of the task description, and the inclusion of feature definitions can dramatically affect the LLM’s output.  While systematic prompt engineering can mitigate this sensitivity, it adds an extra layer of complexity and reduces reproducibility across datasets.

5. **Limited Exploitability of Prior Knowledge** – Existing approaches treat the LLM as a black‑box predictor, thereby underusing the rich factual and causal knowledge embedded during pre‑training.  The LLM could, in principle, generate *rules* or *feature transformations* that capture domain‑specific relationships (e.g., “age > 65 increases heart‑failure risk”).  However, most current pipelines never extract or reuse such intermediate artifacts, missing an opportunity for more interpretable and efficient downstream models.

These limitations motivate a shift from the conventional paradigm of end‑to‑end LLM prediction toward a *feature‑engineering* mindset.  By prompting the LLM to output explicit decision rules, one can compile these rules into binary features, train a lightweight classifier on the resulting representation, and perform inference without further LLM calls.  This is precisely the insight behind the **FeatLLM** framework, which combines in‑context learning, rule extraction, and ensemble bagging to obtain high‑quality few‑shot tabular models while dramatically reducing inference cost and overcoming prompt size restrictions.
