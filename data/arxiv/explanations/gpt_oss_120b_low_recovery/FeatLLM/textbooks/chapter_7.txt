\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 7: **Ensembling and Feature Bagging**

In this chapter we examine the ensemble machinery that underpins the FeatLLM framework.  While a single large‑language model (LLM) query can already produce a set of interpretable rules, relying on one such query is fragile: the generated rules may be incomplete, may contain noise, and the query itself may be limited by the prompt‑size constraints of the underlying model.  By repeating the rule‑extraction process many times, each with a slightly different view of the data, FeatLLM converts many weak rule generators into a strong, robust predictor.  We explore why this is necessary, how bagging of features and instances is performed, how temperature‑controlled randomness yields diverse rule sets, how the predictions from all trials are combined, and what the trade‑offs are in terms of computational cost and prompt length.

---

### 1.  Why a Single LLM Query May Produce Incomplete or Noisy Rules

The rule‑generation stage in FeatLLM asks the LLM to articulate, in natural language, conditions that characterize each class.  Several phenomena can limit the quality of the rule set returned from a single query:

1. **Limited Context Window** – LLMs have a maximal token budget (often a few thousand tokens).  When a tabular dataset contains many columns, the serialized description of all features, together with the few‑shot examples, can already occupy a large fraction of this window.  Consequently the model may be forced to truncate or ignore some columns, leading to rules that are blind to those omitted features.

2. **Prompt‑Induced Bias** – The order in which few‑shot examples appear, as well as the ordering of feature descriptions, can steer the model toward particular patterns.  Empirically, re‑ordering demonstrations changes the generated rules, indicating that a single deterministic prompt yields only one of many plausible rule sets.

3. **Stochastic Generation** – Even with a fixed prompt, the LLM’s sampling process (controlled by the temperature parameter) introduces randomness.  At low temperature the model often repeats the same high‑probability answer; at higher temperature it may explore alternative phrasings or alternative logical branches.  This stochasticity can be beneficial for diversity but also produces occasional nonsensical or overly specific rules.

4. **Hallucination and Syntax Variability** – LLMs sometimes fabricate conditions that do not correspond to any real column, or they may phrase a relational operator in an unexpected way (“greater than” instead of “>”).  Such noise can be difficult to parse automatically and, if left unchecked, would degrade downstream performance.

Because each of these issues can affect a single rule set, FeatLLM does not trust any one LLM query.  Instead, it treats the rule generator as a weak learner and harnesses ensemble techniques to average out errors, much like bagging in classical decision‑tree ensembles.

---

### 2.  Bagging of Features and/or Instances for Each Ensemble Member

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) is a simple yet powerful way to introduce variation among ensemble members.  In the context of FeatLLM, bagging is applied to two distinct dimensions:

#### 2.1 Feature‑Level Bagging  

When the number of columns $d$ is large relative to the prompt length, we randomly select a **subset of features** to include in the prompt for a given trial.  Formally, for trial $t$, we draw a subset $F^{(t)} \subseteq F$ of size $d_{b}$ (e.g., $d_{b}=30$ when $d=120$).  The basic‑information portion of the prompt then contains only the descriptions of features in $F^{(t)}$, together with their values in the few‑shot examples.  The LLM can therefore devote its full reasoning capacity to this manageable slice of the dataset, producing rules that involve only the chosen features.  Across the $T$ trials, different slices are explored, ensuring that eventually **every column has a chance to be examined** and possibly turned into a rule.

#### 2.2 Instance‑Level Bagging  

Even when the feature set is modest, the few‑shot examples themselves can be varied.  For each trial we randomly **sample without replacement** a subset of the available $k$ labeled instances (often the entire set, but possibly a bootstrap sample when $k$ is larger).  This changes the concrete evidence that the LLM sees when it moves from the “causal reasoning” step to the “rule‑deduction” step.  Different examples may highlight different attribute‑label relationships, prompting the LLM to emit alternative rules.

Both levels of bagging serve two purposes:

* They **reduce the effective prompt length**, allowing FeatLLM to handle datasets that would otherwise exceed the model’s token limit.
* They **inject stochasticity** into the rule‑generation process, which, when aggregated, smooths out idiosyncratic mistakes.

---

### 3.  Temperature‑Controlled Diversity of Generated Rule Sets

The **temperature** parameter $τ$ in the LLM’s sampling distribution governs how much probability mass is allocated to lower‑likelihood tokens.  A higher temperature (e.g., $τ=0.7$) makes the model more exploratory, yielding a wider variety of rule expressions across trials; a lower temperature (e.g., $τ=0.2$) makes the output more deterministic.

FeatLLM deliberately sets a **moderately high temperature** (commonly $τ=0.5$) for rule generation.  This choice balances two competing forces:

* **Diversity** – By allowing the model to sometimes pick a less‑probable token, distinct logical structures (different thresholds, alternative conjunctions, or even alternative feature selections) appear in the rule pool, enriching the ensemble’s hypothesis space.
* **Reliability** – Excessively high temperatures can lead to incoherent or contradictory rules, raising the parsing burden and potentially injecting noise.  Empirically, a temperature around 0.5 produces sufficiently varied yet still parseable rule sets.

Because each trial also uses its own random seed (affecting both sampling of features/instances and the stochastic decoding), the ensemble ends up with a **collection of $T$ rule corpora** that—while overlapping in spirit—exhibit enough variation to make the final prediction robust.

---

### 4.  Aggregating Predictions Across $T$ Trials

After a trial produces a rule set, the rules are parsed into **binary features**.  For class $k$ and trial $t$, let the binary vector be $\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{R}$, where $R$ is the fixed number of rules per class (e.g., $R=10$).  A simple linear model with non‑negative weights $\mathbf{w}^{(t)}_{k}\ge 0$ produces a logit for that trial:

\[
\text{logit}^{(t)}_{k}= \max(\mathbf{w}^{(t)}_{k},0)\cdot\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}.
\]

Applying a softmax over all classes yields a probability vector $\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\in\Delta^{c-1}$ for trial $t$:

\[
\mathbf{p}^{(t)} = \operatorname{Softmax}\!\bigl(\text{logit}^{(t)}_{1},\dots,\text{logit}^{(t)}_{c}\bigr).
\]

The final prediction is the **average of the class‑wise probabilities** across all $T$ trials:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle 
\mathbf{p}^{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{p}^{(t)} } .
\]

This averaging is equivalent to **majority voting** in the probability domain and has several desirable properties:

* **Noise Cancellation** – If a few trials generate spurious rules that misclassify certain instances, their influence is diluted by the majority of well‑behaved trials.
* **Self‑Consistency** – Rules that repeatedly appear across many trials receive reinforcement because the corresponding binary features will be active in multiple $\mathbf{z}^{(t)}_{k}$ vectors, leading to consistently high weights.
* **Calibration** – Averaging probabilities rather than logits tends to produce better‑calibrated confidence scores, which is useful when downstream systems need to threshold predictions.

### 5.  Effect on Computational Cost and Prompt Length

Ensembling inevitably introduces extra computation, but FeatLLM’s design mitigates the overhead in two key ways:

| Aspect | Single‑Query Baseline | FeatLLM Ensemble |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Number of LLM Calls** | One call per test sample (expensive at inference) | $T$ calls **only during training** (each call processes the few‑shot examples, not every test instance) |
| **Inference Latency per Sample** | Requires feeding each sample through the LLM → high latency | Uses only the lightweight linear model → inference comparable to logistic regression |
| **Prompt Length per Call** | Must contain all features → may exceed token limit | Feature‑bagging reduces the prompt to a manageable subset (e.g., 30 features) |
| **Parallelism** | Limited; each test sample must wait for LLM response | LLM calls are *embarrassingly parallel* across trials; can be dispatched concurrently on multiple API workers |
| **API Cost** | Grows linearly with number of test instances | Fixed cost proportional to $T$ (typically 20‑30) regardless of test set size |

Thus, while FeatLLM incurs a modest upfront cost (a handful of LLM queries to generate the rule pool), the **asymptotic inference cost** is dramatically lower than approaches that query the LLM for every test record.  Moreover, because each query only needs to handle a reduced feature subset, the **prompt length stays comfortably within the LLM’s context window**, eliminating the need for truncation or sophisticated feature‑selection heuristics.

In practice, the authors observed that with $T=20$ and a rule count $R=10$, the total number of API calls per dataset was under 30, a cost that can be parallelized and is dwarfed by the savings in per‑sample latency.  The trade‑off is therefore highly favorable: a small, bounded training‑time expense in exchange for a robust, low‑latency, and scalable predictor.

---

### 6.  Summary

Ensembling and feature bagging are the linchpins that turn the LLM from a *single* noisy rule generator into a **reliable feature engineer**.  By repeatedly sampling subsets of features and examples, and by exploiting temperature‑driven stochasticity, FeatLLM collects a diverse collection of rule‑derived binary features.  Averaging the class probabilities obtained from simple linear models trained on each rule set yields a final prediction that is both **accurate** (as demonstrated by consistent AUC gains) and **efficient** (requiring no LLM inference at test time).  The ensemble thus addresses the three central challenges of LLM‑based tabular learning:

1. **Incomplete or noisy rules** – mitigated by diversity and averaging.  
2. **Prompt‑size limitations** – mitigated by feature‑level bagging.  
3. **Robustness to spurious correlations** – mitigated by the self‑consistency effect of multiple trials.

Through these mechanisms, FeatLLM achieves state‑of‑the‑art few‑shot performance on a wide variety of tabular datasets while remaining practical for real‑world deployment.
