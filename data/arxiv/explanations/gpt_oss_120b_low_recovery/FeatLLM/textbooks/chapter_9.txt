\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 9: # Experimental Evaluation

In this chapter we provide a comprehensive account of the empirical study that validates the proposed **FeatLLM** framework.  We describe the collection and salient properties of the thirteen tabular benchmarks, the families of baselines against which FeatLLM is compared, the evaluation protocol—including the few‑shot regime and the use of the area under the ROC curve (AUC) as the primary metric—and the main quantitative findings.  We also discuss the contexts in which FeatLLM delivers the most pronounced advantages, such as high‑dimensional tables and extremely limited labeled data.

---

## Dataset Collection and Characteristics

The experimental suite comprises **thirteen heterogeneous classification datasets** that span a broad spectrum of domains, sizes, and feature types.  All datasets are publicly available, and each provides natural‑language feature names and, where applicable, brief textual definitions of the attributes.  The collection can be grouped into three broad categories:

1. **Classic benchmark datasets** that have been widely used in the tabular learning literature:
   - **Adult** – predicting whether an individual’s annual income exceeds \$50 k.  
   - **Bank** – predicting subscription to a term deposit.  
   - **Blood** – predicting donor return.  
   - **Car** – predicting automobile quality.  
   - **Communities** – predicting crime rate in a region.  
   - **Credit‑g** – predicting good versus bad credit risk.  
   - **Diabetes** – predicting presence of diabetes.  
   - **Heart** – predicting coronary artery disease.  
   - **Myocardial** – predicting chronic heart failure.

2. **More recent or synthetic datasets** that were unlikely to have been seen during the pre‑training of the LLM used (GPT‑3.5‑turbo‑0613):
   - **Cultivars** – predicting soybean grain‑yield level.  
   - **NHANES** – predicting an individual's age group from health records.  
   - **Sequence‑type** – classifying numeric sequences as arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz.  
   - **Solution‑mix** – predicting whether the concentration of a four‑solution mixture exceeds 0.5.

The **size and dimensionality** of these tables vary substantially.  Some datasets (e.g., *Adult*, *Bank*) contain only a few dozen attributes, while others (*Communities*, *Myocardial*) exceed one hundred columns, thereby stressing the prompt‑length limitations of LLM‑centric approaches.  Table 1 in the original manuscript summarises the number of records, number of features, and the number of classes for each benchmark; this variety ensures that the experimental results are not tied to a single data regime.

All datasets are presented with **natural‑language feature names** (the set \(F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d\)) and, when available, textual definitions that the FeatLLM prompts can incorporate.  This aligns with the premise that LLMs can exploit such metadata to extract meaningful rules, as described in the method section.

---

## Baseline Categories

To contextualise FeatLLM’s performance, nine baseline methods are evaluated.  These fall into three principal families:

1. **Classical supervised tabular learners** that do not use any external knowledge:
   - **Logistic Regression (LogReg)** – a linear model with L2 regularisation.  
   - **XGBoost** – gradient‑boosted decision trees, a strong non‑linear baseline.  
   - **Random Forest** – an ensemble of decision trees with bagging.

2. **Semi‑supervised or pre‑training‑based methods** that leverage unlabeled data or synthetic pre‑training:
   - **SCARF** – a contrastive learning approach that learns representations from unlabeled tables.  
   - **STUNT** – a self‑training technique that augments few‑shot data with pseudo‑labels.  
   - **TabPFN** – a Bayesian neural network pre‑trained on a mixture of synthetic tabular distributions; inference is performed by feeding both training and test instances jointly.

3. **LLM‑centric approaches** that directly employ large language models for prediction:
   - **In‑context learning** – few‑shot examples are serialized into the prompt and the LLM outputs a class label for each test sample.  
   - **TABLET** – enriches the prompt with auxiliary hints (rule‑sets and prototypes) generated by an external classifier.  
   - **TabLLM** – fine‑tunes an LLM (via parameter‑efficient techniques such as IA3) on the few‑shot examples before inference.

All LLM‑based baselines use the same underlying language model (GPT‑3.5 for the in‑context baseline; T0 for TabLLM according to the original settings) to ensure a fair comparison of algorithmic ideas rather than model capacity.

---

## Evaluation Protocol

### Few‑Shot Regime

The central focus of the study is **low‑shot learning**, where only a tiny subset of labeled instances is available for training.  For each dataset, a random subset of \(k\) examples is drawn, with \(k\) taking values such as 2, 4, 8, or 16 depending on the experiment.  The remainder of the dataset is reserved for evaluation.  By varying \(k\), the authors assess how each method scales as the amount of labelled data grows.

### Repeated Random Splits

To reduce variance due to a particular train‑test split, the experiments are **repeated three times** with different random seeds for the selection of the few‑shot training set and the accompanying test set.  The reported numbers are the **mean and standard deviation of the AUC** (area under the receiver‑operating‑characteristic curve) across these repetitions.  AUC is chosen because it is insensitive to class‑balance variations and reflects the ranking quality of the predicted probabilities.

### Model Training and Hyper‑Parameters

- **FeatLLM**: Uses a temperature of 0.5 for LLM queries, extracts ten rules per class, and forms an ensemble of 20 independent models (each built with a different randomisation of demonstration order and feature bagging).  The downstream linear model is trained with Adam (learning rate 0.01) for 200 epochs, with early stopping guided by \(k\)-fold cross‑validation on the few‑shot set.
  
- **Classical baselines**: Hyper‑parameters are tuned via grid‑search combined with cross‑validation on the few‑shot data.

- **Semi‑supervised baselines**: Follow the implementations described in their original papers; they are provided with the same few‑shot labelled set but may also exploit additional unlabeled data where applicable.

- **LLM baselines**: Follow the prescribed prompting or fine‑tuning procedures (e.g., IA3 for TabLLM) and use the same temperature and sampling settings as FeatLLM when appropriate.

All methods are evaluated under identical computational settings (an A100 GPU for training, API calls for LLM inference) to ensure comparable runtime measurements.

## Main Results: Performance Gains and Statistical Significance

The aggregated results, displayed in Tables 1 and 2 of the original manuscript, reveal a **consistent superiority of FeatLLM** across the thirteen datasets.  Key observations include:

1. **Top‑ranked performance**: FeatLLM attains the highest average AUC on the majority of datasets.  In the few‑shot setting with only four labelled examples, its mean AUC is comparable to that of conventional baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, Random Forest) that are given **eight times more labelled data** (32 shots).  This demonstrates that the rule‑generation step effectively injects prior knowledge, compensating for the scarcity of supervised signals.

2. **Robustness to high dimensionality**: On datasets with large numbers of attributes—*Communities* (≈100 features) and *Myocardial* (also >100)—many LLM‑based baselines fail to produce predictions because the serialized prompt exceeds the model’s context window.  FeatLLM, by contrast, employs **feature bagging** and an ensemble of smaller prompts, thus maintaining viable performance even when the raw feature count is large.

3. **Statistical significance**: Across repeated splits, the standard deviations of FeatLLM’s AUC are modest (often < 0.02), indicating stable performance.  In pairwise comparisons with the strongest non‑LLM baseline (typically XGBoost), the **difference in mean AUC exceeds one standard error** on most datasets, suggesting statistical significance at the 95 % confidence level.

4. **Comparison with other LLM methods**: In‑context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM either under‑perform or outright collapse on high‑dimensional tables due to prompt‑length constraints.  Even when they succeed, their average gains over classical methods are modest.  FeatLLM’s design—extracting **compact binary rule features** and then applying a lightweight linear model—circumvents the need for per‑sample LLM inference, dramatically lowering both computational cost and error propagation.

5. **Effect of ensembling**: The ensemble of 20 rule‑generation trials yields a noticeable lift over a single FeatLLM instance (≈ 1–2 % AUC increase on average).  This confirms the value of stochasticity (temperature‑driven diversity, demonstration ordering, feature bagging) in producing complementary rule sets.

## Analysis of Where FeatLLM Shines

### High‑Dimensional Tables

When the number of columns approaches or exceeds the model’s context window, naïve serialization becomes infeasible for most LLM approaches.  FeatLLM’s **feature‑bagging strategy** selects a random subset of columns for each rule‑generation trial, thereby keeping the prompt within the allowed length.  Because the ensemble aggregates many such partial views, the final predictor benefits from a **coverage of the full feature space** without ever requiring the LLM to process the entire table at once.  Empirically, this yields **stable AUCs** on *Communities* and *Myocardial* where baselines either fail or suffer a sharp performance drop.

### Extreme Low‑Shot Scenarios

In the **2‑shot** regime, the scarcity of labelled examples threatens overfitting for any data‑driven learner.  FeatLLM mitigates this by anchoring rule extraction in the LLM’s **world knowledge** (Step‑1 of the reasoning instruction).  The LLM’s prior understanding of domain‑relevant relationships (e.g., “older age increases disease risk”) guides it to propose sensible conditions even when only two labelled rows are shown.  Ablation experiments (Table 3) demonstrate that removing the reasoning step or the feature‑description component leads to a pronounced drop in performance under 2‑shot conditions, underscoring the importance of leveraging prior knowledge.

### Robustness to Spurious Correlations

A controlled experiment on the **Heart** dataset injected irrelevant columns taken from the **Adult** dataset.  As the number of spurious columns increased, most baselines exhibited rapidly declining AUC, reflecting their tendency to latch onto accidental correlations present in the few‑shot examples.  FeatLLM’s AUC decreased only modestly, indicating that its rule‑generation process **filters out irrelevant features** during the initial causal reasoning stage.  The analysis showed that rules based on noisy columns constituted merely 1–2 % of the total rule set, confirming that the LLM preferentially reasons about features that align with the task description.

### Computational Efficiency

Because FeatLLM requires the LLM **only during the offline feature‑engineering phase** (a bounded set of 30 API calls for the standard configuration), its **inference latency** for new samples is comparable to that of classical linear models—orders of magnitude faster than per‑sample LLM inference required by the in‑context, TABLET, and TabLLM baselines.  Training time is also modest; the downstream linear model converges within a few hundred gradient steps on the few‑shot data, and the entire pipeline can be parallelised across the ensemble members.

---

### Summary

The experimental evaluation establishes **FeatLLM** as a highly effective few‑shot tabular learner.  By converting LLM reasoning into compact binary rule features and coupling these with a simple, trainable linear model, FeatLLM achieves **state‑of‑the‑art performance** across a diverse set of datasets while remaining **computationally tractable** and **robust to high dimensionality** and **spurious correlations**.  These results substantiate the central claim of the paper: large language models can be harnessed not as end‑to‑end predictors but as **feature engineers**, delivering superior data efficiency and practical applicability in real‑world tabular domains.
