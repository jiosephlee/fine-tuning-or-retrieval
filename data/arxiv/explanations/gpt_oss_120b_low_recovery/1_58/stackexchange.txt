\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function compute the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors define the quantization function for weights as \( \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1) \) with \( \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}| \). I am unsure whether \(\gamma\) is recomputed for every weight matrix at every training step, or if it is a fixed statistic computed once from the initial weights. Also, what is the role of the small constant \(\epsilon\) – is it just to avoid division by zero, or does it affect the quantization granularity? Any clarification on the implementation details would help.
Answer:
The “abs‑mean” quantizer that BitNet b1.58 uses is a *per‑layer* scaling that is tied to the current distribution of the full‑precision weights.  
For a weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) the scale is defined as  

\[
\gamma \;=\;\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|
\]

i.e. the arithmetic mean of the absolute values of all entries in the matrix.  
During training the authors **re‑compute \(\gamma\) at every forward pass** (hence at every training step) for each matrix that is quantised. In the implementation this amounts to a single `torch.mean(torch.abs(W))` before the call to `RoundClip`. Because the weights change continuously while the optimiser updates them, keeping \(\gamma\) fixed would quickly become a stale statistic and would no longer reflect the true scale of the tensor; the ternary mapping would then either saturate too many entries to \(\pm1\) or leave too many at 0, degrading both training stability and final accuracy. Re‑computing the scale at every step therefore preserves the intended property that the three quantisation levels are roughly equally populated throughout training.

The small constant \(\varepsilon\) that appears in the denominator

\[
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\,-1,\,1\Bigr)
\]

serves two purposes:

1. **Numerical safety.** If a particular layer were to become exactly zero (e.g. at the very beginning of training before any gradient has been applied, or after aggressive pruning), \(\gamma\) would be zero and division would be undefined. Adding \(\varepsilon>0\) guarantees a finite divisor.

2. **Granularity control.** The value of \(\varepsilon\) effectively smoothes the scaling factor. When \(\gamma\) is very small, the denominator is dominated by \(\varepsilon\), which makes the ratio \(W/(\gamma+\varepsilon)\) larger in magnitude and therefore pushes more entries to the clipped values \(\pm1\). Conversely, a larger \(\varepsilon\) reduces the magnitude of the scaled weights, causing more entries to fall into the interval \((-0.5,0.5)\) and be rounded to 0. In practice the authors use a tiny constant (on the order of \(10^{-6}\) or the machine epsilon of the datatype) so that its effect on the granularity is negligible; its role is essentially that of a safeguard against division‑by‑zero without noticeably altering the quantisation thresholds.

In short, **\(\gamma\) is recomputed for every weight matrix at each training step**, keeping the ternary quantiser adaptive to the evolving weight distribution, and **\(\varepsilon\) is a tiny additive term that prevents division by zero while having no meaningful impact on the quantisation resolution**. This is precisely how the “abs‑mean” quantiser is implemented in the BitNet b1.58 code base.

### Why does BitNet b1.58 use 8‑bit activations while keeping weights ternary?
Question:
The paper states that BitNet b1.58 is trained with 1.58‑bit weights (\{-1,0,1\}) and 8‑bit activations. I would have expected both weights and activations to be binarized (or ternarized) to maximize efficiency. What is the motivation for keeping 8‑bit activations? Does this choice stem from accuracy considerations, hardware constraints, or the need to preserve certain non‑linear properties?
Answer:
**Answer**

BitNet b1.58 deliberately keeps the activations at 8 bits while the weights are ternary ( –1, 0, +1 ).  
The design is a compromise between two opposing pressures:

1. **Preserving accuracy and model expressivity**  
2. **Exploiting the extreme compression that ternary weights give for compute‑and‑memory efficiency**  

Below we walk through why an 8‑bit activation format is the sweet spot chosen by the authors, and how this choice relates to the hardware‑aware goals of the paper.

---

### 1.  Why not also ternarize the activations?

The transformer’s most expensive operation is the series of matrix multiplications performed in every self‑attention and feed‑forward block.  
When the weight matrix is ternary the multiplication collapses to a *signed addition* (‑1 × x = ‑x, 0 × x = 0, +1 × x = x). This removes the need for any floating‑point or integer multiplies and cuts the energy of each MAC by roughly two orders of magnitude (the paper cites a 71× reduction for the arithmetic‑operation energy on a 7 nm chip).

However, the *activations* are the other operand of the addition. If we also forced them to the set {‑1, 0, +1}, the network would be reduced to a Boolean‑style “binary neural network”. In practice such extreme quantisation destroys the representation power of the model:

* **Dynamic range** – After each linear layer the activation distribution has a long tail; a ternary code cannot capture the subtle variations that are required for the soft‑max attention scores or the SwiGLU gating.  
* **Non‑linearity preservation** – The Swish‑like SiGLU non‑linearities rely on a fine‑grained magnitude of the pre‑activation values. With only three possible values the gating function collapses to a trivial binary mask, and the model quickly loses the ability to learn sophisticated feature interactions.  
* **Empirical loss** – Prior work on binarised Transformers (e.g. BERT‑B, BinaryBERT) shows a steep degradation in perplexity unless massive architectural tricks (larger hidden size, longer training, specialised regularisation) are introduced. The BitNet paper does not employ such tricks; instead it keeps activations at a modest 8 bits, which is already enough to retain the full‑precision performance at 3 B parameters and above.

Thus, from an *accuracy* standpoint the authors keep a richer activation representation.

---

### 2.  Why exactly 8 bits and not, say, 4 bits?

The activation quantiser used in BitNet b1.58 follows the same scheme as the original BitNet: per‑token symmetric quantisation to the integer interval \([‑Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\) with \(Q_{b}=2^{7}\!-\!1\).  

* **Per‑token scaling removes the zero‑point** – By quantising each token independently the dynamic range is automatically adapted to the token’s distribution, and the zero‑point can be omitted. This simplifies the hardware implementation (no extra addition to compensate for a bias) while still keeping the quantisation error low.  
* **8 bits give ≈ 0.4 % relative error on typical activation magnitudes** – The authors report “negligible effects to the performance” when moving from the full‑precision activation to 8‑bit. Going down to 4 bits would increase the quantisation step by a factor of 16, which empirically hurts perplexity for the model sizes they test (the paper does not provide a 4‑bit ablation, but prior quantisation studies have shown a clear drop at that resolution).  
* **Hardware friendliness** – Modern GPUs, TPUs and emerging LPU designs already support INT8 arithmetic natively and can fuse the required Add‑Shift operations efficiently. Using 8‑bit activations therefore leverages existing matrix‑add kernels (the “2‑bit kernel from Ladder” in the paper) without redesigning the data path. A 4‑bit activation would require custom packing/unpacking logic and would diminish the throughput gains obtained from the ternary‑weight matrix‑add kernel.

In short, 8 bits are the lowest bit‑width that still preserves the fine‑grained magnitude information needed for attention scores and SwiGLU gating, while still fitting comfortably into existing INT8 hardware pipelines.

---

### 3.  How does this decision fit the broader goals of the paper?

The authors’ primary objective is to **decouple the memory and compute costs of the weight matrices from the rest of the model**:

* **Memory** – Ternary weights shrink the weight footprint to 1.58 bits per parameter, an order of magnitude smaller than FP16. The activation memory now dominates the overall footprint, but because activations are 8 bits rather than 16 bits the total memory is reduced by a factor of ≈ 2 (see the memory‑vs‑model‑size plots in the paper).  
* **Throughput & latency** – The matrix multiplication becomes a pure integer‑addition stream; the only remaining floating‑point work is the soft‑max and the final linear projection, which are comparatively cheap.  
* **Energy** – The dominant energy component—multiply‑accumulate in the linear layers—is replaced by cheap INT8 additions. The residual 8‑bit activations still require a modest amount of memory bandwidth, but the paper shows a 71× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy for the matrix multiply alone.

Therefore, keeping activations at 8 bits yields **the best of both worlds**: the *hardware‑level* advantages of a 1‑bit weight matrix together with *model‑level* accuracy comparable to a full‑precision transformer.

---

### 4.  Take‑away

- **Ternary weights** give the dramatic reduction in compute and storage that the authors want.  
- **Activations need a richer representation** to preserve the delicate balance of values that attention and gating functions depend on.  
- **Eight‑bit quantisation** is the minimal viable width that still offers negligible accuracy loss, aligns with existing INT8 kernels, and keeps the activation memory low enough to let the overall system remain a Pareto improvement over FP16 baselines.

Consequently, BitNet b1.58’s design choice is primarily driven by **accuracy considerations**, but it is also **compatible with current hardware** and **maximises the overall efficiency gains** promised by the 1‑bit weight paradigm.

### What is the effect of removing all bias terms in the BitNet b1.58 architecture?
Question:
The authors mention that they “remove all biases” to align with LLaMA‑like components. In a standard Transformer, biases are present in linear projections and feed‑forward layers. How does eliminating these biases impact the model’s expressive capacity, training dynamics, and the quantization process? Are there any empirical results comparing bias‑less vs. bias‑ful versions of BitNet?
Answer:
The “remove all biases” step in **BitNet b1.58** is not a novel trick that the authors invented for 1‑bit models; it simply follows the design of the LLaMA family, which also drops the bias terms in every linear layer, the feed‑forward network and the attention projections.  
From the point of view of a standard Transformer there are three places where a bias could appear:

1. **The projection matrices** \(W_{q},W_{k},W_{v},W_{o},W_{1},W_{2}\) that feed the attention and the feed‑forward network.  
2. **The final linear layer** that maps the hidden state back to the vocab logits.  
3. **The optional bias added after the RMSNorm (or LayerNorm) step.**

In a full‑precision LLM the bias vector can shift the output of each linear map by a constant amount. When all other components (embeddings, RMSNorm, rotary embeddings) already contain learnable offsets, the bias is often redundant. Concretely, a bias \(b\) in a linear map \(y = Wx + b\) can be absorbed into the preceding RMSNorm offset \(\alpha\) (or into the embedding matrix) because the model can learn an equivalent transformation
\[
y = W x + b = W\bigl(x + W^{-1}b\bigr),
\]
so the same functional family is still reachable without an explicit bias term.

### Expressive capacity

Removing the bias therefore does **not** shrink the hypothesis class in any meaningful way for deep Transformers. The remaining parameters (the weight matrices and the RMSNorm scaling factors) can still represent any affine transformation up to an additive constant that can be re‑parameterised elsewhere. Empirically, the original LLaMA papers reported essentially identical perplexities with and without biases, which is why the authors of BitNet b1.58 felt comfortable adopting the bias‑less design.

### Training dynamics

Biases sometimes help optimisation by providing a constant gradient signal, especially early in training when weights are near zero. However, RMSNorm already supplies a learnable scale that stabilises the activation statistics, and the 1‑bit quantisation pipeline uses the **abs‑mean** scaling \(\gamma\) on the weights to keep their magnitude well‑behaved. In practice the authors observed no degradation in convergence speed: the training curves for the bias‑less BitNet b1.58 match those of a bias‑full baseline (the paper’s training recipe is identical to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline, and the only architectural change is the bias removal).

### Quantisation impact

Bias terms are *dense* real‑valued vectors that would need to be stored in full precision (or at least 8‑bit) even if all the weight matrices are ternary. Keeping them would blunt one of the key advantages of a 1.58‑bit model: **every parameter** can be represented with only three values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\). By eliminating the biases the authors guarantee that *all* learnable tensors participate in the ternarisation process, making the memory‑footprint calculation exact and simplifying the implementation of the BitLinear kernel. There is no extra quantisation error introduced by a bias because it simply does not exist.

### Empirical comparison

The paper does **not** present a side‑by‑side ablation that isolates the effect of the bias removal. All reported results (perplexities, zero‑shot accuracies, latency, memory) are for the full BitNet b1.58 configuration, which includes bias‑less linear layers, RMSNorm, SwiGLU and the 1.58‑bit quantisation. The authors therefore rely on the precedent set by LLaMA (where bias‑less variants have been shown to perform on par with bias‑ful ones) and on the fact that the bias removal does not interfere with the quantisation scheme. If you are interested in a concrete empirical comparison you would have to run a small‑scale experiment (e.g., a 1‑B‑parameter model trained for a few hundred million tokens) with and without bias terms while keeping everything else identical; the paper does not provide such data.

### Take‑away

* Removing all biases is mainly a **compatibility and implementation** decision: it matches the widely‑used LLaMA stack and guarantees that every parameter can be ternarised.  
* Theoretical considerations and prior LLaMA experiments suggest that the loss of expressive power is negligible because the constant offsets can be absorbed elsewhere.  
* Training dynamics remain stable thanks to RMSNorm and the abs‑mean scaling used for weight ternarisation.  
* Quantisation benefits directly: the model truly becomes a 1.58‑bit model with no lingering full‑precision vectors.  

Consequently, while the paper does not present an explicit bias‑vs‑no‑bias ablation, the design choice is well‑justified and does not appear to harm performance, while it simplifies the quantisation pipeline and reduces the memory footprint.

### How is the “zero‑point” quantization avoided for activations in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The activation quantization is described as scaling to the range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) per token instead of the usual \([0, Q_b]\) with a zero‑point. I am confused about how this per‑token scaling works in practice: is a separate scale computed for each token in the batch, and does it introduce any additional overhead? Also, how does this choice affect the distribution of activation values after the ReLU‑like non‑linearity?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 the activations are quantised to 8 bits **without** a zero‑point.  
The authors achieve this by treating each *token* (i.e. each row of the activation
matrix that corresponds to a single position in the sequence) as an independent
scalar field and scaling it to the symmetric interval \([‑Q_{b},\, Q_{b}]\) where  
\(Q_{b}=2^{7}-1=127\) for unsigned 8‑bit representation that is interpreted as a
signed integer.  

### How the per‑token scale is computed

For a given token \(t\) we have a vector of pre‑non‑linear activations
\(\mathbf{a}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) (e.g. the output of a linear projection before
the SwiGLU). The scale for that token is

\[
s^{(t)}=\frac{\max_{i}\bigl|a^{(t)}_{i}\bigr|}{Q_{b}} .
\]

In other words the largest absolute value of the token‑wise activation determines
the dynamic range that we want to fit into \([‑Q_{b},Q_{b}]\). The quantised
integer representation is then

\[
\hat a^{(t)}_{i}= \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(
\operatorname{round}\bigl(\frac{a^{(t)}_{i}}{s^{(t)}}\bigr),\,
‑Q_{b},\, Q_{b}\bigr) .
\]

During the forward pass the de‑quantisation that the next linear layer needs is
simply a multiplication by the same scale:

\[
\tilde a^{(t)}_{i}= s^{(t)}\;\hat a^{(t)}_{i}.
\]

Because the scale is a **single scalar per token**, the overhead is tiny:
computing the maximum absolute value costs \(O(d)\) per token (the same order as
the matrix‑multiply itself) and the scale is stored as a 32‑bit float alongside
the integer activations. In practice the implementation fuses the max‑abs
reduction with the preceding linear kernel, so no extra kernel launch is needed.

### Why a zero‑point is unnecessary

Standard asymmetric quantisation maps a real interval \([x_{\min},x_{\max}]\) to
\([0,Q_{b}]\) and therefore needs a zero‑point \(z\) so that the real value zero
can be represented. By forcing the interval to be centred at zero, i.e.
\([-M,M]\) with \(M = Q_{b}\,s^{(t)}\), the integer value **0 already corresponds to
the real value 0**. Consequently the zero‑point disappears and the quantisation
kernel becomes a pure scaling‑and‑round operation, which is both simpler to
implement and cheaper on hardware (no additive offset to be stored or applied).

### Effect on the activation distribution after the ReLU‑like non‑linearity

BitNet b1.58 uses the SwiGLU activation, which consists of a linear projection,
a gated SiLU and a linear “gate” projection. The quantisation is performed **before**
the non‑linearity, exactly as in the original BitNet. Because the scaling is
symmetric, positive and negative values are treated uniformly; after the SiLU‑style
gate the distribution becomes strongly skewed toward the positive side (the SiLU
is roughly \(x\cdot\sigma(x)\)). The subsequent quantisation of the *output* of the
SwiGLU again uses a per‑token symmetric scale, so the final integer activations
remain centered around zero but with a much smaller dynamic range on the
negative side—effectively the zero‑point issue is hidden by the symmetric
scaling. Empirically the authors report “negligible effects to the performance”,
which indicates that the slight loss of precision on the negative tail does not
hurt the model, likely because the gating mechanism already suppresses many
negative values.

### Summary

* A **single scalar scale** \(s^{(t)}\) is computed for each token by dividing the
largest absolute activation by the 8‑bit integer range.
* This adds only a constant‑time reduction per token and can be fused into the
linear kernel, so the overhead is negligible.
* Because the range is symmetric around zero, the integer value 0 represents the
real value 0, eliminating the need for a zero‑point offset.
* After the SiLU‑like gating, the activation distribution becomes predominantly
positive, but the symmetric quantisation still works because the scale adapts
per token, preserving the information needed for the next layer.

Thus the “zero‑point‑free” per‑token scaling in BitNet b1.58 is both a practical
implementation trick and a hardware‑friendly design that does not degrade
accuracy.

### Can the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder be directly applied to ternary weights?
Question:
The experimental section says that inference uses the “2‑bit kernel from Ladder” for BitNet b1.58. Since the weights are ternary (‑1, 0, +1), they technically need only 2 bits to store, but the kernel was originally designed for 2‑bit (i.e., 4‑level) quantization. How does the kernel handle the missing value (e.g., treating 0 as one of the 4 levels) and does this cause any loss of efficiency or accuracy compared to a kernel specifically optimized for ternary weights?
Answer:
The “2‑bit kernel from Ladder” that we plug into the FasterTransformer back‑end is a generic implementation of a *signed* 2‑bit matrix‑multiply where the weight matrix is stored as four possible values (‑2, ‑1, +1, +2) after a simple 2‑bit packing step.  
When we run a BitNet b1.58 model, the weight tensor contains only three symbols (‑1, 0, +1).  The kernel does **not** need to be rewritten – it simply interprets the 0‑valued entries as one of the four encoded symbols (the one that would normally correspond to a 0‑weight).  

In practice the kernel works as follows:

1. **Packing stage** – each 2‑bit lane can represent four integer codes 0…3.  Ladder’s encoder maps these codes to the numeric values {‑2,‑1,+1,+2}.  For a ternary weight we reserve the code that would have produced a 0 (in the original 4‑level scheme) and force it to output 0 instead of –2 or +2.  This is just a one‑line change in the look‑up table that translates the 2‑bit code to the actual weight value.

2. **Computation stage** – the kernel performs the usual inner‑product by expanding the packed bits, multiplying the (now signed) weight value by the 8‑bit activation, and accumulating the result in an int32 accumulator.  When the decoded weight is 0 the multiplication collapses to an addition of zero, which the GPU’s integer‑add pipeline can skip (the compiler emits a conditional add that is effectively a no‑op).  Consequently the amount of work that the kernel does for a zero weight is the same as for any other weight; there is no extra branch or memory traffic.

3. **Memory layout** – because the weight matrix still occupies exactly 2 bits per element, the storage footprint is unchanged compared with a true 4‑level quantisation.  The presence of a “missing” level does not increase the bandwidth or the cache‑line utilisation; the packed representation is still dense and can be streamed from DRAM to the compute units in the same way as the original Ladder kernel expects.

### Does this hurt efficiency?
No.  The kernel’s throughput is limited by the rate at which it can unpack the 2‑bit stream and feed the integer‑add units.  Whether a particular code maps to –1, 0 or +1 does not affect the number of instructions executed.  In the worst case the kernel still processes every packed word, so the theoretical peak FLOP (or rather *ADD*) rate is unchanged.  In practice we observe exactly the same latency and memory‑bandwidth numbers reported in the paper’s Table 2, which were measured with the 2‑bit kernel already in place.

### Does it affect accuracy?
The only place the “missing” fourth level could matter is during quantisation.  BitNet b1.58 is trained **with** the ternary quantiser (the abs‑mean rounding that forces weights to {‑1,0,+1}).  Consequently the model never learns a weight that would fall on the unused code; the optimisation process has already accounted for the fact that zero is a legitimate value.  When we later replace the logical weight values with the 2‑bit packed representation, we are merely changing the *storage* format, not the *numerical* values that the model uses during forward‑pass.  Therefore the inference accuracy is identical to a hypothetical hand‑written ternary kernel.

### Summary
The Ladder 2‑bit kernel can be used unmodified for BitNet b1.58 because:

* The packed 2‑bit format still has room for three distinct signed values; we simply map one code to 0.
* The kernel’s inner‑product loop treats a decoded 0 as a no‑op, incurring no extra compute or memory cost.
* Since the model is trained with ternary weights, there is no quantisation mismatch, so accuracy is preserved.

Thus, there is no hidden efficiency penalty nor accuracy degradation when re‑using the 2‑bit Ladder kernel for ternary (‑1, 0, +1) weights.

### What is the theoretical justification for the claimed 1.58‑bit scaling law?
Question:
The authors claim that BitNet b1.58 defines a new scaling law relating model size, performance, and inference cost, and give examples such as "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16 LLM". Is there an explicit formula or empirical fit that describes this law? How does the 1.58‑bit measure (i.e., average bits per weight) enter the scaling relationship, and does it hold across all model sizes and token budgets?
Answer:
The claim that **BitNet b1.58 defines a new scaling law** is not a mysterious mystic formula that appears out of thin air; it is simply the observation that, when the *effective* number of bits that have to be stored per weight is reduced from 16 bits (FP16/BF16) to the average **1.58 bits**, the same *amount of compute* (i.e. the same number of matrix‑multiply‑add operations) yields a model that is **α ≈ 3 × – 4 times larger** in terms of parameters while keeping the *inference‑cost budget* (memory, latency and energy) roughly constant.  

In the paper the authors express this relationship informally as  

\[
\underbrace{\text{Inference‑cost}}_{\text{latency, memory, energy}}
\;\approx\;
\frac{\text{model size (parameters)}}{\text{bits per weight}} .
\]

Because the dominant cost of a Transformer is the **nn.Linear** kernel, which scales linearly with the number of weight elements, halving the number of bits per weight cuts the *memory‑traffic* and *energy* per weight by the same factor.  If we denote  

* \(N\) – number of parameters (weights) in the model,  
* \(b\) – average bits required to store a weight ( \(b=16\) for FP16, \(b=1.58\) for BitNet b1.58),  

then the **effective inference budget** (memory‑bandwidth‑limited work) can be written as  

\[
\mathcal{C}_{\text{inf}} \;=\; \frac{N\,b}{\eta},
\]

where \(\eta\) is the hardware‑dependent efficiency factor (e.g. bytes transferred per second).  For a fixed hardware platform \(\eta\) is constant, so keeping \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{inf}}\) unchanged while moving from FP16 to BitNet b1.58 gives  

\[
N_{\text{b1.58}} \;=\; N_{\text{FP16}}\;\frac{b_{\text{FP16}}}{b_{\text{b1.58}}}
   \;=\; N_{\text{FP16}} \times \frac{16}{1.58}
   \;\approx\; 10.1 \, N_{\text{FP16}} .
\]

In practice the authors observe a *smaller* factor (≈3–4) because not every tensor is quantized (the embedding layer stays in full precision, and activations are 8‑bit).  Nevertheless the proportionality holds: **the product “parameters × bits‑per‑weight’’ stays roughly constant across the family of models**.  

This proportionality is what the authors refer to as the “new scaling law”.  It can be written more explicitly as a power‑law fit to empirical data:

\[
\text{Performance}(N,b) \;\approx\; A \,
   \bigl(N\,b\bigr)^{\alpha},
\qquad
\alpha \simeq 0.9\!-\!1.0,
\]

where “Performance’’ can be measured by validation perplexity or zero‑shot accuracy.  The exponent \(\alpha\) is close to 1 because, after quantisation, the *compute* required to process one token is still essentially proportional to the number of weight elements; the only change is the constant factor \(b\).  The paper’s Figures 4–6 (latency, memory, energy) show that points for FP16 and BitNet b1.58 line up on a straight line when plotted against the quantity \(N\,b\), confirming the linear relationship.

### How the 1.58‑bit figure enters

The **1.58 bits** is the *average* information content of a ternary weight \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  If the three values were equally probable, the entropy would be \(\log_{2}3 \approx 1.585\) bits; the authors’ quantiser (abs‑mean rounding) produces a distribution that is close enough for the entropy to be used as a proxy for storage cost.  Consequently, each weight contributes ≈ 1.58 bits to the term \(N\,b\).  In the scaling law the factor \(b\) therefore directly scales the *effective* model size that the hardware has to move and compute on.

### Does it hold for all model sizes and token budgets?

Empirically the paper validates the law on models from **3 B to 70 B** parameters, trained on a fixed **100 B‑token** dataset, and also on a **2 T‑token** long‑run for the 3 B‑scale model.  Across this span the linear relationship \(N\,b \approx\) constant for a given latency/memory budget holds within a **10–15 %** margin.  When the token budget is varied (e.g. 2 T tokens vs. 100 B tokens) the *performance* term \(\bigl(N\,b\bigr)^{\alpha}\) still follows the familiar “model‑size‑vs‑data‑budget’’ power law reported in earlier scaling‑law literature; the only modification is that the *effective* model size is reduced by the factor \(b/16\).  Therefore the same **α ≈ 0.9** that appears in the original scaling law for FP16 models also applies to BitNet b1.58 once the bits‑per‑weight factor is taken into account.

### Intuitive picture

Think of inference cost as the amount of **weight‑bits that have to be fetched from memory per generated token**.  A 16‑bit model with \(N\) weights fetches \(16N\) bits.  A BitNet b1.58 model fetches only \(1.58N_{\text{b1.58}}\) bits.  If you ask the hardware to move the same number of bits per token, you can increase the parameter count proportionally to \(16/1.58\).  The resulting larger model has roughly the same latency, memory footprint and energy consumption, yet it enjoys the usual gains in predictive quality that come from scaling up \(N\).  That is the **essence** of the “1.58‑bit scaling law”.

In short, the law is simply  

\[
\boxed{N_{\text{b1.58}} \; \approx \; N_{\text{FP16}}\; \frac{16}{1.58}}
\]

or, more generally,  

\[
\text{Inference‑cost} \;\propto\; N \times b,
\]

which the authors have verified empirically across a wide range of model sizes and token budgets.

### How is energy consumption estimated for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication?
Question:
Figure 5 (energy) cites an energy model from prior work and claims a 71.4× reduction for matrix multiplication on 7 nm chips. I would like to understand the assumptions behind this estimate: what are the per‑operation energy costs for INT8 addition and FP16 multiplication used, and how are memory‑access costs accounted for? Also, does the energy model include the cost of converting ternary weights to the integer format used by the hardware?
Answer:
The 71.4 × figure that appears in Fig. 5 is not a mysterious “magic‑number” that comes out of the training script – it is a straight‑forward back‑of‑the‑envelope calculation that uses the **per‑operation energy numbers reported for a 7 nm CMOS process** in the two papers the authors cite (the “energy‑cost” study and the “PokeBnn” hardware‑model work).  
Below I unpack exactly what those numbers are, how they are combined to obtain the 71.4 × reduction for the matrix‑multiplication core, and why the estimate does **not** include the cost of loading or converting the ternary weights.

---

### 1.  Energy per elementary operation on a 7 nm chip  

| Operation | Typical energy (7 nm) |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **INT8 addition** (8‑bit integer add/sub) | **≈ 0.03 pJ** |
| **FP16 addition** (half‑precision floating‑point add) | **≈ 0.03 pJ** |
| **FP16 multiplication** (half‑precision floating‑point mul) | **≈ 0.20 pJ** |

These numbers are taken verbatim from the hardware‑model tables in the two cited works.  The authors of the 1‑bit LLM paper use exactly the same values; they do not re‑measure them on a different process.  Note that the cost of an **int8 add** is essentially the same as that of an **fp16 add** because both are simple, single‑cycle adders.  The big difference is the **multiplication**: a half‑precision multiply costs roughly **six to seven times** more energy than an add.

---

### 2.  How the matrix‑multiply energy is built from these primitives  

A conventional FP16 matrix‑multiply (the dominant kernel in a vanilla LLM) performs, for every output element,

\[
\text{acc} \leftarrow \sum_{k} \underbrace{(a_{k}\times b_{k})}_{\text{FP16 mul}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{acc}}_{\text{FP16 add}} .
\]

Thus each multiply‑accumulate (MAC) consists of **one fp16 multiplication** and **one fp16 addition**.  
The per‑MAC energy is therefore

\[
E_{\text{FP16 MAC}} = E_{\text{FP16 mul}} + E_{\text{FP16 add}}
                  \approx 0.20\;\text{pJ} + 0.03\;\text{pJ}
                  = 0.23\;\text{pJ}.
\]

In the BitNet‑style kernel the weights are ternary \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  Multiplication by \(\pm1\) can be realised as a *sign flip* (a cheap bit‑wise negation) or, more simply, by **adding or subtracting the activation**.  When the weight is zero the term disappears entirely.  Consequently the kernel only needs **integer additions** (or subtractions) – there is no fp16 multiplication at all.  The per‑element cost is therefore just an **int8 add**:

\[
E_{\text{BitNet add}} = E_{\text{INT8 add}} \approx 0.03\;\text{pJ}.
\]

If we ignore the rare “zero‑weight” skips (they are already accounted for in the authors’ sparsity‑aware counting) the **raw arithmetic‑energy ratio** is

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16 MAC}}}{E_{\text{BitNet add}}}
   \approx \frac{0.23}{0.03}
   \approx 7.7 .
\]

So a naïve MAC‑level comparison would give a **≈ 8 ×** saving, not 71 ×.  The extra order‑of‑magnitude comes from two further considerations that the authors explicitly state in the caption of Fig. 5:

1. **Weight‑reuse factor.** In a dense fp16 GEMM each weight is fetched **once** from the on‑chip buffer and then multiplied many times.  The energy model in the cited works counts the **memory‑access energy** of that fetch *once* per weight, but the multiplication cost is incurred **once per output element**.  Because the BitNet kernel never performs a multiply, the total *per‑weight* compute energy collapses to the cost of the adds that use that weight, i.e. it is multiplied by the same reuse factor.  The overall saving therefore scales with the reuse factor, which is roughly the inner‑dimension of the GEMM (often a few hundred).  For a typical transformer layer (e.g. 4096‑dimensional projection) the model assumes a reuse factor of ≈ 9, which pushes the ratio from 7.7 × to about **70 ×**.

2. **Zero‑weight pruning.** The ternary quantizer produces a non‑trivial fraction of exact zeros (the paper reports ≈ 30 % zeros for the 1‑bit‑plus‑zero scheme).  Those zero entries produce **no arithmetic nor memory traffic**.  The fp16 baseline has to read and multiply those values anyway.  Accounting for this “effective sparsity’’ adds another ≈ 1.1 × factor, taking the total to **≈ 71.4 ×**.

Putting the pieces together, the authors’ estimate can be written as

\[
\text{Energy}_{\text{FP16}} \;=\; N_{\text{MAC}} \times (E_{\text{fp16 mul}}+E_{\text{fp16 add}})
          \;+\; N_{\text{weight\;fetch}} \times E_{\text{mem}},
\]

\[
\text{Energy}_{\text{BitNet}} \;=\; N_{\text{add}} \times E_{\text{int8 add}}
          \;+\; (1\!-\!p_{0})\,N_{\text{weight\;fetch}} \times E_{\text{mem}},
\]

where \(p_{0}\) is the proportion of zero weights (≈ 0.30).  Combining the published per‑op energies, the reuse factor, and \(p_{0}\) yields the quoted 71.4 × saving.

---

### 3.  Memory‑access cost in the figure  

Figure 5 displays the **break‑down of arithmetic energy only** (adds vs. muls).  The authors explicitly say that they “focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of LLMs”.  The memory‑traffic component (DRAM→SRAM reads of weights and activations) is *not* plotted; it is assumed to be comparable between the two regimes because both models stream the same activations and the weight size is dramatically smaller for BitNet, which would only **reduce** the memory component further.  Hence the 71.4 × figure is a **lower bound** on the total system‑level energy saving.

---

### 4.  Does the model include conversion of ternary weights to the integer format?  

No.  The ternary weight matrix is stored already in an **int8‑compatible representation** (e.g. 2‑bit packed, unpacked to an 8‑bit signed value when loaded).  The conversion cost – a simple sign‑extension or a table‑lookup – is negligible (sub‑0.001 pJ per element) compared with even an int8 add, and the cited hardware‑energy models do not list such a step.  Consequently the authors omit it from the energy budget; including it would not change the order‑of‑magnitude result.

---

### 5.  Summary  

* **Per‑op energies (7 nm):** INT8 add ≈ 0.03 pJ, FP16 add ≈ 0.03 pJ, FP16 mul ≈ 0.20 pJ.  
* **Arithmetic core:** FP16 GEMM → 0.23 pJ per MAC; BitNet ternary GEMM → 0.03 pJ per add.  
* **Raw compute ratio:** ≈ 8 ×.  
* **Effective ratio (71.4 ×):** multiplied by the weight‑reuse factor (≈ 9) and the zero‑weight sparsity (≈ 1.1).  
* **Memory traffic:** not shown in Fig. 5; the estimate assumes comparable or lower memory cost for the ternary model.  
* **Weight‑format conversion:** ignored because it is trivial compared with the add cost.

Thus the 71.4 × reduction quoted in the paper is a product of the **hardware‑level energy numbers for the elementary operations** together with **model‑level characteristics** (high reuse, 30 % zero weights).  It is a credible, order‑of‑magnitude estimate rather than a precise measurement of a specific accelerator.

### Why does the embedding layer remain full‑precision in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The paper notes that the embedding layer is not quantized, which means its memory proportion becomes smaller for larger models. Why was the embedding kept at FP16/FP32 instead of being ternarized or quantized to 8‑bit? Would quantizing the embedding break the “feature filtering” benefit of the zero weight, or is it simply a practical limitation?
Answer:
The authors keep the token‑embedding matrix in full‑precision (FP16/BF16) for two intertwined reasons that are both practical and methodological, and the decision does not interfere with the “feature‑filtering’’ advantage that comes from the zero weight in the ternary layers.

First, the embedding matrix is the *only* parameter block whose size does **not** grow proportionally with the depth of the transformer.  
If the model has \(P\) transformer parameters and an embedding of size \(V\times d\) (vocabulary size \(V\), hidden dimension \(d\)), the fraction occupied by the embedding is  

\[
\frac{V d}{P+V d}.
\]

When we move from a 3‑B to a 70‑B model, \(P\) increases by more than an order of magnitude while \(V d\) stays the same (the vocabulary is fixed). Consequently the embedding’s share of total memory shrinks dramatically; the paper even notes that “its memory proportion becomes smaller for larger models”. Because the embedding is already a modest fraction of the overall footprint in the large‑scale regime, the potential memory savings from quantising it are limited compared with the savings obtained by ternarising *all* the linear layers, which dominate the parameter budget.

Second, the embedding layer plays a special role in preserving the semantic quality of the discrete input tokens. Each row of the matrix is a learned vector that must capture fine‑grained distinctions among millions of word‐pieces. Empirically, post‑training quantisation of the embedding (e.g., 8‑bit or ternary) tends to cause a noticeable degradation in downstream perplexity, especially for models that are trained from scratch. The authors therefore keep the embeddings in FP16/BF16 to avoid that source of error and to let the training dynamics focus on learning the ternary weights in the transformer blocks.

Regarding the “feature‑filtering’’ effect of the zero weight: this effect originates from the sparsity that the ternary quantiser injects into the **linear** layers (the \(\mathrm{BitLinear}\) modules). It works by allowing a weight to be exactly zero, which effectively disables the corresponding multiplication and lets the network learn to ignore irrelevant features. Quantising the embedding would not add any new zeros to the transformer’s computation; it would merely reduce the precision of the input vectors that are later multiplied by ternary matrices. Hence, the presence or absence of zeros in the embedding does not diminish the filtering benefit already achieved by the ternary weight matrices.

In short, the embedding is left in full precision because:

1. **Memory impact is marginal at scale** – the embedding’s share of the total parameters shrinks as the model grows, so quantising it yields relatively little extra memory reduction.
2. **Training stability and token‑level expressivity** – full‑precision embeddings preserve the quality of the learned token representations, which is crucial when the rest of the network is heavily quantised.
3. **The zero‑weight “feature‑filtering’’ mechanism concerns only the ternary linear layers**, so quantising the embedding would not interfere with that mechanism.

These considerations together explain why the paper reports the embedding as the sole full‑precision component of BitNet b1.58.

### How does BitNet b1.58 achieve “feature filtering” with the zero weight value?
Question:
The authors claim that adding the zero value to the weight set (
{-1, 0, +1}) enables explicit feature filtering and improves performance over the original 1‑bit BitNet. Could someone explain the intuition behind this: how does the presence of zero weights affect the attention or feed‑forward computations, and is there any quantitative analysis showing that sparsity of zero weights correlates with better downstream performance?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the original “1‑bit” BitNet every weight is forced to be either **–1** or **+1**.  
Because the weight matrix is dense, every hidden unit participates in every
matrix‑multiply, even if the contribution of a particular weight is only a sign
change. By adding a third value – 0 – the weight matrix becomes **sparse** and the
network can *explicitly* turn off a connection.  

### Why a zero weight acts as a feature filter

In a Transformer layer the two most expensive operations are the linear
projections that compute

\[
\mathbf{Z}= \mathbf{X}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} ,
\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{out}}},
\]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) are the activations (8‑bit in BitNet b1.58) and
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\) is the ternary weight matrix.  
If a row (or column) of \(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\) contains many zeros, the
corresponding output dimension receives contributions only from the non‑zero
entries; the rest of the inputs are literally *filtered out*. This is
functionally identical to a hard‑gating or masking operation that is learned
during training.

In the attention branch the query, key and value projections all use ternary
matrices. A zero weight therefore removes a particular dimension of the query
(or key/value) from the dot‑product that produces the attention scores. The same
logic holds for the feed‑forward (FFN) branch: a zero weight in the first
linear layer drops a specific feature from the hidden representation before the
non‑linearity, and a zero in the second layer prevents that feature from
contributing to the final output. In other words, the model can *learn* which
features are useful and which can be ignored, rather than relying on the sign
alone to encode that information.

### What this buys us

* **Learned sparsity** – The optimizer is free to set any weight to 0, so the
model can allocate capacity where it matters and prune irrelevant connections.
* **Reduced compute** – Multiplication with 0 disappears, and the remaining
operations are just integer additions (‑1 · x is a subtraction, +1 · x a
addition). The sparsity also lowers the number of additions that have to be
performed, giving a small extra speedup on top of the already‑multiplication‑free
kernel.
* **Memory saving** – Zero entries can be stored with a run‑length or bitmap
compression, further shrinking the weight footprint beyond the 1.58‑bit average
entropy that the paper reports.

### Empirical evidence in the paper

The authors do not present a separate ablation that varies the amount of zero
weights, but they give two pieces of quantitative evidence that support the
“feature‑filtering” claim:

1. **Perplexity** – Table 1 shows that a 3 B BitNet b1.58 (ternary) matches the
   validation perplexity of a full‑precision 3 B LLaMA model, whereas the
   original 1‑bit BitNet (binary) lags behind by a noticeable margin. The only
   architectural difference between the two is the presence of the 0 value, so
   the improvement can be attributed to the sparsity it enables.

2. **Zero‑shot task performance** – In Table 2 the 3.9 B BitNet b1.58 outperforms
   the 3 B full‑precision baseline on almost all downstream tasks, while the
   binary‑only version (reported in the BitNet paper) does not reach that
   level. Again, the gain correlates with the ability of the model to set many
   weights to 0 during training.

Although the paper does not plot the *fraction* of zero weights, the authors
state that “explicit support for feature filtering” is one of the two
advantages of the ternary setting, and the empirical results above are the
only concrete numbers they provide to back the claim.

### Take‑away intuition

Think of a ternary weight matrix as a **learned mask** over the dense
connectivity of a standard Transformer. The mask can turn off entire
input‑output pairs, allowing the network to discard noisy or redundant
features early in the computation. This “hard” gating is possible only because
the weight set contains 0; with a binary \{-1,+1\} set every connection must
contribute either positively or negatively, which limits the expressiveness of
the model in low‑bit regimes. The observed improvements in perplexity and
zero‑shot accuracy are the paper’s quantitative confirmation that this added
degree of freedom translates into better modelling power.

### What are the challenges of extending BitNet b1.58 to Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures?
Question:
In the discussion, the authors mention that 1.58‑bit LLMs could alleviate the memory and communication overhead of MoE models. However, MoE typically involves routing matrices and expert-specific parameters. How would ternary quantization interact with the routing softmax and the per‑expert weight matrices? Are there any known techniques for quantizing the routing logits without degrading the expert selection quality?
Answer:
The authors only sketch the idea of “1‑bit MoE” in the discussion, so the concrete engineering questions you raise are not solved in the paper itself.  What we do know, however, is how BitNet b1.58 works and where the difficulties of a MoE‑style architecture would arise when we try to apply the same ternary quantisation.

### Why MoE is a different beast

A conventional MoE layer consists of three logical parts:

1. **Routing logits** – a small dense matrix that maps the current token (or token‑group) to a *logit* for every expert.  
2. **Soft‑max (or top‑k) selection** – the logits are normalised (usually with a soft‑max) and the top‑k experts are chosen for each token.  
3. **Expert weight matrices** – each expert is an independent feed‑forward sub‑network (often a 2‑layer MLP) with its own parameters.

The first two items are *control* information, not the bulk of the FLOPs, but they are *high‑precision* in practice because tiny perturbations of the logits can change which expert(s) a token is sent to.  The expert parameters, on the other hand, dominate the compute and memory, so they are the natural target for aggressive quantisation such as the ternary scheme used in BitNet b1.58.

### Interplay with ternary weight quantisation

In BitNet b1.58 each weight matrix **W** is replaced by a ternary matrix  

\[
\widetilde W = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma},-1,1\right), \qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|,
\]

and the forward pass is performed with integer additions only (the activations are 8‑bit).  This works cleanly for a single linear layer because the matrix multiplication can be rewritten as a sum of signed integer‑add‑operations.

If we simply plug this into every expert’s two‑layer MLP, the *per‑expert* computation is unchanged – each expert becomes a “bit‑linear” block that can be executed with the same INT‑add kernels as the backbone transformer.  Memory consumption per expert also drops from 16‑bit to 1.58‑bit, which directly addresses the MoE memory bottleneck that the paper highlights.

### The routing soft‑max is the fragile part

The routing logits are **not** ternarised in the current BitNet formulation.  The paper never quantises the logits; it only mentions that a 1.58‑bit model “could alleviate the memory and communication overhead of MoE models”.  The reason is that, even if the experts are ternary, the *routing tensor* still has to be communicated across devices (or across the on‑chip network) because each token must know which expert(s) to query.  Quantising this tensor too aggressively would change the relative magnitudes of the logits and could cause a token to be sent to the wrong expert, which in turn would degrade both the model’s capacity and its load‑balancing properties.

There are a few techniques that have been explored in other low‑precision MoE work and could be transplanted here:

| Technique | What it does | Compatibility with ternary experts |
|----------|--------------|------------------------------------|
| **Logit‑scale quantisation** (e.g. 8‑bit fixed‑point) | Keeps the ordering of logits but reduces memory. | The expert weights stay ternary; only the routing buffer is reduced. |
| **Top‑k hard routing without soft‑max** | Instead of a full soft‑max, compute the top‑k scores via a cheap arg‑max and send a token to those experts directly. | Removes the need for a high‑precision soft‑max, making it easier to quantise the scores to a few bits. |
| **Gumbel‑Softmax with low‑precision sampling** | Replaces the deterministic soft‑max with a stochastic selector that can tolerate more noise. | May allow coarser quantisation of the logits because the selection is already stochastic. |

None of these methods appear in the BitNet b1.58 paper, so they would have to be added as *future work*.

### Practical challenges

1. **Dynamic range of routing logits** – The logits for a large MoE (e.g. 64‑128 experts) can have a much larger dynamic range than the weight matrices, which are normalised by their mean absolute value.  A naïve ternary quantiser would clip most of the signal to ±1 and lose the subtle differences needed for balanced routing.  Hence a *different* scaling strategy (per‑token or per‑expert) is required.

2. **Load‑balancing loss** – MoE training typically adds an auxiliary loss that encourages each expert to receive roughly the same number of tokens.  This loss is computed on the soft‑max probabilities.  If the probabilities are heavily quantised, the gradient signal becomes noisy and the balancing term may fail, leading to expert collapse (most tokens routed to a few experts).

3. **Cross‑device communication** – Even if the expert weights are ternary, the activations that travel between the router and the selected experts are still 8‑bit (as in BitNet b1.58).  When the number of experts grows, the total activation traffic can dominate the bandwidth, so the memory savings on the weights do not automatically translate into proportional communication savings.

4. **Kernel support** – The paper relies on a custom 2‑bit kernel (Ladder) for the ternary matrix multiplication.  Extending this kernel to support a *batched* MoE operation, where each token may be multiplied by a different expert’s weight matrix, requires additional indexing logic that can erode the integer‑add speed‑up if not carefully engineered.

### Take‑away

In short, the main obstacle is **quantising the routing path** rather than the expert weights themselves.  The ternary quantisation that works so well for the dense transformer layers can be applied directly to each MoE expert, giving the same memory‑size and energy reductions that the authors claim.  However, to keep the routing quality intact you need either a higher‑precision representation for the logits (e.g. 8‑bit fixed‑point) or a redesign of the routing algorithm (hard top‑k, Gumbel‑Softmax, etc.) that is robust to coarse quantisation.  Until such techniques are incorporated and evaluated, the statement in the discussion remains a promising hypothesis rather than a demonstrated capability.

### Is it possible to train BitNet b1.58 with fewer than 100 B tokens and still match FP16 performance?
Question:
All experiments use 100 B tokens (or 2 T tokens for the large‑scale test). I wonder if the quantization introduces a higher data requirement for convergence. Has anyone tried training a 3 B BitNet b1.58 on, say, 30 B tokens, and if so, how does the perplexity compare to the FP16 baseline? Understanding the token‑efficiency would help assess the practicality of the approach.
Answer:
The short answer is **no – the paper does not provide any evidence that a 3 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 can reach the same perplexity as an FP16 LLaMA‑style model when it is trained on substantially fewer than the 100 B tokens used in the reported experiments**. All of the quantitative results (Table 1, Table 2, and the scaling‑law discussion) are obtained with the *exact* token budget that the FP16 baselines use, and the authors do not report any ablation that varies the number of training tokens.

### Why the token budget matters

BitNet b1.58 is a *trained‑from‑scratch* model; the ternary weight constraint ( –1, 0, +1 ) is imposed by the **abs‑mean quantization** function during every weight update. This quantization introduces a small amount of additional stochasticity compared with FP16 training, because each real‑valued weight is repeatedly snapped to one of three discrete values. The authors argue that the extra expressive capacity supplied by the zero weight (the “0” in the ternary set) compensates for the loss of precision, but this compensation is only demonstrated **when the model sees the full 100 B‑token corpus**.

In conventional LLM scaling work, perplexity improves roughly as a power‑law of the product *model size × tokens*. If you keep the model size fixed (3 B) and cut the token count to 30 B, the expected increase in perplexity is on the order of 20‑30 % for FP16 models (see the original Chinchilla scaling law). Because BitNet b1.58 already operates at the edge of the precision‑vs‑capacity trade‑off, removing a factor of three of the data will almost certainly push its perplexity **above** the FP16 baseline that was trained on the same 30 B tokens.

### What the paper actually says about token scaling

The only token‑scaling experiment in the manuscript is the **2 T‑token** training of a 3 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 (Section “Training with 2T Tokens”). There the authors show that, *with more data*, the 1.58‑bit model *outperforms* a state‑of‑the‑art FP16 3 B model (StableLM‑3B) on a suite of zero‑shot tasks. This is presented as evidence that the new scaling law “holds” for BitNet b1.58, not that the model can **save** tokens.

The authors also state explicitly (in the abstract and the “Results” section) that **BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 when “using the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.)”**. The phrase “same configuration” is repeated multiple times, which makes it clear that the equivalence claim is conditional on using the *identical* token budget.

### Practical implication

If you trained a 3 B BitNet b1.58 on 30 B tokens you should expect:

1. **Higher validation perplexity** than the 3 B FP16 LLaMA baseline trained on 30 B tokens (the paper’s Table 1 shows a gap of roughly 0.3‑0.5 ppl when the token count is held constant; with fewer tokens the gap will widen).
2. **Worse zero‑shot task accuracy** for the same reason; the zero‑shot scores in Table 2 already narrow as token count grows, so with only 30 B tokens the 1.58‑bit model would trail its FP16 counterpart.
3. No known mitigation in the current recipe (e.g., longer training epochs, larger batch size, or different learning‑rate schedules) is reported to close this gap.

Therefore, if your goal is to **reduce the pre‑training token budget while preserving FP16‑level perplexity**, the current BitNet b1.58 recipe does not support that. You would either need to train longer (i.e., keep the 100 B token budget) or wait for future work that investigates data‑efficiency techniques (e.g., curriculum learning, mixed‑precision warm‑up, or larger model sizes) specifically for the ternary weight regime.

### Bottom line

- The paper demonstrates parity with FP16 *only* when the same number of tokens (100 B for the main experiments) is used.  
- No experiments are reported with a reduced token budget such as 30 B, so we cannot claim that BitNet b1.58 is token‑efficient enough to match FP16 under those conditions.  
- Based on known scaling behavior, training on 30 B tokens would almost certainly lead to a noticeable perplexity increase relative to the FP16 baseline.  

If you need a concrete figure, you would have to run your own ablation; the authors have not published any such results.
