\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 7: # New Scaling Laws for 1.58‑Bit LLMs  

In this chapter we examine the striking empirical relationships discovered for the ternary‑weight language model **BitNet b1.58**, a 1.58‑bit Large Language Model (LLM). By comparing it directly to conventional 16‑bit (FP16/BF16) Transformers of the same architectural family, the authors identified a set of *equivalence pairs* that connect model size, predictive performance, and inference‑time cost. These observations motivate a concise *cost‑performance scaling law* that can be used to choose an optimal model given a fixed computational budget. We also discuss the practical consequences of this law for model selection, and we outline the current limitations and open questions that arise when attempting to extrapolate the law to even larger models.

---

## Empirical Equivalence Pairs  

The experiments in the paper train BitNet b1.58 and standard FP16 LLaMA‑style models on the same RedPajama dataset for **100 billion tokens**. For each model size, the authors report validation perplexity on WikiText‑2 and C4, zero‑shot accuracy on a broad suite of language tasks (ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ), and the inference‑time cost measured as *time per output token* together with GPU memory consumption.  

When these numbers are plotted side‑by‑side, clear cross‑over points emerge: a **BitNet b1.58** model with a certain number of parameters attains essentially the same perplexity and task accuracy as a much smaller FP16 model, while consuming dramatically less memory and latency. The paper lists three concrete equivalence pairs that exemplify this phenomenon:

| BitNet b1.58 size | Equivalent FP16 size | Reason for equivalence |
|-------------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **13 B**          | **3 B**              | Same perplexity, comparable zero‑shot scores; 2.71 × faster inference, 3.55 × lower memory |
| **30 B**          | **7 B**              | Matching validation loss; latency reduced by a factor > 3, memory reduced by > 3 |
| **70 B**          | **13 B**             | Comparable end‑task performance; inference 4.1 × faster, memory ≈ 3.5 × lower |

These pairs are not arbitrary; they are derived from the *Pareto improvement* observed across the full set of experiments. For instance, **BitNet b1.58‑3.9 B** outperforms **FP16‑3 B** on both perplexity and downstream tasks while using only one‑third of the GPU memory and being more than twice as fast. Conversely, a **70 B** BitNet b1.58 model remains competitive with a **13 B** FP16 baseline despite having more than five times as many parameters, because the ternary weight representation eliminates virtually all multiplications in the dominant matrix‑multiplication kernels.

Thus, the empirical equivalence pairs provide concrete data points that link **parameter count** (a proxy for training effort) to **inference cost** (latency, memory, energy) while preserving **model quality** (perplexity, task accuracy).

---

## Formulation of a Cost‑Performance Scaling Law  

The empirical observations suggest that, in the ternary regime, the dominant source of inference cost—the matrix‑multiplication in every transformer block—scales **linearly** with the number of parameters *if the weight precision is held constant*. However, because the BitNet b1.58 weight matrix is constrained to the set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), each multiply reduces to a sign‑preserving addition, and the cost per parameter drops by a factor that can be approximated as a constant \(c\) (the paper reports a 71.4 × reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy on a 7 nm process).  

Let us denote:

* \(N\) – number of model parameters (in billions, B).
* \(C_{\text{FP16}}(N)\) – inference cost for an FP16 model of size \(N\), measured in a composite unit that accounts for latency, memory, and energy.
* \(C_{\text{b1.58}}(N)\) – analogous cost for BitNet b1.58 of size \(N\).

Empirically, the paper finds a *piecewise linear* relationship between cost and size within each precision tier:

\[
C_{\text{FP16}}(N) \approx \alpha_{\text{fp}} \, N,
\qquad
C_{\text{b1.58}}(N) \approx \alpha_{\text{b1.58}} \, N,
\]

with \(\alpha_{\text{b1.58}} \ll \alpha_{\text{fp}}\). The measured speed‑up of roughly \(2.7\)–\(4.1\)× for larger models suggests \(\alpha_{\text{b1.58}} \approx \alpha_{\text{fp}} / 3\).  

Performance (e.g., perplexity \(P\) or zero‑shot accuracy \(A\)) is observed to follow the classic *power‑law* scaling with model size when the training data budget is fixed:

\[
P(N) \approx \kappa \, N^{-\beta},
\qquad
A(N) \approx \eta - \zeta \, N^{-\gamma},
\]

where \(\kappa, \beta, \eta, \zeta, \gamma\) are positive constants that depend weakly on the token count (100 B in the experiments). Crucially, the **same exponent** \(\beta\) (or \(\gamma\)) fits both the FP16 and the 1.58‑bit curves: the ternary quantization does not fundamentally alter the asymptotic scaling of quality with model size, only the *offset* in cost.

Combining the cost model with the performance law yields a **cost‑performance scaling law** for the ternary regime:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Performance}(N) \;\approx\; f\!\bigl( C_{\text{b1.58}}(N) \bigr)
\;=\;
f\!\bigl( \tfrac{\alpha_{\text{fp}}}{3} N \bigr)
}
\]

In words, for any given inference budget \(B\), one can solve for the effective FP16 size \(N_{\text{fp}}\) that would exhaust the budget, then *inflate* the model by a factor of roughly three in the 1.58‑bit space while achieving the same performance. This yields the equivalence relationships reported above (e.g., \(N_{\text{fp}} = 3\) B corresponds to \(N_{\text{b1.58}} \approx 13\) B).

Mathematically, if the FP16 cost budget is \(B\),

\[
N_{\text{fp}} = \frac{B}{\alpha_{\text{fp}}}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
N_{\text{b1.58}} = \frac{B}{\alpha_{\text{b1.58}}}
= \frac{\alpha_{\text{fp}}}{\alpha_{\text{b1.58}}}\, N_{\text{fp}}
\approx 3 \, N_{\text{fp}}.
\]

Thus the **scaling factor** \(\frac{\alpha_{\text{fp}}}{\alpha_{\text{b1.58}}}\) captures the *efficiency multiplier* that the ternary representation confers.

## Implications for Model Selection Under a Fixed Budget  

Practitioners often face a hard ceiling on inference latency, GPU memory, or total energy consumption, especially in commercial or edge deployments. The scaling law provides a systematic decision procedure:

1. **Quantify the budget** \(B\) in terms of the composite cost unit used in the paper (e.g., milliseconds per token plus memory in GB).  
2. **Determine the largest FP16 model** that fits within \(B\): \(N_{\text{fp}} = B / \alpha_{\text{fp}}\).  
3. **Scale up** by the efficiency multiplier \(\approx 3\) to obtain the admissible BitNet b1.58 size: \(N_{\text{b1.58}} \approx 3 N_{\text{fp}}\).  
4. **Check performance** using the power‑law relation (or directly from tabulated results) to ensure the inflated model reaches the desired perplexity or zero‑shot accuracy.

For example, suppose a GPU server can run at most a 4 GB model in FP16 for a given latency target. The corresponding budget is \(B = \alpha_{\text{fp}} \times 4\). The scaling law predicts that a **BitNet b1.58** model with **≈ 12 B** parameters will respect the same budget while delivering the same or better quality. The experimental results confirm this: **BitNet b1.58‑13 B** matches the perplexity of **FP16‑3 B** and exceeds its downstream task scores, all while using less than one‑third of the memory and being over four times faster.

Moreover, the **energy savings** (71.4 × reduction per matrix‑multiply) imply that for battery‑constrained edge devices, the feasible model size can be increased even further without violating power limits. The paper’s energy‑cost plot shows that the advantage grows with model size because the proportion of computation residing in linear layers (the part most benefitted by ternary weights) rises.

In practice, the scaling law therefore steers engineers toward **larger ternary models** when latency, memory, or energy are bottlenecks, without sacrificing the predictive power that usually accompanies more parameters.

## Limitations and Open Questions About Extrapolation  

While the observed equivalence pairs and derived scaling law are compelling, several caveats temper their universal applicability:

1. **Finite Training Token Budget** – All reported models were trained for a fixed 100 B tokens. The scaling law assumes that performance continues to follow the same power trend when the token count is held constant. If the number of training tokens is increased (as in the 2 T‑token experiment), the relative advantage of larger ternary models may shift, and the simple linear cost factor could change.

2. **Hardware‑Specific Constants** – The constants \(\alpha_{\text{fp}}\) and \(\alpha_{\text{b1.58}}\) embed details of the GPU architecture, the 2‑bit kernel implementation, and memory hierarchy. The reported 3× speed‑up stems from NVIDIA A100 hardware combined with the Ladder 2‑bit kernel. On other platforms (e.g., CPUs, custom LPUs, or future 1‑bit‑optimized ASICs) the multiplier could be higher or lower, altering the equivalence ratios.

3. **Model Architecture Variations** – BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA‑style components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and removes biases to ease integration. Introducing other architectural tweaks—such as deeper feed‑forward networks, alternative attention mechanisms, or MoE layers—may change both the cost per parameter and the performance scaling exponent, breaking the simple linear relationship.

4. **Non‑Linear Scaling at Extreme Sizes** – The equivalence pairs are anchored at 13 B, 30 B, and 70 B. Extrapolating beyond 70 B (or below 3 B) could encounter diminishing returns: the embedding layer remains in full precision, and its memory share grows relatively for very small models, reducing the overall cost advantage; for extremely large models, communication overheads (e.g., inter‑GPU bandwidth) may dominate and the linear cost model may no longer hold.

5. **Task‑Specific Sensitivity** – Zero‑shot scores on the evaluated suite converge as model size increases, but certain downstream tasks (e.g., those demanding fine‑grained numerical reasoning) might be more sensitive to weight quantization. The scaling law, derived from aggregate metrics, does not capture such task‑specific degradations.

6. **Quantization Artifacts** – The ternary quantization uses an *absmean* scaling followed by rounding and clipping. While the paper reports negligible impact on performance, subtle distributional shifts could become significant for models trained with vastly more tokens or for specialized domains, potentially requiring a different scaling factor.

Addressing these open questions will likely involve **systematic ablation studies** across multiple hardware back‑ends, token regimes, and architectural variants. Moreover, extending the analysis to *mixed‑precision* pipelines (e.g., retaining higher precision in the embedding or the final classifier) could refine the cost model and lead to **adaptive scaling laws** that more accurately predict performance under diverse deployment constraints.

---

### Summary  

The **new scaling law** for 1.58‑bit LLMs emerges from a clear empirical pattern: ternary‑weight transformers achieve the same predictive quality as much smaller FP16 models while consuming roughly one‑third of the inference cost. By formalizing the cost as a linear function of parameter count and preserving the power‑law relationship between size and performance, the authors obtain a compact expression that directly maps a fixed inference budget to an optimal ternary model size. This law has immediate practical value for selecting models under latency, memory, or energy constraints, and it invites further research to validate its robustness across hardware platforms, training regimes, and architectural innovations.
