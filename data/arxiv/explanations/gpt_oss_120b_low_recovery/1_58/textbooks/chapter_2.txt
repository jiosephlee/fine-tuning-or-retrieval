\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 2: # Background: 1‑Bit Model Architectures and BitNet  

The rapid expansion of large language models (LLMs) has been accompanied by an ever‑growing demand for computational resources.  Modern Transformer‑based LLMs are typically trained and run with 16‑bit floating‑point formats (FP16 or BF16).  While these formats provide a good trade‑off between numeric range, precision, and hardware support, the dominant cost of inference and training remains the massive number of floating‑point multiply‑accumulate (MAC) operations required for the dense linear layers that comprise the bulk of a Transformer.  In this chapter we examine the original BitNet design, which replaces the conventional floating‑point matrix multiplication with a binary (or 1‑bit) weight representation and integer‑only accumulation.  By eliminating multiplications, BitNet achieves orders‑of‑magnitude reductions in energy consumption, memory bandwidth, and latency, paving the way for the newer 1.58‑bit variant discussed in later chapters.

## Review of Traditional Transformer Arithmetic (FP16/BF16)  

A standard Transformer layer consists of a multi‑head self‑attention (MHSA) block followed by a feed‑forward network (FFN).  Both blocks are built from dense linear transformations implemented as calls to `nn.Linear`.  In an FP16 or BF16 setting, each weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\) and activation vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\) are stored in 16‑bit floating‑point format.  The forward pass computes  

\[
y = W x + b ,
\]

where \(b\) is an optional bias term.  The operation decomposes into \(n m\) floating‑point multiplications and an equal number of floating‑point additions.  Modern GPUs and TPUs implement these MACs with fused floating‑point units that simultaneously perform a multiplication and an addition, but each such unit consumes a substantial amount of dynamic power.  Empirical measurements on contemporary 7 nm silicon report that a single FP16 multiplication consumes roughly \(3.7\) pJ, while an FP16 addition consumes about \(0.9\) pJ \cite{energycost}.  When a model contains billions of parameters, the total energy spent on these MACs dominates the overall inference cost.

Beyond energy, the arithmetic intensity of floating‑point operations imposes bandwidth requirements on the memory subsystem.  Every weight and activation must be fetched from DRAM or on‑chip SRAM at 2 bytes per element.  For a 30 B‑parameter model, the weight tensor alone exceeds 60 GB, far larger than the capacity of typical accelerator on‑chip memory.  Consequently, inference pipelines must repeatedly stream data between DRAM and compute units, incurring latency penalties and limiting throughput.

## BitNet’s Replacement of `nn.Linear` with `BitLinear`  

BitNet introduces a new linear layer called **BitLinear** that fundamentally changes the arithmetic performed during matrix multiplication.  Instead of storing each weight as a 16‑bit floating‑point number, BitNet constrains the weights to the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\).  The quantization is applied during training: after each weight update, the full‑precision matrix \(W\) is scaled by its average absolute value \(\gamma\) and then projected onto the nearest integer in \([-1, 1]\).  Formally, the quantized weight matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) is obtained by  

\[
\widetilde{W}
= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\, -1,\, 1\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\bigl(a,\, \min\!\bigl(b,\, \operatorname{round}(x)\bigr)\bigr),
\]

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} |W_{ij}|,
\]

where \(\epsilon\) is a small constant to avoid division by zero.  The resulting \(\widetilde{W}\) contains only \(-1\), \(0\), or \(+1\) entries, which can be stored using 2 bits per weight (the extra bit distinguishes the zero value from the two signed values).  In practice, the authors refer to this representation as “1.58 bit” because the average information content per weight is slightly less than 2 bits.

During inference, **BitLinear** performs the matrix multiplication  

\[
y = \widetilde{W} x + b,
\]

but because each non‑zero entry of \(\widetilde{W}\) is either \(+1\) or \(-1\), the multiplication reduces to a sign flip (or a no‑op if the weight is zero).  Consequently, the core computation becomes a series of **integer additions** (or subtractions) of the activation values, which are quantized to 8 bits.  In hardware terms, a MAC is replaced by a simple integer addition, eliminating the expensive floating‑point multiplication unit entirely.

Implementation wise, BitLinear can be expressed as a sequence of three steps:  

1. **Mask generation** – identify the locations of non‑zero weights.  
2. **Signed accumulation** – add or subtract the corresponding 8‑bit activation values according to the sign of the weight.  
3. **Bias addition** – optionally add a bias term stored in higher precision.

Since the mask and sign information are static after quantization, they can be packed efficiently, enabling SIMD‑friendly integer add kernels.  The authors of the original BitNet paper integrated a custom 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder library, achieving substantial speedups over the standard FP16 kernels in NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer.

## Binary Weight Representation and Integer‑Only Accumulation  

The ternary weight set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) can be interpreted as a binary representation with an additional “zero” state.  In a pure 1‑bit regime, each weight would be either \(-1\) or \(+1\), which requires exactly one bit per weight.  Adding the zero value introduces an extra state, raising the average information content to \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits per weight, hence the terminology “1.58‑bit.”  

Storing the weights in this compact format dramatically reduces the memory footprint of the model.  A 30 B‑parameter Transformer that would normally occupy roughly \(60\) GB in FP16 can be stored in about \(55\) GB when using the 1‑bit representation (the slight increase comes from auxiliary scaling factors such as \(\gamma\) and possible index tables).  More importantly, the **bandwidth** required to fetch the weights during inference is cut by a factor of roughly eight, because each weight now occupies only 1–2 bits instead of 16.

The accumulation process, being purely integer, benefits from the much lower energy per operation of integer adders.  State‑of‑the‑art 7 nm ASIC designs report an energy cost of roughly \(0.03\) pJ per 8‑bit addition \cite{pokebnn}, which is more than an order of magnitude cheaper than a 16‑bit floating‑point addition.  Because the multiplication step is eliminated, the total arithmetic‑operation energy for a BitLinear layer is dominated by these cheap adds, leading to a theoretical reduction of **71.4×** in matrix‑multiplication energy on such silicon, as reported in the BitNet experiments.

From an algorithmic perspective, the absence of multiplications also simplifies the data path.  No floating‑point units, no rounding units, and no complex exception handling (e.g., NaNs or infinities) are needed for the core linear layers.  This simplification opens the door to specialized hardware accelerators that can dedicate more silicon area to high‑throughput integer adders, on‑chip SRAM, and parallel reduction networks.

## Energy Model for Floating‑Point vs. Integer Operations  

To quantify the benefit, consider a matrix multiplication with dimensions \(n \times m\) and \(m \times p\).  The total number of floating‑point MACs required in FP16 is  

\[
N_{\text{FP}} = n m p,
\]

each consisting of one multiplication and one addition.  Using the per‑operation energy values \(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}\) and \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}\), the total energy consumption is  

\[
E_{\text{FP}} = N_{\text{FP}} \bigl(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}\bigr).
\]

For a BitNet layer, the number of *effective* multiplications is reduced to zero, and the number of integer additions equals the number of non‑zero weight entries, which we denote \(N_{\text{nz}}\).  Assuming a sparsity factor \(s = N_{\text{nz}}/(nm)\), the energy becomes  

\[
E_{\text{Bit}} = N_{\text{nz}} \, E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}} = s \, n m p \, E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}.
\]

With typical ternary quantization, the sparsity \(s\) is around \(0.66\) because one third of the weights are zero on average.  Substituting the measured per‑operation energies (\(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} \approx 3.7\) pJ, \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}} \approx 0.9\) pJ, \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}} \approx 0.03\) pJ), we obtain  

\[
\frac{E_{\text{Bit}}}{E_{\text{FP}}}
\approx \frac{0.66 \times 0.03}{3.7 + 0.9}
\approx \frac{0.0198}{4.6}
\approx 0.0043,
\]

which corresponds to a **~230×** reduction in energy for the matrix‑multiply sub‑operation.  The paper reports a more conservative 71.4× reduction after accounting for overheads such as activation quantization and memory transfers, confirming that even with practical considerations the energy savings are dramatic.

## Prior Results and Open Questions from the Original BitNet Paper  

The original BitNet study validated the 1‑bit architecture on a range of model scales from 300 M to 6 B parameters.  Empirically, BitNet achieved perplexities on benchmarks such as WikiText‑2 and C4 that were within a few percentage points of the FP16 baselines, while delivering **2.5–4×** speedups and **3–5×** reductions in GPU memory usage.  Notably, the performance gap narrowed as model size increased, suggesting that the expressive capacity of ternary weights scales favorably with larger architectures.

One of the key observations was that the **zero weight** in the ternary set serves as an implicit feature filter.  By allowing a weight to be exactly zero, BitNet can selectively prune connections during training, effectively learning a sparse connectivity pattern without explicit regularization.  This capability contributed to the observed performance parity with full‑precision models, especially for models larger than 3 B parameters.

Despite these successes, several open questions remained:  

* **Training stability:** Quantizing weights to \{-1,0,+1\} after each gradient step can introduce gradient noise.  The original work mitigated this with the “absmean” scaling and a small additive epsilon, but a systematic analysis of convergence dynamics across learning rates and optimizer families was not fully explored.  

* **Activation quantization:** BitNet employed 8‑bit activations scaled to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\).  The impact of more aggressive activation quantization (e.g., 4‑bit) on downstream tasks, especially those sensitive to subtle probability differences, remains an open avenue.  

* **Hardware specialization:** While the authors demonstrated speedups using a custom 2‑bit kernel, the broader hardware community has yet to design ASICs that can exploit the pure integer‑add paradigm at the scale required for 70 B‑parameter models.  Questions about memory hierarchy design, on‑chip SRAM allocation, and inter‑chip communication for massive models are still active research topics.  

* **Extending to other architectures:** BitNet focused on the standard Transformer stack.  Whether similar binary‑weight strategies can be applied to encoder‑decoder models, vision‑language multimodal systems, or diffusion models without loss of fidelity is an open research direction.  

These unresolved issues motivated subsequent work, including the development of the **BitNet b1.58** variant that adds an explicit zero state to improve feature filtering and demonstrates parity with FP16 performance starting at 3 B parameters.  The next chapters will build on this foundation, exploring the detailed training recipe, scaling laws, and system‑level implications of 1.58‑bit LLMs.
