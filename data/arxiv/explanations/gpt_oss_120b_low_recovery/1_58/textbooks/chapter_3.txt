Chapter 3: **Design of BitNet b1.58 – A 1.58‑Bit Large Language Model**

### Why ternary weights? Introducing the zero state  

Traditional 1‑bit LLMs constrain every weight to the set $\{-1,+1\}$, which eliminates any notion of “no connection’’ between a pair of neurons. While this extreme binarisation yields dramatic savings in memory and arithmetic, it also removes a useful degree of freedom: the ability of a weight to completely suppress the contribution of its input. By augmenting the binary alphabet with a zero element we obtain a *ternary* alphabet $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  

From an information‑theoretic standpoint the ternary alphabet requires $\log_{2}(3)\approx1.58$ bits per weight, hence the name **BitNet b1.58**. The addition of the zero value confers two practical benefits. First, it enables *feature filtering*: a zero weight can explicitly block irrelevant features, reducing noise that would otherwise be propagated through the network. Second, it provides a richer representational capacity without a substantial increase in storage because the three‑state encoding can be packed efficiently (e.g., two ternary values per byte). Empirically the paper demonstrates that this modest increase in representational richness allows a 3 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 to match the perplexity and downstream task performance of a full‑precision FP16 LLaMA model of the same size.

### AbsMean quantization function for weights  

The core of BitNet b1.58’s weight quantisation is the **absolute‑mean (AbsMean) quantizer**. Given a full‑precision weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$, the goal is to map each entry $W_{ij}$ to a ternary value $\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ while preserving as much of the original scale as possible. The procedure consists of three steps:

1. **Compute the average absolute magnitude** of the matrix:  

   \[
   \gamma \;=\;\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|.
   \tag{1}
   \]

   The constant $\gamma$ captures the typical size of the weights and serves as a normalisation factor. Adding a tiny constant $\epsilon$ (e.g., $10^{-8}$) guards against division‑by‑zero when $W$ is all zeros.

2. **Scale the matrix** by the inverse of this magnitude:  

   \[
   \hat{W}\;=\;\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}.
   \tag{2}
   \]

   After scaling, the majority of entries in $\hat{W}$ lie in the interval $[-1,1]$, which is precisely the range we need to quantise to ternary values.

3. **Round‑and‑clip** each scaled entry to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,+1\}$:  

   \[
   \widetilde{W}\;=\;\operatorname{RoundClip}\!\bigl(\hat{W},\,-1,\,1\bigr),
   \tag{3}
   \]
   where the auxiliary function `RoundClip` is defined as  

   \[
   \operatorname{RoundClip}(x,\,a,\,b)
   \;=\;
   \max\!\bigl(a,\;\min\!\bigl(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x)\bigr)\bigr).
   \tag{4}
   \]

   Equation (4) first rounds $x$ to the nearest integer and then enforces the bounds $a$ and $b$ (in our case $a=-1$, $b=+1$). The result is a matrix whose entries are exactly $-1$, $0$, or $+1$.

The overall mapping can be written compactly as  

\[
\boxed{\;\widetilde{W}
   \;=\;
   \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\,1\Bigr)\;}
\tag{5}
\]

which is precisely the formulation reported in the paper. The AbsMean quantiser differs from a naïve sign function because the scaling by $\gamma$ preserves the *dynamic range* of each layer, allowing the ternary representation to capture variations in weight magnitude indirectly.

### Mathematical derivation of RoundClip and scaling factor $\gamma$  

The necessity of the clipping operation can be understood by inspecting the distribution of the scaled weights $\hat{W}$. Even after normalisation, outliers can push values slightly beyond $\pm1$. Direct rounding would then produce $\pm2$, violating the ternary constraint. By inserting the clipping step we guarantee that any rounded value larger than $+1$ gets truncated to $+1$, and any value smaller than $-1$ gets truncated to $-1$.  

Consider a scalar entry $w$ from $W$ and its scaled counterpart $\hat{w}=w/(\gamma+\epsilon)$. The quantisation error $e = \widetilde{w} - w$ can be decomposed as  

\[
e
=
\underbrace{\bigl(\operatorname{RoundClip}(\hat{w},-1,1)-\hat{w}\bigr)}_{\text{clipping error}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\bigl(\hat{w} - \frac{w}{\gamma+\epsilon}\bigr)}_{\text{rounding error}}.
\]

Because $\operatorname{RoundClip}$ differs from the identity only when $|\hat{w}|>1$, the clipping error is incurred only for extreme outliers, which are rare when $\gamma$ is a robust estimator of the average absolute magnitude. The rounding error is bounded by $0.5$ (the maximum distance from a real number to its nearest integer), and is further scaled back to the original magnitude by multiplying with $\gamma+\epsilon$. This derivation shows that the expected quantisation distortion scales linearly with $\gamma$, which in turn adapts to each layer’s statistics, preserving a balanced trade‑off between quantisation error and model capacity.

### Activation quantization strategy (signed 8‑bit, removal of zero‑point)  

While the weights are ternary, the activations are kept at **8‑bit signed integer** precision. The original BitNet pipeline quantises activations to the unsigned range $[0, Q_{b}]$, where $Q_{b}=2^{b}-1$ for $b$ bits, and introduces a *zero‑point* offset to map signed values into that range. BitNet b1.58 simplifies this process:

1. **Symmetric scaling per token** – for each token’s activation vector $a$, we compute its maximal absolute value  

   \[
   s_{a}\;=\;\max_{k}\bigl|a_{k}\bigr|,
   \]

   then scale the entire vector to the signed range $[-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]$:  

   \[
   \hat{a}_{k}\;=\;\operatorname{clip}\!\Bigl(\operatorname{round}\!\bigl(\frac{a_{k}}{s_{a}}\,Q_{b}\bigr),\,-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}\Bigr).
   \tag{6}
   \]

2. **Zero‑point elimination** – because the scaling is symmetric around zero, no additive offset is required; the quantised value $0$ already corresponds to a zero activation. This eliminates the need for storing a per‑tensor zero‑point, reduces bookkeeping, and simplifies hardware pipelines that otherwise must perform an extra addition before the main arithmetic.

3. **Per‑token scaling** – unlike static per‑layer scales, a per‑token scale adapts to the dynamic range of each token’s representation, which is especially useful for language models where token embeddings can vary dramatically in magnitude. Empirically the paper shows that this simplification incurs **negligible performance loss**.

The result is an activation representation that can be stored in a single byte per element, compatible with widely used INT8 matrix‑multiply kernels (e.g., the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder used for BitNet b1.58) while preserving the signed nature needed for the additive nature of transformer attention and feed‑forward networks.

### Adopting LLaMA‑style components  

To maximise compatibility with the vibrant open‑source ecosystem, BitNet b1.58 adopts the **LLaMA‑style architectural components** rather than inventing bespoke alternatives. Each component is retained in its original form, except for minor adjustments needed to accommodate ternary weights and low‑precision activations.

| Component | Role in Transformer | Adaptation for BitNet b1.58 |
|-----------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| **RMSNorm** (Root‑Mean‑Square Normalization) | Normalises activation statistics without learnable bias terms, offering stable training for low‑precision models. | Directly reused; bias‑free design aligns with BitNet’s bias‑free philosophy. |
| **SwiGLU** (Swish‑Gate Linear Unit) | Provides a gated non‑linearity that has been shown to improve expressivity over ReLU/GeLU. | Operates on 8‑bit signed activations; the gating matrix is also ternary, so the element‑wise product reduces to simple sign manipulations. |
| **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)** | Encodes token positions via complex rotations applied to query/key vectors. | No modification needed; the rotation matrices are applied in integer arithmetic after scaling the vectors to INT8. |
| **Bias‑free linear layers** | Removes additive bias terms from linear projections, which are redundant when using RMSNorm and can be a source of quantisation error. | BitLinear implements `nn.Linear` without biases, simplifying the quantisation pipeline and reducing memory. |

By mirroring the LLaMA backbone, BitNet b1.58 can be **drop‑in substituted** into existing pipelines such as HuggingFace Transformers, vLLM, or llama.cpp with only the change of the linear layer class. This design decision dramatically lowers the engineering effort required to bring 1.58‑bit models to production.

### Implementation notes for popular libraries  

1. **HuggingFace Transformers**  
   - Replace `torch.nn.Linear` with a custom module `BitLinear`. The forward pass multiplies a ternary weight tensor (stored compactly, e.g., packed two‑bits per weight) with an INT8 activation tensor, then invokes an INT8‑to‑FP32 accumulator (or directly accumulates into INT32 before scaling).  
   - Register the `absmean_quantizer` as a *weight hook* that runs after each optimizer step, ensuring the weights remain ternary throughout training.  
   - Use `torch.quantization` utilities to cast activations to `torch.int8` after the RMSNorm step and before the SwiGLU feed‑forward block.

2. **vLLM**  
   - vLLM’s kernel dispatch expects a contiguous weight buffer. Provide a pre‑packed ternary buffer using the same layout as the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder.  
   - The runtime’s `kv cache` can be stored in INT8 (half the size of FP16), directly benefiting from the zero‑point‑free activation format.  
   - Expose a model configuration flag `bitnet_b1_58=True` that triggers the custom kernel path.

3. **llama.cpp**  
   - The C++ inference engine already supports quantised GGML formats. Extend the GGML file format to include a new enum value `GGML_TYPE_TERNARY`. Implement a loader that reads the packed ternary weights, expands them on‑the‑fly into signed 8‑bit values `{-1,0,+1}` for the SIMD kernels.  
   - Because the kernel operates on 8‑bit activations, the existing INT8 matrix‑multiply routine can be reused with only minor changes to the scaling factor handling.

Across all libraries, a **consistent seed for the scaling factor $\gamma$** must be used during weight updates: after each optimizer step compute $\gamma$ per layer, apply the AbsMean quantizer, and store the ternary weights. The optimizer itself (e.g., AdamW) works on the underlying FP32 shadow copy of the weights, allowing high‑precision gradient accumulation while the forward pass sees only the ternary projection.

### Training‑time considerations: optimizer, learning‑rate schedule, token budget  

Training a BitNet b1.58 model follows the same macro‑level recipe as its FP16 LLaMA counterpart (same number of tokens, same model depth and width). However, several nuances arise from the ternary weight constraint:

1. **Shadow weights and optimizer** – Maintain an FP32 shadow copy of each weight matrix $W_{\text{fp32}}$. The optimizer (typically AdamW) updates this shadow copy based on gradients computed with the ternary forward pass. After the optimizer step, the AbsMean quantizer projects $W_{\text{fp32}}$ back to $\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ for the next forward pass. This “straight‑through” approach preserves the stability of Adam’s adaptive moments while enforcing the hard ternary constraint.

2. **Learning‑rate schedule** – The paper employs the same cosine annealing schedule used for LLaMA, starting with a warm‑up phase of a few million tokens (e.g., 2 % of total tokens). Because the quantised model has lower effective capacity, a slightly **higher peak learning rate** (e.g., $1.5\times$ the FP16 baseline) can help traverse the rugged loss landscape introduced by the projection step. Empirically the authors report no need for schedule modifications when training from scratch on 100 B tokens.

3. **Gradient clipping** – Since the forward pass uses integer arithmetic, gradients can be noisier. Applying a norm‑based clipping (e.g., $1.0$) before the optimizer step mitigates exploding updates, especially early in training when the ternary projection may be far from the optimal direction.

4. **Token budget** – The experiments in the paper demonstrate that **matching the token count** of the FP16 baseline (100 B tokens on RedPajama) suffices for the 3 B BitNet b1.58 to achieve parity in perplexity. Scaling to larger models (7 B, 13 B, 70 B) also used the same 100 B token budget, showing that the ternary representation does not require additional data to compensate for reduced precision. A supplementary experiment with **2 T tokens** (following StableLM‑3B’s recipe) further proves that the model can continue to improve with more data, achieving superior zero‑shot accuracy on several benchmarks.

5. **Mixed‑precision checkpointing** – To reduce training memory, store the ternary weights in their compact packed form on disk, while checkpointing the FP32 shadow copy only periodically (e.g., every $10$ k steps). This leverages the low storage cost of ternary weights while preserving the ability to resume training precisely.

By adhering to these practical guidelines, researchers can reproduce the results reported in the paper: **BitNet b1.58 matches full‑precision LLaMA models in perplexity and downstream task performance while delivering up to 4× speed‑ups, 3.5× lower GPU memory usage, and an order‑of‑magnitude reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy**.
