\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 4: # Training Methodology and Token Regimes

In this chapter we dissect the experimental protocol that underlies the construction of **BitNet b1.58**, the first 1.58‑bit large language model (LLM) capable of matching full‑precision baselines. The discussion is organized around the concrete steps taken by the authors: how they prepared the raw text corpora, the token budgets allotted to different model scales, the hyper‑parameter configurations that were held constant across sizes, the mixed‑precision training pipeline that accommodates ternary weights, and the practical tricks that made the training of such aggressively quantized models stable. All details are drawn directly from the reported study, without any external speculation.

---

## Dataset Preparation – RedPajama and StableLM Token Recipes

The authors based their training data on the **RedPajama** collection, a publicly released dataset that aggregates a wide variety of English‑language web sources (including Common Crawl, Wikipedia, books, and code). RedPajama was chosen because it provides a clean, large‑scale corpus that has become a de‑facto benchmark for open‑source LLM training.  

Two distinct token‑budget regimes are explored:

1. **100 billion (100 B) tokens** – this is the default budget for the primary scaling experiments (3 B, 7 B, 13 B, and 70 B model families). The authors pre‑trained each model from scratch on the full RedPajama corpus for exactly 100 B tokens, ensuring that all models receive the same amount of textual exposure regardless of size.

2. **2 trillion (2 T) tokens** – to probe the limits of scaling when more data is supplied, a separate low‑parameter (≈3 B) BitNet b1.58 model was trained on **2 T tokens**. For this regime the authors followed the **StableLM‑3B token recipe**, which specifies a particular ordering and weighting of the RedPajama constituents (mirroring the data pipeline used in the StableLM‑3B paper). By adhering to the same token ordering, the authors guarantee that any observed performance gains stem from the model architecture and quantization rather than differences in data processing.

In both regimes the tokenization pipeline is identical to that used for the LLaMA and LLaMA‑2 families, preserving compatibility with downstream evaluation harnesses such as *lm‑evaluation‑harness*. No additional preprocessing (e.g., aggressive filtering or data augmentation) was reported; the focus was on a faithful, reproducible reproduction of the standard RedPajama training set.

---

## Token Budgets: 100 B vs. 2 T Tokens

The **100 B token budget** serves as the baseline for comparing BitNet b1.58 against the FP16 LLaMA reference models. By fixing the token count, the authors isolate the effect of quantization on *per‑token* performance (perplexity and downstream accuracy). All model sizes—3 B, 7 B, 13 B, and 70 B—were trained for the same number of optimization steps (derived from the total token count divided by the batch size), which yields comparable training wall‑time across scales.

The **2 T token experiment** pushes the data regime an order of magnitude higher. This massive token budget is comparable to the training regimes of leading commercial LLMs, allowing the authors to assess whether the 1.58‑bit quantization imposes any hidden ceiling on generalization when abundant data is available. The results, summarized in the paper’s Table 4, show that the 2 T‑token BitNet b1.58 **outperforms** the StableLM‑3B baseline on every benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC‑easy). The key takeaway is that the ternary weight constraint does not hinder the model’s ability to absorb large amounts of linguistic information; instead, the richer token budget amplifies its strengths.

---

## Hyper‑Parameter Choices for 3 B, 7 B, 13 B, 70 B Models

Training a family of models that differ only in parameter count but share a common training recipe simplifies the analysis of scaling laws. The authors kept most hyper‑parameters identical across the four scales, mirroring the configuration of the original LLaMA models:

- **Architecture**: Transformer decoder with LLaMA‑style components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU activation, rotary positional embeddings, and **no bias terms** in linear layers. The only architectural deviation is the replacement of the standard `nn.Linear` with **BitLinear**, which performs the ternary weight quantization described later.

- **Embedding dimension and feed‑forward width**: Scaled according to the target model size (e.g., 3 B → 3 200 hidden dimension, 7 B → 4 800, etc.), following the same ratios as LLaMA.

- **Number of layers and attention heads**: Adjusted proportionally so that the overall parameter budget matches the intended model size.

- **Learning rate schedule**: A cosine decay schedule with a warm‑up phase. The peak learning rate was set to \(2 \times 10^{-4}\) for all scales. Warm‑up lasted for 1 % of the total training steps, after which the cosine decay took over until the final step.

- **Batch size**: Determined by the GPU memory budget of the training cluster. For the 100 B‑token runs, the effective per‑GPU batch size was held constant (e.g., 512 tokens per GPU) and scaled up using data parallelism to achieve the desired global batch size. The 2 T‑token experiment used the same per‑GPU batch size but ran for many more optimization steps.

- **Optimizer**: AdamW with \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.95\), weight decay of \(0.1\), and a cosine‑annealed learning rate multiplier.

- **Regularization**: Dropout was set to 0.0 (the original BitNet design omits dropout because the ternary weight constraint already provides regularization). Gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0 was applied to prevent exploding updates.

- **Mixed‑precision training**: The forward pass uses **8‑bit activations**, while the weight tensors are stored in ternary format (effectively 1.58 bits). The optimizer states (momentum buffers) remain in full‑precision FP32 to preserve stability, as discussed below.

By keeping the above settings uniform, the authors demonstrate that the performance gains of BitNet b1.58 arise from the **quantization strategy** rather than from any hidden hyper‑parameter tuning that benefits a particular size.

---

## Mixed‑Precision Training Pipelines for Ternary Weights

Training a model whose *inference* weights are ternary presents a unique challenge: the optimizer must operate on a representation that is more expressive than the final quantized values. The authors therefore employ a **two‑stage weight handling** scheme:

1. **Float‑32 master copy**: During training, each weight matrix \(W\) is kept in full‑precision (FP32) for the purpose of gradient accumulation and optimizer updates. This master copy receives the gradient step computed from the loss and is updated according to AdamW.

2. **Ternary projection**: Before each forward pass, the master weight \(W\) is **projected** onto its ternary counterpart \(\widetilde{W}\) using the **absmean quantization function**:
   \[
   \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|,\qquad
   \widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right),
   \]
   where \(\epsilon\) is a tiny constant to avoid division by zero and `RoundClip` clamps the rounded value to the interval \([-1,1]\). This operation ensures that during the forward computation the model behaves exactly as a ternary‑weight network, while still allowing the optimizer to explore a richer parameter space in the background.

3. **8‑bit activations**: After each linear projection, the resulting activations are quantized to 8 bits per token. Unlike earlier BitNet work, the authors shift the activation range to \([ -Q_b, \, Q_b ]\) (instead of \([0, Q_b]\)) to eliminate a zero‑point offset, simplifying hardware implementation and yielding negligible performance degradation.

4. **Backward pass**: Gradients are back‑propagated through the quantized forward graph, but the straight‑through estimator (STE) is implicitly used: the gradient of the rounding operation is approximated as identity, allowing gradients to flow from the loss to the FP32 master weights.

5. **Optimizer state precision**: Momentum buffers and the AdamW second‑moment estimates remain in FP32. This design choice is critical; quantizing these buffers would otherwise introduce excessive noise into the update direction, destabilizing training.

By iterating this cycle—project to ternary, forward with 8‑bit activations, backward with STE, update FP32 master—we obtain a training pipeline that respects the *inference* constraints of BitNet b1.58 while preserving the expressivity needed for convergence.

## Practical Tips for Stabilizing Training with Extreme Quantization

Training with such an aggressive quantization regime is prone to divergence if certain safeguards are not observed. The authors reported several empirical tricks that proved essential:

- **Scale Normalization via \(\gamma\)**: The absmean scaling factor \(\gamma\) normalizes each weight matrix by its average magnitude before rounding. This prevents the ternary projection from collapsing all values to zero when the raw weights are small, and conversely avoids blowing up the projected values when the raw weights are large.

- **Avoiding Zero‑Point in Activations**: By centering activations around zero (i.e., mapping to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\)), the model eliminates the need for a separate zero‑point offset. This reduces quantization error, especially for symmetric distributions typical of residual stream activations, and simplifies the hardware arithmetic path.

- **Gradient Clipping**: A global norm clip of 1.0 kept the updates within a stable regime, preventing occasional spikes caused by the discontinuities introduced by the rounding operation.

- **Warm‑up Phase**: A modest learning‑rate warm‑up (first 1 % of steps) helped the FP32 master weights settle into a region where the ternary projection would not lead to large sudden changes in the forward pass.

- **Retaining Full‑Precision Embeddings**: While all linear layers are ternarized, the token embedding matrix remains in full‑precision (FP16 in the paper’s implementation). This design decision preserves a rich input representation and ensures that the dominant memory consumption of the embedding does not dominate the overall footprint as the model grows.

- **Batch Size Consistency**: Maintaining the same per‑GPU batch size across scales simplifies the learning‑rate schedule and reduces variance in gradient estimates, which is particularly valuable when the forward pass is heavily quantized.

- **Hardware‑Friendly Kernels**: The authors integrated a 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder library into the FasterTransformer inference stack. Although this kernel is primarily used for evaluation, employing the same low‑precision kernels during training (where possible) can reduce the discrepancy between training and inference behaviors.

Collectively, these practices enabled the successful training of models up to **70 B parameters** with ternary weights, a scale previously thought unattainable for 1‑bit‑style architectures. The stability of the training pipeline is evident in the reported perplexities and zero‑shot accuracies, which match or surpass the FP16 baselines at comparable model sizes.

---

### Summary

The methodology behind BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that **extreme weight quantization (to ternary values) can be combined with conventional large‑scale language modeling pipelines** when careful attention is paid to data preparation, token budgeting, hyper‑parameter uniformity, mixed‑precision training mechanics, and stability tricks. By training from scratch on the RedPajama corpus with either 100 B or 2 T tokens, and by leveraging a master‑weight + ternary‑projection scheme, the authors achieve a **new scaling law**: models with far fewer bits per parameter deliver comparable (or better) performance while cutting latency, memory, and energy consumption dramatically. This chapter has outlined each component of that pipeline, providing a concrete roadmap for researchers who wish to reproduce or extend the 1.58‑bit LLM paradigm.
