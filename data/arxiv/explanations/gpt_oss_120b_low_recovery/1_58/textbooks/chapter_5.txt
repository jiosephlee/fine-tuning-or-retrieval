\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 5: # Performance Evaluation – Perplexity and Zero‑Shot Accuracy  

The empirical core of the “1‑bit LLM” narrative rests on a careful comparison between the newly introduced **BitNet b1.58** and the conventional full‑precision LLaMA family.  In this chapter we unpack the reported results, focusing on language‑modeling perplexity, zero‑shot performance on a diverse set of downstream benchmarks, and the scaling behavior that reveals the point at which the 1.58‑bit model catches up with its FP16 counterpart.  Throughout we remain faithful to the measurements and observations presented in the original work, interpreting them through the lens of statistical significance and the architectural impact of the added zero weight.

---  

## Perplexity Benchmarks on WikiText‑2 and C4  

Perplexity (PPL) is the standard metric for evaluating the quality of a language model’s probability distribution over a held‑out corpus.  Lower values indicate that the model assigns higher likelihood to the test sentences, which in turn correlates with better downstream performance.  The authors measured PPL on two canonical corpora:

1. **WikiText‑2**, a relatively small, well‑curated dataset derived from Wikipedia articles, often used for rapid prototyping.  
2. **C4**, a massive web‑scraped corpus that better reflects the diversity and scale of modern LLM training data.

Both BitNet b1.58 and the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baselines were trained from scratch on the RedPajama dataset for **100 billion tokens**, ensuring a fair token‑budget comparison.  Table 1 (referred to as Table \ref{tab:ppl} in the paper) reports the validation perplexities across model sizes ranging from 3 B to 70 B parameters.

The key observations are:

- For the **3 B** configuration, BitNet b1.58 attains a PPL that is statistically indistinguishable from the FP16 LLaMA 3 B model on both WikiText‑2 and C4.  The numerical gap is within the typical variance observed across repeated training runs (≈ 0.5 % relative difference).  
- As the model scales, the 1.58‑bit variant not only preserves parity but often **improves** the perplexity slightly.  For example, the **3.9 B** BitNet version yields a lower PPL than the FP16 3 B baseline, suggesting that the extra capacity from the larger hidden dimension outweighs any potential loss from quantization.  
- The trend continues up to the **70 B** configuration, where BitNet b1.58 remains within a few percentage points of the FP16 LLaMA perplexity while enjoying dramatically reduced memory and latency (as discussed in later sections).

These results substantiate the claim that a ternary weight matrix—constrained to \{-1, 0, +1\}—does not inherently limit the model’s ability to learn a high‑quality distribution over text, provided that the training recipe (token count, optimizer settings, and architectural tweaks such as RMSNorm and SwiGLU) is preserved.

## Zero‑Shot Evaluation Suite  

Zero‑shot evaluation measures a model’s ability to solve tasks without any task‑specific fine‑tuning, by prompting it directly with a description or a few exemplars.  The authors employed the **lm‑evaluation‑harness** pipeline to assess BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baselines on a broad suite of benchmarks that collectively probe reasoning, factual knowledge, and commonsense understanding:

| Benchmark | Primary Skill | Example Metric |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| ARC‑Easy (ARC‑E) | Elementary science reasoning | Accuracy |
| ARC‑Challenge (ARC‑C) | Harder science reasoning | Accuracy |
| Hellaswag | Common‑sense reasoning over multi‑choice | Accuracy |
| Winogrande | Pronoun disambiguation | Accuracy |
| PIQA | Physical commonsense | Accuracy |
| OpenBookQA | Knowledge retrieval | Accuracy |
| BoolQ | Binary question answering | Accuracy |

All models were evaluated **zero‑shot**, i.e., the prompt consisted only of the task description and the input, with no gradient updates.  Table 2 (Table \ref{tab:zero-shot}) compiles the resulting accuracies.

**Scaling trends:**  
- At the **3 B** scale, BitNet b1.58’s scores are modestly lower than FP16 LLaMA, typically lagging by 1–2 percentage points on the hardest tasks (ARC‑C, Winogrande).  
- When the model size reaches **3.9 B**, the gap shrinks dramatically; BitNet b1.58 actually **outperforms** the FP16 3 B baseline on several tasks while remaining within the statistical noise of the FP16 3.9 B results.  
- For the larger **13 B** and **70 B** models, the performance curves of the two precision regimes converge, with BitNet b1.58 often matching or slightly surpassing the FP16 scores.  The authors explicitly note that “the performance gap narrows as the model size increases,” a phenomenon consistent with the observation that larger models are more robust to quantization noise.

The convergence of zero‑shot accuracy confirms the perplexity findings: the ternary weight representation does not harm the model’s ability to generalize to unseen tasks, and the **additional zero weight** appears to facilitate this robustness.

## Interpretation of Scaling Trends: When Does b1.58 Match FP16?  

The paper emphasizes a **new scaling law** governing the trade‑off between model size, inference cost, and performance in the 1.58‑bit regime.  Empirically, the crossover point where BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 can be summarized as follows:

- **3 B**: Perplexity parity achieved; zero‑shot gap still present but minimal.  
- **≈ 3.5–4 B**: Both perplexity and zero‑shot metrics of BitNet b1.58 become statistically indistinguishable from FP16 LLaMA of the same size.  This is the region the authors label as “starting from a 3 B size” where the 1.58‑bit model **matches** the full‑precision baseline.  
- **≥ 7 B**: BitNet b1.58 begins to **exceed** the FP16 baseline in efficiency (latency, memory) while maintaining equivalent or better task performance, establishing a Pareto improvement.

These observations are visualized in the paper’s latency‑memory‑energy figures (Figures \ref{fig:latency-memory-energy} and \ref{fig:energy}), which show that as the parameter count grows, the proportion of computation devoted to the **nn.Linear** layers—now replaced by **BitLinear**—increases.  Since BitLinear eliminates multiplications, the relative advantage of the ternary representation grows super‑linearly, explaining why the larger models enjoy greater speed‑ups (up to **4.1×** for 70 B) while preserving accuracy.

## Statistical Significance and Error Analysis of the Gaps  

When reporting differences of a few percentage points in zero‑shot accuracy or sub‑percent variations in perplexity, it is essential to assess whether these gaps are meaningful or merely statistical fluctuations.  The authors address this in two ways:

1. **Repeated Runs:** Although not explicitly enumerated, the standard practice in LLM benchmarking involves training at least two seeds per configuration.  The reported “negligible effects” of the activation scaling change (from $[0, Q_b]$ to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$) suggests that the variance across seeds was low enough to treat the numbers as stable.  

2. **Confidence Intervals:** The paper presents the zero‑shot results with **average accuracies** across tasks, implicitly smoothing over task‑specific noise.  For tasks measured both by raw accuracy and normalized accuracy, the authors average the two, further reducing variance.  When the difference between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 falls within **±0.5 %**, it is reasonable to claim statistical parity.

The **error analysis** also highlights the role of the **zero weight**.  By allowing a weight of 0, the ternary matrix can effectively **disable** certain connections, acting as a learned feature filter.  This flexibility helps mitigate the quantization error that would otherwise arise from forcing every weight to be either –1 or +1.  Empirically, the inclusion of 0 leads to modest but consistent gains in both PPL and downstream accuracy, especially at the 3 B–4 B scale where the model’s capacity is still limited and selective pruning of noisy connections is most beneficial.

## Effect of the Additional Zero Weight on Feature Filtering  

The original 1‑bit BitNet constrained all weights to the binary set \{-1, +1\}.  BitNet b1.58 extends this to a **ternary** set \{-1, 0, +1\}, thereby increasing the *effective* information content per weight from 1 bit to **1.58 bits** (the logarithm base‑2 of 3).  This modest increase yields two concrete algorithmic advantages:

1. **Sparse Connectivity:**  A weight of 0 nullifies the contribution of the corresponding input feature to the output neuron.  In matrix multiplication terms, this is equivalent to **skipping an addition**.  Since the overall computation is already dominated by integer additions, the ability to omit certain terms reduces the number of memory fetches and addition operations, providing secondary latency and energy benefits beyond the raw bit‑width reduction.

2. **Feature Filtering:**  By learning to set specific weights to 0, the model can **mask out noisy or irrelevant dimensions** in the activation space.  This acts as an adaptive regularizer, encouraging the network to focus its representational power on the most salient patterns.  The empirical impact of this mechanism is reflected in the **faster convergence of perplexity** and the **narrowing of zero‑shot gaps** observed around the 3‑4 B parameter regime.  The paper explicitly notes that “its modeling capability is stronger due to its explicit support for feature filtering, made possible by the inclusion of 0 in the model weights.”

Thus, the added zero value is not a mere technical curiosity but a functional enhancement that contributes to the observed parity—and occasional superiority—of the 1.58‑bit models relative to full‑precision baselines.

---  

### Synthesis  

The performance evaluation presented in the paper paints a coherent picture: **BitNet b1.58 delivers the same language‑modeling quality and zero‑shot task competence as FP16 LLaMA while dramatically reducing inference cost**.  The empirical evidence—perplexity matching at 3 B, zero‑shot parity by 3.9 B, and superior efficiency at larger scales—supports the authors’ claim of a new scaling law for 1.58‑bit LLMs.  Crucially, the **introduction of the zero weight** furnishes the model with a simple yet powerful mechanism for feature selection, which helps bridge the small residual performance gaps that arise from aggressive quantization.  As model sizes continue to grow, the computational savings compound, positioning 1.58‑bit LLMs as a promising direction for both research exploration and real‑world deployment on cost‑sensitive hardware.
