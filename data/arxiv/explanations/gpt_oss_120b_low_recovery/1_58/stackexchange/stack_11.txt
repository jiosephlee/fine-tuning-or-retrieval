\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is it possible to train BitNet b1.58 with fewer than 100 B tokens and still match FP16 performance?
Question:
All experiments use 100 B tokens (or 2 T tokens for the large‑scale test). I wonder if the quantization introduces a higher data requirement for convergence. Has anyone tried training a 3 B BitNet b1.58 on, say, 30 B tokens, and if so, how does the perplexity compare to the FP16 baseline? Understanding the token‑efficiency would help assess the practicality of the approach.
Answer:
The short answer is **no – the paper does not provide any evidence that a 3 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 can reach the same perplexity as an FP16 LLaMA‑style model when it is trained on substantially fewer than the 100 B tokens used in the reported experiments**. All of the quantitative results (Table 1, Table 2, and the scaling‑law discussion) are obtained with the *exact* token budget that the FP16 baselines use, and the authors do not report any ablation that varies the number of training tokens.

### Why the token budget matters

BitNet b1.58 is a *trained‑from‑scratch* model; the ternary weight constraint ( –1, 0, +1 ) is imposed by the **abs‑mean quantization** function during every weight update. This quantization introduces a small amount of additional stochasticity compared with FP16 training, because each real‑valued weight is repeatedly snapped to one of three discrete values. The authors argue that the extra expressive capacity supplied by the zero weight (the “0” in the ternary set) compensates for the loss of precision, but this compensation is only demonstrated **when the model sees the full 100 B‑token corpus**.

In conventional LLM scaling work, perplexity improves roughly as a power‑law of the product *model size × tokens*. If you keep the model size fixed (3 B) and cut the token count to 30 B, the expected increase in perplexity is on the order of 20‑30 % for FP16 models (see the original Chinchilla scaling law). Because BitNet b1.58 already operates at the edge of the precision‑vs‑capacity trade‑off, removing a factor of three of the data will almost certainly push its perplexity **above** the FP16 baseline that was trained on the same 30 B tokens.

### What the paper actually says about token scaling

The only token‑scaling experiment in the manuscript is the **2 T‑token** training of a 3 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 (Section “Training with 2T Tokens”). There the authors show that, *with more data*, the 1.58‑bit model *outperforms* a state‑of‑the‑art FP16 3 B model (StableLM‑3B) on a suite of zero‑shot tasks. This is presented as evidence that the new scaling law “holds” for BitNet b1.58, not that the model can **save** tokens.

The authors also state explicitly (in the abstract and the “Results” section) that **BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 when “using the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.)”**. The phrase “same configuration” is repeated multiple times, which makes it clear that the equivalence claim is conditional on using the *identical* token budget.

### Practical implication

If you trained a 3 B BitNet b1.58 on 30 B tokens you should expect:

1. **Higher validation perplexity** than the 3 B FP16 LLaMA baseline trained on 30 B tokens (the paper’s Table 1 shows a gap of roughly 0.3‑0.5 ppl when the token count is held constant; with fewer tokens the gap will widen).
2. **Worse zero‑shot task accuracy** for the same reason; the zero‑shot scores in Table 2 already narrow as token count grows, so with only 30 B tokens the 1.58‑bit model would trail its FP16 counterpart.
3. No known mitigation in the current recipe (e.g., longer training epochs, larger batch size, or different learning‑rate schedules) is reported to close this gap.

Therefore, if your goal is to **reduce the pre‑training token budget while preserving FP16‑level perplexity**, the current BitNet b1.58 recipe does not support that. You would either need to train longer (i.e., keep the 100 B token budget) or wait for future work that investigates data‑efficiency techniques (e.g., curriculum learning, mixed‑precision warm‑up, or larger model sizes) specifically for the ternary weight regime.

### Bottom line

- The paper demonstrates parity with FP16 *only* when the same number of tokens (100 B for the main experiments) is used.  
- No experiments are reported with a reduced token budget such as 30 B, so we cannot claim that BitNet b1.58 is token‑efficient enough to match FP16 under those conditions.  
- Based on known scaling behavior, training on 30 B tokens would almost certainly lead to a noticeable perplexity increase relative to the FP16 baseline.  

If you need a concrete figure, you would have to run your own ablation; the authors have not published any such results.
