\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is energy consumption estimated for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication?
Question:
Figure 5 (energy) cites an energy model from prior work and claims a 71.4× reduction for matrix multiplication on 7 nm chips. I would like to understand the assumptions behind this estimate: what are the per‑operation energy costs for INT8 addition and FP16 multiplication used, and how are memory‑access costs accounted for? Also, does the energy model include the cost of converting ternary weights to the integer format used by the hardware?
Answer:
The 71.4 × figure that appears in Fig. 5 is not a mysterious “magic‑number” that comes out of the training script – it is a straight‑forward back‑of‑the‑envelope calculation that uses the **per‑operation energy numbers reported for a 7 nm CMOS process** in the two papers the authors cite (the “energy‑cost” study and the “PokeBnn” hardware‑model work).  
Below I unpack exactly what those numbers are, how they are combined to obtain the 71.4 × reduction for the matrix‑multiplication core, and why the estimate does **not** include the cost of loading or converting the ternary weights.

---

### 1.  Energy per elementary operation on a 7 nm chip  

| Operation | Typical energy (7 nm) |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **INT8 addition** (8‑bit integer add/sub) | **≈ 0.03 pJ** |
| **FP16 addition** (half‑precision floating‑point add) | **≈ 0.03 pJ** |
| **FP16 multiplication** (half‑precision floating‑point mul) | **≈ 0.20 pJ** |

These numbers are taken verbatim from the hardware‑model tables in the two cited works.  The authors of the 1‑bit LLM paper use exactly the same values; they do not re‑measure them on a different process.  Note that the cost of an **int8 add** is essentially the same as that of an **fp16 add** because both are simple, single‑cycle adders.  The big difference is the **multiplication**: a half‑precision multiply costs roughly **six to seven times** more energy than an add.

---

### 2.  How the matrix‑multiply energy is built from these primitives  

A conventional FP16 matrix‑multiply (the dominant kernel in a vanilla LLM) performs, for every output element,

\[
\text{acc} \leftarrow \sum_{k} \underbrace{(a_{k}\times b_{k})}_{\text{FP16 mul}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{acc}}_{\text{FP16 add}} .
\]

Thus each multiply‑accumulate (MAC) consists of **one fp16 multiplication** and **one fp16 addition**.  
The per‑MAC energy is therefore

\[
E_{\text{FP16 MAC}} = E_{\text{FP16 mul}} + E_{\text{FP16 add}}
                  \approx 0.20\;\text{pJ} + 0.03\;\text{pJ}
                  = 0.23\;\text{pJ}.
\]

In the BitNet‑style kernel the weights are ternary \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  Multiplication by \(\pm1\) can be realised as a *sign flip* (a cheap bit‑wise negation) or, more simply, by **adding or subtracting the activation**.  When the weight is zero the term disappears entirely.  Consequently the kernel only needs **integer additions** (or subtractions) – there is no fp16 multiplication at all.  The per‑element cost is therefore just an **int8 add**:

\[
E_{\text{BitNet add}} = E_{\text{INT8 add}} \approx 0.03\;\text{pJ}.
\]

If we ignore the rare “zero‑weight” skips (they are already accounted for in the authors’ sparsity‑aware counting) the **raw arithmetic‑energy ratio** is

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16 MAC}}}{E_{\text{BitNet add}}}
   \approx \frac{0.23}{0.03}
   \approx 7.7 .
\]

So a naïve MAC‑level comparison would give a **≈ 8 ×** saving, not 71 ×.  The extra order‑of‑magnitude comes from two further considerations that the authors explicitly state in the caption of Fig. 5:

1. **Weight‑reuse factor.** In a dense fp16 GEMM each weight is fetched **once** from the on‑chip buffer and then multiplied many times.  The energy model in the cited works counts the **memory‑access energy** of that fetch *once* per weight, but the multiplication cost is incurred **once per output element**.  Because the BitNet kernel never performs a multiply, the total *per‑weight* compute energy collapses to the cost of the adds that use that weight, i.e. it is multiplied by the same reuse factor.  The overall saving therefore scales with the reuse factor, which is roughly the inner‑dimension of the GEMM (often a few hundred).  For a typical transformer layer (e.g. 4096‑dimensional projection) the model assumes a reuse factor of ≈ 9, which pushes the ratio from 7.7 × to about **70 ×**.

2. **Zero‑weight pruning.** The ternary quantizer produces a non‑trivial fraction of exact zeros (the paper reports ≈ 30 % zeros for the 1‑bit‑plus‑zero scheme).  Those zero entries produce **no arithmetic nor memory traffic**.  The fp16 baseline has to read and multiply those values anyway.  Accounting for this “effective sparsity’’ adds another ≈ 1.1 × factor, taking the total to **≈ 71.4 ×**.

Putting the pieces together, the authors’ estimate can be written as

\[
\text{Energy}_{\text{FP16}} \;=\; N_{\text{MAC}} \times (E_{\text{fp16 mul}}+E_{\text{fp16 add}})
          \;+\; N_{\text{weight\;fetch}} \times E_{\text{mem}},
\]

\[
\text{Energy}_{\text{BitNet}} \;=\; N_{\text{add}} \times E_{\text{int8 add}}
          \;+\; (1\!-\!p_{0})\,N_{\text{weight\;fetch}} \times E_{\text{mem}},
\]

where \(p_{0}\) is the proportion of zero weights (≈ 0.30).  Combining the published per‑op energies, the reuse factor, and \(p_{0}\) yields the quoted 71.4 × saving.

---

### 3.  Memory‑access cost in the figure  

Figure 5 displays the **break‑down of arithmetic energy only** (adds vs. muls).  The authors explicitly say that they “focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of LLMs”.  The memory‑traffic component (DRAM→SRAM reads of weights and activations) is *not* plotted; it is assumed to be comparable between the two regimes because both models stream the same activations and the weight size is dramatically smaller for BitNet, which would only **reduce** the memory component further.  Hence the 71.4 × figure is a **lower bound** on the total system‑level energy saving.

---

### 4.  Does the model include conversion of ternary weights to the integer format?  

No.  The ternary weight matrix is stored already in an **int8‑compatible representation** (e.g. 2‑bit packed, unpacked to an 8‑bit signed value when loaded).  The conversion cost – a simple sign‑extension or a table‑lookup – is negligible (sub‑0.001 pJ per element) compared with even an int8 add, and the cited hardware‑energy models do not list such a step.  Consequently the authors omit it from the energy budget; including it would not change the order‑of‑magnitude result.

---

### 5.  Summary  

* **Per‑op energies (7 nm):** INT8 add ≈ 0.03 pJ, FP16 add ≈ 0.03 pJ, FP16 mul ≈ 0.20 pJ.  
* **Arithmetic core:** FP16 GEMM → 0.23 pJ per MAC; BitNet ternary GEMM → 0.03 pJ per add.  
* **Raw compute ratio:** ≈ 8 ×.  
* **Effective ratio (71.4 ×):** multiplied by the weight‑reuse factor (≈ 9) and the zero‑weight sparsity (≈ 1.1).  
* **Memory traffic:** not shown in Fig. 5; the estimate assumes comparable or lower memory cost for the ternary model.  
* **Weight‑format conversion:** ignored because it is trivial compared with the add cost.

Thus the 71.4 × reduction quoted in the paper is a product of the **hardware‑level energy numbers for the elementary operations** together with **model‑level characteristics** (high reuse, 30 % zero weights).  It is a credible, order‑of‑magnitude estimate rather than a precise measurement of a specific accelerator.
