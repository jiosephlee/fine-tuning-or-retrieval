\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function compute the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors define the quantization function for weights as \( \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1) \) with \( \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}| \). I am unsure whether \(\gamma\) is recomputed for every weight matrix at every training step, or if it is a fixed statistic computed once from the initial weights. Also, what is the role of the small constant \(\epsilon\) – is it just to avoid division by zero, or does it affect the quantization granularity? Any clarification on the implementation details would help.
Answer:
The “abs‑mean” quantizer that BitNet b1.58 uses is a *per‑layer* scaling that is tied to the current distribution of the full‑precision weights.  
For a weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) the scale is defined as  

\[
\gamma \;=\;\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|
\]

i.e. the arithmetic mean of the absolute values of all entries in the matrix.  
During training the authors **re‑compute \(\gamma\) at every forward pass** (hence at every training step) for each matrix that is quantised. In the implementation this amounts to a single `torch.mean(torch.abs(W))` before the call to `RoundClip`. Because the weights change continuously while the optimiser updates them, keeping \(\gamma\) fixed would quickly become a stale statistic and would no longer reflect the true scale of the tensor; the ternary mapping would then either saturate too many entries to \(\pm1\) or leave too many at 0, degrading both training stability and final accuracy. Re‑computing the scale at every step therefore preserves the intended property that the three quantisation levels are roughly equally populated throughout training.

The small constant \(\varepsilon\) that appears in the denominator

\[
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\,-1,\,1\Bigr)
\]

serves two purposes:

1. **Numerical safety.** If a particular layer were to become exactly zero (e.g. at the very beginning of training before any gradient has been applied, or after aggressive pruning), \(\gamma\) would be zero and division would be undefined. Adding \(\varepsilon>0\) guarantees a finite divisor.

2. **Granularity control.** The value of \(\varepsilon\) effectively smoothes the scaling factor. When \(\gamma\) is very small, the denominator is dominated by \(\varepsilon\), which makes the ratio \(W/(\gamma+\varepsilon)\) larger in magnitude and therefore pushes more entries to the clipped values \(\pm1\). Conversely, a larger \(\varepsilon\) reduces the magnitude of the scaled weights, causing more entries to fall into the interval \((-0.5,0.5)\) and be rounded to 0. In practice the authors use a tiny constant (on the order of \(10^{-6}\) or the machine epsilon of the datatype) so that its effect on the granularity is negligible; its role is essentially that of a safeguard against division‑by‑zero without noticeably altering the quantisation thresholds.

In short, **\(\gamma\) is recomputed for every weight matrix at each training step**, keeping the ternary quantiser adaptive to the evolving weight distribution, and **\(\varepsilon\) is a tiny additive term that prevents division by zero while having no meaningful impact on the quantisation resolution**. This is precisely how the “abs‑mean” quantiser is implemented in the BitNet b1.58 code base.
