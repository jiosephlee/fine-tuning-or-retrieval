\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What are the challenges of extending BitNet b1.58 to Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures?
Question:
In the discussion, the authors mention that 1.58‑bit LLMs could alleviate the memory and communication overhead of MoE models. However, MoE typically involves routing matrices and expert-specific parameters. How would ternary quantization interact with the routing softmax and the per‑expert weight matrices? Are there any known techniques for quantizing the routing logits without degrading the expert selection quality?
Answer:
The authors only sketch the idea of “1‑bit MoE” in the discussion, so the concrete engineering questions you raise are not solved in the paper itself.  What we do know, however, is how BitNet b1.58 works and where the difficulties of a MoE‑style architecture would arise when we try to apply the same ternary quantisation.

### Why MoE is a different beast

A conventional MoE layer consists of three logical parts:

1. **Routing logits** – a small dense matrix that maps the current token (or token‑group) to a *logit* for every expert.  
2. **Soft‑max (or top‑k) selection** – the logits are normalised (usually with a soft‑max) and the top‑k experts are chosen for each token.  
3. **Expert weight matrices** – each expert is an independent feed‑forward sub‑network (often a 2‑layer MLP) with its own parameters.

The first two items are *control* information, not the bulk of the FLOPs, but they are *high‑precision* in practice because tiny perturbations of the logits can change which expert(s) a token is sent to.  The expert parameters, on the other hand, dominate the compute and memory, so they are the natural target for aggressive quantisation such as the ternary scheme used in BitNet b1.58.

### Interplay with ternary weight quantisation

In BitNet b1.58 each weight matrix **W** is replaced by a ternary matrix  

\[
\widetilde W = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma},-1,1\right), \qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|,
\]

and the forward pass is performed with integer additions only (the activations are 8‑bit).  This works cleanly for a single linear layer because the matrix multiplication can be rewritten as a sum of signed integer‑add‑operations.

If we simply plug this into every expert’s two‑layer MLP, the *per‑expert* computation is unchanged – each expert becomes a “bit‑linear” block that can be executed with the same INT‑add kernels as the backbone transformer.  Memory consumption per expert also drops from 16‑bit to 1.58‑bit, which directly addresses the MoE memory bottleneck that the paper highlights.

### The routing soft‑max is the fragile part

The routing logits are **not** ternarised in the current BitNet formulation.  The paper never quantises the logits; it only mentions that a 1.58‑bit model “could alleviate the memory and communication overhead of MoE models”.  The reason is that, even if the experts are ternary, the *routing tensor* still has to be communicated across devices (or across the on‑chip network) because each token must know which expert(s) to query.  Quantising this tensor too aggressively would change the relative magnitudes of the logits and could cause a token to be sent to the wrong expert, which in turn would degrade both the model’s capacity and its load‑balancing properties.

There are a few techniques that have been explored in other low‑precision MoE work and could be transplanted here:

| Technique | What it does | Compatibility with ternary experts |
|----------|--------------|------------------------------------|
| **Logit‑scale quantisation** (e.g. 8‑bit fixed‑point) | Keeps the ordering of logits but reduces memory. | The expert weights stay ternary; only the routing buffer is reduced. |
| **Top‑k hard routing without soft‑max** | Instead of a full soft‑max, compute the top‑k scores via a cheap arg‑max and send a token to those experts directly. | Removes the need for a high‑precision soft‑max, making it easier to quantise the scores to a few bits. |
| **Gumbel‑Softmax with low‑precision sampling** | Replaces the deterministic soft‑max with a stochastic selector that can tolerate more noise. | May allow coarser quantisation of the logits because the selection is already stochastic. |

None of these methods appear in the BitNet b1.58 paper, so they would have to be added as *future work*.

### Practical challenges

1. **Dynamic range of routing logits** – The logits for a large MoE (e.g. 64‑128 experts) can have a much larger dynamic range than the weight matrices, which are normalised by their mean absolute value.  A naïve ternary quantiser would clip most of the signal to ±1 and lose the subtle differences needed for balanced routing.  Hence a *different* scaling strategy (per‑token or per‑expert) is required.

2. **Load‑balancing loss** – MoE training typically adds an auxiliary loss that encourages each expert to receive roughly the same number of tokens.  This loss is computed on the soft‑max probabilities.  If the probabilities are heavily quantised, the gradient signal becomes noisy and the balancing term may fail, leading to expert collapse (most tokens routed to a few experts).

3. **Cross‑device communication** – Even if the expert weights are ternary, the activations that travel between the router and the selected experts are still 8‑bit (as in BitNet b1.58).  When the number of experts grows, the total activation traffic can dominate the bandwidth, so the memory savings on the weights do not automatically translate into proportional communication savings.

4. **Kernel support** – The paper relies on a custom 2‑bit kernel (Ladder) for the ternary matrix multiplication.  Extending this kernel to support a *batched* MoE operation, where each token may be multiplied by a different expert’s weight matrix, requires additional indexing logic that can erode the integer‑add speed‑up if not carefully engineered.

### Take‑away

In short, the main obstacle is **quantising the routing path** rather than the expert weights themselves.  The ternary quantisation that works so well for the dense transformer layers can be applied directly to each MoE expert, giving the same memory‑size and energy reductions that the authors claim.  However, to keep the routing quality intact you need either a higher‑precision representation for the logits (e.g. 8‑bit fixed‑point) or a redesign of the routing algorithm (hard top‑k, Gumbel‑Softmax, etc.) that is robust to coarse quantisation.  Until such techniques are incorporated and evaluated, the statement in the discussion remains a promising hypothesis rather than a demonstrated capability.
