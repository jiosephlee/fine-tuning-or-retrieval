\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does the embedding layer remain full‑precision in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The paper notes that the embedding layer is not quantized, which means its memory proportion becomes smaller for larger models. Why was the embedding kept at FP16/FP32 instead of being ternarized or quantized to 8‑bit? Would quantizing the embedding break the “feature filtering” benefit of the zero weight, or is it simply a practical limitation?
Answer:
The authors keep the token‑embedding matrix in full‑precision (FP16/BF16) for two intertwined reasons that are both practical and methodological, and the decision does not interfere with the “feature‑filtering’’ advantage that comes from the zero weight in the ternary layers.

First, the embedding matrix is the *only* parameter block whose size does **not** grow proportionally with the depth of the transformer.  
If the model has \(P\) transformer parameters and an embedding of size \(V\times d\) (vocabulary size \(V\), hidden dimension \(d\)), the fraction occupied by the embedding is  

\[
\frac{V d}{P+V d}.
\]

When we move from a 3‑B to a 70‑B model, \(P\) increases by more than an order of magnitude while \(V d\) stays the same (the vocabulary is fixed). Consequently the embedding’s share of total memory shrinks dramatically; the paper even notes that “its memory proportion becomes smaller for larger models”. Because the embedding is already a modest fraction of the overall footprint in the large‑scale regime, the potential memory savings from quantising it are limited compared with the savings obtained by ternarising *all* the linear layers, which dominate the parameter budget.

Second, the embedding layer plays a special role in preserving the semantic quality of the discrete input tokens. Each row of the matrix is a learned vector that must capture fine‑grained distinctions among millions of word‐pieces. Empirically, post‑training quantisation of the embedding (e.g., 8‑bit or ternary) tends to cause a noticeable degradation in downstream perplexity, especially for models that are trained from scratch. The authors therefore keep the embeddings in FP16/BF16 to avoid that source of error and to let the training dynamics focus on learning the ternary weights in the transformer blocks.

Regarding the “feature‑filtering’’ effect of the zero weight: this effect originates from the sparsity that the ternary quantiser injects into the **linear** layers (the \(\mathrm{BitLinear}\) modules). It works by allowing a weight to be exactly zero, which effectively disables the corresponding multiplication and lets the network learn to ignore irrelevant features. Quantising the embedding would not add any new zeros to the transformer’s computation; it would merely reduce the precision of the input vectors that are later multiplied by ternary matrices. Hence, the presence or absence of zeros in the embedding does not diminish the filtering benefit already achieved by the ternary weight matrices.

In short, the embedding is left in full precision because:

1. **Memory impact is marginal at scale** – the embedding’s share of the total parameters shrinks as the model grows, so quantising it yields relatively little extra memory reduction.
2. **Training stability and token‑level expressivity** – full‑precision embeddings preserve the quality of the learned token representations, which is crucial when the rest of the network is heavily quantised.
3. **The zero‑weight “feature‑filtering’’ mechanism concerns only the ternary linear layers**, so quantising the embedding would not interfere with that mechanism.

These considerations together explain why the paper reports the embedding as the sole full‑precision component of BitNet b1.58.
