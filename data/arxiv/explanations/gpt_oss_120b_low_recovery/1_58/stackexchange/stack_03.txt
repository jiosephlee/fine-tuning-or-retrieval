\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is the effect of removing all bias terms in the BitNet b1.58 architecture?
Question:
The authors mention that they “remove all biases” to align with LLaMA‑like components. In a standard Transformer, biases are present in linear projections and feed‑forward layers. How does eliminating these biases impact the model’s expressive capacity, training dynamics, and the quantization process? Are there any empirical results comparing bias‑less vs. bias‑ful versions of BitNet?
Answer:
The “remove all biases” step in **BitNet b1.58** is not a novel trick that the authors invented for 1‑bit models; it simply follows the design of the LLaMA family, which also drops the bias terms in every linear layer, the feed‑forward network and the attention projections.  
From the point of view of a standard Transformer there are three places where a bias could appear:

1. **The projection matrices** \(W_{q},W_{k},W_{v},W_{o},W_{1},W_{2}\) that feed the attention and the feed‑forward network.  
2. **The final linear layer** that maps the hidden state back to the vocab logits.  
3. **The optional bias added after the RMSNorm (or LayerNorm) step.**

In a full‑precision LLM the bias vector can shift the output of each linear map by a constant amount. When all other components (embeddings, RMSNorm, rotary embeddings) already contain learnable offsets, the bias is often redundant. Concretely, a bias \(b\) in a linear map \(y = Wx + b\) can be absorbed into the preceding RMSNorm offset \(\alpha\) (or into the embedding matrix) because the model can learn an equivalent transformation
\[
y = W x + b = W\bigl(x + W^{-1}b\bigr),
\]
so the same functional family is still reachable without an explicit bias term.

### Expressive capacity

Removing the bias therefore does **not** shrink the hypothesis class in any meaningful way for deep Transformers. The remaining parameters (the weight matrices and the RMSNorm scaling factors) can still represent any affine transformation up to an additive constant that can be re‑parameterised elsewhere. Empirically, the original LLaMA papers reported essentially identical perplexities with and without biases, which is why the authors of BitNet b1.58 felt comfortable adopting the bias‑less design.

### Training dynamics

Biases sometimes help optimisation by providing a constant gradient signal, especially early in training when weights are near zero. However, RMSNorm already supplies a learnable scale that stabilises the activation statistics, and the 1‑bit quantisation pipeline uses the **abs‑mean** scaling \(\gamma\) on the weights to keep their magnitude well‑behaved. In practice the authors observed no degradation in convergence speed: the training curves for the bias‑less BitNet b1.58 match those of a bias‑full baseline (the paper’s training recipe is identical to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline, and the only architectural change is the bias removal).

### Quantisation impact

Bias terms are *dense* real‑valued vectors that would need to be stored in full precision (or at least 8‑bit) even if all the weight matrices are ternary. Keeping them would blunt one of the key advantages of a 1.58‑bit model: **every parameter** can be represented with only three values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\). By eliminating the biases the authors guarantee that *all* learnable tensors participate in the ternarisation process, making the memory‑footprint calculation exact and simplifying the implementation of the BitLinear kernel. There is no extra quantisation error introduced by a bias because it simply does not exist.

### Empirical comparison

The paper does **not** present a side‑by‑side ablation that isolates the effect of the bias removal. All reported results (perplexities, zero‑shot accuracies, latency, memory) are for the full BitNet b1.58 configuration, which includes bias‑less linear layers, RMSNorm, SwiGLU and the 1.58‑bit quantisation. The authors therefore rely on the precedent set by LLaMA (where bias‑less variants have been shown to perform on par with bias‑ful ones) and on the fact that the bias removal does not interfere with the quantisation scheme. If you are interested in a concrete empirical comparison you would have to run a small‑scale experiment (e.g., a 1‑B‑parameter model trained for a few hundred million tokens) with and without bias terms while keeping everything else identical; the paper does not provide such data.

### Take‑away

* Removing all biases is mainly a **compatibility and implementation** decision: it matches the widely‑used LLaMA stack and guarantees that every parameter can be ternarised.  
* Theoretical considerations and prior LLaMA experiments suggest that the loss of expressive power is negligible because the constant offsets can be absorbed elsewhere.  
* Training dynamics remain stable thanks to RMSNorm and the abs‑mean scaling used for weight ternarisation.  
* Quantisation benefits directly: the model truly becomes a 1.58‑bit model with no lingering full‑precision vectors.  

Consequently, while the paper does not present an explicit bias‑vs‑no‑bias ablation, the design choice is well‑justified and does not appear to harm performance, while it simplifies the quantisation pipeline and reduces the memory footprint.
