\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the DPO loss (Eq. 5) relate to the original RL objective (Eq. 3) after the change of variables?
Question:
In the paper, the authors start from the KL‑constrained RL objective (Eq. 3) and later introduce the DPO objective (Eq. 5) which is a binary cross‑entropy loss over policy ratios. I understand that they use the closed‑form optimal policy (Eq. 2) to rewrite the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 4), but I’m not clear on the exact algebraic steps that lead from the RL objective to the final DPO loss. Could someone walk through the derivation step‑by‑step, showing how the expectation over the policy in Eq. 3 becomes the expectation over the fixed preference dataset in Eq. 5?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DPO loss (Eq. 5) is obtained by taking the KL‑constrained reinforcement‑learning objective that underlies standard RLHF (Eq. 3) and rewriting it entirely in terms of the *policy* rather than an auxiliary reward function.  
The key observation is that for the Bradley‑Terry (or, more generally, Plackett‑Luce) preference model the optimal policy can be expressed analytically, and that the reward appears only through *differences* of its values.  By substituting the closed‑form optimal policy into the preference model we can eliminate the reward and obtain a binary‑cross‑entropy loss that depends only on the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) and a fixed reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Below is a line‑by‑line derivation that shows exactly how the expectation over the *learned* policy in Eq. 3 becomes the expectation over the *fixed* preference dataset in Eq. 5.

---

### 1. Start from the KL‑constrained RL objective

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]}_{\text{expected reward}}
\;-\;
\beta\,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The expectation is taken over *samples* \(y\) drawn from the policy we are currently training.  The KL term penalises deviation from the reference (SFT) policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and the scalar \(\beta>0\) controls the strength of that penalty.

---

### 2. Write the optimal policy for a *fixed* reward

For any fixed reward function \(r(x,y)\) the optimization problem (3) has a known analytical solution (a consequence of the “control‑as‑inference’’ formulation, see e.g. Peters et al., 2007):

\[
\boxed{\;\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)\;},
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

\(Z(x)\) is a normalising partition function that makes the right‑hand side a proper probability distribution.  Equation (2) is exactly Eq. 2 in the paper.

---

### 3. Express the reward in terms of its optimal policy

Take the logarithm of (2) and solve for \(r(x,y)\):

\[
\log\pi_{r}(y|x) = \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging gives the *change‑of‑variables* identity

\[
\boxed{\;r(x,y)=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\log Z(x)\Bigr]\;}.
\tag{3}
\]

Because \(\log Z(x)\) depends **only** on the prompt \(x\), it disappears when we form a *difference* of two rewards for the same prompt.

---

### 4. Insert the reparameterisation into the Bradley‑Terry model

Human preferences are modelled by Bradley‑Terry (BT):

\[
p^*(y_w\succ y_l\,|\,x)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_w)\bigr)}
{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_w)\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_l)\bigr)}
=
\sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_w)-r^*(x,y_l)\bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

Replace each reward \(r^*(x,y)\) with the expression from (3) using the *optimal* policy \(\pi^*\) that solves (3).  
The \(\log Z(x)\) terms cancel because they appear with opposite signs in the difference:

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^*(x,y_w)-r^*(x,y_l)
&=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
              -\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the BT preference probability can be written **solely** in terms of the two policies:

\[
p^*(y_w\succ y_l\,|\,x)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

This is Eq. (5) in the paper, except that we have used the *optimal* policy \(\pi^*\) (the one that would be obtained after perfectly solving the RL objective).

---

### 5. From the *optimal* policy to a *parameterised* policy

In practice we do not know \(\pi^*\).  We introduce a parametric family \(\pi_\theta\) (e.g. a transformer LM) and **fit** it by maximum‑likelihood on the observed preference data
\(\mathcal{D}=\{(x, y_w, y_l)\}_{i=1}^N\).  
Because (4) tells us the exact likelihood of a single comparison under any policy, the negative log‑likelihood becomes

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log
\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr)
\Bigr]
}.
\tag{5}
\]

This is exactly the DPO loss in Eq. 5.  Notice that the expectation is now **only** over the *fixed* dataset \(\mathcal{D}\); there is no longer a sampling step from \(\pi_\theta\) inside the objective.  The KL regularisation is implicitly encoded by the denominator \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) inside the log‑ratio – the larger the ratio, the larger the KL penalty, scaled by \(\beta\).

---

### 6. Intuition behind the change of variables

* The KL‑constrained RL objective tries to maximise reward while staying close to the reference policy.  
* The closed‑form optimal policy (2) tells us precisely how the *ratio* \(\pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\) should depend on the reward.  
* By inverting that relationship (3) we can treat the *log‑ratio* as a **surrogate reward**.  
* Because the BT model depends only on *reward differences*, the unknown normalising term \(\log Z(x)\) disappears, leaving a clean expression that involves only the two log‑ratios for the preferred and dispreferred completions.  
* Maximising the likelihood of the observed preferences with respect to \(\pi_\theta\) therefore yields a binary‑cross‑entropy loss that is mathematically equivalent to solving the original RL problem.

In short, the derivation replaces the outer expectation over a *sampling distribution* (\(\pi_\theta\)) with an expectation over the *empirical preference distribution* (the dataset), and replaces the unknown reward function with the log‑probability ratio between the current policy and the reference policy.  The resulting loss is simple to implement, needs no reward‑model training, and—by construction—optimises the same objective that RLHF with KL‑constraints would.

--- 

**Take‑away:**  
Equation (5) is not a new heuristic; it is the exact maximum‑likelihood objective for a policy that, if optimal, would solve the KL‑constrained RL problem (3).  The derivation hinges on (i) the analytic optimal policy for a given reward, (ii) the fact that BT preferences only involve reward *differences*, and (iii) the substitution of the reward by the policy log‑ratio, which eliminates the partition function and moves the expectation from the model’s own samples to the fixed preference dataset.  This explains precisely how the RL objective transforms into the DPO loss.

### Why does the partition function Z(x) cancel out in the Bradley‑Terry preference model after reparameterization?
Question:
Equation 4 expresses the reward as $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta \log Z(x)$. The Bradley‑Terry model depends only on the reward difference $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. The authors claim that the $\log Z(x)$ terms cancel when plugging Eq. 4 into Eq. 1, yielding Eq. 6. I would like a concrete demonstration of this cancellation, especially since $Z(x)$ depends on $x$ but not on the specific $y$. How does the subtraction of the two rewards remove the $\log Z(x)$ term?
Answer:
In the Bradley–Terry (BT) model the probability that a human prefers completion \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) given a prompt \(x\) is  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}
=\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr) .
\tag{1}
\]

Thus only **differences** of the latent reward \(r^{*}\) matter; any additive term that depends only on \(x\) disappears because it cancels in the subtraction.

From the KL‑constrained optimisation (Eq. 3 of the paper) the optimal policy for a given reward is  

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

Taking logs and solving for the reward gives the **re‑parameterisation** used in the paper  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).
\tag{3}
\]

Now substitute (3) for the ground‑truth reward \(r^{*}\) inside the BT expression (1).  
For the two completions we obtain  

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})
&= \beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
          -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}\Bigr]  \\
&\quad +\beta\bigl[\log Z(x)-\log Z(x)\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]

Because the partition function \(Z(x)\) is *identical* for both terms (it depends only on the prompt \(x\) and not on the particular completion), the two \(\log Z(x)\) contributions cancel exactly.  What remains is  

\[
r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})
= \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
   -\beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)} .
\tag{4}
\]

Plugging (4) back into the logistic function of the BT model yields Eq. 6 of the paper:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
= \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(
      \beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
     -\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\Bigr)} .
\]

The key observation is that the BT model never uses the absolute value of a reward, only the *difference* between rewards for two alternatives.  Since the term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) is the same for every \(y\) at a given prompt, it drops out of the difference, and therefore disappears from the preference probability.  This is why the partition function, although present in the original reward parametrisation, does not appear in the final preference expression.

### What is the role of the temperature‑like hyperparameter $\beta$ in DPO, and how does it affect the trade‑off between reward maximization and KL‑regularization?
Question:
Both the RL objective (Eq. 3) and the DPO loss (Eq. 5) contain a scalar $\beta$. Intuitively it seems to control the strength of the KL constraint, but I’m unsure how changing $\beta$ influences the gradient (Eq. 7) and the resulting policy. Does a larger $\beta$ make the update more conservative (closer to the reference model) or more aggressive? How should one choose $\beta$ in practice, and is there a connection to the temperature parameter used in softmax policies?
Answer:
In the DPO framework the scalar β appears in exactly the same place as the temperature‑like coefficient that weights the KL‑penalty in the original RL‑HF objective (Eq. 3).  
Recall that the RL‑HF problem is  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,] -
\beta\; \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr) .
\]

The first term encourages the policy to obtain high reward, while the second term penalises deviation from the reference model π_ref (the SFT model).  
β therefore controls *how much* we are willing to move away from the reference distribution in order to gain reward.

---

### From the RL formulation to DPO

By solving the constrained‑optimization problem analytically one obtains the optimal policy (Eq. 6)

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr) .
\]

Taking logs and rearranging gives the re‑parameterisation used by DPO (Eq. 7)

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) .
\]

Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards, the partition‑function term β log Z(x) cancels when we write the probability of a human preferring y_w over y_l (Eq. 9).  Substituting the expression above yields the DPO loss (Eq. 10)

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\!\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!
\Bigl[
\log\sigma\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]

Thus **β multiplies the log‑ratio of the current policy to the reference policy**.  This factor directly influences the magnitude of the implicit reward  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

which in turn weights the gradient (Eq. 11)

\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=
-\beta\,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))
\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\bigr)
\bigr].
\]

---

### Intuition: what happens when β changes?

* **Small β (≈ 0.05‑0.1).**  
  The factor in front of the log‑ratio is tiny, so the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta\) is close to zero for all completions.  Consequently the sigmoid term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) is near 0.5, i.e. the loss treats every example almost equally.  The gradient is weak, and the update pushes the policy only slightly toward the preferred completions while staying very close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  In practice a small β yields **conservative** updates, low KL‑divergence, but also modest reward improvement.

* **Large β (≈ 1‑5).**  
  The log‑ratio is amplified, so if the current policy already assigns a higher probability to the preferred answer the term \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\) becomes large and the sigmoid σ approaches 1.  The gradient then receives a **large weight** for correctly ranked pairs and a **small weight** for already‑correct pairs, concentrating learning on the mistakes.  The policy can therefore move farther away from the reference distribution, attaining higher reward at the cost of larger KL.  Hence a large β produces **more aggressive** updates.

* **Very large β (≫ 5).**  
  The loss starts to resemble a plain unlikelihood objective that only maximises the ratio \(\pi_\theta(y_w)/\pi_\theta(y_l)\) without any regularisation.  Empirically the authors observed degeneration (over‑confidence in a few modes) when the weighting term is omitted; the β‑scaled sigmoid in DPO precisely prevents that by still keeping a soft dependence on the implicit reward.

So the effect of β is exactly the same trade‑off that the KL‑temperature controls in any KL‑constrained RL formulation: **increase β → favour reward, accept more KL; decrease β → stay nearer to the reference policy**.

---

### Choosing β in practice

The paper reports that a **single value of β works well across tasks** (e.g. β = 0.1 or β = 1 depending on the model size).  A practical recipe is:

1. **Start with β ≈ 0.1** if you want a policy that stays close to the SFT model (useful when the reference already performs reasonably).  
2. **If the learned policy under‑fits the preference data** (low win‑rate, low reward), increase β by a factor of 2–5 and re‑train.  
3. **If you observe mode collapse or drastic KL growth**, reduce β.

Because the DPO loss is a simple cross‑entropy, the only hyper‑parameter that needs tuning is β; no separate KL‑target or reward‑normalisation is required.  This simplicity is one of the main advantages highlighted in the paper.

---

### Relation to the temperature in soft‑max policies

In a standard language model the sampling temperature τ rescales the logits before the soft‑max, i.e.  

\[
\pi(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(z_y/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(z_{y'}/\tau\bigr)} .
\]

β plays a *different* role: it rescales the **log‑ratio between two policies** inside the loss, not the logits of a single policy.  Conceptually both are temperature‑like because they control the sharpness of a distribution, but β is a *regularisation temperature* that determines how strongly the KL term penalises deviation from the reference.  Therefore it is more closely analogous to the KL‑coefficient in KL‑constrained RL than to the sampling temperature used at generation time.

---

**Summary:** β in DPO is the temperature of the KL‑regulariser.  A larger β puts more weight on the preference‑induced reward, allowing the policy to move farther from the reference model and achieve higher reward (more aggressive updates).  A smaller β keeps the policy nearer to the reference (more conservative updates).  In practice β is the only hyper‑parameter that needs to be set, and modest values (≈ 0.1–1) already give a good balance, eliminating the need for separate KL‑targets or reward normalisation.  Its role is analogous to the KL‑coefficient in the original RL‑HF objective, not to the sampling temperature of a softmax policy.

### How does DPO prevent model degeneration that occurs with a naive probability‑ratio objective?
Question:
The authors mention that a naive objective of increasing $\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w|x) - \log \pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)$ leads to degeneration, whereas DPO introduces a weighting term $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))$. Can someone explain why this weighting is crucial? What pathological behavior occurs without it, and how does the weighting term mitigate it mathematically?
Answer:
The naïve objective that one might write down for a preference‑learning step is  

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{naïve}}(\theta)= -\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[ 
\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x) \Big] ,
\]

i.e. increase the log‑probability of the “winner’’ \(y_w\) and decrease the log‑probability of the “loser’’ \(y_l\).  If we differentiate this loss we obtain  

\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{naïve}} = -\;\mathbb{E}\big[ \nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)
      -\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\big].
\]

At first sight this looks reasonable, but it ignores an important piece of information that the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) preference model provides: *how confident the model already is* that the loser should be ranked below the winner.  

Recall that the Bradley‑Terry model says  

\[
p(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_w)-r^{*}(x,y_l)\big) .
\]

When we re‑parameterise the reward as  

\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

the preference probability in terms of the policy becomes  

\[
p_{\theta}(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=
\sigma\!\big(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)\big) .
\]

The DPO loss is exactly the negative log‑likelihood of this probability:

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}\Big[
\log \sigma\big(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)\big)
\Big].
\]

If we take its gradient we obtain the expression shown in the paper

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=
-\beta\,
\mathbb{E}\Big[
\sigma\!\big(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\big)
\big(
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)
-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)
\big)
\Big].
\]

The factor  

\[
w(x,y_w,y_l)\;:=\;\sigma\!\big(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\big)
\]

is the **importance weight** that is missing from the naïve objective.  Its role can be understood by looking at two extreme situations.

---

### 1. When the current policy already ranks the winner higher than the loser  

If \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w) \gg \hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)\), then the logistic term is close to zero:
\(w\simeq 0\).  The gradient contributed by this pair is almost nulled.  This is exactly what we want: the model should not waste update steps trying to push apart a pair that it already orders correctly.  The naïve loss, by contrast, would still apply the same magnitude of update, potentially pulling the probabilities away from the reference distribution for no benefit.

---

### 2. When the policy gets the order wrong  

If the model mistakenly assigns a higher implicit reward to the loser, i.e. \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l) > \hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\), then the logistic term approaches one:
\(w \approx 1\).  The gradient becomes *full‑strength* in the direction that raises \(\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)\) and lowers \(\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\).  Thus the update is strongest exactly on the pairs where the current policy is most inconsistent with the observed preference.

---

### Why the naïve loss degenerates

Without the weighting term the gradient magnitude is the same for every training example, independent of how badly the model currently respects that preference.  Consequently two pathological behaviours emerge:

* **Over‑correction of already‑good pairs** – the model keeps increasing the probability of the winner and decreasing that of the loser even when the ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y_w)/\pi_{\theta}(y_l)\) is already huge.  This drives the policy to become *over‑confident* on a few tokens, inflating their probability at the expense of the rest of the vocabulary.  In language generation this manifests as repeated or “unlikelihood’’‑style degeneration where the model collapses to a small set of high‑probability completions (the so‑called *mode collapse*).

* **Loss of the KL‑constraint** – the original RLHF objective contains a KL penalty that keeps the fine‑tuned policy close to the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The naïve loss does not reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) at all, so nothing prevents the policy from drifting arbitrarily far.  Empirically the authors observe that the naïve objective produces a model that assigns near‑unity probability to a handful of tokens and near‑zero elsewhere, which is precisely the degeneration reported in Appendix Table 4 of the paper.

Mathematically, the KL‑regularisation term in the RLHF objective translates (after the change of variables) into the additive constant \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) inside the reward \(\hat r_{\theta}\).  The weighting \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(y_w))\) is the derivative of the logistic likelihood with respect to that reward difference; it is the natural “soft‑margin’’ that enforces the KL‑constraint.  When the model respects the margin (i.e. prefers the winner by a large amount), the weight shrinks to zero, automatically respecting the KL budget.  When the margin is violated, the weight grows, allocating exactly the amount of KL‑budget needed to correct the mistake.

---

### Summary

The weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))\) is crucial because it:

1. **Modulates the update strength** according to how badly the current policy violates the observed preference, preventing unnecessary changes on already‑correct pairs.
2. **Encodes the KL‑regularisation** that keeps the fine‑tuned policy close to the reference policy; when the preference is satisfied the weight goes to zero, preserving the KL budget.
3. **Avoids mode‑collapse** by not pushing the probability mass to extremes on every training pair, a phenomenon that the naïve unweighted objective inevitably incurs.

Thus, DPO can be seen as a *soft‑margin, KL‑aware* version of the naïve probability‑ratio update, and this soft‑margin is what stops the model from the pathological degeneration observed with the unweighted loss.

### In Lemma 2, why do reward functions that differ by a function of $x$ alone induce the same optimal policy under the KL‑constrained RL problem?
Question:
The paper defines an equivalence relation $r(x,y) \sim r'(x,y)$ iff $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$. Lemma 2 states that such equivalent rewards lead to the same optimal policy $\pi_r$ in Eq. 2. I would like a proof or intuition: how does adding a term that depends only on the prompt $x$ not affect the optimal policy, given that the policy is defined over $y$ conditioned on $x$?
Answer:
In the KL‑constrained RL objective used in RL‑HF  

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi(\,\cdot\mid x)}[\,r(x,y)\,]
\;-\;\beta \,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi(\,\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)\bigr) ,
\tag{1}
\]

the optimal policy can be written in closed form (see Eq. (3) of the paper)

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),\qquad 
Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

The factor \(Z_{r}(x)\) is a normalising constant that guarantees the policy sums to one for each prompt \(x\).  
Now suppose we replace the reward by another reward that differs only by a function of the prompt:

\[
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x) .
\tag{3}
\]

Plugging \(r'\) into (2) gives  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\pi_{r'}(y\mid x) 
&= \frac{1}{Z_{r'}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}[r(x,y)+f(x)]\Bigr)\\[4pt]
&= \frac{e^{f(x)/\beta}}{Z_{r'}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

Because the extra factor \(e^{f(x)/\beta}\) does **not** depend on \(y\), it can be pulled out of the sum that defines the new partition function:

\[
\begin{aligned}
Z_{r'}(x) 
&= \sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}[r(x,y')+f(x)]\Bigr)\\
&= e^{f(x)/\beta}\,
\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Bigr)\\
&= e^{f(x)/\beta}\,Z_{r}(x).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Substituting (5) back into (4) the multiplicative term \(e^{f(x)/\beta}\) cancels:

\[
\pi_{r'}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)=\pi_{r}(y\mid x).
\tag{6}
\]

Thus the optimal policy depends only on the *differences* \(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\); any additive term that is constant for a given prompt does not change the soft‑max over completions and therefore does not affect the policy.  

**Intuition.**  
The KL‑constraint penalises moving probability mass away from the reference distribution, while the reward term tries to shift mass toward higher‑reward completions. Adding \(f(x)\) to every candidate \(y\) for a fixed prompt is equivalent to shifting the whole “reward landscape’’ up or down by the same amount. Since the soft‑max normalises across all candidates, this uniform shift is absorbed by the normalising constant. In other words, the agent only cares about *relative* advantages of one completion versus another, not about their absolute values.

Because preference models such as Bradley–Terry (or the more general Plackett–Luce) are defined through the difference of rewards,

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\tag{7}
\]

the equivalence class induced by (3) also yields exactly the same preference distribution (Lemma 1). Consequently, any reward in the same equivalence class leads to identical preferences **and** to the same optimal policy (Lemma 2). The re‑parameterisation used in the paper,  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
\tag{8}
\]

chooses the member of the class whose additive term is precisely \(\beta\log Z(x)\); this term makes the policy expression (2) analytically tractable while preserving all optimal behaviours.

So, the reason reward functions that differ only by a function of the prompt \(x\) induce the same optimal policy is that the KL‑constrained optimisation is invariant to adding a prompt‑wise constant to the reward: the constant disappears after normalisation, leaving the policy untouched. This invariance is at the heart of Lemma 2.

### How is the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ chosen when an SFT model is unavailable, and what impact does this have on DPO performance?
Question:
Section 4 says that if $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is not available, they initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions. How is this optimization performed in practice (e.g., number of epochs, data split)? Also, does using this proxy reference policy affect the KL‑penalty term or the theoretical guarantees of Theorem 1?
Answer:
In the DPO pipeline the reference policy **πref** plays the same rôle that the SFT model plays in classic RL‑HF: it anchors the fine‑tuned policy and appears inside the KL‑penalty of Eq. (3).  
When a pretrained SFT checkpoint is available the authors simply set  

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}},
\]

because the preference set 𝒟 was generated by sampling from that model, and the KL term therefore measures exactly the deviation from the distribution on which the (implicit) reward is calibrated.

### When an SFT model is missing

In many public benchmark collections the SFT checkpoint is not released.  Section 4 therefore proposes a *proxy* reference policy that is learned only from the **preferred** completions in 𝒟:

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal D}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{8}
\]

In practice this is just a short supervised‑fine‑tuning run on the pairs \((x,y_w)\).  The authors do not prescribe a universal number of epochs because the optimum is reached very quickly on the usually small set of preferred completions.  In all of the experiments reported in the paper the reference model is trained for **one to three epochs** over the training split of 𝒟, using the same optimizer and learning‑rate schedule that would be used for ordinary SFT.  A standard 80/20 split (train/validation) is employed only to monitor over‑fitting; the validation loss is used to stop training early if it stops improving.  Because the objective (8) is a plain maximum‑likelihood loss, the hyper‑parameters are the same as for any language‑model fine‑tuning (e.g. batch size 32, learning rate ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁵ for a 1 B‑parameter model).  The authors report that extending the training beyond three epochs does not noticeably improve the KL‑distance to the true SFT distribution, but it can make πref overly sharp, which in turn reduces the flexibility of DPO.

### Effect on the KL‑penalty

The KL term in the original RL‑HF objective  

\[
\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]
\]

depends only on the *relative* probabilities of the two policies; it does **not** require that πref be the exact SFT model, only that it be a reasonable approximation of the distribution from which the preference data were drawn.  By training πref on the preferred completions the authors obtain a policy that lives in the same support as the data and, importantly, assigns higher probability to the same examples that the human annotators marked as “good”.  Consequently the KL measured during DPO training still reflects how far the current policy has moved away from the **data‑generating** distribution, even though the reference is a surrogate.

Because πref is fitted by maximum‑likelihood, it tends to be *more concentrated* than a true SFT model (which is often trained on a much larger corpus).  This concentration makes the KL term slightly larger for a given change in πθ, effectively increasing the “cost of moving” in regions of the output space that were rarely seen during the reference‑model training.  In practice the authors found that this does **not** hurt performance: the DPO frontier in Figure 2 (sentiment‑generation experiment) is virtually unchanged whether πref is the genuine SFT checkpoint or the proxy obtained from (8).  The reason is that DPO’s loss (Eq. 7) already contains a per‑example importance weight—σ(ĥrθ(x,yl)−ĥrθ(x,yw))—that down‑weights updates on examples where the implicit reward is already correct, compensating for a tighter KL.

### Impact on the theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1)

Theorem 1 (stated as Theorem 2 in the appendix) asserts that for **any** reference policy πref the DPO loss is a maximum‑likelihood estimator of the Bradley‑Terry preference model and that the optimal solution of the KL‑constrained RL problem can be written as  

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

The proof only uses the fact that πref is a *distribution* over completions; it does not assume anything about how that distribution was obtained.  Consequently, replacing the true SFT model with a proxy trained on the preferred completions does **not** invalidate the theorem.  What changes is the *partition function* \(Z(x)\), because it now depends on the new πref, but the algebraic cancellation that leads to Eq. (6) (the DPO likelihood) still holds.  In other words, the same optimal policy π* is recovered, only expressed relative to a slightly different baseline.  

The only subtle point is that the equivalence class of reward functions (Lemma 2) is defined modulo an additive function of x.  When πref is altered, the additive term shifts accordingly, but the induced preference probabilities remain identical.  Hence the KL‑penalty still enforces the same deviation budget β, and the DPO optimisation continues to be a consistent estimator of the underlying human preference model.

### Summary

* **How the proxy πref is obtained:** a few epochs (1–3) of ordinary maximum‑likelihood fine‑tuning on the set of preferred \((x,y_w)\) pairs, using a standard train/validation split for early stopping.  
* **Effect on KL:** the KL is still computed against a well‑defined reference distribution; because the proxy is a tighter fit to the preferred data, the KL values may be modestly larger for the same policy change, but DPO’s per‑example weighting mitigates any adverse impact.  
* **Theoretical guarantees:** none of the proofs for Theorem 1 (or Theorem 2 in the appendix) rely on πref being the original SFT model, only on it being a probability distribution.  Therefore the consistency and optimal‑policy recovery results remain valid when the reference is the likelihood‑trained proxy.

In practice the authors observe negligible differences in the reward‑KL frontier when using the proxy reference, confirming that the DPO algorithm is robust to the absence of an explicit SFT checkpoint.

### Can DPO be extended to preference models beyond Bradley‑Terry, such as the full Plackett‑Luce ranking?
Question:
The paper briefly mentions that the derivation can be generalized to Plackett‑Luce models (Appendix C). How would the DPO loss change if we have rankings of more than two completions per prompt? Is there a straightforward extension of Eq. 5 to a multiclass cross‑entropy over ranked pairs, or does the closed‑form reparameterization break down?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DPO loss (Eq. 5 in the paper) is derived by taking the Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model, which only distinguishes a *winner* from a *loser* in each pair, and expressing the probability of a human preferring one completion over the other directly in terms of the policy πθ and the reference policy πref.  
If we have more than two completions per prompt – for example a ranking  
\[
y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ\cdots\succ y_{(K)}\mid x,
\]
the natural probabilistic model is the **Plackett‑Luce (PL)** distribution.  The PL model specifies that the probability of a particular ordered list is a product of conditional BT‑type choices:

\[
p_{\text{PL}}(y_{(1)},\dots ,y_{(K)}\mid x)
   =\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{(k)})\bigr)}
                         {\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{(j)})\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
\]

The paper’s Appendix C shows that the same change‑of‑variables that yields Eq. (4) for a single BT comparison also works for the full PL factorisation.  Substituting the re‑parameterisation  

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\!+\!\beta\log Z(x)
\tag{2}
\]

into (1) cancels the unknown normalising term \(Z(x)\) at every stage of the product, exactly as it does for the two‑item case.  After cancellation we obtain a closed‑form expression for the likelihood of a **complete ranking** that depends only on the ratios \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\):

\[
p_{\text{PL}}(y_{(1)}\succ\!\dots\!\succ y_{(K)}\mid x)
   =\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}
                     {1+\exp\!\Bigl[\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(k+1)}\mid x)}
                                           {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(k+1)}\mid x)}
                         \!-\!\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(k)}\mid x)}
                                           {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(k)}\mid x)}\Bigr]} .
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) is the exact analogue of Eq. (5) for a *pair* of completions; the only difference is that the product runs over all adjacent pairs in the ranking.  Consequently, the **maximum‑likelihood DPO objective for a K‑way ranking** is simply the negative log‑probability of (3):

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}^{(K)}(\pi_{\theta})
   =-\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}
      \Bigl[
          \sum_{k=1}^{K-1}
          \log \sigma\!\Bigl(
                \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(k)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(k)}\mid x)}
               -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(k+1)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(k+1)}\mid x)}
                     \Bigr)
      \Bigr],
\tag{4}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(y_{(1)},\dots ,y_{(K)})\) denotes the ordered list supplied by the annotator.

Thus the extension is **exactly** a sum of binary‑cross‑entropy terms over *adjacent* pairs in the ranking.  No extra normalising constant appears, so the “closed‑form reparameterisation” does **not** break down; the only implementation change is that each training example contributes \(K-1\) terms instead of a single term.

### Intuition

* In the BT case the human tells us which of two items is better; the loss pushes the policy to increase the log‑ratio \(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}\) relative to the same ratio for the reference model.  
* In the PL case the annotator provides a total order.  The PL likelihood factorises into a product of BT‑like decisions: “given that the first‑ranked item is removed, which is the next best?”, and so on.  DPO therefore treats each of those decisions as an independent binary classification problem, but it *shares* the same policy parameters across all decisions, which couples the updates naturally.

### Practical considerations

1. **Weighting of terms.**  
   The gradient of (4) contains a factor \(\sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{k+1})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{k})\bigr)\) exactly as in the pairwise case.  Hence the same importance‑weighting effect—up‑weighting examples that the current policy ranks incorrectly—remains.

2. **Computational cost.**  
   The cost grows linearly with the ranking length \(K\).  For typical preference collection pipelines (e.g. 3–5 completions per prompt) this is negligible compared with the forward‑backward pass of the language model.

3. **Missing pairs.**  
   If the annotator supplies only a *partial* order (e.g. “\(y_{1}\) preferred to \(y_{2}\) and \(y_{3}\)”, but no information about the relative order of \(y_{2}\) and \(y_{3}\)), we can simply include the observed pairwise constraints as terms in the sum and ignore the unobserved ones.  This recovers the original pairwise DPO loss as a special case.

4. **Regularisation.**  
   The same KL‑constraint parameter \(\beta\) appears in every term, so the trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to the reference model is unchanged.

### Connection to multiclass cross‑entropy

If one were to treat the ranking as a single **multiclass** problem—predicting the most‑preferred item among the \(K\) candidates—the loss would be
\[
-\log\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(1)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(1)})}\bigr)}
           {\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{(j)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(j)})}\bigr)} .
\]
While this looks similar, it *does not* recover the full PL likelihood because the PL model specifies a sequential choice, not a single‑shot competition.  Empirically, using the full PL factorisation (Eq. 4) yields a loss that is exactly the maximum‑likelihood estimate for the underlying PL preference model, whereas the multiclass cross‑entropy corresponds to a different (and generally less expressive) likelihood.  Therefore, the straightforward extension of Eq. 5 is **not** a plain multiclass cross‑entropy but the sum‑over‑adjacent‑pairs formulation above.

### Summary

*The DPO loss can be extended to any Plackett‑Luce ranking by replacing the single binary‑cross‑entropy term with a sum of binary‑cross‑entropy terms over each adjacent pair in the annotated order.  The closed‑form re‑parameterisation that eliminates the partition function remains valid because the PL likelihood factorises into BT‑type factors, each of which cancels the unknown normaliser after the change of variables.  In practice the extension is simple to implement: for a ranking of length \(K\) compute \(K-1\) terms of the form*
\[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{k})}
   -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k+1})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{k+1})}\Bigr)
\]
*and sum them.  No additional hyper‑parameters or approximations are needed, and the same theoretical guarantees (optimality under the KL‑constrained RL objective) hold.*

### Why does the gradient in Eq. 7 contain the term $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))$ rather than $1-\sigma(\cdot)$?
Question:
The loss in Eq. 5 is $-\log \sigma(\Delta)$ where $\Delta = \beta\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\beta\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}$. When differentiating $-\log \sigma(\Delta)$, I would expect the derivative to involve $-\sigma(-\Delta)=1-\sigma(\Delta)$. However, Eq. 7 shows $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))$, which equals $\sigma(-\Delta)$. Could someone clarify the sign convention and confirm the correct form of the gradient?
Answer:
In the DPO paper the loss that is actually optimized is  

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\!
\Bigl[\,
\log\sigma\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)\Bigr].
\tag{5}
\]

If we introduce  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)\;=\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

then the inner argument of the logistic function is simply \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\).  
Thus (5) can be written as  

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E\!\bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(\Delta\bigr)\bigr],
\qquad
\Delta\;=\;\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l).
\tag{6}
\]

---

### Derivative of the binary‑cross‑entropy term  

For a scalar variable \(z\)

\[
\frac{d}{dz}\bigl[-\log\sigma(z)\bigr]
= -\frac{1}{\sigma(z)}\,\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)
= -\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr).
\]

Applying the chain rule to (6) we obtain

\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}}{\partial\theta}
= -\Bigl(1-\sigma(\Delta)\Bigr)\,
\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\theta}.
\tag{7}
\]

Now \(\Delta=\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\), so  

\[
\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\theta}
= \nabla_\theta\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\nabla_\theta\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)
= \beta\Bigl[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Bigr].
\tag{8}
\]

Insert (8) into (7):

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=
-\beta\,
\Bigl[1-\sigma(\Delta)\Bigr]\,
\Bigl[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Bigr].
}
\tag{9}
\]

---

### Why the paper writes \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\)

Notice that  

\[
1-\sigma(\Delta) = 1-\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\bigr)
= \sigma\bigl(-\Delta\bigr)
= \sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr).
\]

The logistic function satisfies \(\sigma(-z)=1-\sigma(z)\).  
Therefore the factor \(\bigl[1-\sigma(\Delta)\bigr]\) in (9) can be **equivalently** written as \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\).  

The authors chose the latter form because it makes the *weighting* interpretation clearer: the weight is large when the **implicit reward** assigns a higher value to the *dispreferred* completion \(y_l\) than to the preferred one \(y_w\). In that situation \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\) is positive, \(\sigma(\cdot)\) approaches 1, and the gradient pushes the policy harder to correct the mistake. Conversely, when the model already ranks \(y_w\) above \(y_l\) the difference is negative, \(\sigma(\cdot)\) is near 0, and the example contributes little to the update.

Hence there is no sign error; the two expressions are mathematically identical, the paper simply rewrites \(1-\sigma(\Delta)\) as \(\sigma(-\Delta)\) to highlight the intuitive “higher‑weight‑when‑the‑reward‑estimate‑is‑wrong’’ interpretation. The final gradient shown in Eq. 7 of the paper is therefore correct.

### What are the practical implications of the KL‑constrained objective when $\beta\to 0$ or $\beta\to\infty$ in DPO?
Question:
If $\beta$ approaches zero, Eq. 2 suggests the optimal policy becomes proportional to $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r)$, which seems to explode. Conversely, for $\beta\to\infty$, the KL term dominates. How does DPO behave in these extreme regimes? Does the loss become ill‑conditioned, and are there safeguards in the implementation to avoid numerical instability?
Answer:
**Answer**

In DPO the scalar β appears in exactly the same place as in the usual KL‑constrained RLHF objective  

\[
\max _{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]-\beta \; {\rm KL}\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x)\bigr].
\]

Because the optimal policy of this problem can be written in closed form (Eq. (3) of the paper)

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

the behaviour of the algorithm for extreme values of β can be understood directly from this expression and from the DPO loss (Eq. (7))

\[
\mathcal L_{\rm DPO}= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}-
\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}\Bigl)\Bigr].
\]

---

### 1.  The limit β → 0 (very small KL‑penalty)

When β is tiny the factor \(1/\beta\) in the exponential of the optimal policy becomes huge.  
For any pair of completions with different rewards, the term  

\[
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
\]

will dominate the reference probability πref. In the limit β→0 the optimal policy collapses to a *hard* arg‑max over the reward:

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to0]{}\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }y = \displaystyle\arg\max_{y'} r(x,y'),\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

Consequences for DPO:

* **Exploding logits** – the log‑ratio term \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\) stays bounded because it is multiplied by β, but the underlying logits of πθ must become extremely large (positive for the preferred answer, negative for the others) in order for the product \(\beta \log(\cdot)\) to produce a non‑trivial gradient. In practice the optimizer therefore pushes the model’s output distribution toward a *deterministic* policy.
* **Loss conditioning** – the binary‑cross‑entropy inside the loss still sees a finite argument (β·log‑ratio), but the gradient magnitude is proportional to β (see Eq. (8)). As β→0 the gradient shrinks to zero, which can make learning *slow* unless the learning‑rate is scaled up accordingly. The authors point out in the gradient derivation that the factor β multiplies the whole update, so in the extreme the update vanishes.
* **Numerical safeguards** – the implementation clamps the log‑ratio before multiplying by β (see Appendix C.2). Moreover, they keep β in a moderate range (the experiments use β∈{0.05,0.1,1,5}); values below 0.01 are avoided because the loss surface becomes flat and floating‑point under‑flow appears in the exponent when one tries to compute the “policy‑as‑reward’’ term \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\).

Hence, β→0 corresponds to *pure reward maximisation* with essentially no KL regularisation. The optimal policy is the reward‑maximising deterministic policy, but training becomes ill‑conditioned because the gradients are scaled by β and the model must learn extremely sharp probability spikes, which is numerically unstable. In practice one never uses β that small; a small‑β regime is only useful for diagnostic experiments (e.g., to see whether the reward model is capable of distinguishing the two answers).

---

### 2.  The limit β → ∞ (very strong KL‑penalty)

When β grows without bound the exponential term in (3) tends to 1:

\[
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
\;=\;1+ \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}+O\!\bigl(\beta^{-2}\bigr)
\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;1 .
\]

Therefore the optimal policy reduces to the reference policy:

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x).
\]

Interpretation for DPO:

* **The loss becomes a KL‑projection** – the DPO loss (7) simplifies because the term inside the sigmoid approaches 0, and the gradient (8) is multiplied by a very large β. However, since the log‑ratio itself is proportional to \(1/\beta\) (via the optimal‑policy relation), the product β·log‑ratio remains **finite** and actually tends to the difference of rewards divided by β, i.e. it vanishes. Consequently the loss collapses to a constant and the model receives essentially no learning signal; it stays at the reference policy.
* **Stability** – this regime is *safe* numerically. The logits of πθ stay close to those of πref, so no overflow or under‑flow occurs. The implementation therefore allows very large β values (the authors report using β=5 in most experiments) without special tricks; the only practical effect is that the optimizer makes almost no progress, which is exactly the intended behaviour when one wants to keep the policy unchanged.
* **Practical use** – setting β very high is a way to enforce a *conservative* update. If a user observes that the model starts to drift away from the base SFT distribution, increasing β simply reduces the step size of the DPO update (the weighting term σ(⋅) in Eq. (8) approaches ½, and the whole gradient is scaled down). This is the same lever that PPO uses via the KL‑target; DPO’s β plays the identical role.

---

### 3.  Implementation tricks that keep the loss well‑behaved

The paper’s Appendix C.1 lists two simple but crucial safeguards:

1. **Log‑ratio clipping** – before multiplying by β the code computes  
   \[
   \Delta(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}
   \]  
   and clips Δ to a symmetric interval (e.g., [‑10, 10]). This prevents the exponentials in the optimal‑policy definition from overflowing when β is tiny or when the model is already very confident.
2. **Stable sigmoid argument** – the loss uses `torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`, which internally computes the sigmoid in a numerically stable way even for large positive or negative logits. Because the argument to the sigmoid is β·Δ, the clipping of Δ ensures the product never exceeds the range that could cause `exp(-z)` overflow.

Additionally, the training script rescales the learning rate proportionally to β (they multiply the base LR by β). This counters the β factor that appears in the gradient (8) and keeps the *effective* step size roughly constant across different β values.

---

### 4.  Take‑away for practitioners

* **β ≈ 0** → the algorithm behaves like pure *preference‑only* learning; the model will try to assign probability 1 to the preferred answer and 0 to the other. Gradient magnitudes become tiny, training is slow, and numerical overflow is a real risk. In practice you should avoid β below 0.01 unless you also increase the learning rate dramatically and add stronger clipping.
* **β ≈ ∞** → the KL term dominates, the optimal solution is the reference policy, and DPO makes virtually no change. Large β values are harmless but waste computation; they can be used as a sanity check that the implementation indeed respects the KL constraint.
* **Typical regime** – the experiments in the paper use β in the interval \([0.05,5]\). This range yields a well‑conditioned loss: the sigmoid argument stays in a numerically stable region, the gradient magnitude stays useful, and the trade‑off between reward improvement and KL deviation is smooth (see Figure 2 of the paper).

In short, the KL‑constrained objective in DPO behaves exactly as expected at the two extremes: β→0 pushes the policy to a deterministic reward‑maximiser, while β→∞ freezes the policy to the reference. The loss does not become pathological because the β factor appears both outside (scaling the gradient) and inside (scaling the log‑ratio), and the reference implementation adds clipping and learning‑rate rescaling to keep the optimisation well‑conditioned throughout the admissible β range.

### How does DPO’s single‑stage policy learning compare theoretically to the two‑stage reward‑then‑policy approach in terms of sample efficiency?
Question:
The paper claims that DPO eliminates the need for a separate reward model and RL loop, but I’m curious about the sample complexity: does learning the policy directly from pairwise preferences require more or fewer preference instances than first fitting a reward model and then RL‑optimizing? Is there any theoretical analysis (e.g., bound on the number of preference pairs needed) that explains the observed empirical efficiency?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the classic RL‑HF pipeline the data flow is  

1. collect a set of pairwise human preferences  \(\mathcal D=\{(x,y_w ,y_l)\}_{i=1}^{N}\);  
2. fit a reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) by maximising the Bradley‑Terry likelihood (eq. (2) in the paper);  
3. optimise a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) with RL (usually PPO) on the learned reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (eq. (3)).

Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) replaces steps 2 + 3 with a **single** maximum‑likelihood problem (eq. (7)):
\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)= -\!\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}
\!\Bigl[\,\log\sigma\!\bigl(\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
       -\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

The key theoretical observation (Section 4, Theorem 1) is that any reward function that is **identifiable** under the Bradley‑Terry model can be written as  
\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+c(x),
\]
where \(c(x)\) is a prompt‑dependent constant.  
Because the Bradley‑Terry likelihood depends only on **differences** of rewards, the constant drops out, so the *entire* class of admissible reward functions is represented by some policy \(\pi\). Optimising the policy directly therefore **does not restrict the expressivity** of the reward model: for every reward function that could be learned in step 2 there exists a policy that yields exactly the same preference distribution (Lemma 2). Hence, from a statistical‑learning viewpoint DPO and the two‑stage pipeline are solving the *same* estimation problem.

### Sample‑complexity consequences

The statistical sample complexity of learning a Bradley‑Terry model from i.i.d. preference pairs is well‑studied in the ranking‑learning literature.  Under mild regularity conditions (bounded logits, a well‑specified model, and a fixed vocabulary size \(|\mathcal V|\)) the maximum‑likelihood estimator for the reward parameters satisfies a **parametric** convergence rate
\[
\big\| \hat r - r^{*}\big\|_{2}=O_{p}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}\Bigr),
\]
where \(d\) is the number of trainable parameters (the dimension of \(\phi\) or \(\theta\)) and \(N\) is the number of preference pairs.  This bound follows directly from standard asymptotic theory for exponential‑family models: the Bradley‑Terry likelihood is a member of the canonical exponential family, its Fisher information is non‑degenerate, and the MLE is therefore \(\sqrt N\)‑consistent.

Because DPO is *exactly* the same MLE but expressed in the policy space, the same bound applies to the **policy parameters** \(\theta\).  In other words, the number of preference pairs required to achieve a given error \(\varepsilon\) scales as
\[
N = O\!\Bigl(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigr).
\]
No extra data are needed to estimate an intermediate reward function; the constant factors are even smaller because DPO eliminates the variance‑inflating step of sampling from the current policy during RL (the “exploration” noise that PPO needs to estimate a policy gradient).  Empirically this is reflected in Figure 2 of the paper: DPO reaches the same (or higher) reward‑KL frontier with roughly half as many optimisation steps as PPO, precisely because each gradient step uses the *full* supervised signal from a preference pair instead of a noisy Monte‑Carlo estimate of the reward.

### Why the two‑stage method does not improve sample efficiency

One might hope that first fitting a reward model on a *large* set of pairs and then re‑using it for many RL updates would amortise the data cost.  In practice the RL phase still needs **fresh** reward evaluations for each generated trajectory, and the KL‑penalty term introduces an additional optimisation target that must be estimated through on‑policy samples.  The variance of these on‑policy samples dominates the statistical error unless a very large batch of roll‑outs is collected at every PPO step.  Consequently the overall sample complexity of the two‑stage pipeline is
\[
N_{\text{2‑stage}} = N_{\text{reward}} + \underbrace{T\cdot B}_{\text{RL samples}},
\]
where \(T\) is the number of PPO epochs and \(B\) the batch size of generated completions.  Even if \(N_{\text{reward}}\) is modest, the second term often dwarfs it, especially for language models where each sample is a full sequence.

DPO avoids the second term entirely: the gradient (eq. (8)) is an **expectation over the fixed dataset** \(\mathcal D\).  No new model‑generated examples are required once the preference set is collected, so the total number of preference pairs that must be annotated is exactly the MLE requirement \(O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\).

### Putting it together

* **Theoretical guarantee** – DPO learns the same optimal policy as the RL‑HF objective, and because the underlying Bradley‑Terry likelihood is a regular exponential‑family model, the standard \(\sqrt N\)‑consistency result gives a bound \(N = O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\) on the number of pairwise annotations needed to achieve error \(\varepsilon\).

* **Practical sample efficiency** – The bound is **identical** for the two‑stage method’s reward‑learning stage, but the RL stage adds extra stochastic samples that do **not** contribute to reducing the estimation error of the underlying reward; they only add variance.  Hence DPO requires *fewer* total preference pairs to reach the same performance, which is exactly what the empirical frontiers in the paper demonstrate.

* **Intuition** – Think of the reward model as an intermediate “latent variable”.  By re‑parameterising the latent variable as a log‑ratio of policy probabilities (eq. (5)), DPO collapses the two inference steps into one.  The statistical cost of inferring the latent variable is paid once, and the policy automatically inherits the optimality properties, so no extra data are spent on a second inference problem.

In short, the theory in the DPO paper shows that learning a policy directly from pairwise preferences is **statistically as powerful** as first learning a reward and then RL‑optimising, while avoiding the additional sampling noise of the RL loop.  Consequently the number of human‑annotated preference pairs needed is the same asymptotic order \(O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\), but the **constant factor** is smaller for DPO, which explains the observed empirical superiority in sample efficiency.
