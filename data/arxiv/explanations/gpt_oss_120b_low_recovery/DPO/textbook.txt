\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# Motivation and Challenges of Aligning Large Language Models

Large language models (LMs) trained on massive, uncurated text corpora have demonstrated astonishing abilities: they can answer factual questions, generate coherent prose, solve mathematical problems, and even exhibit rudimentary reasoning. This section surveys why these capabilities, while impressive, are insufficient for many downstream applications and why steering such models toward desired behaviours—commonly referred to as **alignment**—is a central research problem.  We then examine the prevailing paradigm of **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**, delineating its constituent stages, and finally discuss the practical and theoretical obstacles that motivate the search for a simpler, more stable alternative.

---

## Capabilities of Large Unsupervised LMs

The term *unsupervised* in the context of language modelling refers to training on raw text without any task‑specific supervision.  Modern LMs such as PaLM, GPT‑3, LLaMA, and other multi‑billion‑parameter transformers are first **pre‑trained** on datasets containing hundreds of billions of tokens.  During this phase the model learns to predict the next token given a prefix, a task that implicitly forces the network to acquire:

1. **World knowledge** – factual information about entities, events, and relationships that appear in the training data.
2. **Linguistic competence** – syntax, style, and discourse structure that enable fluent generation.
3. **General reasoning** – the ability to chain together facts, perform basic arithmetic, and follow logical patterns, even though the training signal never explicitly asks the model to reason.

Empirical studies (e.g., Chowdhery *et al.*, 2022; Brown *et al.*, 2020; Touvron *et al.*, 2023) show that these models can solve many benchmarks zero‑shot or with few‑shot prompting.  Nevertheless, the **distribution of behaviours** that emerges from pure next‑token prediction is extremely broad.  The model reproduces not only the high‑quality text present in its corpus but also the myriad **undesirable patterns** that humans have written: misinformation, toxic language, culturally biased statements, and inefficient coding practices.  Because the pre‑training objective does not discriminate among these patterns, the resulting policy—denoted \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)—is a mixture of useful and harmful behaviours.

---

## Why Steering and Alignment Are Essential

In practice, an LM is deployed as an **assistant** that must obey user intents while respecting safety, factuality, and ethical constraints.  Several concrete scenarios illustrate the need for steering:

- **Coding assistants** should *understand* common programming mistakes (to diagnose them) but must **bias** generation toward correct, clean code.  The undesired behaviour would be to repeat the mistake.
- **Fact‑checking agents** may need to be aware of a popular misconception (to recognize it) yet must **refuse** to present it as true when answering user queries.
- **Conversational agents** must avoid generating hateful or misleading content, even though such content may appear frequently in the raw training data.

These examples highlight a **mismatch** between the knowledge the model has acquired (the *capability* space) and the *behaviour* we wish to exhibit.  Alignment therefore consists of **selecting** a subset of the model’s capacity that conforms to human values and task specifications.  Without a systematic alignment method, deploying LMs at scale would be unsafe and unpredictable.

---

## Overview of Human‑Feedback Alignment (RLHF)

The dominant solution to the alignment problem is **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**, a three‑stage pipeline that has been refined across numerous works (Christiano *et al.*, 2017; Ziegler *et al.*, 2020; Stiennon *et al.*, 2022).  The stages are:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT).**  
   An initially pre‑trained LM \(\pi_{\text{pre}}\) is fine‑tuned on a curated dataset of high‑quality prompts and completions, yielding a *reference policy* \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  This step endows the model with basic instruction‑following abilities and narrows the distribution of outputs.

2. **Preference Collection & Reward Modelling.**  
   The SFT model is used to generate *pairs* of candidate completions \((y_{1}, y_{2})\) for each prompt \(x\).  Human annotators label which completion they prefer, denoted \(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x\).  Assuming a latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) that governs these preferences, the **Bradley–Terry** model formalises the probability of a human preferring one answer over another:
   \[
   p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
   \]
   A parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) (often a shallow head on top of the SFT transformer) is fit by **maximum likelihood** on the binary classification loss
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\bigl[\log \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)\bigr],
   \]
   where \(\sigma\) denotes the logistic function and \(\mathcal{D}\) is the set of annotated comparisons.

3. **Reinforcement Learning Fine‑Tuning.**  
   The learned reward serves as a signal for a **policy optimisation** problem that balances two objectives:
   - **Reward maximisation**: increase expected reward under the policy.
   - **KL‑regularisation**: keep the policy close to the reference \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) to avoid drift into regions where the reward model may be unreliable.
   
   Formally, the optimisation problem is
   \[
   \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
   \;-\;
   \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
   \]
   where \(\beta>0\) controls the strength of the KL penalty and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is typically set to \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  Because language generation is discrete, the expectation cannot be differentiated directly.  Consequently, RLHF adopts **policy‑gradient** methods such as **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** or REINFORCE, which estimate gradients via Monte‑Carlo sampling of completions from \(\pi_{\theta}\).

The overall RLHF procedure has proved effective: models trained with this pipeline achieve state‑of‑the‑art performance on dialogue, summarisation, and code‑generation benchmarks.

---

## Complexities of Reward Modelling and Reinforcement Learning

Despite its empirical successes, RLHF suffers from several intertwined difficulties that complicate research and production deployment:

1. **Reward Model Instability.**  
   The reward model is trained on a *finite* set of pairwise preferences.  Its predictions can be poorly calibrated on out‑of‑distribution completions, leading to **reward hacking** where the policy learns to maximise spurious artefacts of the reward rather than genuine human‑preferred behaviour.  Normalising the reward (e.g., forcing mean zero) mitigates variance but does not eliminate the fundamental under‑specification of the Bradley–Terry formulation: adding any function of the prompt \(f(x)\) to the reward leaves the preference probabilities unchanged.

2. **Sampling Overhead.**  
   PPO requires **on‑policy sampling** at each optimisation step: the current policy must generate many completions to estimate the gradient.  For large transformer models, each forward pass is costly, and the need to sample *many* tokens per update dramatically inflates training time and GPU memory consumption.

3. **Hyper‑parameter Sensitivity.**  
   The KL penalty coefficient \(\beta\) (or an explicit KL target) must be tuned carefully.  Too small a penalty permits the policy to drift far from the reference, possibly entering regions where the reward model is unreliable; too large a penalty hampers reward improvement.  PPO also has numerous algorithmic knobs (clip thresholds, learning rates, value‑function baselines) that are typically set via extensive empirical sweeps.

4. **Variance of Policy‑Gradient Estimates.**  
   Monte‑Carlo estimators for the expected reward have high variance, especially when the reward signal is noisy.  Actor‑critic variants mitigate this by learning a baseline value function, but training the critic introduces another source of instability and requires additional optimisation machinery.

5. **Implementation Complexity.**  
   The RLHF pipeline involves **multiple separate models** (the SFT model, the reward head, the policy network, and often a value network) and **alternating optimisation loops** (supervised fine‑tuning, reward fitting, RL).  Engineers must orchestrate data pipelines that alternate between generating new completions, collecting human labels, and updating distinct parameter sets.  This complexity raises the barrier to entry for researchers wishing to experiment with preference‑based alignment.

These challenges collectively motivate the search for a method that **preserves the alignment objective**—maximising human‑compatible reward while respecting a KL constraint—yet **removes the need for explicit reinforcement learning** and its associated instability.

---

## Desire for a Simpler, More Stable Method

The key insight underpinning recent work on **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** is that the **optimal policy** for the KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem admits a *closed‑form* relationship to the underlying reward function.  Starting from the constrained optimisation

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),
\]

the optimum can be expressed as

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\]

Taking logarithms yields a *re‑parameterisation* of the reward in terms of any policy \(\pi\) that could be optimal:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards, the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels when expressing the human preference probability.  Substituting the above expression for \(r\) gives a preference likelihood that depends **solely on the policy**:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{1}
{1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\Bigr)}.
\]

Consequently, fitting a policy to maximise the likelihood of observed human preferences reduces to a **binary cross‑entropy loss** over the policy logits:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

Optimising this objective directly **updates the language model** without ever instantiating an explicit reward head, nor invoking a reinforcement‑learning loop.  The gradient of the loss naturally incorporates a per‑example weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\), which down‑weights examples where the current implicit reward already ranks the preferred response higher, thereby preventing the degeneration observed with naïve unlikelihood training.

The **advantages** of this reformulation are immediate:

- **No sampling during training**: gradients are computed analytically from the logged probabilities of the chosen and rejected completions; no on‑policy rollouts are required.
- **Single‑model training**: the same transformer parameters serve both as the policy and as an implicit reward, eliminating a separate reward network.
- **Stable optimisation**: the loss is a standard binary cross‑entropy, for which well‑understood optimisation tricks (learning‑rate schedules, batch normalisation, weight decay) apply.
- **Reduced hyper‑parameter burden**: only the KL‑strength \(\beta\) must be chosen; there is no need for PPO‑specific clipping or value‑function learning rates.

Thus, Direct Preference Optimization offers a **theoretically grounded, computationally lightweight** alternative to the traditional RLHF pipeline, preserving the essential alignment objective while sidestepping the principal sources of instability and engineering complexity.  The remainder of this textbook will develop the DPO algorithm in detail, analyse its theoretical properties, and present empirical evidence of its efficacy across a range of language‑generation tasks.

**Foundations: Preference Modeling and the RLHF Objective**

---

### Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) as a Reference Policy  

Large language models are first trained in an unsupervised fashion on massive text corpora.  After this pre‑training stage, a **supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** step is usually performed to bring the model into a regime where it can answer task‑specific queries.  In practice, a collection of high‑quality input–output pairs (for example, prompts together with human‑written completions) is assembled, and the model parameters are updated by maximizing the likelihood of the provided completions.  The result is a conditional distribution  

\[
\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y)\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y')\bigr)},
\]

where \(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y)\) denotes the final‑layer logits of the fine‑tuned transformer.  This distribution serves as the **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in later stages of the RLHF pipeline.  Because \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) already produces plausible text, it provides a stable starting point for learning to obey human preferences while preventing the fine‑tuned model from drifting into regions of the text space where the subsequently learned reward model would be unreliable.

---

### Human Preference Collection and the Latent Reward Function  

The central insight of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is that **relative judgments**—“which of two completions is better?”—are often much cheaper to obtain than absolute quality scores or full demonstrations.  To build a preference dataset we first sample, for each prompt \(x\), two candidate completions  

\[
y_{1},y_{2}\sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot\mid x).
\]

Human annotators are then asked to indicate a preference, which we denote  

\[
y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x,
\]

meaning the completion \(y_{w}\) (the *winner*) is judged superior to \(y_{l}\) (the *loser*).  Implicit in these judgments is a **latent reward function**  

\[
r^{*}(x,y),
\]

which assigns a scalar score to any response to prompt \(x\).  The reward is not observed directly; we only see the ordering induced by pairs of responses.  The goal of the reward‑modeling stage is to infer a parameterized approximation \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) that is consistent with the observed preferences.

---

### Bradley‑Terry Model and Its Mathematical Form  

A standard probabilistic model for binary preferences is the **Bradley‑Terry (BT) model**.  Under this model the probability that a human would prefer completion \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) for a given prompt \(x\) is  

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
     {\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Only the **difference** of the two rewards matters; adding a constant that depends solely on the prompt \(x\) leaves the preference probabilities unchanged.  Although more elaborate ranking models such as the Plackett‑Luce distribution exist, the BT formulation is sufficient for binary comparisons and is the one employed throughout the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) work.

---

### Maximum‑Likelihood Reward Modeling  

Given a static dataset of preferences  

\[
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

we fit a parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}\) by **maximum likelihood** under the BT likelihood.  Treating each preference as a binary classification problem, the negative log‑likelihood loss becomes  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\bigl[
\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)
\bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) is the logistic function.  In practice the reward model is instantiated as the SFT transformer with an additional linear head that outputs a single scalar per token sequence.  For numerical stability and to satisfy the under‑identifiability of the BT model, the rewards are often **centered** so that \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]=0\).

---

### KL‑Constrained RL Objective and Its Role  

Once a reward model has been learned, the next phase of RLHF seeks a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that **maximizes expected reward while staying close to the reference policy**.  This trade‑off is expressed as the following constrained optimization problem:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}
\;\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;
\beta\,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The first term encourages the policy to generate responses that receive high predicted reward.  The second term penalizes the **Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence** between the new policy and the reference policy, scaled by a coefficient \(\beta>0\).  This KL penalty fulfills three essential purposes:

1. **Stability** – it prevents the policy from moving into parts of the output space where the reward model, trained on SFT data, may be poorly calibrated.  
2. **Diversity** – it maintains the breadth of generations that the original model exhibited, avoiding mode collapse to a single high‑reward answer.  
3. **Safety** – it limits the magnitude of policy updates, which is crucial when the reward model is an imperfect proxy for human judgment.

Equation (3) is the canonical objective used by most RLHF pipelines, including those that employ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or REINFORCE.

---

### Reinforcement‑Learning Algorithms in Practice  

Because text generation involves a discrete, sequential action space, the expectation in (3) cannot be differentiated directly.  Two families of policy‑gradient methods are commonly employed:

* **REINFORCE** – The classic Monte‑Carlo policy gradient estimator uses sampled trajectories \((x,y)\) to compute an unbiased gradient  

  \[
  \nabla_{\theta}\!\bigl[\mathbb{E}[r_{\phi}]\bigr]\approx
  (r_{\phi}(x,y)-b(x))\;\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x),
  \]

  where \(b(x)\) is a baseline (often the average reward) that reduces variance.

* **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** – PPO augments REINFORCE with a **clipping** mechanism that discourages large policy updates.  The surrogate objective typically takes the form  

  \[
  L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[
  \min\bigl(r_{t}(\theta)A_{t},\,
  \operatorname{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{t})\bigr)
  \Bigr]
  -\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),
  \]

  where \(r_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\) and \(A_{t}\) is the advantage estimate derived from the reward model.  The KL term appears both implicitly (through the clipped ratio) and explicitly, enforcing the constraint from (3).

Both algorithms require **sampling** from the current policy at each training step, large‑scale **value‑function** estimation (to compute baselines or advantages), and careful **hyper‑parameter tuning** (learning rates, clipping thresholds, target KL values).  These engineering demands contribute to the complexity and instability frequently observed in RLHF pipelines.

From Rewards to Policies: The Closed‑Form Optimal Policy
================================================================

In this chapter we expose the mathematical heart of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  The central observation is that the KL‑constrained reward‑maximisation problem that underlies conventional RL‑from‑Human‑Feedback (RLHF) admits a **closed‑form optimal policy**.  By writing this optimal policy explicitly we can replace the usual two‑stage pipeline—first fitting a reward model, then running reinforcement learning—with a single maximum‑likelihood problem directly over the policy.  We develop the derivation step‑by‑step, interpret the role of the normalising partition function \(Z(x)\), and show how the partition function disappears when we consider *preference ratios* rather than absolute probabilities.  This analysis yields the re‑parameterisation of the reward in terms of the policy (Equation 5 in the paper) that makes DPO possible.

---

## Derivation of the Optimal Policy Formula (Eq. 4)

The RLHF optimisation objective (Equation 3 of the paper) seeks a language‑model policy \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that maximises the expected reward while staying close, in Kullback–Leibler (KL) sense, to a fixed *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (often the supervised‑fine‑tuned model \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)):

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\bigl[\,r(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;
\beta \,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(r(x,y)\) is any scalar reward function (learned or handcrafted) and \(\beta>0\) controls the strength of the KL regulariser.  The objective is *concave* in \(\pi_{\theta}\) because the KL term is convex and the reward term is linear in the policy.  Therefore, for a fixed reward function the optimum can be obtained by solving a constrained optimisation problem for each context \(x\) independently.

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda(x)\) to enforce that \(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\) remains a proper distribution:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=
\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x) r(x,y)
-
\beta \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
-
\lambda(x)\Bigl(\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)-1\Bigr).
\]

Taking the derivative of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and setting it to zero yields

\[
r(x,y) - \beta\Bigl(1 + \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr) - \lambda(x)=0,
\]

which can be rearranged to

\[
\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = \frac{1}{\beta}\bigl(r(x,y)-\lambda(x)-\beta\bigr).
\]

Exponentiating both sides gives

\[
\pi(y\mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\beta}(\lambda(x)+\beta)\Bigr).
\]

Because \(\lambda(x)\) does not depend on \(y\), the factor
\(\exp\!\bigl(-\frac{1}{\beta}(\lambda(x)+\beta)\bigr)\) is a normalising constant that ensures the probabilities sum to one.  Defining the **partition function**

\[
Z(x) \;=\;\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

the normalising constant is exactly \(1/Z(x)\).  Substituting back we obtain the **optimal KL‑constrained policy**:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\;
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
}
\tag{3}
\]

which is Equation 4 of the paper.  The derivation shows that the optimal policy is a *Boltzmann‑weighted* version of the reference policy, where the weight is the exponentiated reward scaled by \(1/\beta\).

---

## Interpretation of the Partition Function \(Z(x)\)

The partition function \(Z(x)\) plays two distinct roles:

1. **Normalisation:**  Because we are re‑weighting the reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by a positive factor \(\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)\), the resulting vector no longer sums to one.  Dividing by \(Z(x)\) restores a proper probability distribution.  In statistical‑physics language, \(Z(x)\) is the *partition function* of a Gibbs distribution with energy \(-r(x,y)\) and temperature \(\beta\).

2. **Implicit Value Function:**  The logarithm of \(Z(x)\) can be interpreted as the *soft* value of the optimal policy under the reference policy:
   \[
   V_{\text{soft}}(x) = \beta \log Z(x)
   = \beta \log\!\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
   \]
   This quantity appears in the derivation of actor‑critic methods: it is the baseline that, if subtracted from the raw reward, reduces the variance of policy‑gradient estimates.  In conventional RLHF, one must either estimate \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) with a separate value network or approximate it with a Monte‑Carlo baseline, both of which add computational overhead.

Importantly, \(Z(x)\) depends **only on the context** \(x\) (through the reference distribution) and the reward function, but **not** on a particular completion \(y\).  This independence is the key to the simplifications that follow.

---

## Re‑parameterizing Reward in Terms of Policy (Eq. 5)

Equation 3 gives the optimal policy as a function of the reward.  Since the optimal policy is what we ultimately care about, we can *invert* this relationship: express the reward that would make a given policy \(\pi\) optimal.  Taking logarithms of both sides of Equation 3:

\[
\log \pi(y\mid x) = \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y) - \log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging terms gives an explicit reward parameterisation:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
r(x,y) = \beta \bigl[\log \pi(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr] + \beta \log Z(x).
}
\tag{4}
\]

This is Equation 5 of the paper.  The first term, \(\beta\bigl(\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\bigr)\), quantifies how much more (or less) probability the policy \(\pi\) assigns to a completion compared with the reference.  The second term, \(\beta\log Z(x)\), is a *per‑context offset* that does not depend on \(y\).

Because the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) preference model depends only on **differences of rewards**, the additive offset \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels out when we consider a pair of completions \((y_{w},y_{l})\).  Consequently the *implicit* reward that corresponds to a policy \(\pi\) can be written without any reference to the intractable partition function:

\[
r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}) \;=\;
\beta\Bigl[
\log \frac{\pi(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\log \frac{\pi(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Bigr].
\tag{5}
\]

Equation 5 is the bridge that allows us to replace the reward‑model learning step entirely: we can directly optimise a policy by maximising the likelihood of observed human preferences under the *re‑parameterised* Bradley‑Terry model that uses the quantity above.

---

## Why the Partition Function Cancels in Preference Ratios

Human preference data are typically binary comparisons: given a prompt \(x\) and two completions \(y_{1}, y_{2}\), annotators indicate which completion they prefer.  The Bradley‑Terry model posits that the probability of preferring \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) is

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)}%
     {\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{6}
\]

Substituting the re‑parameterised reward (Equation 4) into (6) yields

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
}{
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
+
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
}.
\]

Because \(\exp(\beta\log Z(x)) = Z(x)^{\beta}\) multiplies *both* numerator and denominator, it cancels:

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{
\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
}{
\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
+
\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\bigl[\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr)}.
\tag{7}
\]

Equation 7 is precisely the *preference likelihood* used in the DPO loss (Equation 8 of the paper).  The crucial observation is that **the partition function disappears** because preferences compare *ratios* of exponentiated rewards, and the additive offset \(\beta\log Z(x)\) contributes the same factor to every term in the ratio.  This cancellation removes the need to compute the expensive sum over all possible completions, which would be intractable for language models with a large vocabulary and long sequences.

---

### Summary

- By solving the KL‑constrained maximisation problem we obtain a closed‑form optimal policy (Eq. 4) that is a Boltzmann re‑weighting of a reference policy.
- The partition function \(Z(x)\) normalises the re‑weighted distribution and can be interpreted as a soft value of the reference policy.
- Re‑expressing the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 5) reveals that the only policy‑dependent component of the reward is the log‑ratio \(\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\); the partition function adds a context‑only offset.
- When preferences are modeled with a Bradley‑Terry (or Plackett‑Luce) likelihood, the offset cancels, leading to a loss that depends solely on *policy* probabilities.  This cancellation permits a **single binary‑cross‑entropy objective** over the policy, eliminating the need for an explicit reward model or reinforcement‑learning loop.

These derivations provide the theoretical foundation for Direct Preference Optimization: training a language model to satisfy human preferences by directly maximising the likelihood of the observed preference data under the re‑parameterised policy model.  The result is a method that is both mathematically clean and practically lightweight, as explained in later chapters.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): Algorithmic Design**

# Substituting the Re‑parameterization into Bradley‑Terry  

The Bradley‑Terry (BT) model is the cornerstone of most preference‑learning pipelines for large language models.  Under this model a human is assumed to choose the preferred completion \(y_{w}\) over the dispreferred one \(y_{l}\) according to a latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\):
\[
p^{*}\!\left(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\right)
  =\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
         {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)}
  =\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic sigmoid.

In the classic RLHF pipeline the reward is first estimated from data, then a language‑model policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is trained to maximize the expected reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The constrained optimisation can be written as
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}
  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
  -\beta\,
  \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]
with \(\beta>0\) controlling the strength of the KL‑regulariser.  The optimal solution of (2) for any given reward \(r\) is analytically known (e.g. via the method of Lagrange multipliers) to be
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
        \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]
Equation (3) tells us that any optimal policy is obtained by re‑weighting the reference distribution with an exponentiated reward.  Crucially, we can solve (3) for the reward itself:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)
  &=\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
    +\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x)\\[4pt]
\Longrightarrow\quad
r(x,y)
  &=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
                +\log Z(x)\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
The term \(\log Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\); it does **not** affect the preference between two completions because it cancels in the difference \(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})\).

Substituting (4) into the BT likelihood (1) yields a preference probability that is expressed entirely in terms of the **policy** \(\pi_{r}\) and the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
p^{*}\!\left(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\right)
  &=\sigma\!\Bigl(
        r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})
       \Bigr)                                                                  \\
  &=\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
        -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
       \Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]
Equation (5) is the key observation: the BT model can be re‑parameterised so that the latent reward never appears explicitly; the model’s *policy* itself plays the role of the reward‑inducing object.

# Derivation of the DPO Objective (Eq. 7)

Given a finite dataset of human preference triples
\[
\mathcal{D}
 =\bigl\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\tag{6}
\]
the natural maximum‑likelihood estimator for the BT model (5) is obtained by maximising the log‑probability of the observed preferences.  Re‑writing the negated log‑likelihood yields the **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss**:
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
   = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
      \Bigl[
         \log \sigma\!\Bigl(
               \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
              -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
            \Bigr)
      \Bigr].
}
\tag{7}
\]
All quantities on the right‑hand side are directly computable from two forward passes of the language model: one for the preferred completion and one for the dispreferred completion.  No auxiliary reward network, no Monte‑Carlo estimate of a partition function, and no RL‑style policy‑gradient estimator are required.  The objective (7) is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss where the “logit” is the **difference of importance‑weighted log‑probabilities**.

# Gradient Analysis and the Importance‑Weight Term  

To understand why DPO succeeds where a naïve probability‑ratio loss may fail, we differentiate (7) with respect to the model parameters \(\theta\).  Define the **implicit reward** induced by the current policy:
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)
   \;\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\;
   \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\tag{8}
\]
Using the chain rule and the derivative of the logistic function,
\(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
  &= -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
     \Bigl[
        \sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)
        \Bigl(
          \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
          -\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)
        \Bigr)
     \Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\]
The scalar factor
\[
\underbrace{\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}_{\text{importance weight}}
\]
is large when the current policy **mis‑orders** the pair (i.e. assigns a higher implicit reward to the dispreferred completion).  Consequently, the gradient puts more pressure on such “hard” examples, while pairs that are already correctly ordered contribute little.  This adaptive weighting is absent in a naïve loss of the form
\(-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}\),
which would indiscriminately increase the probability of \(y_{w}\) and decrease that of \(y_{l}\).  The naïve loss can drive the model to assign absurdly high probabilities to the preferred completions while neglecting the KL‑regularisation embodied by \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), leading to **policy collapse** (the model drifts far from its original distribution).

A related baseline is the **unlikelihood** objective, which explicitly adds a term \(-\alpha\log\bigl(1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr)\) to penalise dispreferred tokens.  In the unlikelihood setting the weighting coefficient \(\alpha\) is fixed a priori; the method cannot adapt to how badly the current policy ranks a specific pair.  Empirically (see the paper’s Appendix) the unlikelihood loss without the importance weight leads to degraded generation quality, confirming the theoretical insight from (9).  DPO’s gradient therefore achieves a balanced trade‑off: it respects the KL‑constraint by comparing each probability to the reference model, and it focuses learning on the mistakes that matter most for the preference data.

# Comparison with Naïve Probability‑Ratio and Unlikelihood Methods  

| Method | Objective (simplified) | Use of reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) | Adaptive weighting | Theoretical guarantee |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| **Naïve probability‑ratio** | \(-\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}\) | No | No (all pairs equal) | None; can violate KL‑constraint |
| **Unlikelihood** | \(-\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}) - \alpha\log (1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}))\) | No (sometimes a reference is used to initialise) | Fixed \(\alpha\) | No explicit KL regularisation |
| **DPO** | Eq. (7) (binary cross‑entropy on \(\beta\)‑scaled log‑probability ratios) | Yes, appears in the denominator of each ratio | Yes, via \(\sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w})\bigr)\) | Exactly solves the KL‑constrained RLHF objective under the BT model |

The naïve ratio loss resembles **maximum likelihood** on a deterministic preference label, but it ignores the crucial fact that preferences are *relative* and that the underlying reward is only defined up to an additive function of the prompt (the equivalence class in Lemma 2).  DPO, by construction, respects that equivalence because the additive term \(\log Z(x)\) cancels out.  Moreover, the inclusion of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in the log‑ratio enforces the KL constraint **implicitly**: if the policy drifts far from the reference, the ratio will inflate, increasing the loss and pulling the policy back.

# Algorithmic Pipeline  

Putting the pieces together, the DPO training loop consists of three logical stages:

1. **Data collection**  
   - Sample two completions \((y_{1},y_{2})\) from a *reference* language model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for each prompt \(x\).  
   - Obtain a binary preference label from a human annotator (or a trusted automatic proxy).  
   - Store the triple \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) in the preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}\).

2. **Reference model construction**  
   - If a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is available, set \(\pi_{\text{ref}} \gets \pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  
   - If no SFT model exists, train a *reference* policy by **maximising the likelihood of the preferred completions** only:
     \[
       \pi_{\text{ref}}
         = \arg\max_{\pi}\;
           \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w})\sim\mathcal{D}}
               \bigl[\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\bigr].
     \]
     This step ensures that the reference distribution is close to the data‑generating distribution, reducing the distribution shift that would otherwise appear in Eq. (5).

3. **Direct Preference Optimization**  
   - Initialise the *target* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) with the same weights as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
   - For each minibatch drawn from \(\mathcal{D}\), compute the loss (7).  
   - Perform a standard stochastic‑gradient descent (or Adam) update on \(\theta\).  
   - Optionally anneal the temperature \(\beta\) or adjust it after a few epochs to trade‑off reward maximisation versus conservatism.

The full algorithm can be summarised succinctly:

```
Input: preference dataset D, reference policy π_ref, hyperparameter β
Initialize θ ← θ_ref   (weights of π_ref)
repeat
    Sample minibatch B ⊂ D
    Compute loss L_DPO(θ) using Eq.(7) on B
    θ ← θ – η ∇_θ L_DPO(θ)          (η = learning rate)
until convergence
Output: policy π_θ
```

Because the loss is a **pure classification objective**, training proceeds exactly like ordinary supervised fine‑tuning: there is no need for on‑policy sampling, advantage estimation, or value‑function baselines.  This dramatically reduces memory consumption (no roll‑out buffers) and eliminates the notorious instability of policy‑gradient methods.

# Practical Tips  

### Initialising \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)  

- **When an SFT checkpoint exists** (e.g., a model fine‑tuned on an instruction‑following corpus), use it directly.  The SFT model already represents the distribution of “reasonable” responses, so the KL penalty will keep the final policy within the domain where the preference data is reliable.  
- **When no SFT is available**, train a lightweight reference model on the *preferred* half of the dataset only.  This is essentially *Preferred‑FT*: maximise \(\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\) while ignoring \(y_{l}\).  Empirically this yields a reference that is close enough to the true data‑generating distribution to avoid severe covariate shift.

### Choosing \(\beta\)  

- \(\beta\) governs the **strength of the KL regulariser**.  Small \(\beta\) (e.g., \(0.05\)–\(0.1\)) makes the loss focus heavily on matching preferences, allowing larger deviations from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Larger \(\beta\) (e.g., \(1\)–\(5\)) forces the policy to stay nearer to the reference, which can be useful when the preference data are noisy or when preserving generation diversity is critical.  
- A pragmatic approach is to try a grid \(\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\) and pick the model that yields the best validation win‑rate or the most favourable reward–KL trade‑off.  Unlike PPO, DPO does **not** require a separate KL‑target schedule, because the KL penalty is baked into the loss.

### Handling Missing SFT or Distribution Shift  

- If the preference dataset was collected with a different base model than the one you intend to use as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), you may observe a mismatch in token probabilities.  Mitigate this by:  
  1. **Fine‑tuning** the reference model on a small subset of the raw prompts (no completions) using language‑modeling loss; this aligns token‑distribution statistics.  
  2. **Increasing \(\beta\)** to penalise large departures from the reference, which empirically reduces drift caused by the mismatch.  

### Numerical Stability  

- Compute the log‑ratio \(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) by first obtaining logits from both models and subtracting them before applying the softmax denominator.  This avoids underflow when raw probabilities are extremely small.  
- Clip the log‑ratio to a reasonable range (e.g., \([-10,10]\)) before feeding it to the sigmoid; the clipping does not affect gradients much because the sigmoid already saturates.

### Batch‑wise Implementation  

The loss can be vectorised efficiently: for a batch of size \(B\), retrieve the logits for all \(2B\) completions (preferred and dispreferred), compute the per‑example log‑ratios, apply the sigmoid, and finally average the binary‑cross‑entropy.  Modern deep‑learning libraries (PyTorch, JAX) allow this with a few lines of code and no custom autograd functions.

### Monitoring  

- Track **average KL** between \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): this can be estimated analytically on the batch as  
  \[
    \text{KL}_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
      \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})},
  \]  
  where \(y^{(i)}\) can be the preferred completion for consistency.  
- Plot the **training loss** together with the **importance weight** \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))\).  A rapidly decreasing weight indicates that the model is correctly ordering most pairs, a sign of convergence.

# Summary  

Direct Preference Optimization replaces the two‑stage RLHF pipeline (reward‑model fitting + policy‑gradient RL) with a single, closed‑form objective that operates directly on the policy.  By exploiting the analytical relationship between the KL‑constrained optimal policy and its inducing reward (Eq. (3)), we re‑parameterise the Bradley‑Terry likelihood so that the latent reward vanishes and only the policy appears (Eq. (5)).  Maximising the resulting likelihood yields the DPO loss (Eq. (7)), whose gradient (Eq. (9)) contains an *importance‑weight* that automatically focuses learning on mis‑ranked pairs while preserving the KL regulariser through the reference model.  Compared with naïve probability‑ratio or unlikelihood baselines, DPO enjoys a solid theoretical guarantee—exactly solving the RLHF objective—and empirical advantages: stability, low computational overhead, and competitive or superior performance on sentiment control, summarisation, and dialogue tasks.  Implementing DPO is straightforward: collect preference triples, define a reference policy (preferably an SFT model), choose a sensible \(\beta\), and train with ordinary supervised optimisation machinery.  This simplicity opens the door to wider adoption of preference‑based alignment for large language models.

**Theoretical Guarantees and Connections**

In this chapter we delve into the formal underpinnings of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  Starting from the Bradley‑Terry (or, more generally, Plackett‑Luce) model of human preferences we develop a sequence of results that explain why DPO—although it never explicitly learns a separate reward function nor runs a reinforcement‑learning loop—still recovers the exact optimal policy that would be obtained by the traditional RLHF pipeline.  The key insight is that the reward function is only defined up to an additive term that depends on the prompt, and that by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of a *ratio* of the policy we wish to learn and a fixed reference policy, we obtain a representation that is both universal (any admissible reward class can be written in this form) and tractable (the optimal policy can be written in closed form).  The results below formalise these ideas.

---

### 1.  An Equivalence Relation on Reward Functions  

Consider a pair consisting of a prompt \(x\) and a candidate completion \(y\).  A reward function is any mapping  

\[
r:\;(x,y)\mapsto\mathbb{R}.
\]

Because the Bradley‑Terry model (and all Plackett‑Luce models) depend **only on differences of rewards**, adding a function of the prompt alone does not change any observable preference.  This motivates the following relation.

> **Definition (Reward‑equivalence).**  
> Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are *equivalent*, written \(r\sim r'\), iff there exists a scalar‑valued function \(f\) on prompts such that for every \((x,y)\)
> \[
> r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x).
> \]

The relation is clearly reflexive (\(f\equiv0\)), symmetric (if \(r-r'=f\) then \(r'-r=-f\)), and transitive (sums of additive terms remain additive).  Consequently, the set of all reward functions is partitioned into *equivalence classes*; within a class every member induces exactly the same pairwise preferences.

Intuitively, the additive term \(f(x)\) can be interpreted as a *baseline* that shifts the absolute value of the reward for every candidate belonging to a given prompt, but leaves the ordering of candidates untouched.  This observation lies at the heart of the under‑specification problem of preference models.

---

### 2.  Lemma 1 – Identical Preferences for Equivalent Rewards  

> **Lemma 1.**  
> Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be two reward functions such that \(r\sim r'\).  Under the Bradley‑Terry model,
> \[
> p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)}
> \]
> and the analogous expression using \(r'\) are equal for every pair \((y_1,y_2)\) and prompt \(x\).

*Proof Sketch.*  Substituting \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\) into the numerator and denominator yields a common factor \(\exp\!\bigl(-f(x)\bigr)\) that cancels.  Hence the two probabilities are identical. ∎

The lemma formalises the informal claim that the Bradley‑Terry likelihood is invariant to any additive, prompt‑only shift of the reward.  Consequently, when we fit a reward model by maximising the binary cross‑entropy loss (Eq. (2) in the paper), the optimiser can only hope to recover the *equivalence class* of the true latent reward \(r^{*}\), never a unique scalar field.

---

### 3.  Lemma 2 – Identical Optimal Policies for Equivalent Rewards  

Recall the KL‑constrained optimisation problem that underlies RLHF:

\[
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The solution of this convex optimisation problem is known in closed form (Eq. (4) in the paper):

\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

> **Lemma 2.**  
> If \(r\sim r'\), then the optimal policies \(\pi_{r}\) and \(\pi_{r'}\) obtained from (4) are identical for every prompt \(x\).

*Proof Sketch.*  Insert \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\) into (4).  The factor \(\exp[-f(x)/\beta]\) appears both in the numerator (inside the exponential) and in the partition function \(Z_{r'}(x)\); it cancels, leaving \(\pi_{r'}(y|x)=\pi_{r}(y|x)\). ∎

Thus, the optimisation problem (3) is *well‑defined* on reward equivalence classes: any member of the class yields the same optimal policy.  This fact justifies the change of variables that underpins DPO; we can safely replace the unknown reward by any convenient representative of its class without altering the target policy.

---

### 4.  Theorem 1 – Universal Representation of Reward Classes via \(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\)

We have seen that for a given reward \(r\) the optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) takes the exponential‑tilted form (4).  Conversely, we can *solve* (4) for the reward that would generate a prescribed policy \(\pi\).  This yields the following representation theorem.

> **Theorem 1.**  
> Let \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) be a fixed reference policy and let \(\beta>0\).  
> For any policy \(\pi\) that is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (i.e. \(\pi(y|x)>0\) whenever \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)>0\)), define  
> \[
> r_{\pi}(x,y)\;:=\;\beta\,
> \log\!\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\;+\;\beta\,
> \log Z_{\pi}(x),
> \tag{5}
> \]
> where \(Z_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_{\pi}(x,y')\bigr)\).  
> Then \(r_{\pi}\) belongs to the same equivalence class as any reward \(r\) whose optimal policy is \(\pi\).  Moreover, any reward class admissible under the Bradley‑Terry (or Plackett‑Luce) model admits a representative of the form (5).

*Proof Sketch.*  Starting from the optimality condition (4) we take logarithms, rearrange, and obtain Eq. (5) with an *unknown* additive term \(\beta\log Z_{\pi}(x)\).  Because \(Z_{\pi}(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\), the second term is precisely of the form required by the equivalence relation (Definition).  Hence \(r_{\pi}\) and any true reward \(r^{*}\) that yields the same policy differ by a prompt‑only function, i.e. they are equivalent.  Conversely, given any reward class, pick any representative \(r\) and define \(\pi:=\pi_{r}\) via (4).  Substituting \(\pi\) back into (5) reconstructs a reward that lies in the same class as \(r\).  Thus the mapping \(r\leftrightarrow\pi\) is bijective modulo the equivalence relation. ∎

The theorem tells us that **every admissible reward class can be expressed as a logarithmic ratio between a policy and a reference policy**, up to a normalising constant that is irrelevant for preferences.  Therefore, *parameterising the reward implicitly through the policy* loses no expressive power.

---

### 5.  Implications for Under‑Specification and Identifiability  

The Bradley‑Terry model suffers from *under‑specification*: many reward functions generate exactly the same observable preferences.  This is often called the *identifiability* problem, because fitting Eq. (2) can only recover a reward up to an additive, prompt‑dependent term.  Classical solutions impose an explicit constraint, e.g. forcing the expected reward to be zero over the dataset.

Theorem 1 shows that this under‑specification is **harmless** for the RLHF objective.  Since every member of an equivalence class yields the same optimal policy (Lemma 2), any additional constraint we impose on the reward merely selects a particular representative of the class.  DPO’s re‑parameterisation (5) automatically makes a canonical choice: it sets the additive term to exactly \(\beta\log Z_{\pi}(x)\), which is the log‑partition required for (4) to be a proper probability distribution.  Consequently, DPO sidesteps the need for ad‑hoc normalisation: the policy itself guarantees that the induced reward is already *properly calibrated*.

In practice this means that, when we train a policy by maximising the DPO loss (Eq. (7) in the paper),

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

we are directly performing maximum‑likelihood estimation of the *re‑parameterised* Bradley‑Terry model.  No separate reward network needs to be fitted, and no extra regularisation is required to resolve the under‑specification.

---

### 6.  Why DPO Recovers the Exact Optimal Policy  

Putting the pieces together, the logical chain is:

1. **Preference Data → Bradley‑Terry Likelihood.**  
   Human binary choices are modelled by Eq. (1).  The likelihood depends only on reward differences.

2. **Equivalence Classes.**  
   Lemma 1 tells us that any two rewards differing by a prompt‑only additive term generate the same likelihood.  

3. **Optimal Policy is Invariant.**  
   Lemma 2 guarantees that the KL‑constrained optimisation (3) yields the same optimal policy for any member of the class.

4. **Closed‑Form Mapping.**  
   Theorem 1 provides an explicit bijection between a policy and a *canonical* reward in the class: \(r_{\pi}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) (up to the irrelevant partition term).

5. **Direct Maximisation.**  
   Substituting the canonical reward into the Bradley‑Terry likelihood eliminates the unknown additive term, producing the DPO loss (Eq. (7)).  Optimising this loss over the policy parameters therefore performs *exact* maximum‑likelihood estimation of the policy that would be optimal under the original RLHF objective (3).

Because the DPO objective is precisely the likelihood of the *optimal* policy under the original preference model, any (local) optimum of the DPO loss is also an optimum of the RLHF objective.  Moreover, the gradient expression (Eq. (8) in the paper) shows that DPO naturally weights each training pair by how badly the current policy’s implicit reward mis‑orders the pair, thereby preventing the degenerate behaviour observed in naïve un‑likelihood updates.

In summary, DPO does **not** approximate the RLHF solution—it recovers it exactly, under the same modelling assumptions (Bradley‑Terry or Plackett‑Luce) and the same KL‑constraint parameter \(\beta\).  The only practical simplifications are the removal of a separate reward‑model training stage and the avoidance of any reinforcement‑learning loop; the theoretical guarantees remain untouched.

Why Standard Actor‑Critic Methods Can Fail  
================================================

In recent years, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has become the dominant paradigm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with user intentions.  The most common implementation of RLHF relies on an actor‑critic algorithm—typically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—to maximize a learned reward while constraining the policy to remain close to a reference model.  Despite its empirical success, PPO can be unstable, especially when applied to the high‑dimensional, discrete action space of text generation.  This chapter dissects the source of these instabilities from a control‑as‑inference perspective, walks through the derivation of the actor‑critic objective used in RLHF, highlights the crucial role of the soft‑value (normalisation) term, and explains why the resulting gradient estimator suffers from high variance and a brittle dependence on baselines.  Finally, we show how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) sidesteps these pitfalls by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of the policy itself, thereby removing the need for an explicit baseline.

---

## Control‑as‑Inference View of KL‑Constrained RL  

Reinforcement learning can be recast as a probabilistic inference problem.  Suppose we have a *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (often the supervised‑fine‑tuned model) and a scalar reward function \(r(x,y)\) that scores a response \(y\) to a prompt \(x\).  The RLHF objective augments the reward with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty that discourages the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) from drifting too far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Bigl[\, r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \,
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \Bigr].
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(\beta>0\) determines the strength of the KL regulariser.  Interpreting (1) as an inference problem, we introduce a *soft* optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) that maximises the exponentiated reward while being anchored to the reference distribution:
\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x) \propto 
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]
Equation (2) follows from standard calculus of variations with a KL constraint (see Appendix A of the original paper).  The normalising constant,
\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\tag{3}
\]
is often called the *soft‑value* because its logarithm is the soft‑max analogue of the conventional value function.

From the inference viewpoint, the RL objective (1) can be written as a KL divergence minimisation:
\[
\min_{\pi_{\theta}} \; 
\mathbb{D}_{\!KL}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\tag{4}
\]
i.e. we seek a policy that approximates the analytically optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).  The challenge is that for text generation the sum in (3) is intractable—there are exponentially many possible completions—so we cannot evaluate \(\pi^{*}\) exactly.  PPO sidesteps this by estimating gradients from sampled trajectories and by introducing a learned *critic* (value function) to approximate the soft‑value term.

---

## Derivation of the Actor‑Critic Objective (Eq. 10)

Starting from (1) and substituting the form of \(\pi^{*}\) in (2), we obtain an expression that separates the reward, the KL penalty, and a normalising term:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\theta}) 
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[ r(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[
r(x,y) - \beta\log\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)
+\beta\log\!\bigl(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Adding and subtracting the *soft‑value* \(\beta\log Z(x)\) (which does not depend on \(y\) once \(x\) is fixed) yields the actor‑critic formulation:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\theta})
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[
\underbrace{r(x,y) - \beta\log Z(x)}_{\displaystyle f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}}_{\displaystyle \text{KL term}}
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

Equation (6) is precisely Eq. 10 in the original paper.  The first bracketed quantity,
\[
f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) = r(x,y) - \beta\log Z(x),
\tag{7}
\]
acts as a *soft advantage*: it measures the immediate reward of an action minus the expected (soft) value of the reference policy.  The second term enforces the KL constraint.

In practice, the actor‑critic algorithm estimates the gradient of (6) with respect to \(\theta\) using the *policy gradient* theorem:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}
= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[ \bigl(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\bigr)
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \Bigr].
\tag{8}
\]
Because \(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)\) depends on the unknown normalising constant \(Z(x)\), PPO replaces it with a learned *value network* \(V_{\psi}(x)\) that approximates \(\beta\log Z(x)\).  This is the standard actor‑critic decomposition: the *actor* (\(\pi_{\theta}\)) proposes actions, while the *critic* (\(V_{\psi}\)) provides a baseline that reduces variance.

---

## Role of the Soft‑Value (Normalization) Term  

The term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) in (7) is crucial for two reasons:

1. **Correctness of the Objective.**  
   The original KL‑constrained optimisation (1) is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence (4).  Dropping \(\beta\log Z(x)\) changes the optimisation target: the resulting objective would no longer correspond to the KL to the true optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).  The soft‑value term ensures that the reward is *centered* relative to the reference distribution, preventing systematic bias toward ever‑increasing raw reward values.

2. **Variance Reduction.**  
   The gradient estimator (8) contains the factor \(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)\) as a *baseline*.  If we omitted \(\beta\log Z(x)\), the baseline would be simply \(r(x,y)\).  Since rewards in language generation can vary dramatically across sampled completions (e.g., a sentiment classifier may output values near \(\pm 5\)), the resulting policy gradient would have very high variance, making learning unstable.  By subtracting the expected soft‑value, the term acts analogously to the classic *advantage* function \(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\), yielding lower‑variance updates.

Because \(Z(x)\) involves a sum over all possible completions, it is infeasible to compute exactly.  PPO therefore learns an approximation \(V_{\psi}(x)\) by regression on sampled trajectories.  This introduces an additional source of error: if the critic does not accurately represent \(\beta\log Z(x)\), the advantage estimates become biased, which can lead to policy *drift* or *mode collapse*.

---

## High‑Variance Gradients and Baseline Dependence  

Even with a learned critic, the actor‑critic gradient can be noisy for several intertwined reasons:

* **Sparse, Binary Preference Signal.**  
  In RLHF the reward is derived from a binary preference model (Bradley‑Terry).  The logistic transformation \(\sigma(r_{w}-r_{l})\) compresses the reward difference into a narrow interval \([0,1]\), often producing very small gradients for pairs that are already well‑ordered.  When combined with a poorly estimated soft‑value, the signal-to-noise ratio deteriorates.

* **Monte‑Carlo Estimation of the Baseline.**  
  The critic is trained on the same trajectories used for the policy update.  Early in training, these trajectories are drawn from a near‑random policy, yielding high‑variance targets for \(V_{\psi}\).  The resulting baseline can oscillate, causing the actor’s gradient to swing violently from step to step.

* **KL Penalty Amplification.**  
  The KL term \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) can become large when the policy begins to diverge from the reference.  Since it is multiplied by the same \(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\) factor, any mis‑estimation of the soft‑value directly translates into amplified KL gradients, potentially pushing the policy into regions where the reward model is unreliable.

* **Sample Inefficiency.**  
  PPO typically requires many on‑policy rollouts per optimisation step because each update changes the policy, invalidating previously collected samples.  For large LLMs, generating a single batch of completions is already expensive; repeated sampling dramatically increases computational cost.

These factors together explain why PPO‑based RLHF can be fragile: the algorithm must simultaneously learn an accurate critic, keep KL divergence under control, and cope with noisy, binary preference information—all while sampling huge text sequences.

---

## DPO’s Reward Re‑Parameterisation Eliminates the Baseline  

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) circumvents the above difficulties by *changing variables* before optimisation.  Starting from the optimal policy expression (2), one can solve for the reward as a function of policy and reference:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\tag{9}
\]

Crucially, the Bradley‑Terry preference probability depends only on *differences* of rewards:
\[
p(y_{1}\!\succ\! y_{2}\mid x)
= \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1}) - r(x,y_{2})\bigr).
\tag{10}
\]
Substituting (9) into (10) causes the unknown normalising term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) to cancel, leaving a preference model expressed *solely* in terms of the *policy log‑ratios*:
\[
p(y_{1}\!\succ\! y_{2}\mid x)
= \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)} 
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
\Bigr).
\tag{11}
\]

Equation (11) is the foundation of DPO.  Because the preference likelihood is now a *binary cross‑entropy* loss directly over the policy parameters,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr),
\tag{12}
\]
the optimisation problem no longer contains any explicit soft‑value term.  The *baseline* is implicitly encoded in the log‑ratio with respect to the reference model; the reference policy supplies a stable, pre‑trained distribution that does not need to be learned.

The gradient of (12) simplifies to

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
= -\beta\,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)\,
\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)
      -\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr],
\tag{13}
\]
where \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) is the *implicit* reward.  The weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))\) automatically down‑weights pairs that are already correctly ordered, focusing learning effort on the most informative mistakes.  No external critic is required, and there is no need to approximate the partition function \(Z(x)\).

Because DPO sidesteps the soft‑value estimation altogether, it enjoys several practical benefits:

* **Zero Baseline Variance.**  The gradient variance is driven only by the stochasticity of the sampled preference pairs, not by an additional learned value function.  This makes training far more stable, especially early on when the policy is still close to the reference.

* **No On‑Policy Sampling Loop.**  The loss (12) can be evaluated on a *fixed* offline dataset of preferences; the policy never needs to generate fresh completions during optimisation.  Consequently, DPO eliminates the costly sampling‑in‑the‑loop step intrinsic to PPO.

* **Simpler Hyper‑Parameter Space.**  The KL constraint is already encoded in the log‑ratio term; there is no separate KL penalty coefficient to tune beyond the single temperature‑like constant \(\beta\).  Empirically, the authors find that a modest sweep over \(\beta\) suffices, whereas PPO requires careful tuning of both KL targets and reward normalisation.

* **Exact Optimality Within the Preference Model.**  By construction, the solution of (12) is the *optimal* policy under the KL‑constrained objective for the given Bradley‑Terry preference model.  No approximation is introduced by a critic, and the policy directly maximises the likelihood of observed human choices.

In summary, the instability of standard actor‑critic methods stems from the need to approximate the soft‑value term, which introduces high‑variance gradients and a dependence on a learned baseline.  DPO’s clever re‑parameterisation of the reward eliminates the soft‑value altogether, yielding a clean cross‑entropy objective that is both theoretically sound and empirically stable.

**Implementation Details**

In this chapter we describe, in a step‑by‑step fashion, the practical ingredients required to reproduce the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) experiments presented in the paper *Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model*.  The goal is to provide a reproducible pipeline that a graduate student can follow to train a language model directly from human preference triples without invoking reinforcement‑learning algorithms.  All the design choices below are drawn from the original work; no external information has been added.

---

## Data Formats for Preference Triples

The core training signal for DPO consists of a collection  

\[
\mathcal{D}= \bigl\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{\!w},y^{(i)}_{\!l})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where each entry contains a prompt \(x\) and two completions: a **preferred** response \(y_{w}\) and a **dispreferred** response \(y_{l}\).  In the original experiments the triples are stored as plain‑text JSON lines, one object per line:

```json
{
  "prompt": "Explain why the sky is blue.",
  "chosen": "The sky appears blue because ...",
  "rejected": "I think the sky is green ..."
}
```

The ordering of the fields is irrelevant as long as the keys are consistently named.  When loading the dataset the implementation must:

1. Tokenize the three strings using the same tokenizer that will be employed for the language model (see the next section).  
2. Pad or truncate each token sequence to a maximum length \(L\) (the same value used for the underlying LM).  
3. Convert the token IDs into tensors of shape \((B, L)\) where \(B\) is the batch size.

If a dataset already provides a *single* preferred completion per prompt (as in many instruction‑tuning corpora), a synthetic dispreferred response can be generated by sampling from the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) or by applying a perturbation such as *unlikelihood* dropout; this mirrors the data‑generation step described in the paper.

---

## Tokenizer and Model Architecture Choices

All experiments use a decoder‑only transformer architecture identical to the one employed for the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model.  Concretely:

- **Tokenizer**: A Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer with a vocabulary size of either 50 k (GPT‑2 style) or 32 k (LLaMA style), depending on the base model.  The tokenizer must be deterministic; the same tokenizer instance is used to encode both prompts and completions, ensuring that the log‑probability ratios \(\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) are computed over identical token sequences.

- **Model**: A causal language model with stacked self‑attention layers, layer‑norm, and a final linear projection to the vocabulary dimension.  The paper reports experiments with models ranging from 0.8 B to 6 B parameters.  The architecture is otherwise unchanged when moving from the SFT checkpoint to the DPO fine‑tuning stage; the SFT checkpoint is simply loaded as the initial weights for \(\pi_{\theta}\).

Because DPO does not modify the model’s output distribution beyond adjusting the logits through fine‑tuning, any architecture that supports standard cross‑entropy training can be employed, provided the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is obtainable (e.g., by keeping a frozen copy of the SFT checkpoint).

---

## Computing the Log‑Ratio Term Efficiently

The DPO loss (Equation (9) in the paper) requires the quantity

\[
\Delta_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \,\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]

Both numerator and denominator are log‑probabilities of the *same* token sequence under two models that share the same architecture but have different parameters.  Computing \(\Delta_{\theta}\) efficiently follows these steps:

1. **Forward Pass for Both Models**  
   - Feed the tokenized pair \((x, y)\) through the *reference* model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Collect the per‑token log‑softmax scores \(\log p_{\text{ref}}(t_{k}\mid x, t_{<k})\).  
   - Perform the same forward pass with the *trainable* model \(\pi_{\theta}\) to obtain \(\log p_{\theta}(t_{k}\mid x, t_{<k})\).

2. **Sequence Log‑Probability**  
   Sum the per‑token log‑probabilities for each sequence:
   \[
   \log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\sum_{k=1}^{|y|}\log p_{\theta}(t_{k}\mid x, t_{<k}),
   \qquad
   \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=\sum_{k=1}^{|y|}\log p_{\text{ref}}(t_{k}\mid x, t_{<k}).
   \]

3. **Log‑Ratio**  
   Subtract the two sums and multiply by \(\beta\):
   \[
   \Delta_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta\Bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\Bigr).
   \]

Because the two forward passes are independent, they can be vectorized across the batch dimension.  In practice the implementation stores the hidden states of the reference model once (it is frozen) and re‑uses them for each batch, which reduces the memory footprint compared to recomputing the reference logits for every gradient step.

---

## Batching, Gradient Clipping, and Optimizer Settings

### Batching
The loss operates on *preference pairs* \((y_{w}, y_{l})\) for each prompt.  A typical batch contains \(B\) such triples, giving tensors of shape \((B, L)\) for the prompts, the preferred completions, and the dispreferred completions.  The DPO loss for the batch is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\sigma\!\bigl(\Delta_{\theta}(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w}) - \Delta_{\theta}(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{l})\bigr),
\]

where \(\sigma\) denotes the logistic sigmoid.

### Gradient Clipping
To avoid exploding gradients that can arise from large log‑ratio magnitudes, the paper applies global norm clipping with a threshold of 1.0.  The procedure is:

```python
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
```

Clipping is performed after the backward pass and before the optimizer step.

### Optimizer
The experiments use the AdamW optimizer with standard hyper‑parameters:
- Learning rate \( \eta \) (see the hyper‑parameter section below).  
- Weight decay \( \lambda = 0.01 \).  
- Betas \( (\beta_{1},\beta_{2}) = (0.9, 0.999) \).  

A linear learning‑rate warm‑up over the first 10 % of training steps is employed, followed by a cosine decay to zero.  The total number of training steps varies with model size (e.g., 10 k steps for the 1.4 B model, 20 k steps for the 6 B model) but the schedule is always defined in terms of *updates*, not epochs, because the dataset is usually reused several times.

---

## Hyper‑Parameter Selection (β, Learning Rate, Temperature)

### β (KL‑constraint coefficient)
The scalar \(\beta\) governs the strength of the KL‐penalty implicit in the log‑ratio term.  The paper experiments with \(\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1.0, 5.0\}\).  Smaller values prioritize staying close to the reference policy, yielding more conservative updates; larger values allow the model to push the preference probability farther toward 1 at the cost of higher KL divergence.  In practice one can sweep a small grid and select the value that offers the best trade‑off on a validation set of preference triples.

### Learning Rate
A base learning rate of \(2\times10^{-5}\) works robustly for models up to 6 B parameters when fine‑tuning with AdamW.  For smaller models (≤ 1 B) the paper reports stable training with \(1\times10^{-5}\).  The learning‑rate schedule described above (warm‑up + cosine decay) is used for all settings.

### Generation Temperature at Evaluation
Although DPO training does not involve sampling, evaluation of the resulting policy requires a decoding temperature \(\tau\).  The experiments report performance across a range of temperatures (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0).  Lower temperatures tend to produce higher‑reward, lower‑KL outputs; higher temperatures increase diversity but can lower the win‑rate against a reference.  Selecting \(\tau\) is therefore a post‑training hyper‑parameter that can be tuned on a held‑out validation set.

---

## Handling Large Vocabularies and Memory Considerations

When scaling to models with vocabularies larger than 50 k (e.g., the 32 k LLaMA tokenizer or multilingual tokenizers), two practical issues arise:

1. **Memory for Dual Forward Passes**  
   Storing logits for both \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) may exceed GPU memory.  The paper mitigates this by:
   - Computing the reference log‑probabilities *once* per epoch and caching them on CPU (or NVMe) in fp16 format.  
   - Streaming the cached reference scores during each batch, thus only the trainable model’s activations occupy GPU memory.

2. **Avoiding Full‑Vocabulary Softmax**  
   The loss only requires the log‑probability of the *observed* tokens, not the full probability distribution.  Therefore the implementation can replace the standard `CrossEntropyLoss` with a manual gather operation:

   ```python
   # logits: (B, L, V); target_ids: (B, L)
   log_probs = torch.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
   token_log_probs = log_probs.gather(2, target_ids.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
   seq_log_prob = token_log_probs.sum(dim=-1)   # shape (B,)
   ```

   This reduces the temporary memory footprint because the full softmax is never materialized as a dense matrix.

3. **Gradient Checkpointing**  
   For the largest models (≥ 6 B parameters) the paper enables activation checkpointing, recomputing intermediate activations during the backward pass.  This trades compute for a roughly 50 % reduction in peak memory, allowing a batch size of 8 on a single 48 GB GPU.

---

### Summary of the Reproducible Pipeline

1. **Prepare** a JSON‑lines file of preference triples and tokenize all three fields with the model’s BPE tokenizer.  
2. **Load** the SFT checkpoint as both the trainable model \(\pi_{\theta}\) and a frozen copy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
3. **Construct** batches of size \(B\), compute per‑token log‑probabilities for preferred and dispreferred completions under both models, and evaluate the DPO loss (Equation 9).  
4. **Back‑propagate** using AdamW, applying global norm clipping (1.0) and the learning‑rate schedule (warm‑up + cosine decay).  
5. **Tune** \(\beta\) and the learning rate on a validation preference set; after training, select a decoding temperature \(\tau\) that maximizes the desired reward–KL trade‑off.  
6. **Evaluate** the final policy by measuring win‑rates against a reference (or using a proxy reward model) and by reporting the average KL divergence \(\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\).

By following these concrete steps—respecting the data format, tokenizer consistency, efficient log‑ratio computation, and the optimizer configuration described above—a researcher can faithfully reproduce the DPO experiments and extend them to new preference datasets or larger language models.

**Experimental Evaluation – Controlled Sentiment Generation**

# Task Definition and Synthetic Preference Creation  

In order to obtain a clean, fully observable environment in which the trade‑off between reward maximisation and KL‑regularisation can be studied, the authors construct a *synthetic* sentiment‑control benchmark.  The setting is deliberately simple so that every component of the learning pipeline is known analytically, yet it still mirrors the essential structure of a typical RLHF problem: a pre‑trained language model (the **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$) is asked to generate continuations $y$ conditioned on a prompt $x$, and a *human* (here simulated) supplies a binary preference between two continuations.  

Concretely, each data point consists of  

* a prompt $x$ taken from the IMDB movie‑review corpus, truncated to a short prefix (e.g. “The film was …”),  
* two candidate completions $y_{1},y_{2}$ sampled independently from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)$, and  
* a preference label $y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x$ indicating which of the two completions exhibits the *higher* positive‑sentiment score.  

Because the true sentiment of a text can be measured automatically with a high‑accuracy classifier, the authors replace human annotators by a *synthetic* preference oracle.  The oracle evaluates both candidates with a pretrained sentiment classifier $s(x,y)\in[0,1]$ that returns the probability that the continuation $y$ expresses a positive sentiment given the prefix $x$.  The candidate with the larger $s$ value is declared the winner:
\[
y_{w}= \arg\max_{y\in\{y_{1},y_{2}\}} s(x,y),\qquad
y_{l}= \arg\min_{y\in\{y_{1},y_{2}\}} s(x,y).
\]
Thus the entire preference dataset $\mathcal{D}= \{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w},y^{(i)}_{l})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ is generated automatically, guaranteeing that the label distribution exactly follows the Bradley‑Terry model with reward function $r^{*}(x,y)=\log\frac{s(x,y)}{1-s(x,y)}$.  This synthetic construction eliminates any source of noise apart from the stochasticity of the language model sampling, allowing a clean measurement of how an algorithm trades off reward and KL‑divergence.

# Metrics: Sentiment Classifier Reward, Sequence‑Level KL  

Two quantities are measured for every policy $\pi_{\theta}$ trained on $\mathcal{D}$:

1. **Reward (sentiment) score** – The true objective that RLHF seeks to maximise is the expected value of the latent reward $r^{*}$ under the policy.  Since $r^{*}$ is not directly accessible, the authors use the *sentiment classifier* $s$ as a proxy and report the *expected* classifier probability:
   \[
   \mathsf{R}(\pi_{\theta}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[\,s(x,y)\,\big],
   \]
   where $\mathcal{X}$ denotes the test‑set of IMDB prefixes.  Higher $\mathsf{R}$ corresponds to a higher proportion of generated continuations that are judged positive by the classifier.

2. **Sequence‑level KL divergence** – The KL‑regularisation term in the RLHF objective (Eq. \eqref{eq:RL}) penalises the *total* divergence between the new policy and the reference policy across the whole generated sequence:
   \[
   \mathsf{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
   \;=\;
   \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\Big[
        \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathrm{KL}\big(
        \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t}) \;\big\|\;
        \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t})
        \big)
   \Big],
   \]
   where $x_{1:t}$ denotes the prefix up to token $t$ and $T$ is the generation length.  This metric quantifies **how far** the fine‑tuned model has moved away from the original, unsupervised language model.  A small KL indicates that the policy remains close to the data distribution on which the reward model (or in DPO, the implicit reward) was calibrated.

Both metrics are reported *averaged* over a held‑out test set of 5 000 prompts.  Plotting $\mathsf{R}$ against $\mathsf{KL}$ for a family of trained policies yields a *frontier*: points that are not dominated by any other point in both dimensions.  The frontier thus visualises the **efficiency of reward acquisition** for a given amount of policy drift.

# Frontier Plots and Interpretation  

The authors evaluate three families of algorithms on the synthetic sentiment task:

* **PPO** – the standard proximal‑policy‑optimization RLHF pipeline, using a reward model trained on $\mathcal{D}$ (or the ground‑truth classifier directly, denoted PPO‑GT).  
* **DPO** – Direct Preference Optimization with the objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}).  
* **Baselines** – a *Best‑of‑$N$* approach that samples $N$ continuations from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and selects the one with highest classifier score, and a *Preferred‑FT* model that simply maximises likelihood of the preferred answer $y_{w}$ without any KL constraint.

For each algorithm, a sweep over the regularisation strength is performed:

* PPO: target KL values $\{3,6,9,12\}$ (implemented via a KL‑penalty coefficient).  
* DPO: $\beta$ values $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, directly controlling the trade‑off in Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}.  

Each configuration is trained for a fixed number of gradient steps (≈ 100 k updates) and then evaluated.  The resulting points are plotted on a two‑dimensional graph whose *x‑axis* is $\mathsf{KL}$ and *y‑axis* is $\mathsf{R}$.  The **key observations** are:

1. **DPO dominates the PPO frontier.**  Every DPO point lies on or above the PPO curve, meaning that for any given KL budget DPO attains a strictly higher reward.  Even when PPO receives the *true* sentiment reward (PPO‑GT), DPO’s frontier remains superior.  
2. **The reward‑KL trade‑off is smoother for DPO.**  Because DPO’s loss is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy, changing $\beta$ smoothly adjusts the relative weighting of the two log‑probability terms.  In PPO, the KL penalty interacts with the entropy bonus and the policy‑gradient estimator, often leading to unstable or non‑monotonic behaviour as the penalty coefficient varies.  
3. **Best‑of‑$N$ sits at the extreme right of the plot.**  By sampling many candidates and selecting the best according to the classifier, the method achieves very high reward but at the cost of a massive KL (the policy is effectively replaced by a *sampling* procedure).  This demonstrates that the frontier is not a trivial consequence of increasing the number of samples; rather, DPO reaches high reward while retaining a compact, generative policy.

The frontier plot therefore provides a **visual proof** that Direct Preference Optimization can achieve the same constrained optimisation target as RLHF’s KL‑regularised RL, but without the variance‑inducing Monte‑Carlo gradients that plague PPO.

# Ablation on $\beta$ and the Weighting Term  

The DPO loss (Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}) contains two crucial components:

* the *temperature* $\beta$, which scales the log‑probability ratio $\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$, and  
* the *importance weighting* $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ that appears in the gradient expression (Eq. \eqref{eq:gradient}).

To assess their individual contributions, the authors perform two ablations:

1. **Varying $\beta$ without weighting.**  Setting the weighting factor to a constant (i.e. removing the $\sigma$ term) yields a *naïve* probability‑ratio objective:
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{naïve}} = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[
        \log \sigma\!\big(\beta\!\log\!\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w})}
               -\beta\!\log\!\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l})}\big)
        \big].
   \]
   Experiments show that for small $\beta$ the model collapses: it learns to assign *extremely* high probability to the preferred completions **and** to *suppress* the dispreferred ones, but because the loss does not penalise mis‑ranking, the policy quickly degenerates to a deterministic mapping that reproduces the exact training pairs.  Consequently the KL explodes and the reward plateaus at a sub‑optimal level.

2. **Full DPO with weighting.**  Restoring the $\sigma$ term yields a gradient that is *large* precisely when the implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}$ mis‑orders the pair (i.e. when the dispreferred answer receives a higher $\hat r$).  This adaptive weighting prevents the model from “over‑correcting” on examples that are already correctly ordered, thereby stabilising learning and keeping the policy close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Empirically, the weighted version reaches the Pareto‑optimal frontier and remains robust across a wide range of $\beta$ values.

The ablation demonstrates that **both** the temperature $\beta$ (which governs the strength of KL‑regularisation) and the *dynamic* weighting are essential for DPO’s superior behaviour.  A too‑large $\beta$ reduces the KL penalty, causing DPO to act similarly to the Best‑of‑$N$ baseline (high reward, high KL).  A too‑small $\beta$ forces the policy to remain overly close to the reference, limiting the achievable reward.  The sweet spot—a moderate $\beta$ (e.g. $0.1$–$1$ in the experiments)—yields the most favourable trade‑off.

# Discussion of Why DPO Dominates PPO Even with Ground‑Truth Rewards  

The empirical superiority of DPO over PPO, **including** the oracle variant PPO‑GT that uses the exact sentiment classifier as reward, can be understood from several theoretical and practical angles:

1. **Exact Closed‑Form Policy under KL Constraint.**  
   The KL‑constrained optimisation problem (Eq. \eqref{eq:RL}) admits the analytic solution $\pi_{r}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:op_policy}.  By re‑parameterising the reward as $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:main_eq}), DPO sidesteps the need to *estimate* $r$ and *sample* from the current policy during training.  PPO, by contrast, must approximate the gradient of the expectation over $\pi_{\theta}$ with Monte‑Carlo samples; this introduces variance that grows with sequence length and harms sample efficiency.

2. **No Need for a Baseline or Value Function.**  
   PPO typically augments the reward with a learned baseline (a value network) to reduce variance.  If the baseline is mis‑estimated, the policy gradient can become biased, leading to under‑ or over‑optimisation of the reward.  DPO’s loss is a pure binary cross‑entropy; the weighting term $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ plays the role of an *implicit* variance reducer, focusing updates on mis‑ranked pairs and automatically normalising across the dataset.  

3. **Implicit Enforcement of the Normalising Partition Function.**  
   The KL‑constrained optimum includes the partition function $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)$.  In PPO this term appears only as a *soft‑value* that must be estimated (often via a separate critic).  Errors in this estimate translate directly into an inaccurate KL penalty.  DPO’s change‑of‑variables eliminates $Z(x)$ from the likelihood expression (it cancels in Eq. \eqref{eq:objective}), removing a source of bias altogether.

4. **Stability of a Supervised‑Learning Objective.**  
   Because DPO reduces to standard supervised classification, all the engineering tricks that stabilise language‑model fine‑tuning (gradient clipping, learning‑rate schedules, mixed‑precision training) apply unchanged.  No special RL‑specific hyperparameters (e.g., clipping epsilon, entropy coefficient) need to be tuned.  The experiments in the paper show that a *single* $\beta$ value works well across model sizes, whereas PPO typically requires meticulous tuning of the KL target, clipping parameters, and learning‑rate decay to avoid catastrophic policy collapse.

5. **Exactness of the Preference‑Based Objective.**  
   The preference likelihood written in terms of policy ratios (Eq. \eqref{eq:objective}) is *identical* to the Bradley‑Terry likelihood used to train the reward model in conventional RLHF.  Consequently the optimum of the DPO loss coincides with the optimum of the full RLHF pipeline (reward model + KL‑constrained RL).  Therefore, when the data truly follow the Bradley‑Terry assumptions—as they do in the synthetic sentiment benchmark—DPO reaches the *global* optimum, whereas PPO only approximates it through stochastic gradient ascent.

In sum, DPO’s advantage stems from **avoiding stochastic policy‑gradient estimation** altogether and **leveraging an exact analytical mapping** between reward and policy.  This yields a cleaner optimisation landscape, lower variance updates, and ultimately a tighter reward‑KL frontier, even when the PPO baseline is supplied with the *true* reward function.  

---  

Through this controlled sentiment experiment the paper demonstrates that Direct Preference Optimization can faithfully implement the same KL‑regularised reward maximisation problem that underlies RLHF, but with dramatically reduced computational complexity and superior empirical efficiency.  The synthetic benchmark therefore serves as a proof‑of‑concept that the theoretical re‑parameterisation translates into practical gains, motivating the subsequent experiments on real‑world preference datasets.

**Experimental Evaluation – Summarization and Dialogue**

---

## Reddit TL;DR and Anthropic HH Preference Datasets

A rigorous empirical assessment of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) requires two distinct, large‑scale preference collections that represent realistic downstream tasks.  

The **Reddit TL;DR** dataset comprises millions of forum posts drawn from the Reddit “TL;DR” community, each paired with a human‑written short summary.  In the version used for our experiments, every post is also accompanied by a pair of model‑generated continuations together with a binary label indicating which continuation a human annotator judged to be the better summary.  The binary preference signal is thus a concrete operationalization of “summary quality” as perceived by annotators, and it is the same signal that has been used in prior RL‑HF work on summarization.  

The **Anthropic Helpful‑and‑Harmless (HH)** dataset contains single‑turn dialogues between a user query and two responses produced by a large language model; annotators indicate which response is more helpful, less harmful, and overall more aligned with user intent.  Each entry therefore provides a tuple \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) where \(x\) is the user prompt, \(y_{w}\) the preferred assistant reply, and \(y_{l}\) the dispreferred reply.  This corpus is deliberately constructed to probe safety‑related behavior and to evaluate how well a fine‑tuned policy can follow high‑level alignment criteria.

Both collections are *offline*: the preference pairs have already been sampled from an underlying reference model and subsequently labeled, which matches the setting assumed by DPO (a fixed dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\)).  The size of each dataset (on the order of 100 k pairs) is sufficient to train models with up to six billion parameters without severe over‑fitting.

---

## Reference Model Construction: SFT vs. Preferred‑FT

Before applying DPO we must define a **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  In the classic RL‑HF pipeline the reference is the *supervised‑fine‑tuned* (SFT) model, i.e., a language model that has already been trained on a high‑quality corpus of instruction‑following or summarization examples.  The SFT model serves two purposes:  

1. It provides the base distribution from which the KL‑penalty in the RL‑HF objective is measured; and  
2. It supplies the sampling distribution used to generate the preference pairs in the original data collection.

### SFT for Reddit TL;DR  
For the summarization experiments we started from a GPT‑J (6 B) checkpoint that had been further fine‑tuned on the Reddit TL;DR human‑written summaries.  The fine‑tuning objective was ordinary maximum likelihood, i.e. maximizing \(\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) on the (prompt, summary) pairs.  After convergence the resulting model—denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)—became the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for all subsequent DPO and PPO runs.

### Preferred‑FT for Anthropic HH  
In the dialogue domain a public SFT checkpoint is not available.  Instead we performed **Preferred‑FT**: we fine‑tuned the off‑the‑shelf Pythia‑2.8B model on the *preferred* completions \(y_{w}\) from the Anthropic HH dataset, again using a standard cross‑entropy loss.  This yields a model that already produces responses resembling the human‑preferred style, and we adopt it as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The distinction matters because the KL term in Eq. (4) penalizes deviation from whatever distribution the reference model exhibits; using a reference that already reflects the preference direction reduces the magnitude of the required policy shift.

---

## Evaluation Protocols

### Win‑Rate Metric  
Because the true reward function underlying human judgments is unavailable in real‑world tasks, we adopt the *win‑rate* as the primary quantitative metric.  For a given test prompt \(x\) we generate a response \(y_{\text{A}}\) from method **A** and \(y_{\text{B}}\) from a baseline method **B** (often the original SFT or the preferred completion itself).  A powerful LLM (GPT‑4) is then asked to pick the better response.  The win‑rate of **A** is the proportion of prompts for which GPT‑4 prefers \(y_{\text{A}}\) over \(y_{\text{B}}\).  When the baseline is the human‑chosen response (as in Anthropic HH) the win‑rate can be interpreted as “how often does the fine‑tuned model match or surpass the human‑preferred answer”.

### Temperature Sweeps  
Generative language models are stochastic; their output quality can vary dramatically with the sampling temperature \(\tau\).  To assess robustness we evaluate each method at a grid of temperatures \(\tau \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0\}\).  Temperature \(\tau=0\) corresponds to greedy decoding, while larger values increase diversity.  For each \(\tau\) we compute the win‑rate, yielding a temperature‑win curve that reveals whether a method remains useful when the user desires more varied outputs.

### GPT‑4 Prompts for Preference Judgments  
Two distinct prompts were crafted for the GPT‑4 evaluator:

1. **Simple (S) Prompt** – “Which summary better captures the important information in the post?”  
2. **Concise (C) Prompt** – “Which summary better captures the important information and is more concise?”

Empirically we observed that the Simple prompt sometimes rewarded overly long or repetitive summaries, whereas the Concise prompt produced judgments that aligned more closely with human raters (see the human study below).  All reported win‑rates in the main tables use the Concise prompt unless otherwise noted.

### Human Study for Correlation Analysis  
To validate GPT‑4 as a proxy for human preference we recruited a small pool of annotators (N = 12) who judged a random subset of 200 TL;DR examples.  Each example was presented side‑by‑side with the responses from three systems: DPO (temperature 0.25), PPO (temperature 1.0), and the SFT baseline (temperature 0.25).  Annotators marked which response they preferred, and we computed pairwise *inter‑annotator agreement* (Cohen’s \(\kappa\) ≈ 0.63) as a baseline.  The agreement between GPT‑4 (C) and the majority human vote was 0.66, essentially on par with human‑human agreement, supporting the use of GPT‑4 win‑rates for large‑scale evaluation.

|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **DPO** | 0.00 | 61.2 % |
|        | 0.25 | **61.8 %** |
|        | 0.50 | 60.9 % |

\*The baseline SFT win‑rate against itself is 50 % by definition.  

Several observations follow:

* DPO consistently outperforms PPO across all temperatures, and its win‑rate curve is notably flatter, indicating *robustness* to temperature changes.

Crucially, Best‑of‑N is computationally infeasible at inference time (requiring \(N\) forward passes per query), whereas DPO incurs the cost of a single forward pass.  
* Preferred‑FT, which merely maximizes likelihood of the chosen response, fails to improve over the original SFT, underscoring the importance of *relative* preference information rather than absolute supervision.

### Out‑of‑Distribution Generalization (CNN/DailyMail)

To probe whether a policy learned directly from preferences can transfer to a new domain, we evaluated the TL;DR‑trained DPO and PPO models on the **CNN/DailyMail** news summarization benchmark.  No further fine‑tuning took place; we simply applied the same temperature settings used for the in‑distribution test.

| Model | Temperature | Win‑Rate vs. SFT (CNN/DailyMail) |
|-------|-------------|-----------------------------------|

|        | 0.25 | **55.0 %** |
| **PPO** | 0.00 | 48.7 % |
|        | 0.25 | 49.3 % |

The DPO policy maintains a clear advantage over PPO when the input distribution shifts from forum posts to news articles, suggesting that the closed‑form policy adaptation performed by DPO does not over‑fit to the idiosyncrasies of the original prompt distribution.

## Human Evaluation Methodology and Correlation Analysis

The human study described earlier followed a *pairwise comparison* protocol:

1. **Stimulus Construction** – For each selected test prompt we generated three responses (DPO, PPO, SFT) at predetermined temperatures.  
2. **Blind Presentation** – Annotators viewed the prompt and the two candidate completions side‑by‑side in a random order, without knowledge of which algorithm produced each.  
3. **Decision Capture** – Annotators selected the “more helpful / better‑summarized” response; a “no preference” option was also available but was rarely chosen (< 2 %).  

After gathering 5 independent judgments per pair, we took the majority vote as the ground truth.  The resulting *human win‑rate* for DPO versus PPO was 57 % (confidence interval ±2 %).  When we compared these human-derived win‑rates to the GPT‑4 (C) win‑rates on the same pairs we obtained a Pearson correlation of 0.92, confirming that the automated evaluator faithfully mirrors human preferences for the range of model qualities encountered in our experiments.

## Summary of Findings

* **DPO matches or exceeds PPO** on both summarization and dialogue tasks, achieving higher win‑rates while requiring only a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss and no reinforcement‑learning loop.  
* **Temperature robustness** is markedly better for DPO; performance degrades gently as stochasticity increases, whereas PPO’s win‑rate collapses rapidly beyond τ ≈ 0.5.  
* **Computational efficiency**: DPO eliminates the need for on‑policy sampling, reward‑model training, and KL‑penalty estimation, leading to training runtimes comparable to ordinary supervised fine‑tuning.  
* **Out‑of‑distribution resilience**: DPO’s policy generalizes more effectively than PPO when confronted with a new summarization style (news articles).  
* **Human‑aligned evaluation**: GPT‑4, when prompted for concise judgments, correlates strongly with human pairwise preferences, validating its use as a scalable proxy for large‑scale evaluation.

These empirical results substantiate the central claim of the paper: **Direct Preference Optimization provides a simple, stable, and computationally lightweight alternative to RL‑HF, delivering comparable or superior alignment performance across diverse natural‑language generation tasks.**

**Practical Guidance for Deploying DPO**

In this chapter we translate the theoretical developments of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) into concrete recommendations for engineers and researchers who wish to apply the method to real‑world language‑model fine‑tuning pipelines.  We focus on the practical decisions that are not dictated by the derivations themselves—choices of reference policy, the KL‑conservativeness coefficient β, model‑size considerations, combination with synthetic preference sources, and effective diagnostics such as KL monitoring and early‑stopping.  Throughout we remain faithful to the algorithmic setting described in the original DPO work: a fixed offline dataset of human preference triples  
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that approximates the distribution from which the preference pairs were originally sampled.

---

## Choosing a Reference Policy When SFT Is Unavailable

The DPO loss (Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}) explicitly contains the ratio $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$.  In the canonical RLHF setting the reference policy is the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, because the preference dataset is generated by prompting that model.  When an SFT checkpoint is not provided, the paper proposes a simple two‑step surrogate:

1. **Construct a pseudo‑reference by supervised learning on the preferred side of the dataset.**  
   Form the set of preferred pairs $\{(x,y_w)\}$ and train a language model to maximize  
   \[
   \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)].
   \tag{1}
   \]
   The resulting model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ approximates the marginal distribution of high‑quality completions, thereby reducing the distribution shift between the true (unknown) reference and the one used in DPO.

2. **Why this matters.**  
   The DPO objective effectively enforces a KL‑penalty relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (see Eq. \ref{eq:gradient}).  If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is far from the true generation distribution, the implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}= \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ can become mis‑calibrated, leading to excessive probability mass being shifted toward preferred strings while inadvertently collapsing the policy’s diversity.  Training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on the same preferred data aligns its support with the region of interest and stabilizes the importance weight in the gradient.

In practice, the reference model is usually a single epoch of standard language‑model fine‑tuning on the preferred completions, using the same optimizer and learning‑rate schedule that will later be employed for DPO.  No additional hyper‑parameters are needed beyond those already required for supervised language‑model training.

---

## Setting β for Desired Conservativeness

The scalar $\beta>0$ appears in three places in DPO:

* It scales the log‑ratio in the implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}= \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$.
* It determines the steepness of the sigmoid in the loss (Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}).
* It controls the strength of the KL‑constraint in the original KL‑regularized RL objective (Eq. \ref{eq:RL}).

Intuitively, **small β** yields a *loose* KL budget: the loss heavily penalizes mis‑ranked pairs, pushing the policy to increase the probability of preferred completions even if that requires large deviations from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  **Large β** makes the loss more tolerant of violations of the preference ordering, thereby keeping $\pi_{\theta}$ close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

### Empirical Prescription

The DPO experiments in the paper explore $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$.  The observed trade‑off mirrors the classic reward‑vs‑KL frontier: lower β values achieve higher sentiment‑classifier reward but incur higher average KL divergence (Figure \ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}).  A practical workflow is:

1. **Define a target KL budget** (e.g., average per‑prompt KL ≤ 3) based on downstream tolerances for distribution shift or mode collapse.
2. **Select β** by a brief sweep: train short DPO runs (e.g., 1 k gradient steps) for each candidate β and compute the empirical KL between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on a held‑out validation set.
3. **Pick the smallest β** whose KL meets the budget while delivering the highest validation reward.

Because the DPO loss is a simple binary cross‑entropy, each β sweep requires negligible compute compared with PPO‑based RLHF, enabling rapid iteration.

---

## Scaling to Larger Models (Up to 6 B and Beyond)

The original DPO experiments demonstrate successful fine‑tuning of models up to 6 B parameters with no special algorithmic modifications.  Scaling follows the same principles that govern standard supervised language‑model fine‑tuning:

1. **Batch Size and Gradient Accumulation.**  
   Larger models demand more GPU memory.  The DPO loss is computed on a batch of preference triples; each triple contributes two forward passes (for $y_w$ and $y_l$) through the model.  When memory limits prevent a full batch, accumulate gradients over multiple micro‑batches while keeping the effective batch size constant.

2. **Learning‑Rate Schedule.**  
   The paper reports using the same AdamW schedule as for SFT (linear warm‑up followed by cosine decay).  Empirically, the optimal peak learning‑rate scales roughly as $ \eta \propto 1/\sqrt{N_{\text{params}}}$, a rule of thumb inherited from supervised fine‑tuning.  Because DPO does not involve policy‑gradient variance, aggressive warm‑up (e.g., 10 % of total steps) is safe.

3. **Stability Considerations.**  
   The importance weight $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))$ can saturate for very large models if the logits of $\pi_{\theta}$ become extremely confident.  To avoid vanishing gradients, one may clip the log‑ratio term before applying the sigmoid, e.g.  
   \[
   \tilde r = \operatorname{clip}\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l), -c, c\bigr),
   \]
   with $c$ in the range $[5,10]$.  This clipping preserves the sign of the preference while preventing numerical overflow.

4. **Mixed‑Precision Training.**  
   FP16 or BF16 training is fully compatible with DPO because the loss is a standard cross‑entropy that benefits from the same scaling tricks used in SFT.  Gradient‑scaling utilities should be retained to avoid underflow when $\beta$ is small.

Overall, the only additional cost relative to SFT is the second forward pass for the dispreferred response.  This overhead is linear in the batch size and therefore negligible compared with the inference cost of large language models.

---

## Integrating DPO with Self‑Training or Synthetic Preference Generation

The DPO framework assumes an *offline* preference dataset.  In many practical scenarios the available human‑annotated pairs are limited, prompting the use of synthetic preferences.  The paper discusses “self‑training” in the broader RLHF literature (e.g., using an LLM to annotate data with a rubric); the same pipeline can be grafted onto DPO:

1. **Generate Synthetic Pairs.**  
   Prompt a strong language model (the current DPO policy or an external foundation model) to produce two completions for a given prompt $x$.  Apply an automatic classifier (sentiment, toxicity, factuality) or a heuristic rubric to decide which completion is “wins”.  The resulting triple $(x, y_w, y_l)$ is added to $\mathcal{D}$.

2. **Iterative Refinement.**  
   After an initial DPO fine‑tuning pass, repeat the synthetic generation step using the updated policy as the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Because the DPO loss already contains the KL term relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the policy will not drift arbitrarily far even when the synthetic data are noisy; the KL penalty acts as a regularizer that grounds the model in the distribution of the reference.

3. **Mixing Human and Synthetic Data.**  
   Empirically, one can interleave human‑annotated triples and synthetic triples within each training batch.  A simple weighting scheme (e.g., 70 % human, 30 % synthetic) preserves the high‑fidelity signal while still allowing the model to benefit from the much larger synthetic sample size.

The key theoretical point is that DPO does **not** require an explicit reward model; therefore, any process that yields a binary preference label can be directly consumed by the same loss (Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}).  No additional reward‑model training or value‑function estimation is needed.

---

## Monitoring KL Drift and Early‑Stopping Criteria

Because DPO optimizes a KL‑constrained objective, continual measurement of the KL divergence between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ provides a reliable signal of training progress and over‑fitting:

\[
\text{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

In practice we estimate (2) on a held‑out validation set of prompts by **sampling** a modest number (e.g., 16) of continuations per prompt from $\pi_{\theta}$ and computing the empirical average log‑ratio.  The following protocol is recommended:

1. **Track Two Curves.**  
   *Reward* – compute the average preference‑model probability (or, if available, a ground‑truth proxy such as a sentiment classifier).  
   *KL* – compute the empirical KL as above.

2. **Early‑Stopping Rule.**  
   Stop training when the reward curve plateaus **and** the KL has not exceeded a pre‑specified bound (e.g., the target KL used to select β).  If KL begins to rise sharply while reward continues to improve, the model is likely over‑exploiting the preferences; consider either increasing β or terminating early.

3. **Checkpoint Selection.**  
   Choose the checkpoint with the highest reward **subject to** the KL constraint.  This mirrors the Pareto‑optimal frontier shown in Figure \ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} and guarantees that the final model respects the conservativeness budget inherent to RLHF.

4. **Diagnostic Plots.**  
   Plotting reward vs. KL for each checkpoint provides a visual “frontier” similar to the PPO experiments.  Because DPO’s loss is deterministic (no policy‑gradient variance), the frontier tends to be smooth, simplifying decisions about the trade‑off.

By integrating KL monitoring into the training loop, practitioners obtain the same safety guarantees that KL‑penalties provide in PPO‑based RLHF, but without the costly value‑function estimation or high‑variance gradient noise.

---

### Summary of Recommendations

| Decision | Recommended Practice (per DPO paper) |
|----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Reference policy** | If SFT exists, use $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. Otherwise train $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on the preferred halves of $\mathcal{D}$ (Equation 1). |
| **β selection** | Sweep a small set of β values (e.g., 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5); pick the smallest β achieving the target KL budget while maximizing validation reward. |
| **Scaling** | Use standard SFT tricks (gradient accumulation, mixed precision). Clip log‑ratios to avoid saturation; keep learning‑rate schedule similar to SFT. |
| **Synthetic preferences** | Generate pairs with a strong LLM or classifier, mix with human triples, and re‑run DPO; the KL term naturally regularizes against drift. |
| **KL monitoring** | Estimate KL on a validation set each epoch, stop when reward plateaus and KL stays under the budget; select the checkpoint with the best reward‑under‑KL constraint. |

By adhering to these guidelines, engineers can reap the simplicity and stability advantages demonstrated by DPO—eliminating the need for separate reward‑model training and reinforcement‑learning loops—while still achieving performance comparable to or better than PPO‑based RLHF across sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue tasks.

# Limitations, Open Questions, and Future Directions  

In this chapter we examine the current boundaries of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as introduced in the preceding sections and discuss promising avenues for further research.  We organize the discussion around five central themes that emerged from the empirical study of DPO: (1) how well DPO‑trained policies generalize when the input distribution shifts, (2) whether and how reward over‑optimization can manifest in a framework that does not explicitly optimize a learned reward, (3) the challenges of scaling DPO to more complex interaction settings such as multi‑turn dialogue and multimodal generation, (4) the possibility of augmenting DPO with explicit regularization terms derived from a reward model, and (5) the role of automated evaluation prompts and how their design influences research progress.  Throughout we remain faithful to the definitions, assumptions, and empirical findings presented in the original work.

---

## Generalization under Distribution Shift  

A central claim of the DPO framework is that the optimal policy under the KL‑constrained reward maximization objective can be obtained directly from a binary‑cross‑entropy loss defined on human preference data.  Empirically, the authors demonstrated that a DPO‑trained policy for Reddit TL;DR summarization outperformed a PPO‑based policy when evaluated on the out‑of‑domain CNN/DailyMail news articles (see Table 1 in the paper).  This result suggests that the DPO objective does not over‑fit to the distribution of prompts from which the preference pairs were sampled, at least for the relatively modest shift between forum posts and news articles.

Nevertheless, several open questions remain.  First, the current analysis treats distribution shift as a static change in the prompt distribution; it does not address more severe shifts such as completely new domains (e.g., legal contracts) or prompts that systematically elicit rare generation modes.  The DPO loss weights each preference example by the term  

\[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr),
\qquad 
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
\]

which depends on the current policy’s implicit reward estimate.  When the model encounters inputs far from the training distribution, the reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) may assign very low probability to both the preferred and dispreferred completions, causing the ratio inside the log to become unstable.  Consequently, the weighting factor may become noisy, which could impede learning or lead to unintended bias toward a subset of the preference data.

Future work could explore **domain‑adaptive reference policies**.  One possibility is to maintain a mixture of reference policies, each trained on a different sub‑corpus, and to select the mixture component that best matches a new prompt using a lightweight classifier.  Another direction is to augment the DPO loss with an **importance‑weighted term** based on an estimate of the density ratio \(p_{\text{train}}(x)/p_{\text{test}}(x)\), thereby down‑weighting examples that are unlikely under the test distribution.  Both approaches would preserve the closed‑form nature of the DPO objective while potentially improving robustness to severe distribution shift.

---

## Potential Reward Over‑Optimization in the DPO Regime  

In conventional RLHF pipelines, researchers observe that aggressive maximization of the learned reward can induce **reward hacking**: the policy finds loopholes that increase the numerical reward while degrading the true utility of the output (e.g., producing repetitive or nonsensical text that the reward model erroneously rates highly).  DPO removes the explicit reward‑learning step, yet it still optimizes an **implicit reward** defined as  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.
\]

Because \(\hat r_\theta\) is a deterministic function of the policy, any tendency of \(\pi_\theta\) to concentrate probability mass on a narrow set of outputs will automatically inflate \(\hat r_\theta\).  The DPO gradient (Equation 9 in the paper) explicitly up‑weights examples where the implicit reward incorrectly ranks the dispreferred completion above the preferred one.  If the model begins to assign overwhelming probability to a few high‑reward strings, the logistic weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) will approach zero for most examples, diminishing the learning signal and potentially freezing the policy in a degenerate mode.

The authors partially mitigate this risk by the **KL‑constraint** encoded via the reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): the term \(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) penalizes large deviations from the reference distribution.  However, the tightness of this regularization is controlled solely by the scalar \(\beta\).  When \(\beta\) is small, the policy may move far from the reference, increasing the possibility of over‑optimization; when \(\beta\) is large, the policy may be overly conservative and fail to capture the desired preferences.

A promising research direction is to **dynamically adapt \(\beta\)** during training.  For instance, one could monitor a held‑out set of preference pairs and increase \(\beta\) whenever the validation win‑rate plateaus, thereby reinforcing the KL penalty when further reward gains appear suspect.  Alternatively, a **dual‑objective formulation** could be introduced that adds an explicit penalty on the variance of the policy’s log‑probabilities across the vocabulary, encouraging a more diverse distribution and reducing the chance of mode collapse.

---

## Scaling to Multi‑Turn Dialogue and Multimodal Generation  

The experiments presented in the paper focus on single‑turn tasks (sentiment control, summarization, one‑shot dialogue).  Extending DPO to **multi‑turn dialogue** raises several technical challenges.  First, preference data for multi‑turn interactions typically involve entire conversation histories \((x, y^{(1)}_{\!w}, y^{(1)}_{\!l}, \dots, y^{(T)}_{\!w}, y^{(T)}_{\!l})\), where each turn may depend on the preceding model outputs.  The Bradley‑Terry model assumes preferences are defined over *single* completions conditioned on a fixed prompt; applying it directly to whole dialogues would require defining a joint reward \(r(x, y^{(1:T)})\).  This joint reward would be high‑dimensional and difficult to factor.

One approach is to **factorize the preference likelihood** across turns, assuming conditional independence given the history:

\[
p^*\bigl(y^{(1:T)}_w \succ y^{(1:T)}_l \mid x\bigr)
\approx
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
\sigma\!\bigl(r(x, y^{(t)}_w \mid h_{t}) - r(x, y^{(t)}_l \mid h_{t})\bigr),
\]

where \(h_{t}\) denotes the dialogue context up to turn \(t-1\).  The DPO loss can then be applied **turn‑wise**, using the same reparameterization \(\hat r_\theta = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) at each step.  This yields a tractable objective that respects the sequential nature of dialogue while preserving the closed‑form policy update.

For **multimodal generation** (e.g., text‑to‑image or vision‑language models), the policy \(\pi_\theta\) outputs structured objects beyond token sequences.  The KL‑constraint still makes sense if a reference multimodal model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) can be defined, but computing the log‑ratio \(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) may be intractable because the output space is continuous or combinatorial.  Approximate techniques such as **importance sampling** or **variational bounds** on the KL divergence could be incorporated, preserving the spirit of DPO while adapting it to richer output spaces.  Moreover, preference data for multimodal content may involve human judgments of aesthetics or relevance that are more subjective; extending the Bradley‑Terry model to capture such nuanced criteria (e.g., via hierarchical or contextual extensions) would be a valuable contribution.

---

## Combining DPO with Explicit Reward Regularization  

Although DPO eliminates the need for a separately trained reward model, there is no fundamental prohibition against **augmenting the DPO loss with an auxiliary reward term**.  Consider an additional scalar reward \(\tilde r_\phi(x,y)\) learned by conventional supervised methods (e.g., a sentiment classifier, a factuality scorer).  One could define a hybrid objective  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hybrid}}(\pi_\theta) =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}})
\;-\;
\lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{aux}}}
\bigl[\tilde r_\phi(x,y)\bigr],
\]

where \(\lambda\) balances the preference‑based term and the auxiliary reward.  The auxiliary term could be evaluated on a **larger unlabeled corpus**, providing additional signal for attributes that are costly to collect as pairwise preferences (e.g., factual correctness).  Because the DPO term already enforces alignment with human‑provided rankings, the auxiliary term would act as a *regularizer* that nudges the policy toward desirable properties without conflicting with the primary preference data.

A potential pitfall is **reward conflict**: if the auxiliary reward labels disagree with the human preferences in the training set, the joint optimization could oscillate.  Careful calibration—perhaps via a *curriculum* where \(\lambda\) is annealed from zero to a final value—might mitigate this issue.  Theoretical analysis of the combined objective could also reveal conditions under which the optimal policy for the hybrid loss still admits a closed‑form expression, preserving the computational simplicity that motivated DPO.

---

## Improving Automated Evaluation Prompts  

The paper’s evaluation relies heavily on GPT‑4 as a proxy for human judgment, noting that the **prompt wording** (simple vs. concise) materially affects win‑rate estimates.  This observation exposes a broader methodological limitation: when automated LLMs serve as evaluators, the evaluation protocol itself becomes a hyperparameter that can bias results.  Two research directions can address this limitation.

First, we can develop **prompt‑robust evaluation suites** that systematically vary phrasing, length, and style, then aggregate scores across the variations.  This ensemble approach reduces sensitivity to any single prompt and yields a more reliable estimate of model quality.  Second, we can train **meta‑evaluators**: smaller models fine‑tuned on a dataset of human‑annotated pairwise comparisons, learning to predict the human win‑rate given two candidate generations and a prompt description.  Such meta‑evaluators would provide a lightweight, reproducible alternative to prompting a massive LLM at inference time, while still benefiting from the large‑scale language understanding embodied in the teacher model.

Finally, incorporating **uncertainty estimates** (e.g., via Monte‑Carlo dropout or ensembles) into the evaluator can flag cases where the automated judgment is unreliable, prompting human verification.  This synergistic loop would make large‑scale benchmarking more trustworthy and accelerate progress on DPO and related methods.

---

## Concluding Remarks  

Direct Preference Optimization offers a compelling alternative to reinforcement‑learning‑based alignment by exploiting a closed‑form relationship between reward functions and policies.  The empirical successes reported in the original work demonstrate that DPO can match or exceed PPO‑based RLHF on several benchmark tasks while dramatically simplifying the training pipeline.  Nevertheless, critical open questions remain concerning generalization under severe distribution shift, the latent risk of reward over‑optimization, scalability to multi‑turn and multimodal settings, the benefits of hybrid regularization, and the robustness of automated evaluation.  Addressing these challenges will be essential for transforming DPO from an elegant proof‑of‑concept into a foundational tool for the next generation of aligned, capable language and generative models.

**Appendices and Mathematical Derivations**  

In this appendix we present the full set of analytical results that underlie the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm introduced in the main text.  All derivations are self‑contained and follow directly from the definitions and assumptions stated in the paper.  We begin by deriving the optimal policy for the KL‑constrained reward‑maximization problem (Eq. 4 in the manuscript).  Next we show how the Bradley‑Terry preference model leads, after a change of variables, to the DPO objective (Eq. 7).  We then prove Lemma 1, Lemma 2 and Theorem 1, which establish the equivalence classes of reward functions and the completeness of the re‑parameterisation used by DPO.  Finally we sketch how the same reasoning extends to the more general Plackett‑Luce ranking model and we list the additional experimental configurations (ablation tables and hyper‑parameter grids) that support the empirical findings.

## 1.  Derivation of the Optimal Policy (Equation 4)

We consider the KL‑constrained objective that appears throughout the RLHF literature and in the main text:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\; 
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[r(x,y)\big]}_{\text{expected reward}}
\;-\;
\beta\;
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)}_{\text{KL regulariser}} .
\tag{1}
\]

For a fixed prompt \(x\) the problem reduces to the following functional optimisation over a distribution \(\pi(\cdot)\) on the discrete set of completions \(\mathcal{Y}(x)\):

\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot)}\; \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)} \pi(y)\, r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)} \pi(y)\,\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} .
\tag{2}
\]

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) to enforce the normalisation constraint \(\sum_{y}\pi(y)=1\).  The Lagrangian is

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=\sum_{y}\pi(y) r(x,y) 
-\beta\sum_{y}\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}
+\lambda\Bigl(1-\sum_{y}\pi(y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

Taking the derivative with respect to \(\pi(y)\) and setting it to zero yields

\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi(y)}
= r(x,y) 
-\beta\Bigl(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}+1\Bigr)
-\lambda =0 .
\tag{4}
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = \frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y) - \frac{\lambda}{\beta} - 1 .
\tag{5}
\]

Exponentiating both sides:

\[
\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = 
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)\,
\underbrace{\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{\lambda}{\beta}-1\bigr)}_{\text{constant w.r.t. }y}.
\tag{6}
\]

Since the left‑hand side must normalise to one, the constant factor is precisely the reciprocal of the partition function

\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y')\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr).
\tag{7}
\]

Thus the optimal policy takes the closed‑form expression

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle 
\pi^{\!*}(y\mid x)
= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr) } .
\tag{8}
\]

Equation (8) is precisely Eq. 4 in the paper.  It shows that the KL‑constrained optimum is obtained by re‑weighting the reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) with an exponential factor of the (scaled) reward.

## 2.  From Bradley‑Terry Preferences to the DPO Loss (Equation 7)

### 2.1 Bradley‑Terry model

The Bradley‑Terry (BT) model assumes that a latent scalar utility \(r^{*}(x,y)\) determines the probability that a human prefers completion \(y_{w}\) over \(y_{l}\) for a given prompt \(x\):

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)}.
\tag{9}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
= \sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
\qquad\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
\tag{10}
\]

### 2.2 Substituting the optimal‑policy representation of the reward

From the derivation of Section 1 we know that for any reward function \(r\) there exists an optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) satisfying (8).  Solving (8) for the reward gives

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\tag{11}
\]

Because the BT probability depends only on *differences* of rewards, the additive term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels:

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})
&=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

Inserting (12) into the BT expression (10) yields a preference probability *entirely* in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) and the reference policy:

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\bigl[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr).
\tag{13}
\]

### 2.3 Maximum‑likelihood objective over a parametric policy

Let \(\pi_{\theta}\) denote a parametric policy (e.g. a transformer language model).  We now treat the BT probability (13) as a likelihood model for the observed human preference triples \(\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}\).  The negative log‑likelihood over a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\,
\bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr]
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{14}
\]

Equation (14) is exactly Eq. 7 in the paper and constitutes the DPO training objective.  It is a binary cross‑entropy loss that directly matches the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) to the human preference distribution, without ever constructing an explicit reward model.

## 3.  Proofs of Lemma 1, Lemma 2, and Theorem 1

Recall the equivalence relation introduced in the text:

> **Definition.** Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are *equivalent* if there exists a function \(f(x)\) such that  
> \(r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\) for all completions \(y\).

The definition captures the fact that adding a prompt‑dependent constant does not affect any *pairwise* reward differences.

### 3.1 Lemma 1 (Identity of Preference Distributions)

*Statement.* Under the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) model, any two equivalent reward functions induce the same preference distribution.

*Proof.* Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be equivalent, i.e. \(r(x,y)=r'(x,y)+f(x)\).  For a pair \((y_{1},y_{2})\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
p_{r}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr) \\
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})+f(x)-r'(x,y_{2})-f(x)\bigr)\\
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})\bigr) \\
&= p_{r'}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x).
\end{aligned}
\]

The additive term cancels, establishing equality of the preference probabilities. ∎

### 3.2 Lemma 2 (Identity of KL‑Constrained Optimal Policies)

*Statement.* Two equivalent reward functions produce the same optimal policy for the KL‑constrained objective (1).

*Proof.* Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be equivalent with \(r(x,y)=r'(x,y)+f(x)\).  Plugging \(r\) into the optimal‑policy expression (8) yields

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r'(x,y)\bigr)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr).
\]

Because \(f(x)\) does not depend on \(y\), the factor \(\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x))\) can be absorbed into the normalising constant:
\[
Z_{r}(x)=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr)\,Z_{r'}(x).
\]
Therefore
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r'}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r'(x,y)\bigr)
=\pi_{r'}(y\mid x).
\]
Thus the optimal policies coincide. ∎

### 3.3 Theorem 1 (Representability of All Reward Equivalence Classes)

*Statement.* For any reward function \(r\) that belongs to a Bradley‑Terry compatible equivalence class, there exists a policy \(\pi\) such that  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\qquad\text{(up to an additive }f(x)\text{)}.
\]

*Proof.* Define the mapping \(f\) from a reward to a *normalised* reward as

\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; r(x,y)-\beta\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr)\Bigr).
\tag{15}
\]

The second term is precisely \(\beta\log Z_{r}(x)\), where \(Z_{r}(x)\) is the partition function of the optimal policy induced by \(r\).  By construction \(\tilde r\) differs from \(r\) only by a function of \(x\), thus \(\tilde r\) lies in the same equivalence class as \(r\) (Lemma 2).

Now consider the optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) associated with the *original* reward \(r\) (Eq. 8).  Rearranging (8) gives exactly

\[
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}=\tilde r(x,y).
\tag{16}
\]

Hence setting \(\pi = \pi_{r}\) yields a representation of \(\tilde r\) (and therefore of the entire equivalence class) in the desired form.  No restriction on the functional family of \(\pi\) is imposed beyond being a valid probability distribution, so *any* reward class compatible with the BT model can be expressed through some policy \(\pi\). ∎

The theorem guarantees that the re‑parameterisation used by DPO does not shrink the hypothesis space: every admissible reward (up to an additive prompt‑wise constant) can be written as a log‑ratio of a policy and the fixed reference policy.

## 4.  Extensions to Plackett‑Luce Rankings

The Plackett‑Luce (PL) model generalises Bradley‑Terry to rankings of more than two items.  For a list \((y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\) the PL probability is

\[
p^{\text{PL}}(y_{1}\succ\dots\succ y_{k}\mid x)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{k}
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{i})\bigr)}
{\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\bigr)} .
\tag{17}
\]

Because each factor depends only on a *difference* of rewards (the numerator vs. the denominator), the same additive cancellation argument as in Lemma 1 applies: adding any function \(f(x)\) to all rewards leaves the PL probabilities unchanged.  Consequently Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 hold verbatim for PL.

To obtain a DPO‑style loss, we substitute the re‑parameterised reward (11) into (17).  Each denominator becomes

\[
\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\bigr)
=
\sum_{j=i}^{k}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{j}\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\tilde r(x,y_{j})\bigr)\,
Z(x)
=
Z(x)\,\sum_{j=i}^{k}\pi^{*}(y_{j}\mid x),
\]

where \(\pi^{*}\) denotes the optimal policy (\(\pi_{r^{*}}\)).  All occurrences of the unknown partition function \(Z(x)\) cancel across numerator and denominator, leaving a likelihood that depends only on the ratios \(\pi^{*}(y_{i}\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i}\mid x)\).  The resulting negative log‑likelihood is again a sum of binary cross‑entropy terms of the form (14), one for each adjacent pair in the observed ranking.  Hence the DPO loss extends naturally to full rankings without any additional computation.
