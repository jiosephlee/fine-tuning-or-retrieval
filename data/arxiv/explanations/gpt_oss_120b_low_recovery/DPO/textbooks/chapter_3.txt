\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: From Rewards to Policies: The Closed‑Form Optimal Policy
================================================================

In this chapter we expose the mathematical heart of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  The central observation is that the KL‑constrained reward‑maximisation problem that underlies conventional RL‑from‑Human‑Feedback (RLHF) admits a **closed‑form optimal policy**.  By writing this optimal policy explicitly we can replace the usual two‑stage pipeline—first fitting a reward model, then running reinforcement learning—with a single maximum‑likelihood problem directly over the policy.  We develop the derivation step‑by‑step, interpret the role of the normalising partition function \(Z(x)\), and show how the partition function disappears when we consider *preference ratios* rather than absolute probabilities.  This analysis yields the re‑parameterisation of the reward in terms of the policy (Equation 5 in the paper) that makes DPO possible.

---

## Derivation of the Optimal Policy Formula (Eq. 4)

The RLHF optimisation objective (Equation 3 of the paper) seeks a language‑model policy \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that maximises the expected reward while staying close, in Kullback–Leibler (KL) sense, to a fixed *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (often the supervised‑fine‑tuned model \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)):

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\bigl[\,r(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;
\beta \,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(r(x,y)\) is any scalar reward function (learned or handcrafted) and \(\beta>0\) controls the strength of the KL regulariser.  The objective is *concave* in \(\pi_{\theta}\) because the KL term is convex and the reward term is linear in the policy.  Therefore, for a fixed reward function the optimum can be obtained by solving a constrained optimisation problem for each context \(x\) independently.

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda(x)\) to enforce that \(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\) remains a proper distribution:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=
\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x) r(x,y)
-
\beta \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
-
\lambda(x)\Bigl(\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)-1\Bigr).
\]

Taking the derivative of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and setting it to zero yields

\[
r(x,y) - \beta\Bigl(1 + \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr) - \lambda(x)=0,
\]

which can be rearranged to

\[
\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = \frac{1}{\beta}\bigl(r(x,y)-\lambda(x)-\beta\bigr).
\]

Exponentiating both sides gives

\[
\pi(y\mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\beta}(\lambda(x)+\beta)\Bigr).
\]

Because \(\lambda(x)\) does not depend on \(y\), the factor
\(\exp\!\bigl(-\frac{1}{\beta}(\lambda(x)+\beta)\bigr)\) is a normalising constant that ensures the probabilities sum to one.  Defining the **partition function**

\[
Z(x) \;=\;\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

the normalising constant is exactly \(1/Z(x)\).  Substituting back we obtain the **optimal KL‑constrained policy**:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \;=\;
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
}
\tag{3}
\]

which is Equation 4 of the paper.  The derivation shows that the optimal policy is a *Boltzmann‑weighted* version of the reference policy, where the weight is the exponentiated reward scaled by \(1/\beta\).

---

## Interpretation of the Partition Function \(Z(x)\)

The partition function \(Z(x)\) plays two distinct roles:

1. **Normalisation:**  Because we are re‑weighting the reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by a positive factor \(\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)\), the resulting vector no longer sums to one.  Dividing by \(Z(x)\) restores a proper probability distribution.  In statistical‑physics language, \(Z(x)\) is the *partition function* of a Gibbs distribution with energy \(-r(x,y)\) and temperature \(\beta\).

2. **Implicit Value Function:**  The logarithm of \(Z(x)\) can be interpreted as the *soft* value of the optimal policy under the reference policy:
   \[
   V_{\text{soft}}(x) = \beta \log Z(x)
   = \beta \log\!\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
   \]
   This quantity appears in the derivation of actor‑critic methods: it is the baseline that, if subtracted from the raw reward, reduces the variance of policy‑gradient estimates.  In conventional RLHF, one must either estimate \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) with a separate value network or approximate it with a Monte‑Carlo baseline, both of which add computational overhead.

Importantly, \(Z(x)\) depends **only on the context** \(x\) (through the reference distribution) and the reward function, but **not** on a particular completion \(y\).  This independence is the key to the simplifications that follow.

---

## Re‑parameterizing Reward in Terms of Policy (Eq. 5)

Equation 3 gives the optimal policy as a function of the reward.  Since the optimal policy is what we ultimately care about, we can *invert* this relationship: express the reward that would make a given policy \(\pi\) optimal.  Taking logarithms of both sides of Equation 3:

\[
\log \pi(y\mid x) = \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y) - \log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging terms gives an explicit reward parameterisation:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
r(x,y) = \beta \bigl[\log \pi(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr] + \beta \log Z(x).
}
\tag{4}
\]

This is Equation 5 of the paper.  The first term, \(\beta\bigl(\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\bigr)\), quantifies how much more (or less) probability the policy \(\pi\) assigns to a completion compared with the reference.  The second term, \(\beta\log Z(x)\), is a *per‑context offset* that does not depend on \(y\).

Because the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) preference model depends only on **differences of rewards**, the additive offset \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels out when we consider a pair of completions \((y_{w},y_{l})\).  Consequently the *implicit* reward that corresponds to a policy \(\pi\) can be written without any reference to the intractable partition function:

\[
r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}) \;=\;
\beta\Bigl[
\log \frac{\pi(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\log \frac{\pi(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Bigr].
\tag{5}
\]

Equation 5 is the bridge that allows us to replace the reward‑model learning step entirely: we can directly optimise a policy by maximising the likelihood of observed human preferences under the *re‑parameterised* Bradley‑Terry model that uses the quantity above.

---

## Why the Partition Function Cancels in Preference Ratios

Human preference data are typically binary comparisons: given a prompt \(x\) and two completions \(y_{1}, y_{2}\), annotators indicate which completion they prefer.  The Bradley‑Terry model posits that the probability of preferring \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) is

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)}%
     {\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{6}
\]

Substituting the re‑parameterised reward (Equation 4) into (6) yields

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
}{
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
+
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
}.
\]

Because \(\exp(\beta\log Z(x)) = Z(x)^{\beta}\) multiplies *both* numerator and denominator, it cancels:

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{
\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
}{
\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
+
\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\bigl[\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr)}.
\tag{7}
\]

Equation 7 is precisely the *preference likelihood* used in the DPO loss (Equation 8 of the paper).  The crucial observation is that **the partition function disappears** because preferences compare *ratios* of exponentiated rewards, and the additive offset \(\beta\log Z(x)\) contributes the same factor to every term in the ratio.  This cancellation removes the need to compute the expensive sum over all possible completions, which would be intractable for language models with a large vocabulary and long sequences.

---

### Summary

- By solving the KL‑constrained maximisation problem we obtain a closed‑form optimal policy (Eq. 4) that is a Boltzmann re‑weighting of a reference policy.
- The partition function \(Z(x)\) normalises the re‑weighted distribution and can be interpreted as a soft value of the reference policy.
- Re‑expressing the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 5) reveals that the only policy‑dependent component of the reward is the log‑ratio \(\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\); the partition function adds a context‑only offset.
- When preferences are modeled with a Bradley‑Terry (or Plackett‑Luce) likelihood, the offset cancels, leading to a loss that depends solely on *policy* probabilities.  This cancellation permits a **single binary‑cross‑entropy objective** over the policy, eliminating the need for an explicit reward model or reinforcement‑learning loop.

These derivations provide the theoretical foundation for Direct Preference Optimization: training a language model to satisfy human preferences by directly maximising the likelihood of the observed preference data under the re‑parameterised policy model.  The result is a method that is both mathematically clean and practically lightweight, as explained in later chapters.
