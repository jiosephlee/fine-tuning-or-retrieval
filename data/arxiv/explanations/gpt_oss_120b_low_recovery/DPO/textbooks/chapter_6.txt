\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: Why Standard Actor‑Critic Methods Can Fail  
================================================

In recent years, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has become the dominant paradigm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with user intentions.  The most common implementation of RLHF relies on an actor‑critic algorithm—typically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—to maximize a learned reward while constraining the policy to remain close to a reference model.  Despite its empirical success, PPO can be unstable, especially when applied to the high‑dimensional, discrete action space of text generation.  This chapter dissects the source of these instabilities from a control‑as‑inference perspective, walks through the derivation of the actor‑critic objective used in RLHF, highlights the crucial role of the soft‑value (normalisation) term, and explains why the resulting gradient estimator suffers from high variance and a brittle dependence on baselines.  Finally, we show how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) sidesteps these pitfalls by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of the policy itself, thereby removing the need for an explicit baseline.

---

## Control‑as‑Inference View of KL‑Constrained RL  

Reinforcement learning can be recast as a probabilistic inference problem.  Suppose we have a *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (often the supervised‑fine‑tuned model) and a scalar reward function \(r(x,y)\) that scores a response \(y\) to a prompt \(x\).  The RLHF objective augments the reward with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty that discourages the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) from drifting too far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Bigl[\, r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \,
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \Bigr].
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(\beta>0\) determines the strength of the KL regulariser.  Interpreting (1) as an inference problem, we introduce a *soft* optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) that maximises the exponentiated reward while being anchored to the reference distribution:
\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x) \propto 
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]
Equation (2) follows from standard calculus of variations with a KL constraint (see Appendix A of the original paper).  The normalising constant,
\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\tag{3}
\]
is often called the *soft‑value* because its logarithm is the soft‑max analogue of the conventional value function.

From the inference viewpoint, the RL objective (1) can be written as a KL divergence minimisation:
\[
\min_{\pi_{\theta}} \; 
\mathbb{D}_{\!KL}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\tag{4}
\]
i.e. we seek a policy that approximates the analytically optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).  The challenge is that for text generation the sum in (3) is intractable—there are exponentially many possible completions—so we cannot evaluate \(\pi^{*}\) exactly.  PPO sidesteps this by estimating gradients from sampled trajectories and by introducing a learned *critic* (value function) to approximate the soft‑value term.

---

## Derivation of the Actor‑Critic Objective (Eq. 10)

Starting from (1) and substituting the form of \(\pi^{*}\) in (2), we obtain an expression that separates the reward, the KL penalty, and a normalising term:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\theta}) 
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[ r(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[
r(x,y) - \beta\log\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)
+\beta\log\!\bigl(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Adding and subtracting the *soft‑value* \(\beta\log Z(x)\) (which does not depend on \(y\) once \(x\) is fixed) yields the actor‑critic formulation:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\theta})
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[
\underbrace{r(x,y) - \beta\log Z(x)}_{\displaystyle f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}}_{\displaystyle \text{KL term}}
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

Equation (6) is precisely Eq. 10 in the original paper.  The first bracketed quantity,
\[
f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) = r(x,y) - \beta\log Z(x),
\tag{7}
\]
acts as a *soft advantage*: it measures the immediate reward of an action minus the expected (soft) value of the reference policy.  The second term enforces the KL constraint.

In practice, the actor‑critic algorithm estimates the gradient of (6) with respect to \(\theta\) using the *policy gradient* theorem:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}
= \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi_{\theta}}
\Bigl[ \bigl(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\bigr)
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \Bigr].
\tag{8}
\]
Because \(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)\) depends on the unknown normalising constant \(Z(x)\), PPO replaces it with a learned *value network* \(V_{\psi}(x)\) that approximates \(\beta\log Z(x)\).  This is the standard actor‑critic decomposition: the *actor* (\(\pi_{\theta}\)) proposes actions, while the *critic* (\(V_{\psi}\)) provides a baseline that reduces variance.

---

## Role of the Soft‑Value (Normalization) Term  

The term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) in (7) is crucial for two reasons:

1. **Correctness of the Objective.**  
   The original KL‑constrained optimisation (1) is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence (4).  Dropping \(\beta\log Z(x)\) changes the optimisation target: the resulting objective would no longer correspond to the KL to the true optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).  The soft‑value term ensures that the reward is *centered* relative to the reference distribution, preventing systematic bias toward ever‑increasing raw reward values.

2. **Variance Reduction.**  
   The gradient estimator (8) contains the factor \(f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)\) as a *baseline*.  If we omitted \(\beta\log Z(x)\), the baseline would be simply \(r(x,y)\).  Since rewards in language generation can vary dramatically across sampled completions (e.g., a sentiment classifier may output values near \(\pm 5\)), the resulting policy gradient would have very high variance, making learning unstable.  By subtracting the expected soft‑value, the term acts analogously to the classic *advantage* function \(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\), yielding lower‑variance updates.

Because \(Z(x)\) involves a sum over all possible completions, it is infeasible to compute exactly.  PPO therefore learns an approximation \(V_{\psi}(x)\) by regression on sampled trajectories.  This introduces an additional source of error: if the critic does not accurately represent \(\beta\log Z(x)\), the advantage estimates become biased, which can lead to policy *drift* or *mode collapse*.

---

## High‑Variance Gradients and Baseline Dependence  

Even with a learned critic, the actor‑critic gradient can be noisy for several intertwined reasons:

* **Sparse, Binary Preference Signal.**  
  In RLHF the reward is derived from a binary preference model (Bradley‑Terry).  The logistic transformation \(\sigma(r_{w}-r_{l})\) compresses the reward difference into a narrow interval \([0,1]\), often producing very small gradients for pairs that are already well‑ordered.  When combined with a poorly estimated soft‑value, the signal-to-noise ratio deteriorates.

* **Monte‑Carlo Estimation of the Baseline.**  
  The critic is trained on the same trajectories used for the policy update.  Early in training, these trajectories are drawn from a near‑random policy, yielding high‑variance targets for \(V_{\psi}\).  The resulting baseline can oscillate, causing the actor’s gradient to swing violently from step to step.

* **KL Penalty Amplification.**  
  The KL term \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) can become large when the policy begins to diverge from the reference.  Since it is multiplied by the same \(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\) factor, any mis‑estimation of the soft‑value directly translates into amplified KL gradients, potentially pushing the policy into regions where the reward model is unreliable.

* **Sample Inefficiency.**  
  PPO typically requires many on‑policy rollouts per optimisation step because each update changes the policy, invalidating previously collected samples.  For large LLMs, generating a single batch of completions is already expensive; repeated sampling dramatically increases computational cost.

These factors together explain why PPO‑based RLHF can be fragile: the algorithm must simultaneously learn an accurate critic, keep KL divergence under control, and cope with noisy, binary preference information—all while sampling huge text sequences.

---

## DPO’s Reward Re‑Parameterisation Eliminates the Baseline  

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) circumvents the above difficulties by *changing variables* before optimisation.  Starting from the optimal policy expression (2), one can solve for the reward as a function of policy and reference:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\tag{9}
\]

Crucially, the Bradley‑Terry preference probability depends only on *differences* of rewards:
\[
p(y_{1}\!\succ\! y_{2}\mid x)
= \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1}) - r(x,y_{2})\bigr).
\tag{10}
\]
Substituting (9) into (10) causes the unknown normalising term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) to cancel, leaving a preference model expressed *solely* in terms of the *policy log‑ratios*:
\[
p(y_{1}\!\succ\! y_{2}\mid x)
= \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)} 
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
\Bigr).
\tag{11}
\]

Equation (11) is the foundation of DPO.  Because the preference likelihood is now a *binary cross‑entropy* loss directly over the policy parameters,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr),
\tag{12}
\]
the optimisation problem no longer contains any explicit soft‑value term.  The *baseline* is implicitly encoded in the log‑ratio with respect to the reference model; the reference policy supplies a stable, pre‑trained distribution that does not need to be learned.

The gradient of (12) simplifies to

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
= -\beta\,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)\,
\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)
      -\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr],
\tag{13}
\]
where \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) is the *implicit* reward.  The weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w))\) automatically down‑weights pairs that are already correctly ordered, focusing learning effort on the most informative mistakes.  No external critic is required, and there is no need to approximate the partition function \(Z(x)\).

Because DPO sidesteps the soft‑value estimation altogether, it enjoys several practical benefits:

* **Zero Baseline Variance.**  The gradient variance is driven only by the stochasticity of the sampled preference pairs, not by an additional learned value function.  This makes training far more stable, especially early on when the policy is still close to the reference.

* **No On‑Policy Sampling Loop.**  The loss (12) can be evaluated on a *fixed* offline dataset of preferences; the policy never needs to generate fresh completions during optimisation.  Consequently, DPO eliminates the costly sampling‑in‑the‑loop step intrinsic to PPO.

* **Simpler Hyper‑Parameter Space.**  The KL constraint is already encoded in the log‑ratio term; there is no separate KL penalty coefficient to tune beyond the single temperature‑like constant \(\beta\).  Empirically, the authors find that a modest sweep over \(\beta\) suffices, whereas PPO requires careful tuning of both KL targets and reward normalisation.

* **Exact Optimality Within the Preference Model.**  By construction, the solution of (12) is the *optimal* policy under the KL‑constrained objective for the given Bradley‑Terry preference model.  No approximation is introduced by a critic, and the policy directly maximises the likelihood of observed human choices.

In summary, the instability of standard actor‑critic methods stems from the need to approximate the soft‑value term, which introduces high‑variance gradients and a dependence on a learned baseline.  DPO’s clever re‑parameterisation of the reward eliminates the soft‑value altogether, yielding a clean cross‑entropy objective that is both theoretically sound and empirically stable.
