\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 8: **Experimental Evaluation – Controlled Sentiment Generation**

# Task Definition and Synthetic Preference Creation  

In order to obtain a clean, fully observable environment in which the trade‑off between reward maximisation and KL‑regularisation can be studied, the authors construct a *synthetic* sentiment‑control benchmark.  The setting is deliberately simple so that every component of the learning pipeline is known analytically, yet it still mirrors the essential structure of a typical RLHF problem: a pre‑trained language model (the **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$) is asked to generate continuations $y$ conditioned on a prompt $x$, and a *human* (here simulated) supplies a binary preference between two continuations.  

Concretely, each data point consists of  

* a prompt $x$ taken from the IMDB movie‑review corpus, truncated to a short prefix (e.g. “The film was …”),  
* two candidate completions $y_{1},y_{2}$ sampled independently from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)$, and  
* a preference label $y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x$ indicating which of the two completions exhibits the *higher* positive‑sentiment score.  

Because the true sentiment of a text can be measured automatically with a high‑accuracy classifier, the authors replace human annotators by a *synthetic* preference oracle.  The oracle evaluates both candidates with a pretrained sentiment classifier $s(x,y)\in[0,1]$ that returns the probability that the continuation $y$ expresses a positive sentiment given the prefix $x$.  The candidate with the larger $s$ value is declared the winner:
\[
y_{w}= \arg\max_{y\in\{y_{1},y_{2}\}} s(x,y),\qquad
y_{l}= \arg\min_{y\in\{y_{1},y_{2}\}} s(x,y).
\]
Thus the entire preference dataset $\mathcal{D}= \{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w},y^{(i)}_{l})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ is generated automatically, guaranteeing that the label distribution exactly follows the Bradley‑Terry model with reward function $r^{*}(x,y)=\log\frac{s(x,y)}{1-s(x,y)}$.  This synthetic construction eliminates any source of noise apart from the stochasticity of the language model sampling, allowing a clean measurement of how an algorithm trades off reward and KL‑divergence.

# Metrics: Sentiment Classifier Reward, Sequence‑Level KL  

Two quantities are measured for every policy $\pi_{\theta}$ trained on $\mathcal{D}$:

1. **Reward (sentiment) score** – The true objective that RLHF seeks to maximise is the expected value of the latent reward $r^{*}$ under the policy.  Since $r^{*}$ is not directly accessible, the authors use the *sentiment classifier* $s$ as a proxy and report the *expected* classifier probability:
   \[
   \mathsf{R}(\pi_{\theta}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[\,s(x,y)\,\big],
   \]
   where $\mathcal{X}$ denotes the test‑set of IMDB prefixes.  Higher $\mathsf{R}$ corresponds to a higher proportion of generated continuations that are judged positive by the classifier.

2. **Sequence‑level KL divergence** – The KL‑regularisation term in the RLHF objective (Eq. \eqref{eq:RL}) penalises the *total* divergence between the new policy and the reference policy across the whole generated sequence:
   \[
   \mathsf{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
   \;=\;
   \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\Big[
        \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathrm{KL}\big(
        \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t}) \;\big\|\;
        \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t})
        \big)
   \Big],
   \]
   where $x_{1:t}$ denotes the prefix up to token $t$ and $T$ is the generation length.  This metric quantifies **how far** the fine‑tuned model has moved away from the original, unsupervised language model.  A small KL indicates that the policy remains close to the data distribution on which the reward model (or in DPO, the implicit reward) was calibrated.

Both metrics are reported *averaged* over a held‑out test set of 5 000 prompts.  Plotting $\mathsf{R}$ against $\mathsf{KL}$ for a family of trained policies yields a *frontier*: points that are not dominated by any other point in both dimensions.  The frontier thus visualises the **efficiency of reward acquisition** for a given amount of policy drift.

# Frontier Plots and Interpretation  

The authors evaluate three families of algorithms on the synthetic sentiment task:

* **PPO** – the standard proximal‑policy‑optimization RLHF pipeline, using a reward model trained on $\mathcal{D}$ (or the ground‑truth classifier directly, denoted PPO‑GT).  
* **DPO** – Direct Preference Optimization with the objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}).  
* **Baselines** – a *Best‑of‑$N$* approach that samples $N$ continuations from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and selects the one with highest classifier score, and a *Preferred‑FT* model that simply maximises likelihood of the preferred answer $y_{w}$ without any KL constraint.

For each algorithm, a sweep over the regularisation strength is performed:

* PPO: target KL values $\{3,6,9,12\}$ (implemented via a KL‑penalty coefficient).  
* DPO: $\beta$ values $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, directly controlling the trade‑off in Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}.  

Each configuration is trained for a fixed number of gradient steps (≈ 100 k updates) and then evaluated.  The resulting points are plotted on a two‑dimensional graph whose *x‑axis* is $\mathsf{KL}$ and *y‑axis* is $\mathsf{R}$.  The **key observations** are:

1. **DPO dominates the PPO frontier.**  Every DPO point lies on or above the PPO curve, meaning that for any given KL budget DPO attains a strictly higher reward.  Even when PPO receives the *true* sentiment reward (PPO‑GT), DPO’s frontier remains superior.  
2. **The reward‑KL trade‑off is smoother for DPO.**  Because DPO’s loss is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy, changing $\beta$ smoothly adjusts the relative weighting of the two log‑probability terms.  In PPO, the KL penalty interacts with the entropy bonus and the policy‑gradient estimator, often leading to unstable or non‑monotonic behaviour as the penalty coefficient varies.  
3. **Best‑of‑$N$ sits at the extreme right of the plot.**  By sampling many candidates and selecting the best according to the classifier, the method achieves very high reward but at the cost of a massive KL (the policy is effectively replaced by a *sampling* procedure).  This demonstrates that the frontier is not a trivial consequence of increasing the number of samples; rather, DPO reaches high reward while retaining a compact, generative policy.

The frontier plot therefore provides a **visual proof** that Direct Preference Optimization can achieve the same constrained optimisation target as RLHF’s KL‑regularised RL, but without the variance‑inducing Monte‑Carlo gradients that plague PPO.

# Ablation on $\beta$ and the Weighting Term  

The DPO loss (Eq. \eqref{eq:optimum_model}) contains two crucial components:

* the *temperature* $\beta$, which scales the log‑probability ratio $\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$, and  
* the *importance weighting* $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ that appears in the gradient expression (Eq. \eqref{eq:gradient}).

To assess their individual contributions, the authors perform two ablations:

1. **Varying $\beta$ without weighting.**  Setting the weighting factor to a constant (i.e. removing the $\sigma$ term) yields a *naïve* probability‑ratio objective:
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{naïve}} = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[
        \log \sigma\!\big(\beta\!\log\!\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w})}
               -\beta\!\log\!\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l})}\big)
        \big].
   \]
   Experiments show that for small $\beta$ the model collapses: it learns to assign *extremely* high probability to the preferred completions **and** to *suppress* the dispreferred ones, but because the loss does not penalise mis‑ranking, the policy quickly degenerates to a deterministic mapping that reproduces the exact training pairs.  Consequently the KL explodes and the reward plateaus at a sub‑optimal level.

2. **Full DPO with weighting.**  Restoring the $\sigma$ term yields a gradient that is *large* precisely when the implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}$ mis‑orders the pair (i.e. when the dispreferred answer receives a higher $\hat r$).  This adaptive weighting prevents the model from “over‑correcting” on examples that are already correctly ordered, thereby stabilising learning and keeping the policy close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Empirically, the weighted version reaches the Pareto‑optimal frontier and remains robust across a wide range of $\beta$ values.

The ablation demonstrates that **both** the temperature $\beta$ (which governs the strength of KL‑regularisation) and the *dynamic* weighting are essential for DPO’s superior behaviour.  A too‑large $\beta$ reduces the KL penalty, causing DPO to act similarly to the Best‑of‑$N$ baseline (high reward, high KL).  A too‑small $\beta$ forces the policy to remain overly close to the reference, limiting the achievable reward.  The sweet spot—a moderate $\beta$ (e.g. $0.1$–$1$ in the experiments)—yields the most favourable trade‑off.

# Discussion of Why DPO Dominates PPO Even with Ground‑Truth Rewards  

The empirical superiority of DPO over PPO, **including** the oracle variant PPO‑GT that uses the exact sentiment classifier as reward, can be understood from several theoretical and practical angles:

1. **Exact Closed‑Form Policy under KL Constraint.**  
   The KL‑constrained optimisation problem (Eq. \eqref{eq:RL}) admits the analytic solution $\pi_{r}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:op_policy}.  By re‑parameterising the reward as $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:main_eq}), DPO sidesteps the need to *estimate* $r$ and *sample* from the current policy during training.  PPO, by contrast, must approximate the gradient of the expectation over $\pi_{\theta}$ with Monte‑Carlo samples; this introduces variance that grows with sequence length and harms sample efficiency.

2. **No Need for a Baseline or Value Function.**  
   PPO typically augments the reward with a learned baseline (a value network) to reduce variance.  If the baseline is mis‑estimated, the policy gradient can become biased, leading to under‑ or over‑optimisation of the reward.  DPO’s loss is a pure binary cross‑entropy; the weighting term $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ plays the role of an *implicit* variance reducer, focusing updates on mis‑ranked pairs and automatically normalising across the dataset.  

3. **Implicit Enforcement of the Normalising Partition Function.**  
   The KL‑constrained optimum includes the partition function $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)$.  In PPO this term appears only as a *soft‑value* that must be estimated (often via a separate critic).  Errors in this estimate translate directly into an inaccurate KL penalty.  DPO’s change‑of‑variables eliminates $Z(x)$ from the likelihood expression (it cancels in Eq. \eqref{eq:objective}), removing a source of bias altogether.

4. **Stability of a Supervised‑Learning Objective.**  
   Because DPO reduces to standard supervised classification, all the engineering tricks that stabilise language‑model fine‑tuning (gradient clipping, learning‑rate schedules, mixed‑precision training) apply unchanged.  No special RL‑specific hyperparameters (e.g., clipping epsilon, entropy coefficient) need to be tuned.  The experiments in the paper show that a *single* $\beta$ value works well across model sizes, whereas PPO typically requires meticulous tuning of the KL target, clipping parameters, and learning‑rate decay to avoid catastrophic policy collapse.

5. **Exactness of the Preference‑Based Objective.**  
   The preference likelihood written in terms of policy ratios (Eq. \eqref{eq:objective}) is *identical* to the Bradley‑Terry likelihood used to train the reward model in conventional RLHF.  Consequently the optimum of the DPO loss coincides with the optimum of the full RLHF pipeline (reward model + KL‑constrained RL).  Therefore, when the data truly follow the Bradley‑Terry assumptions—as they do in the synthetic sentiment benchmark—DPO reaches the *global* optimum, whereas PPO only approximates it through stochastic gradient ascent.

In sum, DPO’s advantage stems from **avoiding stochastic policy‑gradient estimation** altogether and **leveraging an exact analytical mapping** between reward and policy.  This yields a cleaner optimisation landscape, lower variance updates, and ultimately a tighter reward‑KL frontier, even when the PPO baseline is supplied with the *true* reward function.  

---  

Through this controlled sentiment experiment the paper demonstrates that Direct Preference Optimization can faithfully implement the same KL‑regularised reward maximisation problem that underlies RLHF, but with dramatically reduced computational complexity and superior empirical efficiency.  The synthetic benchmark therefore serves as a proof‑of‑concept that the theoretical re‑parameterisation translates into practical gains, motivating the subsequent experiments on real‑world preference datasets.
