\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: # Motivation and Challenges of Aligning Large Language Models

Large language models (LMs) trained on massive, uncurated text corpora have demonstrated astonishing abilities: they can answer factual questions, generate coherent prose, solve mathematical problems, and even exhibit rudimentary reasoning. This section surveys why these capabilities, while impressive, are insufficient for many downstream applications and why steering such models toward desired behaviours—commonly referred to as **alignment**—is a central research problem.  We then examine the prevailing paradigm of **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**, delineating its constituent stages, and finally discuss the practical and theoretical obstacles that motivate the search for a simpler, more stable alternative.

---

## Capabilities of Large Unsupervised LMs

The term *unsupervised* in the context of language modelling refers to training on raw text without any task‑specific supervision.  Modern LMs such as PaLM, GPT‑3, LLaMA, and other multi‑billion‑parameter transformers are first **pre‑trained** on datasets containing hundreds of billions of tokens.  During this phase the model learns to predict the next token given a prefix, a task that implicitly forces the network to acquire:

1. **World knowledge** – factual information about entities, events, and relationships that appear in the training data.
2. **Linguistic competence** – syntax, style, and discourse structure that enable fluent generation.
3. **General reasoning** – the ability to chain together facts, perform basic arithmetic, and follow logical patterns, even though the training signal never explicitly asks the model to reason.

Empirical studies (e.g., Chowdhery *et al.*, 2022; Brown *et al.*, 2020; Touvron *et al.*, 2023) show that these models can solve many benchmarks zero‑shot or with few‑shot prompting.  Nevertheless, the **distribution of behaviours** that emerges from pure next‑token prediction is extremely broad.  The model reproduces not only the high‑quality text present in its corpus but also the myriad **undesirable patterns** that humans have written: misinformation, toxic language, culturally biased statements, and inefficient coding practices.  Because the pre‑training objective does not discriminate among these patterns, the resulting policy—denoted \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)—is a mixture of useful and harmful behaviours.

---

## Why Steering and Alignment Are Essential

In practice, an LM is deployed as an **assistant** that must obey user intents while respecting safety, factuality, and ethical constraints.  Several concrete scenarios illustrate the need for steering:

- **Coding assistants** should *understand* common programming mistakes (to diagnose them) but must **bias** generation toward correct, clean code.  The undesired behaviour would be to repeat the mistake.
- **Fact‑checking agents** may need to be aware of a popular misconception (to recognize it) yet must **refuse** to present it as true when answering user queries.
- **Conversational agents** must avoid generating hateful or misleading content, even though such content may appear frequently in the raw training data.

These examples highlight a **mismatch** between the knowledge the model has acquired (the *capability* space) and the *behaviour* we wish to exhibit.  Alignment therefore consists of **selecting** a subset of the model’s capacity that conforms to human values and task specifications.  Without a systematic alignment method, deploying LMs at scale would be unsafe and unpredictable.

---

## Overview of Human‑Feedback Alignment (RLHF)

The dominant solution to the alignment problem is **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**, a three‑stage pipeline that has been refined across numerous works (Christiano *et al.*, 2017; Ziegler *et al.*, 2020; Stiennon *et al.*, 2022).  The stages are:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT).**  
   An initially pre‑trained LM \(\pi_{\text{pre}}\) is fine‑tuned on a curated dataset of high‑quality prompts and completions, yielding a *reference policy* \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  This step endows the model with basic instruction‑following abilities and narrows the distribution of outputs.

2. **Preference Collection & Reward Modelling.**  
   The SFT model is used to generate *pairs* of candidate completions \((y_{1}, y_{2})\) for each prompt \(x\).  Human annotators label which completion they prefer, denoted \(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x\).  Assuming a latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) that governs these preferences, the **Bradley–Terry** model formalises the probability of a human preferring one answer over another:
   \[
   p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
   \]
   A parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) (often a shallow head on top of the SFT transformer) is fit by **maximum likelihood** on the binary classification loss
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\bigl[\log \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)\bigr],
   \]
   where \(\sigma\) denotes the logistic function and \(\mathcal{D}\) is the set of annotated comparisons.

3. **Reinforcement Learning Fine‑Tuning.**  
   The learned reward serves as a signal for a **policy optimisation** problem that balances two objectives:
   - **Reward maximisation**: increase expected reward under the policy.
   - **KL‑regularisation**: keep the policy close to the reference \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) to avoid drift into regions where the reward model may be unreliable.
   
   Formally, the optimisation problem is
   \[
   \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
   \;-\;
   \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
   \]
   where \(\beta>0\) controls the strength of the KL penalty and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is typically set to \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  Because language generation is discrete, the expectation cannot be differentiated directly.  Consequently, RLHF adopts **policy‑gradient** methods such as **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** or REINFORCE, which estimate gradients via Monte‑Carlo sampling of completions from \(\pi_{\theta}\).

The overall RLHF procedure has proved effective: models trained with this pipeline achieve state‑of‑the‑art performance on dialogue, summarisation, and code‑generation benchmarks.

---

## Complexities of Reward Modelling and Reinforcement Learning

Despite its empirical successes, RLHF suffers from several intertwined difficulties that complicate research and production deployment:

1. **Reward Model Instability.**  
   The reward model is trained on a *finite* set of pairwise preferences.  Its predictions can be poorly calibrated on out‑of‑distribution completions, leading to **reward hacking** where the policy learns to maximise spurious artefacts of the reward rather than genuine human‑preferred behaviour.  Normalising the reward (e.g., forcing mean zero) mitigates variance but does not eliminate the fundamental under‑specification of the Bradley–Terry formulation: adding any function of the prompt \(f(x)\) to the reward leaves the preference probabilities unchanged.

2. **Sampling Overhead.**  
   PPO requires **on‑policy sampling** at each optimisation step: the current policy must generate many completions to estimate the gradient.  For large transformer models, each forward pass is costly, and the need to sample *many* tokens per update dramatically inflates training time and GPU memory consumption.

3. **Hyper‑parameter Sensitivity.**  
   The KL penalty coefficient \(\beta\) (or an explicit KL target) must be tuned carefully.  Too small a penalty permits the policy to drift far from the reference, possibly entering regions where the reward model is unreliable; too large a penalty hampers reward improvement.  PPO also has numerous algorithmic knobs (clip thresholds, learning rates, value‑function baselines) that are typically set via extensive empirical sweeps.

4. **Variance of Policy‑Gradient Estimates.**  
   Monte‑Carlo estimators for the expected reward have high variance, especially when the reward signal is noisy.  Actor‑critic variants mitigate this by learning a baseline value function, but training the critic introduces another source of instability and requires additional optimisation machinery.

5. **Implementation Complexity.**  
   The RLHF pipeline involves **multiple separate models** (the SFT model, the reward head, the policy network, and often a value network) and **alternating optimisation loops** (supervised fine‑tuning, reward fitting, RL).  Engineers must orchestrate data pipelines that alternate between generating new completions, collecting human labels, and updating distinct parameter sets.  This complexity raises the barrier to entry for researchers wishing to experiment with preference‑based alignment.

These challenges collectively motivate the search for a method that **preserves the alignment objective**—maximising human‑compatible reward while respecting a KL constraint—yet **removes the need for explicit reinforcement learning** and its associated instability.

---

## Desire for a Simpler, More Stable Method

The key insight underpinning recent work on **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** is that the **optimal policy** for the KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem admits a *closed‑form* relationship to the underlying reward function.  Starting from the constrained optimisation

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),
\]

the optimum can be expressed as

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\]

Taking logarithms yields a *re‑parameterisation* of the reward in terms of any policy \(\pi\) that could be optimal:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards, the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels when expressing the human preference probability.  Substituting the above expression for \(r\) gives a preference likelihood that depends **solely on the policy**:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{1}
{1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\Bigr)}.
\]

Consequently, fitting a policy to maximise the likelihood of observed human preferences reduces to a **binary cross‑entropy loss** over the policy logits:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

Optimising this objective directly **updates the language model** without ever instantiating an explicit reward head, nor invoking a reinforcement‑learning loop.  The gradient of the loss naturally incorporates a per‑example weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\), which down‑weights examples where the current implicit reward already ranks the preferred response higher, thereby preventing the degeneration observed with naïve unlikelihood training.

The **advantages** of this reformulation are immediate:

- **No sampling during training**: gradients are computed analytically from the logged probabilities of the chosen and rejected completions; no on‑policy rollouts are required.
- **Single‑model training**: the same transformer parameters serve both as the policy and as an implicit reward, eliminating a separate reward network.
- **Stable optimisation**: the loss is a standard binary cross‑entropy, for which well‑understood optimisation tricks (learning‑rate schedules, batch normalisation, weight decay) apply.
- **Reduced hyper‑parameter burden**: only the KL‑strength \(\beta\) must be chosen; there is no need for PPO‑specific clipping or value‑function learning rates.

Thus, Direct Preference Optimization offers a **theoretically grounded, computationally lightweight** alternative to the traditional RLHF pipeline, preserving the essential alignment objective while sidestepping the principal sources of instability and engineering complexity.  The remainder of this textbook will develop the DPO algorithm in detail, analyse its theoretical properties, and present empirical evidence of its efficacy across a range of language‑generation tasks.
