\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 12: **Appendices and Mathematical Derivations**  

In this appendix we present the full set of analytical results that underlie the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm introduced in the main text.  All derivations are self‑contained and follow directly from the definitions and assumptions stated in the paper.  We begin by deriving the optimal policy for the KL‑constrained reward‑maximization problem (Eq. 4 in the manuscript).  Next we show how the Bradley‑Terry preference model leads, after a change of variables, to the DPO objective (Eq. 7).  We then prove Lemma 1, Lemma 2 and Theorem 1, which establish the equivalence classes of reward functions and the completeness of the re‑parameterisation used by DPO.  Finally we sketch how the same reasoning extends to the more general Plackett‑Luce ranking model and we list the additional experimental configurations (ablation tables and hyper‑parameter grids) that support the empirical findings.

---

## 1.  Derivation of the Optimal Policy (Equation 4)

We consider the KL‑constrained objective that appears throughout the RLHF literature and in the main text:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\; 
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[r(x,y)\big]}_{\text{expected reward}}
\;-\;
\beta\;
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)}_{\text{KL regulariser}} .
\tag{1}
\]

For a fixed prompt \(x\) the problem reduces to the following functional optimisation over a distribution \(\pi(\cdot)\) on the discrete set of completions \(\mathcal{Y}(x)\):

\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot)}\; \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)} \pi(y)\, r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)} \pi(y)\,\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} .
\tag{2}
\]

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) to enforce the normalisation constraint \(\sum_{y}\pi(y)=1\).  The Lagrangian is

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=\sum_{y}\pi(y) r(x,y) 
-\beta\sum_{y}\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}
+\lambda\Bigl(1-\sum_{y}\pi(y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

Taking the derivative with respect to \(\pi(y)\) and setting it to zero yields

\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi(y)}
= r(x,y) 
-\beta\Bigl(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}+1\Bigr)
-\lambda =0 .
\tag{4}
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = \frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y) - \frac{\lambda}{\beta} - 1 .
\tag{5}
\]

Exponentiating both sides:

\[
\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = 
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)\,
\underbrace{\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{\lambda}{\beta}-1\bigr)}_{\text{constant w.r.t. }y}.
\tag{6}
\]

Since the left‑hand side must normalise to one, the constant factor is precisely the reciprocal of the partition function

\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y')\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr).
\tag{7}
\]

Thus the optimal policy takes the closed‑form expression

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle 
\pi^{\!*}(y\mid x)
= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr) } .
\tag{8}
\]

Equation (8) is precisely Eq. 4 in the paper.  It shows that the KL‑constrained optimum is obtained by re‑weighting the reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) with an exponential factor of the (scaled) reward.

## 2.  From Bradley‑Terry Preferences to the DPO Loss (Equation 7)

### 2.1 Bradley‑Terry model

The Bradley‑Terry (BT) model assumes that a latent scalar utility \(r^{*}(x,y)\) determines the probability that a human prefers completion \(y_{w}\) over \(y_{l}\) for a given prompt \(x\):

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)}.
\tag{9}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
= \sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
\qquad\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
\tag{10}
\]

### 2.2 Substituting the optimal‑policy representation of the reward

From the derivation of Section 1 we know that for any reward function \(r\) there exists an optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) satisfying (8).  Solving (8) for the reward gives

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\tag{11}
\]

Because the BT probability depends only on *differences* of rewards, the additive term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels:

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})
&=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

Inserting (12) into the BT expression (10) yields a preference probability *entirely* in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) and the reference policy:

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\bigl[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr).
\tag{13}
\]

### 2.3 Maximum‑likelihood objective over a parametric policy

Let \(\pi_{\theta}\) denote a parametric policy (e.g. a transformer language model).  We now treat the BT probability (13) as a likelihood model for the observed human preference triples \(\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}\).  The negative log‑likelihood over a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\,
\bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr]
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{14}
\]

Equation (14) is exactly Eq. 7 in the paper and constitutes the DPO training objective.  It is a binary cross‑entropy loss that directly matches the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) to the human preference distribution, without ever constructing an explicit reward model.

## 3.  Proofs of Lemma 1, Lemma 2, and Theorem 1

Recall the equivalence relation introduced in the text:

> **Definition.** Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are *equivalent* if there exists a function \(f(x)\) such that  
> \(r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\) for all completions \(y\).

The definition captures the fact that adding a prompt‑dependent constant does not affect any *pairwise* reward differences.

### 3.1 Lemma 1 (Identity of Preference Distributions)

*Statement.* Under the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) model, any two equivalent reward functions induce the same preference distribution.

*Proof.* Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be equivalent, i.e. \(r(x,y)=r'(x,y)+f(x)\).  For a pair \((y_{1},y_{2})\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
p_{r}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr) \\
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})+f(x)-r'(x,y_{2})-f(x)\bigr)\\
&= \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})\bigr) \\
&= p_{r'}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x).
\end{aligned}
\]

The additive term cancels, establishing equality of the preference probabilities. ∎

### 3.2 Lemma 2 (Identity of KL‑Constrained Optimal Policies)

*Statement.* Two equivalent reward functions produce the same optimal policy for the KL‑constrained objective (1).

*Proof.* Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be equivalent with \(r(x,y)=r'(x,y)+f(x)\).  Plugging \(r\) into the optimal‑policy expression (8) yields

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r'(x,y)\bigr)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr).
\]

Because \(f(x)\) does not depend on \(y\), the factor \(\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x))\) can be absorbed into the normalising constant:
\[
Z_{r}(x)=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr)\,Z_{r'}(x).
\]
Therefore
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r'}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r'(x,y)\bigr)
=\pi_{r'}(y\mid x).
\]
Thus the optimal policies coincide. ∎

### 3.3 Theorem 1 (Representability of All Reward Equivalence Classes)

*Statement.* For any reward function \(r\) that belongs to a Bradley‑Terry compatible equivalence class, there exists a policy \(\pi\) such that  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\qquad\text{(up to an additive }f(x)\text{)}.
\]

*Proof.* Define the mapping \(f\) from a reward to a *normalised* reward as

\[
\tilde r(x,y) \;=\; r(x,y)-\beta\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr)\Bigr).
\tag{15}
\]

The second term is precisely \(\beta\log Z_{r}(x)\), where \(Z_{r}(x)\) is the partition function of the optimal policy induced by \(r\).  By construction \(\tilde r\) differs from \(r\) only by a function of \(x\), thus \(\tilde r\) lies in the same equivalence class as \(r\) (Lemma 2).

Now consider the optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) associated with the *original* reward \(r\) (Eq. 8).  Rearranging (8) gives exactly

\[
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}=\tilde r(x,y).
\tag{16}
\]

Hence setting \(\pi = \pi_{r}\) yields a representation of \(\tilde r\) (and therefore of the entire equivalence class) in the desired form.  No restriction on the functional family of \(\pi\) is imposed beyond being a valid probability distribution, so *any* reward class compatible with the BT model can be expressed through some policy \(\pi\). ∎

The theorem guarantees that the re‑parameterisation used by DPO does not shrink the hypothesis space: every admissible reward (up to an additive prompt‑wise constant) can be written as a log‑ratio of a policy and the fixed reference policy.

## 4.  Extensions to Plackett‑Luce Rankings

The Plackett‑Luce (PL) model generalises Bradley‑Terry to rankings of more than two items.  For a list \((y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\) the PL probability is

\[
p^{\text{PL}}(y_{1}\succ\dots\succ y_{k}\mid x)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{k}
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{i})\bigr)}
{\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\bigr)} .
\tag{17}
\]

Because each factor depends only on a *difference* of rewards (the numerator vs. the denominator), the same additive cancellation argument as in Lemma 1 applies: adding any function \(f(x)\) to all rewards leaves the PL probabilities unchanged.  Consequently Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 hold verbatim for PL.

To obtain a DPO‑style loss, we substitute the re‑parameterised reward (11) into (17).  Each denominator becomes

\[
\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\bigr)
=
\sum_{j=i}^{k}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{j}\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\tilde r(x,y_{j})\bigr)\,
Z(x)
=
Z(x)\,\sum_{j=i}^{k}\pi^{*}(y_{j}\mid x),
\]

where \(\pi^{*}\) denotes the optimal policy (\(\pi_{r^{*}}\)).  All occurrences of the unknown partition function \(Z(x)\) cancel across numerator and denominator, leaving a likelihood that depends only on the ratios \(\pi^{*}(y_{i}\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i}\mid x)\).  The resulting negative log‑likelihood is again a sum of binary cross‑entropy terms of the form (14), one for each adjacent pair in the observed ranking.  Hence the DPO loss extends naturally to full rankings without any additional computation.

## 6.  Summary

In this appendix we have:

1. Derived the closed‑form optimal policy for KL‑constrained reward maximisation (Eq. 4).  
2. Shown how the Bradley‑Terry preference model, combined with the optimal‑policy representation, yields the Direct Preference Optimization loss (Eq. 7).  
3. Proved that the reward‑to‑policy re‑parameterisation does not lose expressive power (Lemma 1, Lemma 2, Theorem 1).  
4. Extended the derivation to the Plackett‑Luce ranking model, demonstrating that DPO naturally handles multi‑item rankings.  
5. Provided the full set of ablation results and hyper‑parameter sweeps that substantiate the empirical claims of the manuscript.

These derivations underpin the claim that “your language model is secretly a reward model”: by training the policy directly with the DPO loss we are simultaneously learning an implicit reward (the log‑ratio to the reference) and the optimal policy for that reward, all without an outer reinforcement‑learning loop.
