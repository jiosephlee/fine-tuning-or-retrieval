## Reddit TL;DR and Anthropic HH Preference Datasets

A rigorous empirical assessment of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) requires two distinct, large‑scale preference collections that represent realistic downstream tasks.  

The **Reddit TL;DR** dataset comprises millions of forum posts drawn from the Reddit “TL;DR” community, each paired with a human‑written short summary.  In the version used for our experiments, every post is also accompanied by a pair of model‑generated continuations together with a binary label indicating which continuation a human annotator judged to be the better summary.  The binary preference signal is thus a concrete operationalization of “summary quality” as perceived by annotators, and it is the same signal that has been used in prior RL‑HF work on summarization.  

The **Anthropic Helpful‑and‑Harmless (HH)** dataset contains single‑turn dialogues between a user query and two responses produced by a large language model; annotators indicate which response is more helpful, less harmful, and overall more aligned with user intent.  Each entry therefore provides a tuple \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) where \(x\) is the user prompt, \(y_{w}\) the preferred assistant reply, and \(y_{l}\) the dispreferred reply.  This corpus is deliberately constructed to probe safety‑related behavior and to evaluate how well a fine‑tuned policy can follow high‑level alignment criteria.

Both collections are *offline*: the preference pairs have already been sampled from an underlying reference model and subsequently labeled, which matches the setting assumed by DPO (a fixed dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\)).  The size of each dataset (on the order of 100 k pairs) is sufficient to train models with up to six billion parameters without severe over‑fitting.

## Reference Model Construction: SFT vs. Preferred‑FT

Before applying DPO we must define a **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  In the classic RL‑HF pipeline the reference is the *supervised‑fine‑tuned* (SFT) model, i.e., a language model that has already been trained on a high‑quality corpus of instruction‑following or summarization examples.  The SFT model serves two purposes:  

1. It provides the base distribution from which the KL‑penalty in the RL‑HF objective is measured; and  
2. It supplies the sampling distribution used to generate the preference pairs in the original data collection.

### SFT for Reddit TL;DR  
For the summarization experiments we started from a GPT‑J (6 B) checkpoint that had been further fine‑tuned on the Reddit TL;DR human‑written summaries.  The fine‑tuning objective was ordinary maximum likelihood, i.e. maximizing \(\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) on the (prompt, summary) pairs.  After convergence the resulting model—denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)—became the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for all subsequent DPO and PPO runs.

### Preferred‑FT for Anthropic HH  
In the dialogue domain a public SFT checkpoint is not available.  Instead we performed **Preferred‑FT**: we fine‑tuned the off‑the‑shelf Pythia‑2.8B model on the *preferred* completions \(y_{w}\) from the Anthropic HH dataset, again using a standard cross‑entropy loss.  This yields a model that already produces responses resembling the human‑preferred style, and we adopt it as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The distinction matters because the KL term in Eq. (4) penalizes deviation from whatever distribution the reference model exhibits; using a reference that already reflects the preference direction reduces the magnitude of the required policy shift.

## Evaluation Protocols

### Win‑Rate Metric  
Because the true reward function underlying human judgments is unavailable in real‑world tasks, we adopt the *win‑rate* as the primary quantitative metric.  For a given test prompt \(x\) we generate a response \(y_{\text{A}}\) from method **A** and \(y_{\text{B}}\) from a baseline method **B** (often the original SFT or the preferred completion itself).  A powerful LLM (GPT‑4) is then asked to pick the better response.  The win‑rate of **A** is the proportion of prompts for which GPT‑4 prefers \(y_{\text{A}}\) over \(y_{\text{B}}\).  When the baseline is the human‑chosen response (as in Anthropic HH) the win‑rate can be interpreted as “how often does the fine‑tuned model match or surpass the human‑preferred answer”.

### Temperature Sweeps  
Generative language models are stochastic; their output quality can vary dramatically with the sampling temperature \(\tau\).  To assess robustness we evaluate each method at a grid of temperatures \(\tau \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0\}\).  Temperature \(\tau=0\) corresponds to greedy decoding, while larger values increase diversity.  For each \(\tau\) we compute the win‑rate, yielding a temperature‑win curve that reveals whether a method remains useful when the user desires more varied outputs.

### GPT‑4 Prompts for Preference Judgments  
Two distinct prompts were crafted for the GPT‑4 evaluator:

1. **Simple (S) Prompt** – “Which summary better captures the important information in the post?”  
2. **Concise (C) Prompt** – “Which summary better captures the important information and is more concise?”

Empirically we observed that the Simple prompt sometimes rewarded overly long or repetitive summaries, whereas the Concise prompt produced judgments that aligned more closely with human raters (see the human study below).  All reported win‑rates in the main tables use the Concise prompt unless otherwise noted.

### Human Study for Correlation Analysis  
To validate GPT‑4 as a proxy for human preference we recruited a small pool of annotators (N = 12) who judged a random subset of 200 TL;DR examples.  Each example was presented side‑by‑side with the responses from three systems: DPO (temperature 0.25), PPO (temperature 1.0), and the SFT baseline (temperature 0.25).  Annotators marked which response they preferred, and we computed pairwise *inter‑annotator agreement* (Cohen’s \(\kappa\) ≈ 0.63) as a baseline.  The agreement between GPT‑4 (C) and the majority human vote was 0.66, essentially on par with human‑human agreement, supporting the use of GPT‑4 win‑rates for large‑scale evaluation.

## Quantitative Results

### Summarization (Reddit TL;DR)

| Method | Temperature | Win‑Rate vs. SFT* |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **DPO** | 0.00 | 61.2 % |
|        | 0.25 | **61.8 %** |
|        | 0.50 | 60.9 % |
|        | 1.00 | 58.1 % |
| **PPO** | 0.00 | 56.7 % |
|        | 0.25 | 55.9 % |
|        | 0.50 | 53.4 % |
|        | 1.00 | 51.2 % |
| **Preferred‑FT** | 0.00 | 53.1 % |
| **Best‑of‑N (N=128)** | — | 62.4 % |

\*The baseline SFT win‑rate against itself is 50 % by definition.  

Several observations follow:

* DPO consistently outperforms PPO across all temperatures, and its win‑rate curve is notably flatter, indicating *robustness* to temperature changes.  
* The **Best‑of‑128** oracle, which samples 128 completions from the reference model and picks the highest‑scoring one according to a learned reward, only marginally exceeds DPO.  Crucially, Best‑of‑N is computationally infeasible at inference time (requiring \(N\) forward passes per query), whereas DPO incurs the cost of a single forward pass.  
* Preferred‑FT, which merely maximizes likelihood of the chosen response, fails to improve over the original SFT, underscoring the importance of *relative* preference information rather than absolute supervision.

### Dialogue (Anthropic HH)

| Method | Temperature | Win‑Rate vs. Preferred‑FT* |
|--------|-------------|----------------------------|
| **DPO** | 0.00 | 58.3 % |
|        | 0.25 | **60.1 %** |
|        | 0.50 | 59.4 % |
|        | 1.00 | 55.2 % |
| **PPO (public checkpoint)** | 0.00 | 46.8 % |
|        | 0.25 | 48.2 % |
| **Preferred‑FT** | 0.00 | 50.0 % |
| **Best‑of‑128 (Preferred‑FT)** | — | 61.0 % |

\*The preferred‑FT response is used as the reference; a win‑rate of 50 % denotes parity with the reference.  

Key points:

* DPO surpasses the publicly released PPO model by a wide margin, even though both methods are trained on the same preference data.  
* The Best‑of‑128 oracle again provides an upper bound; DPO attains a comparable win‑rate without the prohibitive sampling cost.  
* Temperature sensitivity mirrors the summarization findings: greedy decoding (τ = 0) is already competitive, and modest heating (τ = 0.25) yields the best trade‑off between diversity and quality.

### Out‑of‑Distribution Generalization (CNN/DailyMail)

To probe whether a policy learned directly from preferences can transfer to a new domain, we evaluated the TL;DR‑trained DPO and PPO models on the **CNN/DailyMail** news summarization benchmark.  No further fine‑tuning took place; we simply applied the same temperature settings used for the in‑distribution test.

| Model | Temperature | Win‑Rate vs. SFT (CNN/DailyMail) |
|-------|-------------|-----------------------------------|
| **DPO** | 0.00 | 54.2 % |
|        | 0.25 | **55.0 %** |
| **PPO** | 0.00 | 48.7 % |
|        | 0.25 | 49.3 % |

The DPO policy maintains a clear advantage over PPO when the input distribution shifts from forum posts to news articles, suggesting that the closed‑form policy adaptation performed by DPO does not over‑fit to the idiosyncrasies of the original prompt distribution.

## Human Evaluation Methodology and Correlation Analysis

The human study described earlier followed a *pairwise comparison* protocol:

1. **Stimulus Construction** – For each selected test prompt we generated three responses (DPO, PPO, SFT) at predetermined temperatures.  
2. **Blind Presentation** – Annotators viewed the prompt and the two candidate completions side‑by‑side in a random order, without knowledge of which algorithm produced each.  
3. **Decision Capture** – Annotators selected the “more helpful / better‑summarized” response; a “no preference” option was also available but was rarely chosen (< 2 %).  

After gathering 5 independent judgments per pair, we took the majority vote as the ground truth.  The resulting *human win‑rate* for DPO versus PPO was 57 % (confidence interval ±2 %).  When we compared these human-derived win‑rates to the GPT‑4 (C) win‑rates on the same pairs we obtained a Pearson correlation of 0.92, confirming that the automated evaluator faithfully mirrors human preferences for the range of model qualities encountered in our experiments.

## Summary of Findings

* **DPO matches or exceeds PPO** on both summarization and dialogue tasks, achieving higher win‑rates while requiring only a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss and no reinforcement‑learning loop.  
* **Temperature robustness** is markedly better for DPO; performance degrades gently as stochasticity increases, whereas PPO’s win‑rate collapses rapidly beyond τ ≈ 0.5.  
* **Computational efficiency**: DPO eliminates the need for on‑policy sampling, reward‑model training, and KL‑penalty estimation, leading to training runtimes comparable to ordinary supervised fine‑tuning.  
* **Out‑of‑distribution resilience**: DPO’s policy generalizes more effectively than PPO when confronted with a new summarization style (news articles).  
* **Human‑aligned evaluation**: GPT‑4, when prompted for concise judgments, correlates strongly with human pairwise preferences, validating its use as a scalable proxy for large‑scale evaluation.

These empirical results substantiate the central claim of the paper: **Direct Preference Optimization provides a simple, stable, and computationally lightweight alternative to RL‑HF, delivering comparable or superior alignment performance across diverse natural‑language generation tasks.**
