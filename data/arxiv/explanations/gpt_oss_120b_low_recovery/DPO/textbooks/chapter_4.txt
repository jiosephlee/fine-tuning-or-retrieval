Chapter 4: **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): Algorithmic Design**

# Substituting the Re‑parameterization into Bradley‑Terry  

The Bradley‑Terry (BT) model is the cornerstone of most preference‑learning pipelines for large language models.  Under this model a human is assumed to choose the preferred completion \(y_{w}\) over the dispreferred one \(y_{l}\) according to a latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\):
\[
p^{*}\!\left(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\right)
  =\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
         {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)}
  =\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic sigmoid.

In the classic RLHF pipeline the reward is first estimated from data, then a language‑model policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is trained to maximize the expected reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The constrained optimisation can be written as
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}
  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
  -\beta\,
  \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]
with \(\beta>0\) controlling the strength of the KL‑regulariser.  The optimal solution of (2) for any given reward \(r\) is analytically known (e.g. via the method of Lagrange multipliers) to be
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
        \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]
Equation (3) tells us that any optimal policy is obtained by re‑weighting the reference distribution with an exponentiated reward.  Crucially, we can solve (3) for the reward itself:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)
  &=\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
    +\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x)\\[4pt]
\Longrightarrow\quad
r(x,y)
  &=\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
                +\log Z(x)\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
The term \(\log Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\); it does **not** affect the preference between two completions because it cancels in the difference \(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})\).

Substituting (4) into the BT likelihood (1) yields a preference probability that is expressed entirely in terms of the **policy** \(\pi_{r}\) and the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
p^{*}\!\left(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\right)
  &=\sigma\!\Bigl(
        r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})
       \Bigr)                                                                  \\
  &=\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
        -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
       \Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]
Equation (5) is the key observation: the BT model can be re‑parameterised so that the latent reward never appears explicitly; the model’s *policy* itself plays the role of the reward‑inducing object.

# Derivation of the DPO Objective (Eq. 7)

Given a finite dataset of human preference triples
\[
\mathcal{D}
 =\bigl\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\tag{6}
\]
the natural maximum‑likelihood estimator for the BT model (5) is obtained by maximising the log‑probability of the observed preferences.  Re‑writing the negated log‑likelihood yields the **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss**:
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
   = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
      \Bigl[
         \log \sigma\!\Bigl(
               \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
              -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
            \Bigr)
      \Bigr].
}
\tag{7}
\]
All quantities on the right‑hand side are directly computable from two forward passes of the language model: one for the preferred completion and one for the dispreferred completion.  No auxiliary reward network, no Monte‑Carlo estimate of a partition function, and no RL‑style policy‑gradient estimator are required.  The objective (7) is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss where the “logit” is the **difference of importance‑weighted log‑probabilities**.

# Gradient Analysis and the Importance‑Weight Term  

To understand why DPO succeeds where a naïve probability‑ratio loss may fail, we differentiate (7) with respect to the model parameters \(\theta\).  Define the **implicit reward** induced by the current policy:
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)
   \;\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\;
   \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\tag{8}
\]
Using the chain rule and the derivative of the logistic function,
\(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
  &= -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
     \Bigl[
        \sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)
        \Bigl(
          \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
          -\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)
        \Bigr)
     \Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\]
The scalar factor
\[
\underbrace{\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}_{\text{importance weight}}
\]
is large when the current policy **mis‑orders** the pair (i.e. assigns a higher implicit reward to the dispreferred completion).  Consequently, the gradient puts more pressure on such “hard” examples, while pairs that are already correctly ordered contribute little.  This adaptive weighting is absent in a naïve loss of the form
\(-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}\),
which would indiscriminately increase the probability of \(y_{w}\) and decrease that of \(y_{l}\).  The naïve loss can drive the model to assign absurdly high probabilities to the preferred completions while neglecting the KL‑regularisation embodied by \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), leading to **policy collapse** (the model drifts far from its original distribution).

A related baseline is the **unlikelihood** objective, which explicitly adds a term \(-\alpha\log\bigl(1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr)\) to penalise dispreferred tokens.  In the unlikelihood setting the weighting coefficient \(\alpha\) is fixed a priori; the method cannot adapt to how badly the current policy ranks a specific pair.  Empirically (see the paper’s Appendix) the unlikelihood loss without the importance weight leads to degraded generation quality, confirming the theoretical insight from (9).  DPO’s gradient therefore achieves a balanced trade‑off: it respects the KL‑constraint by comparing each probability to the reference model, and it focuses learning on the mistakes that matter most for the preference data.

# Comparison with Naïve Probability‑Ratio and Unlikelihood Methods  

| Method | Objective (simplified) | Use of reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) | Adaptive weighting | Theoretical guarantee |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| **Naïve probability‑ratio** | \(-\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}\) | No | No (all pairs equal) | None; can violate KL‑constraint |
| **Unlikelihood** | \(-\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}) - \alpha\log (1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}))\) | No (sometimes a reference is used to initialise) | Fixed \(\alpha\) | No explicit KL regularisation |
| **DPO** | Eq. (7) (binary cross‑entropy on \(\beta\)‑scaled log‑probability ratios) | Yes, appears in the denominator of each ratio | Yes, via \(\sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w})\bigr)\) | Exactly solves the KL‑constrained RLHF objective under the BT model |

The naïve ratio loss resembles **maximum likelihood** on a deterministic preference label, but it ignores the crucial fact that preferences are *relative* and that the underlying reward is only defined up to an additive function of the prompt (the equivalence class in Lemma 2).  DPO, by construction, respects that equivalence because the additive term \(\log Z(x)\) cancels out.  Moreover, the inclusion of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in the log‑ratio enforces the KL constraint **implicitly**: if the policy drifts far from the reference, the ratio will inflate, increasing the loss and pulling the policy back.

# Algorithmic Pipeline  

Putting the pieces together, the DPO training loop consists of three logical stages:

1. **Data collection**  
   - Sample two completions \((y_{1},y_{2})\) from a *reference* language model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for each prompt \(x\).  
   - Obtain a binary preference label from a human annotator (or a trusted automatic proxy).  
   - Store the triple \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) in the preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}\).

2. **Reference model construction**  
   - If a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is available, set \(\pi_{\text{ref}} \gets \pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  
   - If no SFT model exists, train a *reference* policy by **maximising the likelihood of the preferred completions** only:
     \[
       \pi_{\text{ref}}
         = \arg\max_{\pi}\;
           \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w})\sim\mathcal{D}}
               \bigl[\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\bigr].
     \]
     This step ensures that the reference distribution is close to the data‑generating distribution, reducing the distribution shift that would otherwise appear in Eq. (5).

3. **Direct Preference Optimization**  
   - Initialise the *target* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) with the same weights as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
   - For each minibatch drawn from \(\mathcal{D}\), compute the loss (7).  
   - Perform a standard stochastic‑gradient descent (or Adam) update on \(\theta\).  
   - Optionally anneal the temperature \(\beta\) or adjust it after a few epochs to trade‑off reward maximisation versus conservatism.

The full algorithm can be summarised succinctly:

```
Input: preference dataset D, reference policy π_ref, hyperparameter β
Initialize θ ← θ_ref   (weights of π_ref)
repeat
    Sample minibatch B ⊂ D
    Compute loss L_DPO(θ) using Eq.(7) on B
    θ ← θ – η ∇_θ L_DPO(θ)          (η = learning rate)
until convergence
Output: policy π_θ
```

Because the loss is a **pure classification objective**, training proceeds exactly like ordinary supervised fine‑tuning: there is no need for on‑policy sampling, advantage estimation, or value‑function baselines.  This dramatically reduces memory consumption (no roll‑out buffers) and eliminates the notorious instability of policy‑gradient methods.

# Practical Tips  

### Initialising \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)  

- **When an SFT checkpoint exists** (e.g., a model fine‑tuned on an instruction‑following corpus), use it directly.  The SFT model already represents the distribution of “reasonable” responses, so the KL penalty will keep the final policy within the domain where the preference data is reliable.  
- **When no SFT is available**, train a lightweight reference model on the *preferred* half of the dataset only.  This is essentially *Preferred‑FT*: maximise \(\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\) while ignoring \(y_{l}\).  Empirically this yields a reference that is close enough to the true data‑generating distribution to avoid severe covariate shift.

### Choosing \(\beta\)  

- \(\beta\) governs the **strength of the KL regulariser**.  Small \(\beta\) (e.g., \(0.05\)–\(0.1\)) makes the loss focus heavily on matching preferences, allowing larger deviations from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Larger \(\beta\) (e.g., \(1\)–\(5\)) forces the policy to stay nearer to the reference, which can be useful when the preference data are noisy or when preserving generation diversity is critical.  
- A pragmatic approach is to try a grid \(\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\) and pick the model that yields the best validation win‑rate or the most favourable reward–KL trade‑off.  Unlike PPO, DPO does **not** require a separate KL‑target schedule, because the KL penalty is baked into the loss.

### Handling Missing SFT or Distribution Shift  

- If the preference dataset was collected with a different base model than the one you intend to use as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), you may observe a mismatch in token probabilities.  Mitigate this by:  
  1. **Fine‑tuning** the reference model on a small subset of the raw prompts (no completions) using language‑modeling loss; this aligns token‑distribution statistics.  
  2. **Increasing \(\beta\)** to penalise large departures from the reference, which empirically reduces drift caused by the mismatch.  

### Numerical Stability  

- Compute the log‑ratio \(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) by first obtaining logits from both models and subtracting them before applying the softmax denominator.  This avoids underflow when raw probabilities are extremely small.  
- Clip the log‑ratio to a reasonable range (e.g., \([-10,10]\)) before feeding it to the sigmoid; the clipping does not affect gradients much because the sigmoid already saturates.

### Batch‑wise Implementation  

The loss can be vectorised efficiently: for a batch of size \(B\), retrieve the logits for all \(2B\) completions (preferred and dispreferred), compute the per‑example log‑ratios, apply the sigmoid, and finally average the binary‑cross‑entropy.  Modern deep‑learning libraries (PyTorch, JAX) allow this with a few lines of code and no custom autograd functions.

### Monitoring  

- Track **average KL** between \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): this can be estimated analytically on the batch as  
  \[
    \text{KL}_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
      \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})},
  \]  
  where \(y^{(i)}\) can be the preferred completion for consistency.  
- Plot the **training loss** together with the **importance weight** \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))\).  A rapidly decreasing weight indicates that the model is correctly ordering most pairs, a sign of convergence.

# Summary  

Direct Preference Optimization replaces the two‑stage RLHF pipeline (reward‑model fitting + policy‑gradient RL) with a single, closed‑form objective that operates directly on the policy.  By exploiting the analytical relationship between the KL‑constrained optimal policy and its inducing reward (Eq. (3)), we re‑parameterise the Bradley‑Terry likelihood so that the latent reward vanishes and only the policy appears (Eq. (5)).  Maximising the resulting likelihood yields the DPO loss (Eq. (7)), whose gradient (Eq. (9)) contains an *importance‑weight* that automatically focuses learning on mis‑ranked pairs while preserving the KL regulariser through the reference model.  Compared with naïve probability‑ratio or unlikelihood baselines, DPO enjoys a solid theoretical guarantee—exactly solving the RLHF objective—and empirical advantages: stability, low computational overhead, and competitive or superior performance on sentiment control, summarisation, and dialogue tasks.  Implementing DPO is straightforward: collect preference triples, define a reference policy (preferably an SFT model), choose a sensible \(\beta\), and train with ordinary supervised optimisation machinery.  This simplicity opens the door to wider adoption of preference‑based alignment for large language models.
