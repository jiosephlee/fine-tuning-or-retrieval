\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: **Theoretical Guarantees and Connections**

In this chapter we delve into the formal underpinnings of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  Starting from the Bradley‑Terry (or, more generally, Plackett‑Luce) model of human preferences we develop a sequence of results that explain why DPO—although it never explicitly learns a separate reward function nor runs a reinforcement‑learning loop—still recovers the exact optimal policy that would be obtained by the traditional RLHF pipeline.  The key insight is that the reward function is only defined up to an additive term that depends on the prompt, and that by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of a *ratio* of the policy we wish to learn and a fixed reference policy, we obtain a representation that is both universal (any admissible reward class can be written in this form) and tractable (the optimal policy can be written in closed form).  The results below formalise these ideas.

---

### 1.  An Equivalence Relation on Reward Functions  

Consider a pair consisting of a prompt \(x\) and a candidate completion \(y\).  A reward function is any mapping  

\[
r:\;(x,y)\mapsto\mathbb{R}.
\]

Because the Bradley‑Terry model (and all Plackett‑Luce models) depend **only on differences of rewards**, adding a function of the prompt alone does not change any observable preference.  This motivates the following relation.

> **Definition (Reward‑equivalence).**  
> Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are *equivalent*, written \(r\sim r'\), iff there exists a scalar‑valued function \(f\) on prompts such that for every \((x,y)\)
> \[
> r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x).
> \]

The relation is clearly reflexive (\(f\equiv0\)), symmetric (if \(r-r'=f\) then \(r'-r=-f\)), and transitive (sums of additive terms remain additive).  Consequently, the set of all reward functions is partitioned into *equivalence classes*; within a class every member induces exactly the same pairwise preferences.

Intuitively, the additive term \(f(x)\) can be interpreted as a *baseline* that shifts the absolute value of the reward for every candidate belonging to a given prompt, but leaves the ordering of candidates untouched.  This observation lies at the heart of the under‑specification problem of preference models.

---

### 2.  Lemma 1 – Identical Preferences for Equivalent Rewards  

> **Lemma 1.**  
> Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be two reward functions such that \(r\sim r'\).  Under the Bradley‑Terry model,
> \[
> p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)}
> \]
> and the analogous expression using \(r'\) are equal for every pair \((y_1,y_2)\) and prompt \(x\).

*Proof Sketch.*  Substituting \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\) into the numerator and denominator yields a common factor \(\exp\!\bigl(-f(x)\bigr)\) that cancels.  Hence the two probabilities are identical. ∎

The lemma formalises the informal claim that the Bradley‑Terry likelihood is invariant to any additive, prompt‑only shift of the reward.  Consequently, when we fit a reward model by maximising the binary cross‑entropy loss (Eq. (2) in the paper), the optimiser can only hope to recover the *equivalence class* of the true latent reward \(r^{*}\), never a unique scalar field.

---

### 3.  Lemma 2 – Identical Optimal Policies for Equivalent Rewards  

Recall the KL‑constrained optimisation problem that underlies RLHF:

\[
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The solution of this convex optimisation problem is known in closed form (Eq. (4) in the paper):

\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

> **Lemma 2.**  
> If \(r\sim r'\), then the optimal policies \(\pi_{r}\) and \(\pi_{r'}\) obtained from (4) are identical for every prompt \(x\).

*Proof Sketch.*  Insert \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\) into (4).  The factor \(\exp[-f(x)/\beta]\) appears both in the numerator (inside the exponential) and in the partition function \(Z_{r'}(x)\); it cancels, leaving \(\pi_{r'}(y|x)=\pi_{r}(y|x)\). ∎

Thus, the optimisation problem (3) is *well‑defined* on reward equivalence classes: any member of the class yields the same optimal policy.  This fact justifies the change of variables that underpins DPO; we can safely replace the unknown reward by any convenient representative of its class without altering the target policy.

---

### 4.  Theorem 1 – Universal Representation of Reward Classes via \(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\)

We have seen that for a given reward \(r\) the optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) takes the exponential‑tilted form (4).  Conversely, we can *solve* (4) for the reward that would generate a prescribed policy \(\pi\).  This yields the following representation theorem.

> **Theorem 1.**  
> Let \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) be a fixed reference policy and let \(\beta>0\).  
> For any policy \(\pi\) that is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (i.e. \(\pi(y|x)>0\) whenever \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)>0\)), define  
> \[
> r_{\pi}(x,y)\;:=\;\beta\,
> \log\!\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\;+\;\beta\,
> \log Z_{\pi}(x),
> \tag{5}
> \]
> where \(Z_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_{\pi}(x,y')\bigr)\).  
> Then \(r_{\pi}\) belongs to the same equivalence class as any reward \(r\) whose optimal policy is \(\pi\).  Moreover, any reward class admissible under the Bradley‑Terry (or Plackett‑Luce) model admits a representative of the form (5).

*Proof Sketch.*  Starting from the optimality condition (4) we take logarithms, rearrange, and obtain Eq. (5) with an *unknown* additive term \(\beta\log Z_{\pi}(x)\).  Because \(Z_{\pi}(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\), the second term is precisely of the form required by the equivalence relation (Definition).  Hence \(r_{\pi}\) and any true reward \(r^{*}\) that yields the same policy differ by a prompt‑only function, i.e. they are equivalent.  Conversely, given any reward class, pick any representative \(r\) and define \(\pi:=\pi_{r}\) via (4).  Substituting \(\pi\) back into (5) reconstructs a reward that lies in the same class as \(r\).  Thus the mapping \(r\leftrightarrow\pi\) is bijective modulo the equivalence relation. ∎

The theorem tells us that **every admissible reward class can be expressed as a logarithmic ratio between a policy and a reference policy**, up to a normalising constant that is irrelevant for preferences.  Therefore, *parameterising the reward implicitly through the policy* loses no expressive power.

---

### 5.  Implications for Under‑Specification and Identifiability  

The Bradley‑Terry model suffers from *under‑specification*: many reward functions generate exactly the same observable preferences.  This is often called the *identifiability* problem, because fitting Eq. (2) can only recover a reward up to an additive, prompt‑dependent term.  Classical solutions impose an explicit constraint, e.g. forcing the expected reward to be zero over the dataset.

Theorem 1 shows that this under‑specification is **harmless** for the RLHF objective.  Since every member of an equivalence class yields the same optimal policy (Lemma 2), any additional constraint we impose on the reward merely selects a particular representative of the class.  DPO’s re‑parameterisation (5) automatically makes a canonical choice: it sets the additive term to exactly \(\beta\log Z_{\pi}(x)\), which is the log‑partition required for (4) to be a proper probability distribution.  Consequently, DPO sidesteps the need for ad‑hoc normalisation: the policy itself guarantees that the induced reward is already *properly calibrated*.

In practice this means that, when we train a policy by maximising the DPO loss (Eq. (7) in the paper),

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

we are directly performing maximum‑likelihood estimation of the *re‑parameterised* Bradley‑Terry model.  No separate reward network needs to be fitted, and no extra regularisation is required to resolve the under‑specification.

---

### 6.  Why DPO Recovers the Exact Optimal Policy  

Putting the pieces together, the logical chain is:

1. **Preference Data → Bradley‑Terry Likelihood.**  
   Human binary choices are modelled by Eq. (1).  The likelihood depends only on reward differences.

2. **Equivalence Classes.**  
   Lemma 1 tells us that any two rewards differing by a prompt‑only additive term generate the same likelihood.  

3. **Optimal Policy is Invariant.**  
   Lemma 2 guarantees that the KL‑constrained optimisation (3) yields the same optimal policy for any member of the class.

4. **Closed‑Form Mapping.**  
   Theorem 1 provides an explicit bijection between a policy and a *canonical* reward in the class: \(r_{\pi}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) (up to the irrelevant partition term).

5. **Direct Maximisation.**  
   Substituting the canonical reward into the Bradley‑Terry likelihood eliminates the unknown additive term, producing the DPO loss (Eq. (7)).  Optimising this loss over the policy parameters therefore performs *exact* maximum‑likelihood estimation of the policy that would be optimal under the original RLHF objective (3).

Because the DPO objective is precisely the likelihood of the *optimal* policy under the original preference model, any (local) optimum of the DPO loss is also an optimum of the RLHF objective.  Moreover, the gradient expression (Eq. (8) in the paper) shows that DPO naturally weights each training pair by how badly the current policy’s implicit reward mis‑orders the pair, thereby preventing the degenerate behaviour observed in naïve un‑likelihood updates.

In summary, DPO does **not** approximate the RLHF solution—it recovers it exactly, under the same modelling assumptions (Bradley‑Terry or Plackett‑Luce) and the same KL‑constraint parameter \(\beta\).  The only practical simplifications are the removal of a separate reward‑model training stage and the avoidance of any reinforcement‑learning loop; the theoretical guarantees remain untouched.
