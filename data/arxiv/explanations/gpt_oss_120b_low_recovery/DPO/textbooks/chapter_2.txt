### Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) as a Reference Policy  

Large language models are first trained in an unsupervised fashion on massive text corpora.  After this pre‑training stage, a **supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** step is usually performed to bring the model into a regime where it can answer task‑specific queries.  In practice, a collection of high‑quality input–output pairs (for example, prompts together with human‑written completions) is assembled, and the model parameters are updated by maximizing the likelihood of the provided completions.  The result is a conditional distribution  

\[
\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y)\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y')\bigr)},
\]

where \(s_{\theta_{\text{SFT}}}(x,y)\) denotes the final‑layer logits of the fine‑tuned transformer.  This distribution serves as the **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in later stages of the RLHF pipeline.  Because \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) already produces plausible text, it provides a stable starting point for learning to obey human preferences while preventing the fine‑tuned model from drifting into regions of the text space where the subsequently learned reward model would be unreliable.

### Human Preference Collection and the Latent Reward Function  

The central insight of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is that **relative judgments**—“which of two completions is better?”—are often much cheaper to obtain than absolute quality scores or full demonstrations.  To build a preference dataset we first sample, for each prompt \(x\), two candidate completions  

\[
y_{1},y_{2}\sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot\mid x).
\]

Human annotators are then asked to indicate a preference, which we denote  

\[
y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x,
\]

meaning the completion \(y_{w}\) (the *winner*) is judged superior to \(y_{l}\) (the *loser*).  Implicit in these judgments is a **latent reward function**  

\[
r^{*}(x,y),
\]

which assigns a scalar score to any response to prompt \(x\).  The reward is not observed directly; we only see the ordering induced by pairs of responses.  The goal of the reward‑modeling stage is to infer a parameterized approximation \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) that is consistent with the observed preferences.

---

### Bradley‑Terry Model and Its Mathematical Form  

A standard probabilistic model for binary preferences is the **Bradley‑Terry (BT) model**.  Under this model the probability that a human would prefer completion \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) for a given prompt \(x\) is  

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
     {\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Only the **difference** of the two rewards matters; adding a constant that depends solely on the prompt \(x\) leaves the preference probabilities unchanged.  Although more elaborate ranking models such as the Plackett‑Luce distribution exist, the BT formulation is sufficient for binary comparisons and is the one employed throughout the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) work.

### Maximum‑Likelihood Reward Modeling  

Given a static dataset of preferences  

\[
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

we fit a parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}\) by **maximum likelihood** under the BT likelihood.  Treating each preference as a binary classification problem, the negative log‑likelihood loss becomes  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\bigl[
\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)
\bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) is the logistic function.  In practice the reward model is instantiated as the SFT transformer with an additional linear head that outputs a single scalar per token sequence.  For numerical stability and to satisfy the under‑identifiability of the BT model, the rewards are often **centered** so that \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]=0\).

### KL‑Constrained RL Objective and Its Role  

Once a reward model has been learned, the next phase of RLHF seeks a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that **maximizes expected reward while staying close to the reference policy**.  This trade‑off is expressed as the following constrained optimization problem:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}
\;\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;
\beta\,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The first term encourages the policy to generate responses that receive high predicted reward.  The second term penalizes the **Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence** between the new policy and the reference policy, scaled by a coefficient \(\beta>0\).  This KL penalty fulfills three essential purposes:

1. **Stability** – it prevents the policy from moving into parts of the output space where the reward model, trained on SFT data, may be poorly calibrated.  
2. **Diversity** – it maintains the breadth of generations that the original model exhibited, avoiding mode collapse to a single high‑reward answer.  
3. **Safety** – it limits the magnitude of policy updates, which is crucial when the reward model is an imperfect proxy for human judgment.

Equation (3) is the canonical objective used by most RLHF pipelines, including those that employ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or REINFORCE.

---

### Reinforcement‑Learning Algorithms in Practice  

Because text generation involves a discrete, sequential action space, the expectation in (3) cannot be differentiated directly.  Two families of policy‑gradient methods are commonly employed:

* **REINFORCE** – The classic Monte‑Carlo policy gradient estimator uses sampled trajectories \((x,y)\) to compute an unbiased gradient  

  \[
  \nabla_{\theta}\!\bigl[\mathbb{E}[r_{\phi}]\bigr]\approx
  (r_{\phi}(x,y)-b(x))\;\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x),
  \]

  where \(b(x)\) is a baseline (often the average reward) that reduces variance.

* **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** – PPO augments REINFORCE with a **clipping** mechanism that discourages large policy updates.  The surrogate objective typically takes the form  

  \[
  L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[
  \min\bigl(r_{t}(\theta)A_{t},\,
  \operatorname{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{t})\bigr)
  \Bigr]
  -\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),
  \]

  where \(r_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\) and \(A_{t}\) is the advantage estimate derived from the reward model.  The KL term appears both implicitly (through the clipped ratio) and explicitly, enforcing the constraint from (3).

Both algorithms require **sampling** from the current policy at each training step, large‑scale **value‑function** estimation (to compute baselines or advantages), and careful **hyper‑parameter tuning** (learning rates, clipping thresholds, target KL values).  These engineering demands contribute to the complexity and instability frequently observed in RLHF pipelines.
