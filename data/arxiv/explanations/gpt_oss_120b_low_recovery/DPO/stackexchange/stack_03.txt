\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the role of the temperature‑like hyperparameter $\beta$ in DPO, and how does it affect the trade‑off between reward maximization and KL‑regularization?
Question:
Both the RL objective (Eq. 3) and the DPO loss (Eq. 5) contain a scalar $\beta$. Intuitively it seems to control the strength of the KL constraint, but I’m unsure how changing $\beta$ influences the gradient (Eq. 7) and the resulting policy. Does a larger $\beta$ make the update more conservative (closer to the reference model) or more aggressive? How should one choose $\beta$ in practice, and is there a connection to the temperature parameter used in softmax policies?
Answer:
In the DPO framework the scalar β appears in exactly the same place as the temperature‑like coefficient that weights the KL‑penalty in the original RL‑HF objective (Eq. 3).  
Recall that the RL‑HF problem is  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,] -
\beta\; \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr) .
\]

The first term encourages the policy to obtain high reward, while the second term penalises deviation from the reference model π_ref (the SFT model).  
β therefore controls *how much* we are willing to move away from the reference distribution in order to gain reward.

---

### From the RL formulation to DPO

By solving the constrained‑optimization problem analytically one obtains the optimal policy (Eq. 6)

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr) .
\]

Taking logs and rearranging gives the re‑parameterisation used by DPO (Eq. 7)

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) .
\]

Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards, the partition‑function term β log Z(x) cancels when we write the probability of a human preferring y_w over y_l (Eq. 9).  Substituting the expression above yields the DPO loss (Eq. 10)

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\!\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!
\Bigl[
\log\sigma\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]

Thus **β multiplies the log‑ratio of the current policy to the reference policy**.  This factor directly influences the magnitude of the implicit reward  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

which in turn weights the gradient (Eq. 11)

\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=
-\beta\,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))
\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\bigr)
\bigr].
\]

---

### Intuition: what happens when β changes?

* **Small β (≈ 0.05‑0.1).**  
  The factor in front of the log‑ratio is tiny, so the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta\) is close to zero for all completions.  Consequently the sigmoid term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) is near 0.5, i.e. the loss treats every example almost equally.  The gradient is weak, and the update pushes the policy only slightly toward the preferred completions while staying very close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  In practice a small β yields **conservative** updates, low KL‑divergence, but also modest reward improvement.

* **Large β (≈ 1‑5).**  
  The log‑ratio is amplified, so if the current policy already assigns a higher probability to the preferred answer the term \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\) becomes large and the sigmoid σ approaches 1.  The gradient then receives a **large weight** for correctly ranked pairs and a **small weight** for already‑correct pairs, concentrating learning on the mistakes.  The policy can therefore move farther away from the reference distribution, attaining higher reward at the cost of larger KL.  Hence a large β produces **more aggressive** updates.

* **Very large β (≫ 5).**  
  The loss starts to resemble a plain unlikelihood objective that only maximises the ratio \(\pi_\theta(y_w)/\pi_\theta(y_l)\) without any regularisation.  Empirically the authors observed degeneration (over‑confidence in a few modes) when the weighting term is omitted; the β‑scaled sigmoid in DPO precisely prevents that by still keeping a soft dependence on the implicit reward.

So the effect of β is exactly the same trade‑off that the KL‑temperature controls in any KL‑constrained RL formulation: **increase β → favour reward, accept more KL; decrease β → stay nearer to the reference policy**.

---

### Choosing β in practice

The paper reports that a **single value of β works well across tasks** (e.g. β = 0.1 or β = 1 depending on the model size).  A practical recipe is:

1. **Start with β ≈ 0.1** if you want a policy that stays close to the SFT model (useful when the reference already performs reasonably).  
2. **If the learned policy under‑fits the preference data** (low win‑rate, low reward), increase β by a factor of 2–5 and re‑train.  
3. **If you observe mode collapse or drastic KL growth**, reduce β.

Because the DPO loss is a simple cross‑entropy, the only hyper‑parameter that needs tuning is β; no separate KL‑target or reward‑normalisation is required.  This simplicity is one of the main advantages highlighted in the paper.

---

### Relation to the temperature in soft‑max policies

In a standard language model the sampling temperature τ rescales the logits before the soft‑max, i.e.  

\[
\pi(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(z_y/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(z_{y'}/\tau\bigr)} .
\]

β plays a *different* role: it rescales the **log‑ratio between two policies** inside the loss, not the logits of a single policy.  Conceptually both are temperature‑like because they control the sharpness of a distribution, but β is a *regularisation temperature* that determines how strongly the KL term penalises deviation from the reference.  Therefore it is more closely analogous to the KL‑coefficient in KL‑constrained RL than to the sampling temperature used at generation time.

---

**Summary:** β in DPO is the temperature of the KL‑regulariser.  A larger β puts more weight on the preference‑induced reward, allowing the policy to move farther from the reference model and achieve higher reward (more aggressive updates).  A smaller β keeps the policy nearer to the reference (more conservative updates).  In practice β is the only hyper‑parameter that needs to be set, and modest values (≈ 0.1–1) already give a good balance, eliminating the need for separate KL‑targets or reward normalisation.  Its role is analogous to the KL‑coefficient in the original RL‑HF objective, not to the sampling temperature of a softmax policy.
