\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What are the practical implications of the KL‑constrained objective when $\beta\to 0$ or $\beta\to\infty$ in DPO?
Question:
If $\beta$ approaches zero, Eq. 2 suggests the optimal policy becomes proportional to $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r)$, which seems to explode. Conversely, for $\beta\to\infty$, the KL term dominates. How does DPO behave in these extreme regimes? Does the loss become ill‑conditioned, and are there safeguards in the implementation to avoid numerical instability?
Answer:
**Answer**

In DPO the scalar β appears in exactly the same place as in the usual KL‑constrained RLHF objective  

\[
\max _{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]-\beta \; {\rm KL}\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x)\bigr].
\]

Because the optimal policy of this problem can be written in closed form (Eq. (3) of the paper)

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

the behaviour of the algorithm for extreme values of β can be understood directly from this expression and from the DPO loss (Eq. (7))

\[
\mathcal L_{\rm DPO}= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}-
\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}\Bigl)\Bigr].
\]

---

### 1.  The limit β → 0 (very small KL‑penalty)

When β is tiny the factor \(1/\beta\) in the exponential of the optimal policy becomes huge.  
For any pair of completions with different rewards, the term  

\[
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
\]

will dominate the reference probability πref. In the limit β→0 the optimal policy collapses to a *hard* arg‑max over the reward:

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to0]{}\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }y = \displaystyle\arg\max_{y'} r(x,y'),\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

Consequences for DPO:

* **Exploding logits** – the log‑ratio term \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\) stays bounded because it is multiplied by β, but the underlying logits of πθ must become extremely large (positive for the preferred answer, negative for the others) in order for the product \(\beta \log(\cdot)\) to produce a non‑trivial gradient. In practice the optimizer therefore pushes the model’s output distribution toward a *deterministic* policy.
* **Loss conditioning** – the binary‑cross‑entropy inside the loss still sees a finite argument (β·log‑ratio), but the gradient magnitude is proportional to β (see Eq. (8)). As β→0 the gradient shrinks to zero, which can make learning *slow* unless the learning‑rate is scaled up accordingly. The authors point out in the gradient derivation that the factor β multiplies the whole update, so in the extreme the update vanishes.
* **Numerical safeguards** – the implementation clamps the log‑ratio before multiplying by β (see Appendix C.2). Moreover, they keep β in a moderate range (the experiments use β∈{0.05,0.1,1,5}); values below 0.01 are avoided because the loss surface becomes flat and floating‑point under‑flow appears in the exponent when one tries to compute the “policy‑as‑reward’’ term \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\).

Hence, β→0 corresponds to *pure reward maximisation* with essentially no KL regularisation. The optimal policy is the reward‑maximising deterministic policy, but training becomes ill‑conditioned because the gradients are scaled by β and the model must learn extremely sharp probability spikes, which is numerically unstable. In practice one never uses β that small; a small‑β regime is only useful for diagnostic experiments (e.g., to see whether the reward model is capable of distinguishing the two answers).

---

### 2.  The limit β → ∞ (very strong KL‑penalty)

When β grows without bound the exponential term in (3) tends to 1:

\[
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
\;=\;1+ \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}+O\!\bigl(\beta^{-2}\bigr)
\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;1 .
\]

Therefore the optimal policy reduces to the reference policy:

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x).
\]

Interpretation for DPO:

* **The loss becomes a KL‑projection** – the DPO loss (7) simplifies because the term inside the sigmoid approaches 0, and the gradient (8) is multiplied by a very large β. However, since the log‑ratio itself is proportional to \(1/\beta\) (via the optimal‑policy relation), the product β·log‑ratio remains **finite** and actually tends to the difference of rewards divided by β, i.e. it vanishes. Consequently the loss collapses to a constant and the model receives essentially no learning signal; it stays at the reference policy.
* **Stability** – this regime is *safe* numerically. The logits of πθ stay close to those of πref, so no overflow or under‑flow occurs. The implementation therefore allows very large β values (the authors report using β=5 in most experiments) without special tricks; the only practical effect is that the optimizer makes almost no progress, which is exactly the intended behaviour when one wants to keep the policy unchanged.
* **Practical use** – setting β very high is a way to enforce a *conservative* update. If a user observes that the model starts to drift away from the base SFT distribution, increasing β simply reduces the step size of the DPO update (the weighting term σ(⋅) in Eq. (8) approaches ½, and the whole gradient is scaled down). This is the same lever that PPO uses via the KL‑target; DPO’s β plays the identical role.

---

### 3.  Implementation tricks that keep the loss well‑behaved

The paper’s Appendix C.1 lists two simple but crucial safeguards:

1. **Log‑ratio clipping** – before multiplying by β the code computes  
   \[
   \Delta(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}
   \]  
   and clips Δ to a symmetric interval (e.g., [‑10, 10]). This prevents the exponentials in the optimal‑policy definition from overflowing when β is tiny or when the model is already very confident.
2. **Stable sigmoid argument** – the loss uses `torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`, which internally computes the sigmoid in a numerically stable way even for large positive or negative logits. Because the argument to the sigmoid is β·Δ, the clipping of Δ ensures the product never exceeds the range that could cause `exp(-z)` overflow.

Additionally, the training script rescales the learning rate proportionally to β (they multiply the base LR by β). This counters the β factor that appears in the gradient (8) and keeps the *effective* step size roughly constant across different β values.

---

### 4.  Take‑away for practitioners

* **β ≈ 0** → the algorithm behaves like pure *preference‑only* learning; the model will try to assign probability 1 to the preferred answer and 0 to the other. Gradient magnitudes become tiny, training is slow, and numerical overflow is a real risk. In practice you should avoid β below 0.01 unless you also increase the learning rate dramatically and add stronger clipping.
* **β ≈ ∞** → the KL term dominates, the optimal solution is the reference policy, and DPO makes virtually no change. Large β values are harmless but waste computation; they can be used as a sanity check that the implementation indeed respects the KL constraint.
* **Typical regime** – the experiments in the paper use β in the interval \([0.05,5]\). This range yields a well‑conditioned loss: the sigmoid argument stays in a numerically stable region, the gradient magnitude stays useful, and the trade‑off between reward improvement and KL deviation is smooth (see Figure 2 of the paper).

In short, the KL‑constrained objective in DPO behaves exactly as expected at the two extremes: β→0 pushes the policy to a deterministic reward‑maximiser, while β→∞ freezes the policy to the reference. The loss does not become pathological because the β factor appears both outside (scaling the gradient) and inside (scaling the log‑ratio), and the reference implementation adds clipping and learning‑rate rescaling to keep the optimisation well‑conditioned throughout the admissible β range.
