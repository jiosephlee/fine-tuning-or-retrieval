\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does DPO’s single‑stage policy learning compare theoretically to the two‑stage reward‑then‑policy approach in terms of sample efficiency?
Question:
The paper claims that DPO eliminates the need for a separate reward model and RL loop, but I’m curious about the sample complexity: does learning the policy directly from pairwise preferences require more or fewer preference instances than first fitting a reward model and then RL‑optimizing? Is there any theoretical analysis (e.g., bound on the number of preference pairs needed) that explains the observed empirical efficiency?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the classic RL‑HF pipeline the data flow is  

1. collect a set of pairwise human preferences  \(\mathcal D=\{(x,y_w ,y_l)\}_{i=1}^{N}\);  
2. fit a reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) by maximising the Bradley‑Terry likelihood (eq. (2) in the paper);  
3. optimise a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) with RL (usually PPO) on the learned reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (eq. (3)).

Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) replaces steps 2 + 3 with a **single** maximum‑likelihood problem (eq. (7)):
\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)= -\!\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}
\!\Bigl[\,\log\sigma\!\bigl(\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
       -\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

The key theoretical observation (Section 4, Theorem 1) is that any reward function that is **identifiable** under the Bradley‑Terry model can be written as  
\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+c(x),
\]
where \(c(x)\) is a prompt‑dependent constant.  
Because the Bradley‑Terry likelihood depends only on **differences** of rewards, the constant drops out, so the *entire* class of admissible reward functions is represented by some policy \(\pi\). Optimising the policy directly therefore **does not restrict the expressivity** of the reward model: for every reward function that could be learned in step 2 there exists a policy that yields exactly the same preference distribution (Lemma 2). Hence, from a statistical‑learning viewpoint DPO and the two‑stage pipeline are solving the *same* estimation problem.

### Sample‑complexity consequences

The statistical sample complexity of learning a Bradley‑Terry model from i.i.d. preference pairs is well‑studied in the ranking‑learning literature.  Under mild regularity conditions (bounded logits, a well‑specified model, and a fixed vocabulary size \(|\mathcal V|\)) the maximum‑likelihood estimator for the reward parameters satisfies a **parametric** convergence rate
\[
\big\| \hat r - r^{*}\big\|_{2}=O_{p}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}\Bigr),
\]
where \(d\) is the number of trainable parameters (the dimension of \(\phi\) or \(\theta\)) and \(N\) is the number of preference pairs.  This bound follows directly from standard asymptotic theory for exponential‑family models: the Bradley‑Terry likelihood is a member of the canonical exponential family, its Fisher information is non‑degenerate, and the MLE is therefore \(\sqrt N\)‑consistent.

Because DPO is *exactly* the same MLE but expressed in the policy space, the same bound applies to the **policy parameters** \(\theta\).  In other words, the number of preference pairs required to achieve a given error \(\varepsilon\) scales as
\[
N = O\!\Bigl(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigr).
\]
No extra data are needed to estimate an intermediate reward function; the constant factors are even smaller because DPO eliminates the variance‑inflating step of sampling from the current policy during RL (the “exploration” noise that PPO needs to estimate a policy gradient).  Empirically this is reflected in Figure 2 of the paper: DPO reaches the same (or higher) reward‑KL frontier with roughly half as many optimisation steps as PPO, precisely because each gradient step uses the *full* supervised signal from a preference pair instead of a noisy Monte‑Carlo estimate of the reward.

### Why the two‑stage method does not improve sample efficiency

One might hope that first fitting a reward model on a *large* set of pairs and then re‑using it for many RL updates would amortise the data cost.  In practice the RL phase still needs **fresh** reward evaluations for each generated trajectory, and the KL‑penalty term introduces an additional optimisation target that must be estimated through on‑policy samples.  The variance of these on‑policy samples dominates the statistical error unless a very large batch of roll‑outs is collected at every PPO step.  Consequently the overall sample complexity of the two‑stage pipeline is
\[
N_{\text{2‑stage}} = N_{\text{reward}} + \underbrace{T\cdot B}_{\text{RL samples}},
\]
where \(T\) is the number of PPO epochs and \(B\) the batch size of generated completions.  Even if \(N_{\text{reward}}\) is modest, the second term often dwarfs it, especially for language models where each sample is a full sequence.

DPO avoids the second term entirely: the gradient (eq. (8)) is an **expectation over the fixed dataset** \(\mathcal D\).  No new model‑generated examples are required once the preference set is collected, so the total number of preference pairs that must be annotated is exactly the MLE requirement \(O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\).

### Putting it together

* **Theoretical guarantee** – DPO learns the same optimal policy as the RL‑HF objective, and because the underlying Bradley‑Terry likelihood is a regular exponential‑family model, the standard \(\sqrt N\)‑consistency result gives a bound \(N = O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\) on the number of pairwise annotations needed to achieve error \(\varepsilon\).

* **Practical sample efficiency** – The bound is **identical** for the two‑stage method’s reward‑learning stage, but the RL stage adds extra stochastic samples that do **not** contribute to reducing the estimation error of the underlying reward; they only add variance.  Hence DPO requires *fewer* total preference pairs to reach the same performance, which is exactly what the empirical frontiers in the paper demonstrate.

* **Intuition** – Think of the reward model as an intermediate “latent variable”.  By re‑parameterising the latent variable as a log‑ratio of policy probabilities (eq. (5)), DPO collapses the two inference steps into one.  The statistical cost of inferring the latent variable is paid once, and the policy automatically inherits the optimality properties, so no extra data are spent on a second inference problem.

In short, the theory in the DPO paper shows that learning a policy directly from pairwise preferences is **statistically as powerful** as first learning a reward and then RL‑optimising, while avoiding the additional sampling noise of the RL loop.  Consequently the number of human‑annotated preference pairs needed is the same asymptotic order \(O(d/\varepsilon^{2})\), but the **constant factor** is smaller for DPO, which explains the observed empirical superiority in sample efficiency.
