# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters: Preserving Knowledge in Large Models  

## Introduction  

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or modern vision transformers—are incredibly powerful, but training them from scratch is often out of reach for most practitioners. The practical solution is *finetuning*: we start from a pretrained checkpoint and adapt it to a downstream task (sentiment analysis, image classification, style‑controlled generation, etc.).  

Most finetuning recipes fall into three families:  

* **Model finetuning** – directly update a subset of the original weights.  
* **Adapters** – add small trainable modules that sit alongside the frozen backbone.  
* **Prompt tuning** – prepend learnable tokens to the input.  

All three are popular, but they differ in how aggressively they disturb the pretrained knowledge. In this post we focus on **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a principled approach that keeps the *geometric relationships* between neurons intact while still giving the model enough flexibility to learn new tasks. We'll unpack why preserving angles matters, contrast OFT with the widely used LoRA and adapter methods, and give an intuitive feel for the underlying math—all without assuming a PhD in linear algebra.

## The Geometry Behind a Neural Layer  

Consider a single linear layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). Each column of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is a *neuron* (or feature direction) that maps an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to an output scalar. Two neurons \(\mathbf{w}_i\) and \(\mathbf{w}_j\) have a **pairwise angle**  

\[
\theta_{ij}= \arccos\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\;\|\mathbf{w}_j\|} \right),
\]

which encodes how similar their representations are. During pretraining, the model discovers a whole constellation of such angles that captures linguistic or visual regularities.

If we naïvely update \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with gradient descent, those angles can drift arbitrarily. This *drift* may erase the useful structure learned from billions of tokens or images, leading to poorer generalization on the new task.

## Orthogonal Transformations: Rotating Without Stretching  

An **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\). Geometrically, multiplying by \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates (or reflects) vectors but never changes their lengths or the angles between them:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{b} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{b} \rangle.
\]

OFT leverages this property by reparameterizing the finetuned weight matrix as  

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top},
\]

where we keep the original pretrained matrix frozen and only learn the orthogonal “rotator” \(\mathbf{R}\). In other words, every neuron of the layer is transformed by exactly the same rotation. Because angles are preserved, the *relative geometry* of all neurons stays unchanged—pretrained knowledge is retained even as the whole constellation swings to better align with the downstream data.

### Zero‑Initialization for Safety  

Training starts from \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) (the identity matrix), so initially nothing changes. As learning progresses, \(\mathbf{R}\) gradually rotates the weight space; however, because orthogonality forces a pure rotation, the model never “blows up” or forgets its original structure.

## How OFT Differs from LoRA and Adapters  

| Method | Parameter budget | How it updates weights | What it preserves |
|--------|------------------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| **LoRA** (Low‑Rank Adaptation) | Low (adds two low‑rank matrices) | Adds a low‑rank correction: \(\Delta\!\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\) | No explicit geometric constraint; can change singular values arbitrarily |
| **Adapters** | Low–moderate (small bottleneck modules) | Inserts tiny feed‑forward blocks; original weights stay frozen | Indirectly preserves knowledge but adds new nonlinear pathways |
| **OFT** | Moderate (full orthogonal matrix → many parameters; later reduced by structure) | Multiplies original weights by a shared orthogonal matrix: \(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\) | Exactly preserves pairwise angles and spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) |

- **LoRA** introduces new directions in weight space but may alter the *spectral norm* (largest singular value), sometimes hurting training stability.  
- **Adapters** keep the backbone untouched but add extra compute at inference time.  
- **OFT** keeps computation identical at inference (no extra modules) and guarantees that any rotation leaves the singular values unchanged—this often translates to smoother training and better generalization.

## Making OFT Practical: From Full Orthogonal Matrices to Butterflies  

A naïve orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) needs \(O(d^{2})\) parameters—prohibitive for large models. The original OFT paper mitigates this by forcing a block‑diagonal structure (splitting dimensions into independent groups). While this cuts parameters, it also limits expressivity because each block can only rotate its own subspace.

The recent follow‑up work introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**. The key idea: factorize a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of *sparse* orthogonal matrices arranged in a **butterfly pattern**, inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. 

- Each butterfly factor has only \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries.
- Stacking \(\log d\) such factors yields an overall dense rotation using only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.
- Despite being sparse internally, the composition can transmit information between any input and output coordinate—so angles are still preserved globally.

The butterfly factorization not only restores full expressivity but also injects an inductive bias reminiscent of classic transforms (Fourier, Hadamard), which empirically helps generalization.

## Why Preserving Angles Helps Retain Pretrained Knowledge  

1. **Spectral stability** – Orthogonal updates leave singular values untouched, keeping gradients well‑behaved.
2. **Metric consistency** – Many downstream tasks rely on similarity metrics built on inner products; rotating all neurons together maintains these metrics.
3. **Regularization effect** – The orthogonality constraint restricts the hypothesis space to rotations rather than arbitrary deformations, reducing overfitting risk.

Empirical studies reported in BOFT’s paper show consistent gains over LoRA and adapters across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models—especially when parameter budgets are tight.

## Takeaways  

- **Orthogonal Finetuning** treats adaptation as a *global rotation* of each layer’s weight space, guaranteeing that neuron‑to‑neuron angles remain unchanged.  
- This geometric preservation translates into stable training dynamics and better retention of pretrained knowledge compared with additive low‑rank or adapter approaches.  
- By factorizing rotations with butterfly structures (BOFT), we achieve parameter efficiency comparable to LoRA while keeping OFT’s theoretical benefits.  

If you’re fine‑tuning massive models and care about both performance and memory footprint, give orthogonal finetuning—or its butterfly‑enhanced variant—a try. It offers a clean mathematical guarantee that your model’s learned representations stay geometrically coherent while still learning what your new task demands.



# From Block‑Diagonal to Butterfly: Making Orthogonal Finetuning Parameter‑Efficient  

*In this post we unpack why the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) blows up in parameters, how the authors turned the problem into an “information‑transmission” puzzle, and why borrowing the butterfly graph from the Fast Fourier Transform lets us shrink the trainable budget from \(O(d^{2})\) to \(O(d\log d)\).*  

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning needs a rethink  

Finetuning massive foundation models without blowing up memory or compute is now a central challenge. OFT offers a principled alternative to low‑rank methods like LoRA: it **multiplies** each pretrained linear layer \(\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\),

\[
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R \mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x,
\]

preserving pairwise angles between neurons and therefore keeping much of the pretraining knowledge intact.  

The catch? A full dense orthogonal matrix has \(d^{2}\) entries, far too many to store or optimise when \(d\) is in the thousands (as in vision transformers or large language models). The original OFT paper sidestepped this by **forcing \(\mathbf R\) to be block‑diagonal**, i.e.

\[
\mathbf R = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}),\qquad \mathbf R_{i}\in\mathbb R^{b\times b},\; br=d,
\]

which reduces parameters to \(O(bd)\). Unfortunately, this sparsity pattern is quite rigid: each block transforms a disjoint subset of neuron dimensions independently, limiting expressiveness and preventing the matrix from ever becoming truly dense.

So the question becomes: **Can we obtain a dense orthogonal transformation while keeping the parameter count low?**  

---

## 2. Recasting the problem as information transmission  

Think of \(\mathbf R\) as a *connectivity matrix*: entry \(R_{ij}\neq0\) means information can travel from input dimension \(j\) to output dimension \(i\). If we factor \(\mathbf R\) into a product of sparse matrices,

\[
\mathbf R = \mathbf B_{m}\,\mathbf B_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf B_{1},
\]

each \(\mathbf B_{k}\) can be seen as a “layer” in a **grid‑structured graph** with \(d\) nodes per level and \(m+1\) levels overall. An edge exists between two adjacent levels precisely when the corresponding entry in \(\mathbf B_{k}\) is non‑zero.  

For the final product \(\mathbf R\) to be dense, **every source node (level 0)** must have at least one directed path to **every sink node (level \(m\))**. At the same time we want as few *trainable* edges as possible because each non‑zero entry that isn’t forced by orthogonality becomes a learnable parameter.

Two desiderata emerge:

| Goal | What it means in the graph |
|------|----------------------------|
| **Dense connectivity** | All source–sink pairs are connected via some path |
| **Minimum free edges** | Use the smallest possible number of trainable edges while still allowing orthogonal factors |

A naïve dense factorisation would use a single full matrix (\(O(d^{2})\) edges). The block‑diagonal OFT achieves sparsity but fails dense connectivity. We need a middle ground.

---

## 3. Butterfly graphs – the FFT’s secret weapon  

The Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) repeatedly mixes pairs of data points in a pattern known as a **butterfly**. Graphically, each stage connects each node to exactly one partner, and across \(\log_2 d\) stages every node can reach every other node.  

Key properties:

* **Only \(O(d)\) edges per stage**, because each node links to one partner.
* **\(O(d\log d)\) total edges** after \(\log d\) stages—far fewer than \(d^{2}\).
* The resulting product of stage matrices is dense (the FFT matrix itself is dense).

If we enforce *orthogonality* on every stage matrix, we obtain an **orthogonal butterfly factorisation**: a product of sparse orthogonal matrices whose overall effect is a dense orthogonal transformation.

### 3.1 Formalising a butterfly factor  

For a power‑of‑two size \(k=2^{p}\), define a butterfly factor

\[
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each \(\mathbf d_i\in \mathbb R^{k/2}\). The factor contains only **four diagonal blocks**, each of size \(k/2\times k/2\). By choosing these diagonals via a Cayley transform (or simple 2‑D rotations), we make the entire factor orthogonal.

A full butterfly matrix for dimension \(d=2^{N}\) is built recursively:

```
B(d) = Ȃ(d,d) · [ B₁(d/2)   0
                 0       B₂(d/2) ]
```

where Ȃ(d,k) is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors of size k. Repeating this construction yields roughly \(\log_2 d\) stages, each contributing only \(O(d)\) trainable entries.

### 3.2 From butterflies to BOFT  

The authors call the resulting finetuning scheme **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**. The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{
    \mathbf z = (\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}}_{\displaystyle \mathbf R(m,b)}\, \mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}
    \mathbf x},
}
\]

where each \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) is an orthogonal butterfly component (the superscript \(b\) denotes optional block size > 2).  

- When \(m=1,\; b=d\): we recover the original OFT with an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix.  
- When \(m=1,\; b<d\): BOFT collapses to block‑diagonal OFT.  
- When \(m=\log_2(d/b),\, b=2\): we obtain a *dense* orthogonal matrix using only \(O(d\,log d)\) parameters.

Thus BOFT interpolates smoothly between the two extremes, offering an adjustable trade‑off between expressivity and parameter budget.

---

## 4. Parameter savings in concrete terms  

| Method | Parameters for one layer (size \(d\times n\)) |
|--------|----------------------------------------------|
| Full OFT (dense \(\mathbf R\)) | \(\Theta(d^{2})\) |
| Block‑diagonal OFT (block size \(b\)) | \(\Theta(bd)\) |
| BOFT (\(m,\;b)\) | \(\displaystyle \frac{b-1}{2}\, d\, m = O(d\,log d)\) when using classic butterfly (\(b=2,\; m=\log_2 d)\). |

For a typical transformer layer with \(d=1024\), full OFT would need over one million trainable values per layer, while BOFT needs only ~\(10\,000$–$20\,000$, i.e. two orders of magnitude less.

---

## 5. Why does this work? Intuition beyond counting  

1. **Dense connectivity with few edges** – The butterfly graph guarantees that any input dimension can influence any output after enough stages, satisfying our “dense connectivity” desideratum without needing all possible edges.

2. **Orthogonal constraints prune parameters automatically** – An orthogonal sparse factor must be *full rank*, which forces exactly one non‑zero per row/column pair in each stage. Those non‑zeros are *not* independent; they lie on circles defined by Cayley transforms, further regularising learning.

3. **Implicit inductive bias** – Butterfly structures can represent classic fast linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT). Consequently, BOFT inherits biases that favour globally mixing information—a property that has empirically improved generalisation on vision and language tasks.

4. **Spectral stability** – Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves singular values untouched, preserving the spectral norm of pretrained weights—a known stabiliser for deep training.

---

## 6. Practical tricks introduced in BOFT  

- **Identity initialization**: All butterfly components start as identity matrices (Cayley’s skew‑symmetric part set to zero), guaranteeing zero change at the start of finetuning.
- **Multiplicative Dropout**: Instead of zeroing activations (as in usual Dropout), BOFT randomly replaces selected butterfly components—or even individual diagonal blocks within them—with identity matrices during training. This stochastic regularisation mitigates overfitting while respecting multiplicative updates.
- **Block‑butterfly extensions**: By allowing each scalar entry in a factor to become a small \(b\times b\) matrix, one can trade additional expressivity for slightly more parameters when needed.

---

## 7. Empirical takeaways (brief)

Across large vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models, BOFT consistently outperformed LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT under comparable parameter budgets:

- **Higher accuracy / lower perplexity** on downstream classification and generation tasks.
- **Faster convergence** thanks to preserved spectral norms.
- **No inference latency penalty**: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal product can be folded into the original weight matrix, leaving the forward pass unchanged.

These results suggest that orthogonal finetuning—once thought too costly—can be made practical with butterfly factorisation.

---

## 8. Closing thoughts  

The journey from block‑diagonal matrices to butterfly factorizations demonstrates how rethinking an algorithmic bottleneck through a different lens—in this case, *information transmission on graphs*—can unlock massive efficiency gains. By marrying concepts from signal processing (FFT butterflies), graph theory (dense connectivity with few edges), and geometry (orthogonal manifolds), BOFT offers a sleek, theoretically grounded tool for parameter‑efficient finetuning of today’s gigantic foundation models.

If you’re working on adapting large models but hate blowing up your GPU memory, give Orthogonal Butterfly a try—you’ll likely get better generalisation for far fewer trainable weights, all without any extra inference cost. Happy finetuning!



# The Mathematics Behind BOFT: Orthogonal Butterflies Explained  

In recent years, adapting massive foundation models without retraining them from scratch has become a hot research topic. One promising direction is **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, which updates a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ by left‑multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ so that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. While OFT enjoys strong generalization, its naïve implementation requires $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable parameters for a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix—clearly infeasible for modern large‑scale models.

**BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning)** solves this dilemma by factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of *sparse* orthogonal matrices organised in a **butterfly structure**. The result is an expressive finetuning layer that needs only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable parameters. In this post we unpack the core mathematics that makes BOFT work:

1. **Cayley parameterisation of orthogonal matrices**  
2. **Butterfly factorisation and its recursive definition**  
3. **Why BOFT is strictly more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT**  

Along the way we use simple diagrams and step‑by‑step examples to keep the intuition clear.

---

## 1. From Orthogonal Updates to Cayley Parameterisation  

An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}$. Directly learning $\mathbf{R}$ would demand $d^{2}$ free entries, far too many for finetuning. The **Cayley transform** offers a compact re‑parameterisation:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\qquad\text{with}\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}.
\]

* $\mathbf{Q}$ is **skew‑symmetric**, i.e. $q_{ij} = -q_{ji}$ and $q_{ii}=0$.  
* The number of independent elements in $\mathbf{Q}$ is $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$, half of a full matrix.  
* When $\mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{0}$, the transform yields $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}$, giving us the standard *zero‑initialisation* used in finetuning.

With the Cayley transform we can treat the entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ as our learnable parameters, guaranteeing orthogonality throughout training without needing a costly projection step.

---

## 2. Butterfly Factorisation: Building Dense Orthogonal Matrices from Sparse Pieces  

### 2.1 Why “butterfly”?  

The classic Cooley–Tukey FFT repeatedly mixes pairs of signals in a pattern that looks like a butterfly when drawn as a graph (two inputs crossing to two outputs). This pattern yields an **efficient information transmission**: after $\log_{2} d$ stages each input can reach every output using only $d\log_{2} d$ non‑zero connections.

### 2.2 The elementary butterfly factor  

For an even integer $k$, define a **butterfly factor** $\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)$ as

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\quad 
\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

Each block is diagonal, so the whole factor has exactly $k$ non‑zero entries (one per row/column). By learning the vectors $\mathbf{d}_{i}$ (or better yet their Cayley‑parameterised skew versions) we can make each factor orthogonal while preserving sparsity.

### 2.3 Recursion to full size  

Let $d=2^{N}$ for convenience. A **butterfly matrix** $\mathbf{B}(d)$ is constructed recursively:

1. **Base case ($d=2$):**  
   \[
   \mathbf{B}(2)= \begin{bmatrix}
   \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
   \sin\theta & \phantom{-}\cos\theta
   \end{bmatrix},
   \] 
   which is just a $2\times2$ rotation (orthogonal).

2. **Recursive step ($d>2$):**  
   Partition the $d$ coordinates into two halves of size $d/2$. Apply independent butterfly matrices to each half and then mix them with a *butterfly component*  

   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
   \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf B^{F}(k),\,\dotsc ,\, \mathbf B^{F}(k)}_{d/k}\bigr),
   \]
   where $k$ is the block size at this stage (e.g., $k=d/2,\, d/4,\dotsc , 2$). The overall matrix becomes  

   \[
   \boxed{\;
   \mathbf B(d)=
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \begin{bmatrix}
   \mathbf B(d/2) & 0\\[4pt]
   0 & \mathbf B(d/2)
   \end{bmatrix}
   }.
   \]

Because each $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ contains only $d$ non‑zero entries, the *total* number of trainable elements after all $\log_{2} d$ stages is  

\[
\underbrace{\frac12(b-1)d}_{\text{sparse blocks}}\times\underbrace{\log_{2}\!\frac{2d}{b}}_{\text{stages}}
 =  \mathcal O(d\log d)
\]
when we set the block size $b=2$. The product of these sparse orthogonal components yields a *dense* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$, suitable for OFT.

### 2.4 A tiny example ( $d=4$ )  

| Stage | Factor (non‑zero pattern) | Effect on information flow |
|------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| 1 (k=4) | $\tilde{\mathbf B}(4,4) = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\!a&0\\0&b\end{submatrix},\,\begin{submatrix}c&0\\0&d\end{submatrix}\bigr)$ | Each of the four inputs stays within its own pair |
| 2 (k=2) | $\tilde{\mathbf B}(4,2)$ mixes $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$ pairs (the classic “butterfly” crossing) | After this stage every input can affect every output |

Multiplying the two stages produces a full $4\times4$ orthogonal matrix despite each stage having only four trainable numbers.

---

## 3. BOFT vs. Block‑Diagonal OFT: A Formal Expressivity Argument  

### 3.1 What does “expressivity” mean here?  

Given a target orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R^{*}\!\in O(d)$ we ask: how many trainable parameters are needed to *exactly* represent it? If a parameterisation can express *all* matrices in $O(d)$ then it is *universal*. If it can only express a proper subset, we compare the size of that subset.

### 3.2 Block‑diagonal OFT  

OFT with block size $b$ forces

\[
\mathbf R = 
\operatorname{diag}
(\underbrace{\mathbf R_{1}}_{b\times b},
~\underbrace{\dotsc}_{r-1},
~\underbrace{\mathbf R_{r}}_{b\times b}),
\qquad rb=d.
\]

Each block $\mathbf R_{i}$ is an independent orthogonal matrix with $\frac12 b(b-1)$ free parameters, totalling  

\[
p_{\text{BD}} = r\, \tfrac12 b(b-1)= \tfrac12 d(b-1).
\]

However, any matrix whose off‑block entries are non‑zero cannot be represented; thus the hypothesis class misses most of $O(d)$.

### 3.3 BOFT’s richer class  

BOFT builds

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf R(m,b)=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}
    \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}},
    \qquad m=\log_{2}{(2d/b)},
}
\]

where each factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component with block size $b$. The total number of trainable scalars equals

\[
p_{\text{BOFT}} = 
\tfrac12(b-1)\, d\, m
 = 
\tfrac12(b-1)\, d\, \log_{2}\!\tfrac {2d}{b}
 = 
 {\cal O}(d\log d).
\]

Even though $p_{\text{BOFT}} > p_{\text{BD}}$ for large $b$, BOFT’s *structure* allows dense connectivity while using only linearithmic parameters.

### 3.4 Theorem – BOFT subsumes OFT and exceeds its expressivity  

> **Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT).**  
> Let $\mathcal O_{\text{BD}}(b)$ denote the set of orthogonal matrices realizable by block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and let $\mathcal O_{\text {BF}}(m,b)$ denote those realizable by BOFT$(m,b)$. Then for any fixed $b$ and any feasible $m$,  
> $$ 
>    \mathcal O_{\text {BD}}(b)\subsetneq \mathcal O_{\text {BF}}(m,b).
> $$  
> Moreover, taking $m=\log_2 d$ and $b=2$, compositions of butterfly matrices of the form  
> $$ 
>    \prod_{i=1}^{d-1}
>        \bigl(\,\bm B_{i,1}(d)\,\bm B^{\top}_{i,2}(d)\,\bigr)
> $$  
> span the entire orthogonal group $O(d)$.

**Sketch of proof.**  
*Every butterfly component* is itself orthogonal and contains at least one non‑zero entry in each row and column; therefore its product can generate any permutation of rows/columns combined with rotations inside each pair—precisely what the FFT mixing does. Adding more stages multiplies these permutations, eventually yielding any element of the symmetric group on $d$ symbols with accompanying rotations; this combination generates all orthogonal matrices because any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of Givens rotations (pairwise planar rotations). Block‑diagonal OFT lacks cross‑block permutations entirely, so it cannot realise even simple swaps between blocks, establishing strict containment.

Thus BOFT strictly expands the hypothesis space while still keeping parameter counts subquadratic.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together: A Step‑by‑Step BOFT Finetune

Suppose we want to finetune a linear layer with pretrained weight $\mathbf W^{0}\in \mathbb R^{d\times n}$. The BOFT forward pass looks like:

1. **Initialize** all butterfly components as identity via zero skew matrices in their Cayley transforms – guarantees start at exact pretrained behavior.
2. **Compute** the composite orthogonal matrix:  
   $$ 
   \displaystyle
   \mathbf R = 
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
        (\!\,\underbrace{\bigl(\!\,\mathbb I + \widehat{\!\!\! Q_i}\!\,\bigr)}_{\text{sparse}}
        (\!\,\mathbb I - ̂ Q_i)^{-1}),
        $$  
   where each $\widehat Q_i$ embeds a small skew-symmetric block into its corresponding butterfly factor.
3. **Apply** left multiplication:  
   $$ 
   \boldsymbol z = (\,\mathbf R\, {\bf W}^{0})^{\!\top}{\bf x}.
   $$  
4. Optionally use **multiplicative dropout**: randomly replace whole butterfly components or inner blocks by identity matrices during training; this regularises without breaking orthogonality.

The cost per forward/backward pass grows modestly because we multiply by $m=\mathcal O(\log d)$ sparse factors instead of one dense matrix.

---

## 5. Visual Intuition – Information Transmission Graphs  

A helpful way to see why butterflies succeed is to draw a *grid graph* with layers representing intermediate vectors after each factor multiplication:

```
Level 0      Level 1      Level 2      …      Level m
 (x_1) ——► (y_1) ——► …               —► (z_1)
 (x_2) ——► (y_2) ——► …               —► (z_2)
 …
 (x_d) ——► (y_d) ——► …               —► (z_d)
```

Each edge corresponds to a non‑zero entry in the current sparse factor; edges only connect adjacent levels but are arranged so that after all levels every source node reaches every sink node—exactly what “dense connectivity” means mathematically. In contrast, block‑diagonal OFT would have edges confined within disjoint sub‑grids, never crossing blocks.

---

## 6. Takeaways  

| Concept | Key Formula / Idea | Parameter Cost |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Cayley transform** | $\displaystyle (\mathbb I+\!Q)(\mathbb I-\!Q)^{-1},~ Q^\top=-Q$ | $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$ before sparsity |
| **Butterfly factor** | Diagonal blocks glued together → Eq.(1) | Exactly $k$ non‑zeros per factor |
| **Recursive butterfly matrix** | Product over log‐levels → Eq.(recursive) | $\mathcal O(d\,m)$ where $m=\log_2(2d/b)$ |
| **BOFT expressivity** | Theorem 3 ⇒ spans all of \(O(d)\) when enough factors are used | $\boxed{\mathcal O(d\,\log d)}$ vs.\;block‐diag OFT’s $\mathcal O(db)$ |

BOFT therefore delivers three practical advantages:

1. **Parameter efficiency:** Only linearithmic learnable scalars even for very wide layers.
2. **Preserved geometry:** Orthogonality guarantees unchanged spectral norm and angle preservation.
3. **Higher expressivity:** Butterfly factorizations can approximate any orthogonal transformation, unlike fixed block‑diagonal patterns.

---

## 7. Final Thoughts  

The mathematics behind BOFT bridges classic signal processing (FFT butterflies), graph theory (information transmission on grids), and modern deep learning (parameter‑efficient finetuning). By treating an orthogonal update as a structured sparse product—rather than a monolithic dense matrix—we obtain an elegant trade‑off between *capacity* and *compactness*. The result is a versatile finetuning tool that works across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion generators while staying lightweight enough for real-world deployment.

If you’re curious to experiment yourself:

```python
# Pseudocode sketch
def cayley(q):
    I = torch.eye(q.shape[0])
    return (I + q) @ torch.inverse(I - q)

def butterfly_layer(x, params):
    # params hold skew-symmetric blocks for each stage
    R = torch.eye(x.shape[-1])
    for stage in params:
        Q = embed_skew(stage)          # put small skew blocks into full-size Q
        R = cayley(Q) @ R
    return x @ (R @ W0).t()
```

Replace `embed_skew` with your own mapping from learned vectors to sparse skew matrices following Eq.(1). With identity initialisation you'll recover exactly the pretrained model before any update—just like standard finetuning—while gaining all the theoretical benefits discussed above.

Happy experimenting!

# Practical Guide: How to Implement Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning in Your Projects  

In recent months **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** has emerged as a compelling alternative to classic PEFT methods such as LoRA or adapter tuning. BOFT builds on the orthogonal finetuning (OFT) paradigm, but replaces the dense orthogonal matrix with a **butterfly‑factorized** representation. The result is a parameter‑efficient update that still enjoys the stability and generalisation guarantees of orthogonal transformations, while adding virtually no inference latency.

This tutorial walks you through a concrete implementation of BOFT in PyTorch, shows how to hook it into the Hugging Face Transformers ecosystem, and demonstrates three practical steps that most users need:

1. **Initializing the butterfly components** (the sparse orthogonal factors).  
2. **Applying multiplicative dropout** to avoid over‑fitting during training.  
3. **Merging the learned orthogonal matrix back into the pretrained model** so that inference runs at the speed of a vanilla model.

All code snippets are self‑contained and can be dropped into existing training pipelines with minimal changes.

---  

## 1. Background – Why Butterfly?

A naïve orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable parameters, which defeats the purpose of parameter‑efficient finetuning. BOFT solves this by representing an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of $m$ **butterfly components**:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,1)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,2)}\dots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{$m$ sparse orthogonal factors}},
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)} = \mathbf{I}_{d}.
\]

Each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are **butterfly factors** (Equation 1 in the paper). When $m=\log_2 d$ and each block is $2\times2$, the total number of trainable parameters drops to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$—a huge saving for vision or language models where $d$ can be several thousand.

Because every factor is orthogonal, the product $\mathbf{R}$ remains orthogonal by construction. In practice we enforce orthogonality through a **Cayley transform** applied to a skew‑symmetric trainable matrix $\mathbf{Q}$:

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}.
\]

The same pattern is used for each butterfly component.

## 3. Implementing a Butterfly Component  

Below we implement a generic **ButterflyBlock** that can operate on any hidden dimension `d` and block size `b`. The block size controls how many dimensions are grouped together within each butterfly factor; `b=2` yields the classic Cooley–Tukey topology, while larger `b` reduces depth (`m`) at the cost of slightly more parameters.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import math

def cayley_transform(skew):
    """Cayley transform: (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}, where Q is skew‑symmetric."""
    I = torch.eye(skew.size(-1), device=skew.device, dtype=skew.dtype)
    return torch.linalg.solve(I - skew, I + skew)

class ButterflyFactor(nn.Module):
    """
    Single butterfly factor B^F(k) = [[diag(d1), diag(d2)],
                                    [diag(d3), diag(d4)]]
    where k = 2 * half.
    """
    def __init__(self, half):
        super().__init__()
        self.half = half                # = k/2
        # Trainable vectors that will become diagonal matrices after Cayley
        self.d1 = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(half))
        self.d2 = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(half))
        self.d3 = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(half))
        self.d4 = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(half))

    def forward(self):
        # Build 2*half x 2*half matrix with diagonal blocks
        diag = torch.diag_embed
        top  = torch.cat([diag(self.d1), diag(self.d2)], dim=-1)
        bot  = torch.cat([diag(self.d3), diag(self.d4)], dim=-1)
        B   = torch.cat([top, bot], dim=-2)          # shape (k,k)

        # Convert each 2x2 sub‑block into an orthogonal block via Cayley.
        # For simplicity we treat the whole matrix as skew‑symmetric:
        k = B.size(-1)
        # Create a skew‑symmetric matrix from diagonal entries (zero off‑diag => rotation only)
        Q = torch.zeros(k, k, device=B.device, dtype=B.dtype)
        Q += torch.diag_embed(B)                     # fill diagonal with trainable params
        Q = Q - Q.transpose(-2,-1)                   # make skew‑symmetric
        return cayley_transform(Q)
```

### 3.1 Stacking Factors into a Full Butterfly Matrix  

A full BOFT layer consists of `m` factors with decreasing block sizes (`k = d, d/2, d/4, …`). The following class builds that stack automatically:

```python
class OrthogonalButterfly(nn.Module):
    """
    R(m,b) = Π_{i=1}^m Ȃ_i   where each Ȃ_i is an orthogonal butterfly component.
    - d: input dimension (hidden size of linear layer)
    - m: number of factors (default log2(d) -> dense R)
    - b: block size for the first factor (must divide d)
    """
    def __init__(self, d, m=None, b=2):
        super().__init__()
        assert d % b == 0, "b must divide d"
        self.d = d
        self.b = b
        self.m = m if m is not None else int(math.log2(d // b)) + 1

        # generate list of block sizes: b * 2^{i-1}
        block_sizes = [b * (2 ** i) for i in range(self.m)]
        self.factors = nn.ModuleList([
            self._build_factor(k) for k in reversed(block_sizes)
        ])

    def _build_factor(self, k):
        """Create one butterfly component for block size k."""
        half = k // 2
        # Each component is a block‑diagonal matrix of many ButterflyFactor blocks
        num_blocks = self.d // k
        return nn.ModuleList([ButterflyFactor(half) for _ in range(num_blocks)])

    def forward(self):
        R = torch.eye(self.d, device=next(self.parameters()).device)

        for factor_list in self.factors:   # iterate from coarse → fine scale
            # Assemble block‑diagonal matrix from its ButterflyFactor pieces
            blocks = [blk() for blk in factor_list]          # each shape (k,k)
            B = torch.block_diag(*blocks)                    # shape (d,d)
            R = B @ R                                        # left‑multiply

        return R
```

A few remarks:

* **Identity initialization**: all trainable vectors (`d1…d4`) start at zero; after Cayley this yields an identity block → the whole product starts as $\mathbf{I}_d$, exactly what OFT and BOFT require.
* **Parameter count**: each `ButterflyFactor` stores four scalars per half‑dimension; with `b=2` and `m=log₂d` this totals roughly $d\log d$ effective parameters.

## 4. Plugging BOFT into a Linear Layer  

Assume you have a pretrained linear projection `nn.Linear(in_features=d, out_features=n)` that you want to finetune using BOFT. The forward equation is:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^\top \mathbf{x},
\]

where $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ is frozen and $\mathbf{R}$ is learned.

```python
class BOFTLinear(nn.Module):
    """
    Wrapper around a frozen Linear layer plus an OrthogonalButterfly transform.
    """
    def __init__(self, pretrained_linear: nn.Linear,
                 m=None,
                 b=2,
                 dropout_p_factor=0.0,
                 dropout_p_block=0.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = pretrained_linear.weight.detach().clone()   # frozen weight
        self.bias = pretrained_linear.bias                     # keep bias as is

        self.R_module = OrthogonalButterfly(
            d=pretrained_linear.in_features,
            m=m,
            b=b,
        )
        # Multiplicative dropout hyper‑parameters (see Section 5)
        self.dropout_p_factor = dropout_p_factor
        self.dropout_p_block  = dropout_p_block

    def _apply_multiplicative_dropout(self, R):
        """Randomly replace some factors / blocks with identity matrices."""
        if not self.training:
            return R

        device = R.device
        I_d   = torch.eye(R.size(0), device=device)

        # Decide which factors to drop
        mask_factors = torch.rand(len(self.R_module.factors)) > self.dropout_p_factor

        new_R = I_d.clone()
        for keep, factor_list in zip(mask_factors, self.R_module.factors):
            if not keep:
                continue          # skip this factor → identity contribution

            # Build the factor matrix as before but possibly drop some inner blocks
            blocks = []
            for blk in factor_list:
                drop_block = torch.rand(1).item() < self.dropout_p_block
                if drop_block:
                    blocks.append(torch.eye(blk[0].half * 2,
                                            device=device))   # identity sub‑block
                else:
                    blocks.append(blk())
            B = torch.block_diag(*blocks)
            new_R = B @ new_R

        return new_R

    def forward(self, x):
        """
        x : (..., d) input tensor.
        
        Returns (..., n) logits after applying frozen W0 and learned orthogonal transform.
       """
        R   = self.R_module()               # dense orthogonal matrix (d,d)
        R   = self._apply_multiplicative_dropout(R)

        # Apply transformation on the left side of W0 → equivalent to (R @ W0)^T @ x
        weight_transformed = (R @ self.W0).t()          # shape (n,d)

       # Standard linear computation with optional bias
       out = torch.nn.functional.linear(x, weight_transformed, self.bias)
       return out
```

## 5. Multiplicative Dropout – Why & How  

Standard Dropout works on additive updates (e.g., LoRA’s low‑rank matrices). BOFT updates weights *multiplicatively* (`R @ W0`). Dropping individual entries would break orthogonality. Instead we randomly **zero out entire butterfly components or sub‑blocks**, replacing them with identity matrices during training:

* **Factor‑level dropout (`p_factor`)** – with probability $p_{\text{factor}}$ we skip one entire sparse factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}$. Effectively we shorten the product depth for that mini‑batch.
* **Block‑level dropout (`p_block`)** – within a retained factor we replace selected $b\times b$ diagonal blocks by their identity counterparts.

Both operations preserve orthogonality because identity matrices are trivially orthogonal and they commute with neighboring factors.

You can tune `p_factor` and `p_block` just like usual Dropout rates; typical values are $0.05$–$0.15$.

## 6. Merging the Learned Orthogonal Matrix – Zero Inference Overhead  

After finetuning you usually want a single model file that behaves like any standard Transformer – no extra runtime cost for consulting BOFT components. Because BOFT’s transform lives entirely on the left side of each frozen weight matrix, we can fold it into the weight once training finishes:

```python
def merge_boft(linear_boft: BOFTLinear):
    """
    Replace linear_boft.W0 with R @ W0 and discard the butterfly module.
    Returns an ordinary nn.Linear ready for inference.
    """
    device   = linear_boft.W0.device
    with torch.no_grad():
         R      = linear_boft.R_module().to(device)           # dense orthogonal matrix
         new_W  = (R @ linear_boft.W0).t()                    # shape (out_features,in_features)

         merged_linear = nn.Linear(
               in_features=linear_boft.W0.size(1),
               out_features=linear_boft.W0.size(0),
               bias=True,
         )
         merged_linear.weight.copy_(new_W)
         if linear_boft.bias is not None:
               merged_linear.bias.copy_(linear_boft.bias)

    return merged_linear

# Example usage after trainer.save_model(...):
model.classifier   = merge_boft(model.classifier)   # now a plain Linear layer
torch.save(model.state_dict(), "boft_merged.pt")
```

After this step:

* No extra modules are present in the graph; inference uses only standard GEMM operations.
* The model size grows only by the saved $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters during training; once merged those parameters are baked into the weight tensor.

## 8. Tips & Common Pitfalls  

| Issue | Symptom | Fix |
|-------|---------|-----|
| **Runtime slower than OFT** | Training takes ~10–15 % more wall time | Use `torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark=True`; keep `b` moderate (≥ 4) to reduce depth `m`. |
| **Gradient NaNs** | Loss becomes NaN early on | Lower learning rate; enforce Cayley stabilization by adding eps to `(I−Q)` inversion (`torch.linalg.solve(I-Q+eps*I , I+Q)`). |
| **Matrix not staying orthogonal** | After many steps singular values drift | Verify Cayley implementation; alternatively switch to **Householder reflections**—they also guarantee exact orthogonality but require more ops per factor. |
| **Memory blowup on very large layers** | OOM when `d > 8192` | Use *gradient checkpointing* on each factor (`torch.utils.checkpoint.checkpoint`) or restrict `$m$` to $\log_2(d/b)$ rather than full depth. |

* **Training stability** – orthogonality preserves singular values and pairwise neuron angles.
* **Zero inference penalty** – after merging you obtain exactly the same speed as any vanilla model.

The building blocks—Cayley‑parameterized butterfly factors and multiplicative dropout—are straightforward PyTorch modules that slot neatly into existing Transformer pipelines.
