\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Motivation and Overview of Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning

The rapid ascent of large foundation models—spanning language, vision, and multimodal domains—has dramatically reshaped the landscape of modern machine learning. Models such as GPT‑3, ChatGPT, and Stable Diffusion possess hundreds of billions of parameters and exhibit remarkable zero‑shot generalization. Yet, this impressive capability comes hand‑in‑hand with an increasingly prohibitive computational and financial cost. Training a new model of comparable scale from scratch can require weeks of GPU time on clusters comprising thousands of devices, translating into millions of dollars in hardware usage and energy consumption. Consequently, the community has turned its attention to **efficient adaptation**: the ability to repurpose a pretrained foundation model for a downstream task without incurring the expense of full retraining.

In this chapter we provide a comprehensive motivation for parameter‑efficient finetuning. We first examine why scaling foundation models is both powerful and costly, then review the three dominant families of adaptation methods—model finetuning, adapter‑based augmentation, and prompt‑based tuning. From there we discuss the desiderata of finetuning, focusing especially on **preserving the knowledge encoded in the pretrained weights**. This leads naturally to **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a principled finetuning paradigm that maintains pairwise neuronal relationships by enforcing an orthogonal transformation on each layer. Although OFT offers superior generalization and training stability compared with additive low‑rank approaches, it suffers from a substantial parameter budget because a naïve orthogonal matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) requires \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) free parameters. The remainder of the chapter outlines the central goal of this book: to trace the evolution from OFT toward a **parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning** method that retains OFT’s theoretical advantages while dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters.

---

## Scaling of Foundation Models and the Cost of Full Pre‑training

The last several years have witnessed an exponential increase in the size of state‑of‑the‑art models. Early transformer‑based language models such as BERT contained on the order of 100 M parameters; contemporary successors—GPT‑3, PaLM, and the latest multimodal diffusion models—have anywhere from 10 B to 175 B parameters. This growth is not arbitrary: larger models tend to learn richer, more universal representations, which translate into higher downstream performance across a wide variety of tasks (e.g., translation, summarization, image synthesis). However, **the cost of training scales superlinearly** with model size. Training a 175 B‑parameter model typically consumes several thousand petaflop‑days of compute, and the associated electrical and hardware expenses can exceed several million dollars. Moreover, many research labs lack the infrastructure necessary to sustain such training pipelines, creating a barrier to entry that limits innovation to a few well‑funded institutions.

A direct consequence is that **retraining from scratch is rarely viable for most practitioners**. Instead, researchers and engineers prefer to start from a publicly released pretrained checkpoint and adapt it to a specific downstream problem (e.g., sentiment analysis, medical image classification, or style‑controlled image generation). The challenge, then, is to modify the model in a manner that is both **computationally cheap** (few trainable parameters, modest additional memory) and **knowledge‑preserving** (the pretrained representations remain largely intact).

---

## Three Families of Parameter‑Efficient Adaptation

Parameter‑efficient adaptation (often abbreviated PEFT) can be classified into three broad families. All three share the overarching goal of limiting the number of updated parameters while leaving the bulk of the pretrained network untouched.

### 1. Model Finetuning

In classic model finetuning the **architecture remains unchanged**; only a subset of the existing weights is updated via gradient descent. One may select a small learning rate to keep the updated parameters close to their pretrained values, thereby implicitly preserving the original knowledge. Recent work has refined this idea by **selectively updating certain layers** (e.g., only the final transformer block) or **constraining the update direction**, which leads to approaches such as BitFit or LayerNorm‑only finetuning. Model finetuning is attractive because it **introduces no additional inference latency**: after training, the model’s forward pass is identical to that of the original checkpoint, merely with a different set of weights.

### 2. Adapter Tuning

Adapter methods augment the pretrained network with **small, trainable bottleneck modules** inserted between existing layers. These adapters typically consist of a down‑projection, a non‑linearity, and an up‑projection, adding only a modest number of parameters (often < 1 % of the total). During finetuning only the adapter parameters are updated, while the original weights are frozen. This approach yields a clear **modularity**: adapters can be swapped in and out for different tasks without altering the base model. However, adapters incur a **small inference overhead** because each forward pass must also traverse the added modules.

### 3. Prompt Tuning

Prompt‑based methods prepend **trainable token embeddings** (or “prefixes”) to the input sequence. The pretrained model processes the augmented input as usual, and only the prompt embeddings are learned. Prompt tuning is especially popular for large language models, where a handful of learned tokens can steer the model toward a target behavior. Like adapters, prompt tuning adds **negligible runtime cost**, but the expressive capacity is limited by the short length of the prompt and the fact that only the input representation is altered.

All three families have demonstrated impressive empirical performance across a myriad of tasks, yet each also has inherent trade‑offs concerning **expressivity**, **stability**, and **preservation of pretrained knowledge**. The next section formalizes these desiderata.

---

## Desirable Properties of Model Finetuning – Preserving Pretrained Knowledge

When a large model has been trained on a massive, diverse corpus, its parameters encode a wealth of semantic and syntactic information. An ideal finetuning method should **retain this knowledge** while allowing sufficient flexibility to adapt to the downstream distribution. Several concrete properties capture this intuition:

1. **Small Parameter Deviation** – Using a modest learning rate or initializing updates near zero ensures the finetuned weights stay close to the original ones in Euclidean distance. This heuristic, while simple, has been shown to improve stability.

2. **Preservation of Pairwise Neuronal Geometry** – In a linear layer, each neuron corresponds to a column vector of the weight matrix. The **angles between columns** encode relational information (e.g., which features are correlated). If finetuning drastically rotates these vectors, the model may discard useful priors. Maintaining these angles helps protect the learned representation.

3. **Spectral Norm Conservation** – The singular values of a weight matrix determine how signals are amplified or attenuated. If finetuning alters the spectral norm significantly, gradients may explode or vanish, harming training stability.

4. **Regularization through Structured Updates** – Constraining the form of the update (e.g., low‑rank, orthogonal) reduces the effective hypothesis space, providing an implicit bias that often leads to better generalization, especially when the downstream dataset is small.

A finetuning scheme that explicitly **preserves the geometric relationships among neurons** while remaining **parameter‑efficient** would therefore satisfy many of the above criteria. This observation motivates the development of **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Formulation and Existing Limitations

### 2.1. Core Idea

Orthogonal Finetuning proposes to **multiply** the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) on the left by an **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)). The forward computation for a given input \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) becomes
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]
Since orthogonal transformations preserve inner products, the pairwise angles between the columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) remain unchanged after the update. Consequently, the **relational structure of the neurons** is exactly preserved, satisfying desideratum 2 above.

To guarantee a **zero‑initialization**—i.e., the finetuned model starts identical to the pretrained one—OFT initializes \(\mathbf{R}\) as the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}_{d}\). During training, \(\mathbf{R}\) must stay orthogonal. OFT enforces this by employing the **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a learnable **skew‑symmetric** matrix (\(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\)). Any skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) yields an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) (provided \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\) is invertible), and the mapping is smooth, making gradient‑based optimization feasible.

### 2.2. Parameter‑Efficiency Challenge

A naïve orthogonal matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) has \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom, which is \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\). For modern transformer layers where \(d\) can be several thousands, this parameter count becomes comparable to the full weight matrix itself, eroding the benefits of parameter‑efficient finetuning. To mitigate this, the original OFT paper introduced a **block‑diagonal structure**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}\big),\qquad \mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br = d.
\]
Each block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) is orthogonal and is learned independently. The total number of trainable parameters shrinks to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\), which is linear in \(d\) for a fixed block size \(b\).

While this **block‑diagonal OFT** drastically reduces memory and compute, it introduces a **strong inductive bias**: the dimensions of a neuron are artificially partitioned into disjoint groups, and each group is rotated independently. This sparsity pattern is arbitrary (based solely on index order) and cannot express many useful linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, which involve mixing across all dimensions. Consequently, the block‑diagonal approach may limit expressive power, potentially hampering performance on tasks that require richer transformations.

Thus, the central limitation of existing OFT is the **tension between parameter efficiency and expressiveness**. The remainder of this book is devoted to reconciling these two goals.

---

## Goal of the Book: From OFT to a More Parameter‑Efficient Approach

The overarching objective is to **construct dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer trainable parameters** than the naïve \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) baseline, while avoiding the restrictive block‑diagonal sparsity of the original OFT. To achieve this, we adopt a **factorization perspective**: instead of learning a single dense orthogonal matrix directly, we represent it as a product of several **sparse orthogonal factors**. Each factor possesses a structured sparsity pattern that can be described by a graph; the composition of factors enables “information transmission” from any source dimension to any target dimension.

By interpreting the factorization as an **information transmission problem on a grid‑structured graph**, we can design sparsity patterns that guarantee dense connectivity (every source can reach every destination) using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable entries. The **butterfly structure**, originally introduced in the Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, naturally satisfies this requirement. A butterfly factor consists of a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are themselves small dense matrices (often \(2\times2\) rotations). Stacking \(\log_{2} d\) such factors yields a full orthogonal matrix whose parameter count grows only linearly with \(d\) up to a logarithmic factor.

The resulting method—**Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**—inherits OFT’s theoretical virtues (angle preservation, spectral‑norm invariance, stable training) while achieving a dramatic reduction in trainable parameters. Moreover, the butterfly factorization introduces a **smooth interpolation** between the identity matrix (no adaptation) and a rich set of orthogonal transforms, offering a principled bias that encourages generalization.

In the chapters that follow, we will:

1. Formalize the information‑transmission viewpoint and derive the necessary graph‑theoretic conditions for dense connectivity.
2. Detail the construction of butterfly factors, their orthogonal parameterization via Cayley transforms, and the recursive composition that yields a full orthogonal matrix.
3. Analyze the expressivity and spectral properties of BOFT, contrasting it with both block‑diagonal OFT and low‑rank approaches such as LoRA.
4. Present extensive empirical evaluations across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models, illustrating BOFT’s superior parameter efficiency and generalization.

By the end of this book, readers will possess a deep understanding of how **structured sparsity**—specifically the butterfly topology—can be harnessed to build **parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning** schemes that are both theoretically grounded and practically impactful.

Mathematical Foundations of Orthogonal Transformations
=====================================================

In this chapter we develop the linear‑algebraic tools that underlie **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its parameter‑efficient extension **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**.  The methods presented in the paper rely on the ability to manipulate and reason about orthogonal (or unitary) matrices, to parameterize them in a differentiable way, and to understand the consequences of left‑multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal transformation.  We therefore introduce the necessary definitions, the Cayley transform used to enforce orthogonality during optimisation, and several geometric and spectral properties that explain why orthogonal finetuning tends to be stable and to preserve the knowledge encoded in a large foundation model.

---

## Orthogonal, Unitary, and Skew‑Symmetric Matrices

Let $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the $d$‑dimensional real vector space and $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ the space of real $d\times d$ matrices.  A matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is **orthogonal** if its columns (and rows) form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{d},
\]

where $\mathbf{I}_{d}$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix.  The set of all $d$‑dimensional orthogonal matrices forms the **orthogonal group** $\mathcal{O}(d)$; it is a compact Lie group equipped with a natural manifold structure.

When the entries are complex, the analogous notion is a **unitary** matrix $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{d}$, where $\dagger$ denotes the conjugate transpose.  All results that depend only on the algebraic conditions $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ remain valid for unitary matrices after replacing transpose with conjugate transpose.

A matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is **skew‑symmetric** if $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$.  Skew‑symmetry implies that all diagonal entries are zero and that $\mathbf{Q}$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues (or zero).  The space of skew‑symmetric matrices is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(d)$ of the orthogonal group.  Every element of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ that is not a reflection can be expressed as the exponential of a skew‑symmetric matrix; this observation motivates the **Cayley transform** used in the paper.

---

## Cayley Parameterization and Its Properties

Direct optimisation over the orthogonal group is difficult because the orthogonality constraints are nonlinear.  The paper adopts the **Cayley transform** to embed a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ into an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\qquad\text{with}\quad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}.
\tag{1}
\]

Several important properties follow from this definition:

1. **Orthogonality is guaranteed.**  
   Multiplying (1) by its transpose yields  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
   = \big[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\big]
     (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
   = \mathbf{I},
   \]
   because $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$ makes $(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top} = \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$.

2. **One‑to‑one mapping on a dense open subset.**  
   The map $\mathbf{Q}\mapsto \mathbf{R}$ is smooth and invertible wherever $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ is nonsingular (which holds for all sufficiently small $\mathbf{Q}$).  This permits a **zero‑initialisation** strategy: setting $\mathbf{Q}=0$ yields $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

3. **Parameter count.**  
   A $d\times d$ skew‑symmetric matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ independent entries, half the number of parameters of an unrestricted $d\times d$ matrix.  When additional sparsity patterns—such as block‑diagonal or butterfly structures—are imposed on $\mathbf{Q}$, the effective number of trainable parameters is reduced further, which is the core idea behind parameter‑efficient OFT and BOFT.

Because gradient‑based optimisation can be performed on the unconstrained parameters of $\mathbf{Q}$, the Cayley transform provides a practical way to learn orthogonal matrices while never leaving $\mathcal{O}(d)$ during training.

---

## Preservation of Pairwise Angles and Singular Values

OFT and BOFT rely on the geometric observation that **orthogonal transformations preserve all inner products**.  For any two column vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\rangle
= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u})^{\top}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v})
= \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}
= \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
= \langle \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle.
\]

Consequences:

* **Angles are unchanged.**  
  The cosine of the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$,
  $\cos\theta = \frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}$,
  remains identical after applying $\mathbf{R}$, because both numerator and denominator are invariant.

* **Lengths are unchanged.**  
  $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|$, a direct corollary of orthogonality.

When a pretrained linear layer is written as $\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, its columns $\{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ are precisely the “neurons’’ whose relational geometry we wish to keep.  The finetuned weight matrix in OFT/BOFT is

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) guarantees that **all pairwise angles among the columns of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ are preserved** in $\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}$.  The paper argues that this preservation helps the finetuned model retain the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining, because many learned representations are encoded in the relative orientation of neurons rather than their absolute positions.

Moreover, the singular values of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ are also preserved.  Let $\mathbf{W}^{0} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ be the singular value decomposition (SVD), where $\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}$ are orthogonal and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with non‑negative entries $\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(d,n)}$.  Multiplying on the left by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ yields

\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}
= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top},
\]

and $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is again orthogonal.  Hence the singular values $\sigma_{i}$ are **exactly unchanged**.  This property is central to the discussion of spectral norm invariance.

---

## Spectral Norm Invariance under Orthogonal Left‑Multiplication

The **spectral norm** of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$, denoted $\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}$, is the largest singular value of $\mathbf{A}$.  Because orthogonal left‑multiplication does not alter singular values (see previous section), we have

\[
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2} = \|\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2}.
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) has two practical implications for finetuning large models:

1. **Training stability.**  
   Gradient descent on deep networks can be sensitive to the magnitude of weight matrices; exploding or vanishing spectral norms can cause instability.  By construction, OFT and BOFT keep the spectral norm of every linear layer exactly at its pretrained value, eliminating one source of instability.

2. **Generalization.**  
   Recent theoretical and empirical work (cited in the paper) suggests that controlling the spectral norm of weight matrices improves generalization, because it bounds the Lipschitz constant of the network.  Since the orthogonal transformation does not increase the spectral norm, finetuning with OFT/BOFT inherits this regularising effect.

Thus, orthogonal finetuning can be viewed as a **norm‑preserving re‑parameterisation** of the pretrained model, offering a principled alternative to additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA, which do not guarantee spectral norm preservation.

---

## Implications for Training Stability and Generalization

Putting together the previous results, we can articulate why orthogonal finetuning—and especially the butterfly‑based, parameter‑efficient variant—tends to be robust:

* **Zero‑initialisation and smooth optimisation.**  
  The Cayley transform maps the zero matrix to the identity, so the optimisation starts exactly at the pretrained point.  Small updates to the skew‑symmetric parameters correspond to small orthogonal rotations, ensuring that each training step stays within a neighbourhood of the original model.

* **Preservation of geometry.**  
  Because all pairwise angles between neurons are unchanged, the feature space explored during pretraining is not distorted.  This alleviates catastrophic forgetting and enables the model to adapt to new tasks without “unlearning’’ useful representations.

* **Spectral norm control.**  
  Equation (3) guarantees that the Lipschitz constant of each linear layer does not increase, reducing the risk of gradient explosion and improving the smoothness of the loss landscape.  Empirically, the paper observes more stable convergence for OFT/BOFT compared with LoRA.

* **Inductive bias from structured sparsity.**  
  BOFT achieves the same geometric guarantees while drastically reducing the number of free parameters (from $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$) by factorising the orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse butterfly components.  The butterfly factorisation encodes a specific pattern of information exchange that is known to be capable of representing many classic linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.).  This structured bias limits the hypothesis space to a rich yet regular subset of $\mathcal{O}(d)$, which the paper argues improves generalisation on downstream tasks.

* **Weight interpolation property.**  
  Because the orthogonal matrix is built as a product of components, one can smoothly interpolate between the identity (pretrained) and the learned transformation by scaling the underlying skew‑symmetric parameters.  This provides a natural way to trade off between retaining pretrained knowledge and adapting to the new task, further contributing to stable training dynamics.

In summary, the mathematical foundations presented in this chapter—the definition of orthogonal matrices, the Cayley transform, and the invariance of angles and singular values—explain why orthogonal finetuning is a principled, stable, and generalizable approach.  The butterfly‑based parameterisation builds on these foundations to achieve practical parameter efficiency without sacrificing the core geometric and spectral properties that make orthogonal finetuning effective.

**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Revisited**

# OFT Objective and Forward‑Pass Modification  

The central idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to adapt a pretrained linear transformation while preserving the geometric relations among its neurons.  Consider a frozen pretrained weight matrix  
\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]  
which maps an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to an output \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) through the canonical forward computation  
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}. 
\]  
OFT replaces the original weight matrix by a left‑multiplication with a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  The modified forward pass becomes  
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
          = (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal \((\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_{d})\), the transformation preserves inner products and, consequently, the pair‑wise angles between the column vectors (neurons) of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  Preserving these angles guarantees that the relational structure learned during pretraining is retained after finetuning, an inductive bias that often translates into better generalisation on downstream tasks.

To enforce orthogonality throughout training, OFT adopts the **Cayley parameterisation**.  Any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (\(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\!\top}\)) can be mapped to an orthogonal matrix via  
\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\]  
Optimising over the free entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\) automatically guarantees that \(\mathbf{R}\) remains orthogonal for all optimisation steps.  This multiplicative update differs fundamentally from additive low‑rank schemes such as LoRA, which modify \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by adding a low‑rank matrix \(\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\).  While LoRA changes the absolute direction of each neuron, OFT rotates the entire subspace spanned by the neurons, keeping the spectrum of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) unchanged.

# Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Construction and Parameter Count  

A naïve implementation of OFT would allocate a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).  Such a matrix contains \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) free parameters, which defeats the purpose of parameter‑efficient finetuning.  The original OFT paper therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal orthogonal structure**.  

Let the dimension \(d\) be factored as \(d = b r\) where \(b\) denotes the block size and \(r\) the number of blocks.  The orthogonal matrix is then constrained to the form  
\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}\bigr),
\]  
where each block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) is itself an orthogonal matrix, i.e. \(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}_{i}= \mathbf{I}_{b}\).  The Cayley transform is applied independently to each block, requiring a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) per block.  Because a skew‑symmetric \(b\times b\) matrix has \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) independent entries, the total number of trainable parameters for the block‑diagonal OFT becomes  

\[
\underbrace{r}_{\text{blocks}}\times\underbrace{\frac{b(b-1)}{2}}_{\text{per‑block free entries}}
= \frac{d}{b}\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
= \frac{(b-1)d}{2}.
\]  

When the block size equals the full dimension (\(b=d\)), the parameter count recovers the dense case \(\frac{(d-1)d}{2}\).  Conversely, setting a small block size drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, at the cost of imposing a rigid sparsity pattern on \(\mathbf{R}\).

# Zero‑Initialization via Identity and Training Dynamics  

A crucial practical aspect of OFT is the choice of initialization.  Since finetuning is intended to start from a model that already performs well on the pretraining task, the orthogonal matrix should begin as the identity transformation.  In the Cayley parameterisation this translates to initializing the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (or each \(\mathbf{Q}_{i}\) in the block‑diagonal case) to the zero matrix.  Substituting \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}\) yields  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{0})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{0})^{-1}= \mathbf{I},
\]  

so the forward computation at the outset coincides exactly with the frozen pretrained linear layer.  During optimisation the entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\) evolve slowly, producing an orthogonal matrix that remains close to the identity for a substantial number of steps.  This **zero‑initialisation** confers two benefits:

1. **Stability** – By staying in the neighbourhood of the identity, the optimisation landscape is smoother and the risk of catastrophic drift from the pretrained representation is mitigated.  
2. **Regularisation** – The orthogonal matrix can be interpreted as a learned rotation of the latent space; starting from the identity enforces a prior that only modest rotations are needed, which is analogous to applying a small learning rate in conventional finetuning.

Empirically, OFT exhibits stable training dynamics, converging reliably even with relatively large learning rates on the skew‑symmetric parameters, a property that is not guaranteed for additive low‑rank methods which may introduce uncontrolled changes in the spectrum of the weight matrix.

# Empirical Advantages over Low‑Rank Methods (e.g., LoRA)  

In standard low‑rank finetuning such as LoRA, the update \(\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\) adds a matrix of rank \(r\) to the pretrained weights.  Although this reduces the number of trainable parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(rd)\), it fundamentally alters the singular value spectrum of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  By contrast, OFT’s multiplicative orthogonal update preserves the **spectral norm** of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  To see this, perform the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix:  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}= \mathbf{U}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\!\top},
\]  

with orthogonal \(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\) and diagonal \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).  The OFT‑updated weight becomes  

\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}.
\]  

Since \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\) is still orthogonal, the singular values of the product equal those of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\); therefore the largest singular value (the spectral norm) is unchanged.  This preservation has been linked to improved **training stability** and **generalisation** in prior work on spectral regularisation.

Beyond spectral considerations, several empirical observations reported in the original OFT study highlight its advantages:

* **Faster convergence** – OFT reaches comparable downstream performance in fewer epochs than LoRA, indicating that the orthogonal constraint provides a more informative search direction.
* **Higher robustness to hyper‑parameter choices** – The method is less sensitive to the exact learning rate or rank hyper‑parameter, largely because the orthogonal manifold imposes a strong geometric regulariser.
* **Better performance on vision‑language tasks** – When applied to large vision transformers and text‑to‑image diffusion models, OFT consistently outperformed LoRA‑style low‑rank updates, especially in settings where preserving the pretrained relational structure is critical (e.g., controllable image generation).

These empirical strengths motivate a deeper examination of OFT’s expressivity, particularly when the block‑diagonal sparsity is introduced.

# Analysis of Expressivity Loss Due to Block Sparsity  

The block‑diagonal restriction simplifies the orthogonal matrix by forcing a **partition** of the hidden dimension into \(r\) independent subspaces of size \(b\).  Within each subspace, any orthogonal rotation is permissible, but **no interaction** is allowed between distinct blocks.  Mathematically, for any input vector \(\mathbf{x}\) split as \(\mathbf{x}= \bigl[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\!\top},\dots ,\mathbf{x}_{r}^{\!\top}\bigr]^{\!\top}\) with \(\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}\), the block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) yields  

\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}= \bigl[\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\!\top},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}^{\!\top}\bigr]^{\!\top}.
\]  

Consequently, any linear combination that would require mixing components across different blocks cannot be represented.  This limitation manifests in several concrete ways:

1. **Reduced ability to capture cross‑channel correlations** – In convolutional or transformer layers, important information is often distributed across all channels.  A block‑diagonal rotation cannot align features that reside in different blocks, potentially leaving useful interactions untouched.
2. **Inability to emulate classic dense transforms** – Many useful linear operators (e.g., the full discrete Fourier transform) are dense and cannot be decomposed as a direct sum of independent small orthogonal matrices.  Hence, the block‑diagonal family cannot approximate such transforms regardless of the values within each block.
3. **Expressivity bound** – The set of block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices forms a strict subgroup of the full orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\).  Its cardinality grows as \(\bigl|\mathcal{O}(b)\bigr|^{\,r}\) instead of \(\bigl|\mathcal{O}(d)\bigr|\).  When \(b\ll d\), the measure of this subgroup inside \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) shrinks exponentially, indicating a substantial loss of representational capacity.

Empirically, this expressivity loss is reflected in a performance gap between the dense OFT variant (full orthogonal matrix) and its block‑diagonal counterpart when the downstream task demands rich cross‑feature interactions.  Nevertheless, for many tasks the block‑diagonal version still outperforms LoRA, suggesting that even a limited set of rotations can provide a useful inductive bias while dramatically decreasing the parameter budget.

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning introduces a principled way to adapt large pretrained models by learning a single orthogonal transformation per layer.  The method preserves angular relationships and spectral norms, enjoys stable training dynamics via identity initialization, and empirically outperforms low‑rank additive schemes.  However, the original block‑diagonal parameterisation, while parameter‑efficient, sacrifices expressivity because it blocks information flow across subspaces.  This insight motivates the development of more sophisticated factorizations—such as butterfly‑based parameterisations—that retain dense connectivity with far fewer parameters, a direction explored in subsequent chapters.

Information Transmission Perspective on Matrix Factorization
==========================================================

In modern deep‑learning practice, especially when adapting gigantic foundation models, it is often desirable to modify a pretrained weight matrix with a **dense orthogonal transformation** while keeping the number of trainable parameters small.  At first glance this appears contradictory: a dense orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) lives in a space of \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) free parameters, whereas a parameter‑efficient finetuning method must touch far fewer scalars.  The key insight of the paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* is to view the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as an **information‑transmission problem** on a grid‑structured graph.  By interpreting each factor in a matrix product as a layer of a communication network, we can reason about how sparsity in the factors influences the overall connectivity, and we can formalize desiderata that lead to highly efficient factorizations such as the butterfly structure.

In this chapter we develop this graph‑theoretic viewpoint in detail.  We begin by recalling why a dense orthogonal matrix corresponds to a completely connected bipartite graph.  Next we formalize the notion of factorizing a matrix into a product of sparse matrices and how this factorization is naturally expressed as a multi‑layer graph.  We then introduce two essential desiderata—*dense connectivity* and *minimum free edges*—that any parameter‑efficient factorization should satisfy.  Finally we contrast a naïve dense factorization with more structured sparse designs, foreshadowing the butterfly factorization that later chapters will explore.

---

### Dense Orthogonal Matrices as Full Bipartite Connectivity

Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\).  Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) updates the forward computation by left‑multiplying \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (i.e. \(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\)).  The map
\[
\mathbf{x}\;\mapsto\; (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
\]
applies the same linear transformation \(\mathbf{R}\) to every column (neuron) of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  The entry \(R_{ij}\) quantifies how much the \(j\)-th input dimension contributes to the \(i\)-th output dimension after finetuning.

If we interpret the matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) as the adjacency matrix of a bipartite graph, the **source side** consists of \(d\) nodes representing input dimensions, and the **target side** consists of \(d\) nodes representing output dimensions.  An edge \((j\!\rightarrow\! i)\) exists precisely when the entry \(R_{ij}\neq0\); its weight is the numerical value of \(R_{ij}\).  When \(\mathbf{R}\) is *dense* (i.e. every entry is non‑zero), the bipartite graph is *complete*: every source node can directly influence every target node.  In the orthogonal setting this dense connectivity guarantees that the pairwise angles among the rows (or columns) of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) can be arbitrarily rotated while preserving orthonormality, a property that underlies the strong generalization observed for OFT.

Thus, a dense orthogonal matrix can be seen as a **full bipartite connectivity pattern**, where the structural constraint “orthogonal” imposes algebraic relations among the edge weights but does **not** remove any edges.

---

### Factorizing a Matrix into a Product of Sparse Matrices

A dense matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be expressed as a product of \(m\) square matrices:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1},
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\;\;(\ell=1,\dots,m).
\tag{1}
\]
If each factor \(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\) is **sparse**, then the total number of free parameters is dramatically reduced.  The price we pay is that we must multiply the factors repeatedly during both training and inference (the latter cost can be absorbed after finetuning by pre‑multiplying \(\mathbf{R}\) onto \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\)).  The central question becomes: **which sparsity patterns allow the product (1) to be dense?**  Answering this requires thinking about how information propagates through the sequence of factors.

---

### Grid‑Structured Graph Representation of the Factorization

Unrolling the matrix product (1) yields a natural **layered graph**.  Imagine a grid of \((m+1)\) columns (or “levels”), each containing \(d\) nodes.  Level 0 holds the original source nodes, level \(m\) holds the final target nodes, and the intermediate levels correspond to the hidden representations after each multiplication by a factor.

For a specific factor \(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\), its non‑zero entries define **directed edges** from level \(\ell-1\) to level \(\ell\).  Concretely, if \(\bigl(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\bigr)_{ij}\neq0\), then there is an edge from node \(j\) in level \(\ell-1\) to node \(i\) in level \(\ell\).  The product \(\mathbf{B}_{m}\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1}\) corresponds to concatenating these edge sets: a path that starts at a source node in level 0, follows an edge in \(\mathbf{B}_{1}\) to level 1, then an edge in \(\mathbf{B}_{2}\) to level 2, and so on, finally reaching a node in level \(m\).  The weight contributed by a particular path is the product of the edge weights along that path, and the **sum over all such paths** yields the entry of the final matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).

In this picture it is immediate why a dense \(\mathbf{R}\) requires **reachability**: for every pair \((\text{source } s, \text{target } t)\) there must exist at least one directed path from \(s\) at level 0 to \(t\) at level \(m\).  If any source‑target pair is not connected, the corresponding entry of \(\mathbf{R}\) will be forced to zero, contradicting dense connectivity.

Figure 1 (in the original paper) visualizes a concrete factorization with \(m=5\); the induced graph shows how a modest number of edges can already guarantee reachability for all \(d=4\) source–target pairs.

---

### Desiderata for Parameter‑Efficient Factorizations

From the graph viewpoint we can articulate two **principled desiderata** that any sparse factorization should aim to satisfy:

1. **Dense Connectivity**  
   *Definition:* Every node in the first level must have at least one directed path to every node in the last level.  
   *Motivation:* This condition is exactly equivalent to the resulting product matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) being dense (no zero entries).  In the orthogonal finetuning context, dense connectivity preserves the ability to rotate any direction in the weight space, thereby retaining the expressive power of a full orthogonal matrix.

2. **Minimum Free Edges**  
   *Definition:* Subject to the orthogonality constraint on each factor, the total number of *free* (i.e. trainable) non‑zero entries across all factors should be as small as possible.  
   *Explanation of “free”:* Orthogonal matrices have inherent algebraic restrictions.  For a given factor \(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\) to be orthogonal, each row must be a unit‑norm vector and rows must be mutually orthogonal.  Consequently, some entries are forced to particular values (e.g. \(\pm1\)) and are not learnable.  We count only the entries that can be varied during training; these are the *free edges*.

   The orthogonality requirement also induces **dependency among edges**.  For a \(2\times2\) orthogonal block, setting a zero at position \((1,1)\) forces a zero at \((2,2)\) because the rows must remain orthogonal and unit‑norm.  Hence, designing a sparsity pattern that respects orthogonality while minimizing free edges is a non‑trivial combinatorial problem.

By striving for dense connectivity with as few free edges as possible, we hope to approach the expressive capacity of a full orthogonal matrix while dramatically reducing the parameter count.

---

### Naïve Dense Factorization vs. Structured Sparse Factorizations

A **naïve** approach to satisfy dense connectivity would be to let **one of the factors be dense** (i.e. all \(d^{2}\) entries non‑zero) and all others be identity matrices.  This trivially yields a dense \(\mathbf{R}\) but reintroduces the full \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameter budget, defeating the purpose of parameter efficiency.

A more interesting baseline is to use **block‑diagonal** factors, as in the original OFT.  Suppose each factor \(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\) consists of \(r\) blocks of size \(b\times b\) with \(d = rb\).  The resulting graph contains edges only within each block, i.e. source nodes are grouped and never exchange information across groups.  Consequently, the overall product cannot become dense because nodes belonging to different blocks never become reachable to each other.  While the block‑diagonal design drastically cuts the number of free parameters down to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\), it imposes a strong inductive bias that limits expressivity.

The **structured sparse factorizations** derived from the **butterfly topology** achieve a far better trade‑off.  A butterfly graph, originally discovered in the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform, connects each node to only two nodes in the next level, yet after \(\log_{2} d\) levels every source can reach every target.  In terms of edges, the butterfly network uses only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges, which are all free (each corresponds to a learnable scalar or a small learnable block).  Importantly, each level’s factor can be made orthogonal by parameterizing the tiny \(2\times2\) (or \(b\times b\) in a block‑butterfly extension) sub‑blocks with a Cayley transform or simple rotation matrices.  The resulting product is a dense orthogonal matrix despite the extreme sparsity of each factor.

To summarize:

| Design | Number of factors \(m\) | Non‑zero entries per factor | Total free parameters | Ability to achieve dense \(\mathbf{R}\) |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Single dense factor | 1 | \(d^{2}\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) | Trivial |
| Block‑diagonal (OFT) | 1 | \(bd\) (with \(b\ll d\)) | \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) | **Fails** dense connectivity |
| Butterfly (BOFT) | \(\log_{2} d\) | \(2d\) (each level) | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) | Succeeds, meeting both desiderata |

The butterfly construction therefore illustrates concretely how the **information‑transmission perspective** guides us toward a factorization that is simultaneously sparse, orthogonal, and expressive enough to generate any dense orthogonal matrix (or a large useful subset thereof).  The remainder of the text builds on this foundation, showing how to instantiate the butterfly factors, how to enforce orthogonality via Cayley transforms, and how the resulting *Orthogonal Butterfly* (BOFT) method outperforms prior parameter‑efficient finetuning strategies across vision and language tasks.

**Butterfly Structures and Fast Fourier Transform**

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) is perhaps the most celebrated algorithm in numerical computing.  Its efficiency stems from a very particular sparse factorization of the dense discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix, a factorization that can be visualized as a *butterfly graph*.  In this chapter we unpack the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly diagram, describe the algebraic form of its factor matrices, and explain why this structure provides an optimal information‑transmission topology for constructing dense orthogonal matrices with only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.  Although the FFT was originally invented for signal processing, the same butterfly factorization has been repeatedly leveraged in numerical linear algebra, matrix‑vector multiplication, and, most recently, in parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning (BOFT).

---

### Recursive FFT decomposition and butterfly diagram

The discrete Fourier transform maps a vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to its frequency representation \(\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{F}_{d}\mathbf{x}\), where \(\mathbf{F}_{d}\) is the dense \(d\times d\) Fourier matrix.  The Cooley‑Tukey algorithm observes that when \(d\) is a power of two, the DFT can be expressed recursively as two DFTs of size \(d/2\) applied to the even‑ and odd‑indexed entries of \(\mathbf{x}\).  Formally,
\[
\mathbf{F}_{d}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{I}_{d/2} & \mathbf{I}_{d/2}\\[2pt]
\mathbf{I}_{d/2} & -\mathbf{I}_{d/2}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\text{first butterfly layer}}
\;
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{F}_{d/2} & \mathbf{0}\\[2pt]
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{F}_{d/2}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\text{recursive sub‑DFTs}}
\; .
\]
The first matrix is sparse: each row contains only two non‑zero entries, a “+1’’ and a “±1’’ that connect a pair of inputs to a pair of outputs.  When this decomposition is applied recursively, the overall multiplication \(\mathbf{F}_{d}\mathbf{x}\) can be represented as a cascade of \(\log_2 d\) such sparse layers.  The wiring of these layers is what is commonly drawn as the **butterfly diagram**: nodes representing the components of the vector are placed on \(\log_2 d + 1\) vertical levels, and each layer draws edges that join pairs of nodes in a crossing pattern reminiscent of a butterfly’s wings.

From an information‑transmission viewpoint, each edge of the diagram conveys a linear combination of two source nodes to two destination nodes.  After \(\log_2 d\) layers, every source node can influence every destination node; that is, the composite matrix is dense even though each individual layer is extremely sparse.

---

### Butterfly factor definition and block‑diagonal composition

The sparse layer introduced above can be abstracted as a **butterfly factor**.  For a block size \(k\) that is itself a power of two, define
\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k) \;=\;
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\tag{1}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) is a vector of free parameters.  The four diagonal blocks are placed in the same layout as the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly; each row of \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) has exactly two non‑zero entries, one in the left half and one in the right half.  When \(k=2\), \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(2)\) reduces to a generic \(2\times2\) matrix, and the constraint that the overall factor be orthogonal can be imposed by a Cayley transform (or by a simple rotation angle).

A full **butterfly matrix** of dimension \(d=2^{N}\) is built by stacking several butterfly factors of varying block sizes in a block‑diagonal fashion.  Define the *butterfly component*
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)
\;=\;
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{1}(k),\,
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{2}(k),\dots,
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{d/k}(k)
\bigr) 
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\tag{2}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{B}^{F}_{i}(k)\) is an independent copy of the factor (1).  The component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) therefore contains exactly \(\frac{d}{k}\times 2\frac{k}{2}=d\) non‑zero entries—precisely the minimal number required for a full‑rank, orthogonal mapping between the \(d\) nodes at level \(\ell\) and those at level \(\ell+1\).

The butterfly matrix is the ordered product of such components with decreasing block sizes:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d) \;=\;
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/4)\,
\cdots\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
\tag{3}
\]
Because each factor is orthogonal (by construction of its \(2\times2\) sub‑blocks), the product is also orthogonal.  The recursion in (3) mirrors exactly the recursive FFT decomposition: the first factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\) mixes pairs of neighboring indices, the next factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,4)\) mixes the resulting pairs across a distance of two, and so on, until the final factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\) mixes across the full width of the vector.

---

### Properties: \(O(d\log d)\) edges, full connectivity, invertibility

#### Sparse edge count

Each component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) contains exactly \(d\) non‑zero entries, independent of \(k\).  Since the product in (3) comprises \(\log_2 d\) components, the total number of *trainable* scalar parameters is proportional to
\[
\underbrace{d}_{\text{edges per layer}}\times\underbrace{\log_2 d}_{\text{layers}} \;=\; O(d\log d).
\]
This is a dramatic reduction compared with a fully dense orthogonal matrix, which would require \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}=O(d^{2})\) independent parameters (because orthogonal matrices lie on a manifold of dimension \(d(d-1)/2\)).  The butterfly factorization therefore achieves **dense connectivity**—every input can affect every output after the full product—while only storing a number of parameters that grows quasi‑linearly in \(d\).

#### Full connectivity (dense information transmission)

The butterfly topology guarantees that for any source node at level 0 there exists a path to any destination node at level \(\log_2 d\).  This can be proved by induction on the block size: the first component mixes each node with its immediate neighbor; the second component mixes the resulting pairs across a distance of two, thereby connecting each node to every other node within a block of size four; continuing this process doubles the reachable set at each step, and after \(\log_2 d\) steps the reachable set is the entire set of \(d\) nodes.  Consequently, the composite matrix is *dense*: every entry of \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is generally non‑zero (except for pathological parameter choices).

#### Orthogonal invertibility

Because every factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is orthogonal, their product is orthogonal as well:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)^{\top}\mathbf{B}(d)=\mathbf{I}_{d}.
\]
An orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms, inner products, and singular values.  From a learning perspective, this property ensures that finetuning a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with a left‑multiplication by \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) does not perturb the spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  In the context of orthogonal finetuning, such preservation stabilizes training and mitigates catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge.

---

### Historical uses of butterfly matrices in numerical linear algebra

The butterfly factorization first emerged as a structural description of the Cooley‑Tukey FFT in the 1960s.  Soon after, researchers recognized that the same sparse product could be employed to **accelerate linear algebraic operations**:

1. **Fast matrix–vector multiplication** – By representing a dense linear transform as a butterfly product, one can multiply a vector in \(O(d\log d)\) time rather than \(O(d^{2})\).  This idea underlies fast algorithms for the DFT, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform, each of which can be written as a butterfly matrix possibly followed by a permutation.

2. **Efficient Gaussian elimination** – Parker (1995) introduced butterfly matrices as preconditioners that avoid pivoting, exploiting the fact that each factor is orthogonal and therefore numerically stable.

3. **Stabilization of recurrent networks** – Jing et al. (2017) used parametrized butterfly matrices to enforce orthogonal recurrent weights, yielding long‑term gradient flow while keeping the number of parameters modest.

4. **Kernel approximation** – Munkhoeva et al. (2018) applied butterfly factorizations to construct fast, structured random features for approximating shift‑invariant kernels.

5. **Network compression and training from scratch** – More recent works (e.g., Dao et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022) have shown that replacing dense weight layers with a butterfly product can dramatically reduce memory usage while preserving expressive power, especially when the target transform is known to have a fast algorithmic structure.

In the domain of **parameter‑efficient finetuning**, the butterfly factorization is harnessed in a novel way: instead of reparameterizing each weight matrix directly, a *single* orthogonal butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is learned to multiply the frozen pretrained matrix from the left.  This constitutes the **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** method.  BOFT inherits all the benefits listed above—dense information transmission, orthogonal invertibility, and a quasi‑linear parameter budget—while providing a principled inductive bias for downstream tasks.

---

In summary, the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly graph is more than a clever diagram for the FFT; it is a universal sparsity pattern that enables the construction of dense orthogonal matrices from a limited set of trainable edges.  By interpreting each sparse factor as a layer of information exchange on a grid‑structured graph, we see why the butterfly topology achieves full connectivity with only \(O(d\log d)\) parameters.  This insight is the cornerstone of modern parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning, where butterfly matrices serve both as efficient computational kernels and as structured regularizers that promote stability and generalization.

**Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) Architecture**

# Recursive definition of butterfly matrices  \(\mathbf{B}(d)\)

The butterfly matrix is the cornerstone of the BOFT method.  It is defined recursively for dimensions that are powers of two.  Let \(d=2^{N}\) for some integer \(N\ge 1\).  The construction starts from the elementary **butterfly factor** of size \(k\),

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\qquad
\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2},
\]

where each \(\mathbf{d}_{i}\) is a vector that populates a diagonal block.  The factor \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) therefore contains only \(2k\) non‑zero entries (the diagonals of the four sub‑blocks), a pattern that mirrors the “butterfly” data exchange in the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform.

To obtain a full‑size butterfly matrix we assemble a hierarchy of such factors.  Define the **butterfly component**

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{B}^{F}_{1}(k),\mathbf{B}^{F}_{2}(k),\dots,\mathbf{B}^{F}_{d/k}(k)\bigr)
\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]

which is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors of size \(k\).  With this notation the *recursive* butterfly matrix is

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{B}\!\bigl(d/2\bigr) & \mathbf{0}\\[4pt]
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{B}\!\bigl(d/2\bigr)
\end{bmatrix}
= \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/4)\cdots\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
\]

Thus \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is expressed as a product of \(\log_{2} d\) components, each component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2^{\ell})\) (for \(\ell=0,\dots,\log_{2}d-1\)) having a regular sparse pattern that couples pairs of indices in a way that exactly reproduces the information‑mixing pattern of the FFT butterfly graph.  Because each component is a permutation of a set of \(2\times2\) blocks, the overall matrix can be dense even though every factor is extremely sparse.

# Block‑butterfly components \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,k)\) and orthogonal constraints

The basic butterfly component described above uses scalar entries.  BOFT generalises this to *block* butterfly components, allowing each scalar \(d_{i}\) in \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) to be replaced by a full matrix \(\mathbf{D}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\).  The resulting block‑butterfly factor of size \(k\) becomes

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{(b)}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{kb\times kb},
\]

where each diagonal block now contains a \(b\times b\) matrix.  Stacking these factors yields the *block‑butterfly component*

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{B}^{F}_{(b),1}(k),\dots,\mathbf{B}^{F}_{(b),d/k}(k)\bigr)
\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]

with the requirement that \(kb=d\) for the component to be square.

To use these components in BOFT we must enforce **orthogonality**.  An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}\).  For a block‑butterfly component this condition reduces to requiring each individual \(2b\times2b\) block to be orthogonal, because the blocks are mutually independent (they sit on a block‑diagonal).  Concretely, for each index \(i\) we impose

\[
\bigl[\mathbf{B}^{F}_{(b),i}(k)\bigr]^{\top}\mathbf{B}^{F}_{(b),i}(k)=\mathbf{I}_{2b},
\]

which can be satisfied by parameterising the block with a **Cayley transform**.  Let \(\mathbf{S}_{i}\) be a skew‑symmetric matrix of size \(2b\times2b\) (\(\mathbf{S}_{i}=-\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\top}\)).  The Cayley map

\[
\mathbf{O}_{i}= (\mathbf{I}_{2b}+\mathbf{S}_{i})(\mathbf{I}_{2b}-\mathbf{S}_{i})^{-1}
\]

produces an orthogonal \(\mathbf{O}_{i}\).  By applying this transform independently to each block we obtain an orthogonal \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,k)\) while keeping the number of trainable parameters proportional to the number of independent entries in the skew‑symmetric matrices, i.e. \(\frac{1}{2}(2b)(2b-1)=b(2b-1)\) per block.

# Parameter budget: \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) versus \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) and \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\)

Consider a linear layer with pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\).  In vanilla Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the fine‑tuned weight is \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an unrestricted orthogonal matrix.  Storing a generic orthogonal matrix requires \(\Theta(d^{2})\) real numbers (e.g. a skew‑symmetric matrix of size \(d\) has \(d(d-1)/2\) free entries).  To reduce this cost, the original OFT introduced a **block‑diagonal** orthogonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r})\) with blocks of size \(b\) (\(bd=r\)).  The parameter count then drops to \(\Theta(bd)=\Theta(b\,d)\).

BOFT replaces the single block‑diagonal matrix with a product of \(m\) block‑butterfly components:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}_{(d,i)},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}_{(d,i)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}\!\bigl(d,2^{\,m-i+1}\bigr).
\]

Each component contains \(d/b\) independent \(2b\times2b\) orthogonal blocks, each of which is parameterised by a skew‑symmetric matrix with \(\frac{1}{2}(2b)(2b-1)=b(2b-1)\) free parameters.  Hence the total number of trainable parameters is

\[
\underbrace{m}_{\text{number of components}}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{\frac{d}{b}}_{\text{blocks per component}}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{b(2b-1)}_{\text{params per block}}
\;=\;
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

When we set the depth \(m=\log_{2}d\) and the block size \(b=2\), the budget becomes

\[
\frac{1}{2}(2-1)\,d\,\log_{2}d = \mathcal{O}(d\log d),
\]

a dramatic reduction compared with \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\).  Moreover, for any fixed block size \(b\) we obtain a parameter count that scales linearly in \(d\) and only logarithmically in the desired density of the final orthogonal matrix.

# Special cases: BOFT\((1,b)=\) block‑diagonal OFT, BOFT\((\log d,2)=\) full dense orthogonal

The notation BOFT\((m,b)\) denotes a model that uses \(m\) block‑butterfly components with internal block size \(b\).

* **BOFT\((1,b)\)** – When \(m=1\) the product collapses to a single component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,2^{\log_{2}d})=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,d)\).  Because the block size equals the component’s stride, \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}(d,d)\) is itself block‑diagonal with blocks of size \(b\).  This is exactly the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix used in the original OFT, so BOFT\((1,b)\) recovers the earlier method.

* **BOFT\((\log_{2}d,2)\)** – Setting the block size to the minimal value \(b=2\) and stacking \(\log_{2}d\) components yields the classic butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d)\).  Each component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(2)}(d,2^{\ell})\) permutes the rows/columns of the previous one, and the product of all \(\log_{2}d\) of them yields a **dense orthogonal matrix**.  Consequently BOFT\((\log_{2}d,2)\) achieves the same expressive power as unrestricted OFT while using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

Thus BOFT provides a continuum between the highly sparse block‑diagonal OFT and the fully dense orthogonal transformation, allowing practitioners to trade off parameter budget against expressivity in a principled way.

# Zero‑initialization using identity blocks via Cayley transform

A key requirement in any finetuning scheme is that the initial transformation leaves the pretrained model unchanged.  For BOFT this means initializing the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) to the identity \(\mathbf{I}_{d}\).  Because \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is a product of orthogonal components, it suffices to initialise every component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{(b)}_{(d,i)}\) as the identity.

Each component is built from \(2b\times2b\) blocks \(\mathbf{O}_{i}\) obtained through the Cayley map

\[
\mathbf{O}_{i}= (\mathbf{I}+ \mathbf{S}_{i})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}_{i})^{-1}.
\]

If we set the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{S}_{i}\) to the zero matrix, then \(\mathbf{O}_{i}=\mathbf{I}_{2b}\).  Consequently every block of every component becomes an identity matrix, and the whole component reduces to \(\mathbf{I}_{d}\).  Initialising all skew‑symmetric parameters to zero therefore yields

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\underbrace{\mathbf{I}_{d}\mathbf{I}_{d}\dots\mathbf{I}_{d}}_{m\ \text{times}}=\mathbf{I}_{d},
\]

guaranteeing that the forward pass initially computes \(\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}\) exactly as the pretrained model would.  During optimisation the skew‑symmetric parameters are updated, gradually moving each component away from the identity while preserving orthogonality by construction.

---

In summary, BOFT inserts a series of **orthogonal butterfly components** into the Orthogonal Finetuning framework.  By exploiting the recursive butterfly factorisation, BOFT attains dense orthogonal transformations with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters, interpolates smoothly between the block‑diagonal OFT and a full orthogonal matrix, and enjoys a clean zero‑initialisation scheme via the Cayley transform.  These properties make BOFT a powerful, parameter‑efficient approach for adapting large foundation models across vision and language domains.

# Training Techniques for BOFT

In this chapter we discuss the practical aspects of optimizing **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**, a parameter‑efficient finetuning method that builds a dense orthogonal transformation from a product of sparse orthogonal factors.  Although the mathematical underpinnings of BOFT are described in the earlier chapters, successful deployment in real‑world projects requires a careful treatment of several engineering details: how to regularize the product of orthogonal matrices, how gradients propagate through the factorized structure, what computational overhead to expect during training, and how to implement the method efficiently on modern accelerators.  We also describe how BOFT can be incorporated into existing parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) pipelines such as those based on LoRA or adapters.  The exposition assumes familiarity with basic deep‑learning toolkits (PyTorch, JAX, or TensorFlow) and with the notion of orthogonal matrices parameterized via the Cayley transform.

---

## 1. Multiplicative Dropout for Products of Orthogonal Factors  

### 1.1 Motivation  

Standard dropout randomly zeroes individual activations or weight entries during training, thereby encouraging robustness to missing features.  In LoRA, dropout is applied directly to the low‑rank update because the update is additive: the perturbed weight remains a sum of two terms, and zeroing part of the low‑rank matrix does not break the overall linearity of the forward pass.  

In BOFT the finetuned weight for a linear layer of size \(d\times n\) is
\[
\mathbf{W}_\text{new}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top},
\qquad 
\mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]
where each \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an orthogonal butterfly component.  Dropping a *single* entry of \(\mathbf{R}\) would generally destroy the orthogonality of the product, leading to an uncontrolled distortion of the pretrained weight matrix.  Therefore we require a **multiplicative dropout** that respects the factorized orthogonal structure.

### 1.2 Formulation  

Multiplicative dropout operates at two granularity levels:

1. **Component‑level dropout**: with probability \(p_1\) we replace an entire butterfly component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) by the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}_d\).  Since the identity is orthogonal, this operation does not violate the overall orthogonality constraint.

2. **Block‑level dropout**: each butterfly component is itself block‑diagonal (when viewed with block size \(b\)).  Inside component \(i\) we randomly select a fraction \(p_2\) of the \(b\times b\) diagonal blocks and replace each selected block by the identity \(\mathbf{I}_b\).  Because each block lies on the diagonal of the component, the resulting component remains orthogonal: it is the product of orthogonal blocks (some of which are the identity) and a permutation matrix that reorders the blocks.

Putting these together, the stochastic orthogonal matrix used in a forward pass is
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}} = \prod_{i=1}^{m}
\Bigl( \mathbf{M}^{(i)}_{\text{comp}} \; \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \;
\mathbf{M}^{(i)}_{\text{block}} \Bigr),
\]
where \(\mathbf{M}^{(i)}_{\text{comp}}\in\{\mathbf{I}_d,\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\}\) encodes component‑level dropout and \(\mathbf{M}^{(i)}_{\text{block}}\) is a block‑diagonal mask with identity blocks on the selected positions and the original orthogonal blocks elsewhere.  Both masks are sampled independently for each mini‑batch (or each iteration, depending on the desired regularization strength).  

### 1.3 Practical Choices  

| Hyperparameter | Typical range | Effect |
|----------------|---------------|--------|
| \(p_1\) (component dropout) | \(0.0\) – \(0.3\) | Larger values increase stochasticity, useful when the downstream task is small and overfitting is a concern. |
| \(p_2\) (block dropout) | \(0.0\) – \(0.5\) | Controls the density of each component; higher values yield a more “identity‑biased” transformation. |
| Seed per epoch | Fixed or varying | Using a fixed mask for an entire epoch (instead of per‑batch) reduces variance and speeds up implementation on GPUs. |

Empirically, a modest component dropout (\(p_1\approx0.1\)) combined with a stronger block dropout (\(p_2\approx0.3\)) often yields the best trade‑off between regularization and preserving the expressive power of the butterfly factorization.

---

## 2. Gradient Flow Through Multiple Sparse Orthogonal Layers  

### 2.1 Chain Rule Through a Product  

Consider the loss \(\mathcal{L}\) that depends on the model output \(\mathbf{z}\).  The forward pass uses
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\quad
\mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m} \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}.
\]
Let us denote the intermediate product after the \(k\)-th component as
\[
\mathbf{R}_k = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\quad
\mathbf{R}_0 = \mathbf{I}_d.
\]
The gradient w.r.t. a specific component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,k)}\) is obtained via the chain rule:
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,k)}}
 = \bigl(\mathbf{R}_{k-1}\bigr)^{\!\top}
   \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{R}} 
   \bigl(\mathbf{R}_{m}^{\phantom{\top}}\bigr)^{\!\top}
 = \bigl(\mathbf{R}_{k-1}\bigr)^{\!\top} 
   \bigl[\mathbf{W}^{0} \mathbf{x}\, \mathbf{g}^{\!\top}\bigr] 
   \bigl(\mathbf{R}_{m}\bigr)^{\!\top},
\]
where \(\mathbf{g}= \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \mathbf{z}\) is the back‑propagated error signal.  Because every \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is orthogonal, the matrices \(\mathbf{R}_{k-1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{m}\) are themselves orthogonal, i.e. \(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\).  Consequently the gradient magnitude is **preserved** across the product: multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not amplify or diminish the norm of the upstream gradient.  This property is essential for training stability, especially when \(m\) is large (e.g., \(m\approx \log_2 d\)).  

### 2.2 Parameterization via the Cayley Transform  

Each butterfly component is constrained to be orthogonal.  The standard approach is to parametrize a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) and apply the Cayley transform:
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}^{(i)})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}^{(i)})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{(i)} = -\bigl(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\bigr)^{\!\top}.
\]
The number of free parameters in \(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) equals the number of strictly upper‑triangular entries of each block; for block size \(b\) the total per‑component parameter count is \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2} \times \frac{d}{b}= \frac{(b-1)d}{2}\).  During back‑propagation, the gradient w.r.t. \(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) can be obtained by differentiating the Cayley transform analytically or by using automatic differentiation on the explicit matrix expression.  Care must be taken to avoid numerical instability when \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) is close to singular; in practice the skew‑symmetry and the small magnitude of \(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) (thanks to zero initialization) keep the denominator well‑conditioned.

### 2.3 Avoiding Vanishing/Exploding Gradients  

Even though orthogonal matrices preserve norms, the *learning rate* for the underlying skew‑symmetric parameters needs to be tuned.  Because the Cayley transform is nonlinear, a naive learning rate that works for LoRA can cause the effective update to the orthogonal matrix to be disproportionately large.  A practical heuristic is to **scale the learning rate** for the BOFT parameters by a factor of \(\frac{2}{b-1}\) (the inverse of the per‑component parameter count factor).  This scaling equalizes the magnitude of parameter updates across different block sizes and numbers of components, ensuring comparable training dynamics.

---

## 3. Runtime Analysis: Extra Matrix Multiplications vs. Inference Cost  

### 3.1 Training Overhead  

During training each forward pass incurs a multiplication of the frozen pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\):
\[
\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0} \quad \text{(size } d\times n\text{)}.
\]
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is expressed as a product of \(m\) sparse butterfly components, we can compute \(\mathbf{U}\) by **sequential sparse‑dense multiplications**:
\[
\mathbf{U}^{(0)} = \mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad
\mathbf{U}^{(i)} = \widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \mathbf{U}^{(i-1)}, \quad i=1\ldots m.
\]
Each \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) contains \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries (exactly \(d\) if we ignore the signs), so the total arithmetic cost per layer is \(\mathcal{O}(mdn)\).  By contrast, a dense orthogonal matrix would cost \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}n)\).  For typical transformer dimensions (e.g., \(d=1024\), \(n=4096\)) and \(m=\log_2 d =10\), the BOFT cost is roughly a factor of \(10 \times 1024 / 1024 = 10\) times cheaper than the naive dense product, yet still several times more expensive than a pure LoRA update (which involves an additional low‑rank matrix multiplication of cost \(\mathcal{O}(rn)\) with \(r\ll d\)).

### 3.2 Inference Cost  

After finetuning, the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be **absorbed** into the pretrained weight:
\[
\mathbf{W}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]
Because all butterfly components are fixed at inference time, we may pre‑compute the product \(\mathbf{R}\) (as a dense matrix) once and store \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{final}}\).  The resulting model incurs **zero extra latency** compared to the original pretrained model, exactly like other PEFT methods that merge their updates after training.  If memory is a concern, one can also keep the factorized representation and perform a single sparse‑dense multiplication at inference, which still adds only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) overhead per linear layer—a negligible amount for modern GPUs/TPUs.

### 3.3 Memory Footprint  

During training the dominant memory consumption comes from:
1. The frozen weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) (shared with the base model).
2. The set of skew‑symmetric parameters \(\{\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}\).  
Since each \(\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\) stores only \(\frac{(b-1)d}{2}\) floating‑point numbers, the total BOFT memory overhead is \(\mathcal{O}(md b)\).  For \(m=\log_2 d\) and \(b=2\) this overhead is roughly \(d\log_2 d\) numbers—orders of magnitude smaller than the full \(d^{2}\) for a dense orthogonal matrix and comparable to LoRA’s low‑rank parameters.

---

## 4. Implementation Tips: Exploiting Block Sparsity on GPUs/TPUs  

### 4.1 Sparse‑Dense Multiplication Kernels  

Modern deep‑learning frameworks expose *batched sparse matrix multiplication* primitives (e.g., `torch.sparse.mm` or XLA’s `SparseTensor`).  However, the butterfly pattern is highly regular: each component is a block‑diagonal matrix where each block is either a dense \(2\times 2\) (or \(b\times b\)) rotation or the identity.  Leveraging this regularity yields two practical strategies:

1. **Custom CUDA kernel**: Write a kernel that iterates over the \(d/b\) blocks and performs a small dense matrix multiply (size \(b\times b\)) for each block, then permutes the outputs according to the butterfly permutation.  Since \(b\) is tiny (2 or 4 in most experiments), the kernel can be fully unrolled, leading to an almost memory‑bound operation.

2. **Folding into a `torch.nn.Conv1d`**: When the input dimension matches the number of channels, a butterfly component can be expressed as a depth‑wise 1‑D convolution with a kernel that encodes the block‑wise rotation and a stride‑1 shuffle that implements the permutation.  This allows the use of highly optimized cuDNN kernels without writing custom code.

Both approaches avoid the overhead of generic sparse matrix libraries, which are often sub‑optimal for the extremely structured sparsity of butterfly factors.

### 4.2 Caching Permutations  

Each butterfly component includes a deterministic permutation of the block indices (e.g., the bit‑reversal permutation in the classic FFT).  Pre‑computing the permutation indices and storing them as a `torch.LongTensor` allows us to use `torch.index_select` or `torch.gather` to reorder tensors efficiently.  Since the permutation does not change during training, it can be placed on the same device as the model parameters to avoid host‑device transfers.

### 4.3 Mixed‑Precision Training  

BOFT benefits from mixed‑precision (FP16 or BF16) training in the same way as other large‑scale finetuning methods.  The Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse of \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\); numerically, this inverse is well‑conditioned because \(\mathbf{Q}\) starts at zero and remains small under typical learning rates.  Nonetheless, one should **cast the skew‑symmetric parameters to FP32** before applying the inverse, then cast the resulting orthogonal component back to the training dtype.  This hybrid approach preserves stability without sacrificing the speed advantage of lower precision.

---

## 5. Integration with Existing PEFT Libraries  

### 5.1 API Compatibility  

Most PEFT libraries (e.g., `peft` for PyTorch, `adapter-transformers`, `lora` in HuggingFace) expose a common interface:

```python
model = load_pretrained(...)
peft_config = LoraConfig(r=4, target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"])
model = get_peft_model(model, peft_config)
```

To incorporate BOFT, we introduce a **`ButterflyConfig`** that mirrors the existing configuration objects:

```python
class ButterflyConfig(PeftConfig):
    def __init__(self,
                 num_factors=None,      # m
                 block_size=2,          # b
                 dropout_component=0.1,
                 dropout_block=0.3,
                 target_modules=None):
        super().__init__(target_modules=target_modules)
        self.num_factors = num_factors
        self.block_size = block_size
        self.dropout_component = dropout_component
        self.dropout_block = dropout_block
```

The `get_peft_model` factory can then instantiate a `ButterflyLayer` for each target module, replacing the original linear weight with the product \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  The `ButterflyLayer` holds:

- The frozen pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) (registered as a buffer).
- The list of skew‑symmetric parameters \(\{\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}\}\) (registered as `nn.Parameter`s).
- Optional dropout masks that are regenerated each training step.

Because the forward method returns the same shape as a standard linear layer, downstream code (optimizers, schedulers, gradient clipping) operates unchanged.

### 5.2 Compatibility with Optimizers and Schedulers  

BOFT parameters are typically **fewer** than LoRA’s low‑rank matrices, so a larger learning rate can be employed without destabilizing training.  When using a scheduler such as cosine decay or linear warm‑up, it is advisable to **apply the schedule only to BOFT parameters**, leaving the base model’s learning rate at zero (or a negligible value if one wishes to fine‑tune a subset of the base).  This can be achieved by constructing separate parameter groups:

```python
optimizer = AdamW([
    {"params": model.base_model.parameters(), "lr": 0.0},
    {"params": model.boft_parameters(), "lr": base_lr},
])
```

The scheduler is then attached to the optimizer as usual.

### 5.3 Combining BOFT with Other PEFT Techniques  

BOFT is orthogonal (in the literal sense) to many additive PEFT methods.  For example, one can **stack a LoRA module on top of a BOFT‑augmented linear layer**, thereby allowing both a multiplicative orthogonal transformation and an additive low‑rank correction.  The combined forward pass reads:
\[
\mathbf{z}= \bigl( \underbrace{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}}_{\text{BOFT}} + \underbrace{\Delta\mathbf{W}}_{\text{LoRA}}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\) is the low‑rank update.  Because both updates are small, the overall parameter budget remains modest (roughly the sum of the BOFT and LoRA parameters).  Empirically, this hybrid often yields the best of both worlds: BOFT’s preservation of geometric relations and LoRA’s ability to inject task‑specific directions.

---

## 6. Summary  

Training Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning demands a set of specialized techniques that differ from conventional additive PEFT methods.  The key points are:

1. **Multiplicative dropout** respects the orthogonal factorization by operating at component and block granularity, preventing violation of orthogonality while still regularizing the model.
2. **Gradient propagation** is naturally stable because each factor is orthogonal; the Cayley transform provides a smooth parameterization of the skew‑symmetric generators, and careful learning‑rate scaling avoids overly large effective updates.
3. **Runtime cost** during training grows linearly with the number of butterfly factors and the hidden dimension, but remains far below that of a dense orthogonal matrix; inference incurs no overhead after merging the learned orthogonal matrix into the base weights.
4. **Implementation** benefits from exploiting the regular block‑sparse pattern, either via custom kernels or by recasting the operation as a depth‑wise convolution plus permutation.
5. **Integration** into existing PEFT ecosystems is straightforward through a configuration object and a thin wrapper layer, and BOFT can be combined with additive methods like LoRA for additional flexibility.

By following these guidelines, practitioners can harness the expressive power of dense orthogonal transformations while keeping both the parameter count and computational burden at a level comparable to, or even lower than, state‑of‑the‑art parameter‑efficient finetuning approaches.

# Expressivity and Theoretical Insights  

In this chapter we delve into the representational power of **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** and its theoretical underpinnings.  We begin by formalising why BOFT is strictly more expressive than the block‑diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced in the original work.  We then analyse how products of butterfly matrices can approximate any dense orthogonal transformation, describe the kaleidoscope hierarchy that yields universal approximation of the orthogonal group  $O(d)$, and discuss the delicate balance between expressivity, regularity and generalisation.  Finally, we examine the spectral implications of orthogonal finetuning and why preserving singular values is advantageous for training stability.

---

## 1.  BOFT Is Strictly More Expressive Than Block‑Diagonal OFT  

Recall that the original OFT parameterises a finetuning matrix $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ as a **block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix**  

\[
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf R_{1},\mathbf R_{2},\dots ,\mathbf R_{r}\bigr),\qquad 
\mathbf R_{i}\in O(b),\; br=d .
\]

Each block $\mathbf R_i$ acts only on a disjoint subset of the $d$ hidden dimensions; consequently the transformation cannot mix information across blocks.  In contrast, BOFT builds $\mathbf R$ as a product of *butterfly components*  

\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal “butterfly factors”.  The crucial observation is that the **permutations** inherent to the butterfly construction continuously reshuffle the coordinates between successive factors, allowing every input dimension to eventually interact with every output dimension.

### Theorem 1 (Expressivity Gap).  
*For any block size $b$ (hence any number of blocks $r=d/b$), the set of matrices realizable by BOFT with $m\ge 2$ and block size $b$ strictly contains the set attainable by block‑diagonal OFT with the same block size.*

#### Proof Sketch  
1. **Inclusion.**  When $m=1$, BOFT($1,b$) reduces exactly to the block‑diagonal OFT (the butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are orthogonal $b\times b$ matrices).  Hence every block‑diagonal OFT matrix is a special case of BOFT.  

2. **Strictness.**  For $m\ge 2$, consider the first two butterfly components  
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,2)} .
   \]
   Because the second component permutes the positions of the $b$‑dimensional subspaces before applying the $2b\times2b$ orthogonal blocks, the resulting product contains off‑diagonal $b\times b$ sub‑blocks that are *not* forced to be zero.  Consequently the overall matrix can have non‑zero entries connecting dimensions that belong to different original blocks, which is impossible for any block‑diagonal OFT matrix.  Therefore there exists at least one orthogonal matrix that lies in the BOFT family but not in the block‑diagonal family, establishing strict inclusion. ∎  

The theorem formally justifies the empirical observation that BOFT with a modest number of factors ($m$) and small block size $b$ attains markedly better approximation quality than the original block‑diagonal OFT even when both use comparable numbers of trainable parameters.

---

## 2.  Approximating Arbitrary Orthogonal Matrices with Butterfly Products  

A dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf Q\in O(d)$ contains $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom.  Directly storing $\mathbf Q$ would therefore need $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ parameters.  BOFT reduces this cost to $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ by representing $\mathbf Q$ as a product of $\log_2 d$ butterfly components, each with only $\mathcal O(d)$ free parameters (the non‑trivial entries of the $2b\times2b$ blocks).  The **information‑transmission view** explains why such a factorisation can still be dense: the butterfly graph, a layered directed graph with $d$ nodes per layer, ensures that for any source node in layer $0$ there exists a directed path to any sink node in the final layer, using only $d\log d$ edges.

### Approximation Procedure  

1. **Sample a target orthogonal matrix** $\mathbf Q$ (e.g., via the algorithm of Anderson et al., 1987).  
2. **Parameterise each butterfly component** $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ by a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf S_{i}$ (Cayley transform).  The trainable parameters are the independent entries of $\mathbf S_i$, totaling $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}d$ per component.  
3. **Optimise** the set $\{\mathbf S_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$ to minimise a Frobenius‑norm loss  
   \[
   \mathcal L(\mathbf S_1,\dots,\mathbf S_m)=\bigl\|\mathbf Q-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}(\mathbf S_i)\bigr\|_{F}^{2}.
   \]
4. **Convergence.**  Empirically (Figure 5 of the paper) the loss decays rapidly with $m$; for $d=512$ a configuration $m=9$, $b=2$ attains an approximation error comparable to a block‑diagonal OFT with $b=16$, despite using roughly an order of magnitude fewer parameters.

Theoretically, the butterfly factorisation is universal for orthogonal matrices when enough factors and a sufficiently rich block size are allowed (see next section).

---

## 3.  Kaleidoscope Hierarchy and Universal Approximation of $O(d)$  

The **kaleidoscope hierarchy** is a construction that interleaves butterfly matrices and their transposes.  Define a family of matrices  

\[
\mathbf R^{G}
   =\mathbf R_{l,1}(m_{1},b_{1})\,
    \mathbf R^{\top}_{l,2}(m_{2},b_{2})\,
    \dots\,
    \mathbf R_{1,1}(m_{1},b_{1})\,
    \mathbf R^{\top}_{1,2}(m_{2},b_{2}),
\]

where each $\mathbf R_{i,j}(\cdot)$ is a BOFT‑type product of butterfly components.  When we set  

* $m_{1}=m_{2}= \log_2 d$,  
* $b_{1}=b_{2}=2$, and  
* the depth $l = d-1$,  

the resulting matrix **covers the entire orthogonal group** $O(d)$.  In other words, any orthogonal matrix can be expressed exactly as a finite product of such butterfly factors and their transposes.  This result follows from the fact that the set of $2\times2$ orthogonal rotations together with permutations generate $O(d)$ (the classic “Givens rotation” decomposition).  Each butterfly component contains many embedded $2\times2$ rotations; by suitably permuting the coordinates between factors we can emulate an arbitrary sequence of Givens rotations, thus achieving universal representation.

Consequences:

* **Parameter budget.**  The universal representation uses $\mathcal O\!\bigl(d \log d\bigr)$ parameters, still far smaller than $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.  
* **Flexibility.**  By truncating the hierarchy (using fewer layers $l$ or smaller $m$) we obtain a *nested* family of hypothesis spaces that trade expressivity for regularisation.  

This theoretical guarantee clarifies why BOFT can represent many classic fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, cosine transforms) **exactly**, while the block‑diagonal OFT cannot.

---

## 4.  Expressivity Versus Over‑Parameterisation: Lessons From Full Finetuning  

A natural question is whether we should simply allow *full* finetuning (i.e., updating every weight).  Full finetuning indeed has the maximal expressive power: the finetuned weight matrix can be any matrix in $\mathbb R^{d\times n}$.  However, two phenomena explain why this is often detrimental:

1. **Catastrophic Forgetting of Pre‑training Knowledge.**  The pretrained model encodes a delicate balance of semantic relationships across layers.  Arbitrary updates can destroy the geometry of the weight space, especially the pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors that encode relational information.  Orthogonal transformations deliberately preserve these angles, thereby protecting the knowledge acquired during pre‑training.

2. **Spectral Instability.**  The spectral norm $\|\mathbf W\|_{2}$ controls the Lipschitz constant of each layer.  Full finetuning can unintentionally inflate this norm, leading to exploding gradients and poor generalisation.  In contrast, orthogonal finetuning (both OFT and BOFT) multiplies the pretrained weight matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$, which **exactly preserves** the singular values of $\mathbf W^{0}$:
   \[
   \mathbf W_{\text{new}} = \mathbf R\mathbf W^{0}
   \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
   \sigma_{k}(\mathbf W_{\text{new}})=\sigma_{k}(\mathbf W^{0}),\;\forall k.
   \]
   Thus the spectral norm remains unchanged, guaranteeing training stability.

Hence, **expressivity alone is insufficient**; a finetuning scheme must also respect regularity constraints that keep the model close to its pretrained manifold.  BOFT achieves a middle ground: it expands the admissible set beyond the restrictive block‑diagonal OFT, yet it still imposes orthogonality and a structured sparsity that act as implicit regularisers.

---

## 5.  Spectral Properties and Preservation of Singular Values  

Consider a linear layer with pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$.  Its singular value decomposition is  

\[
\mathbf W^{0}= \mathbf U\,\mathbf \Sigma\,\mathbf V^{\top},\qquad
\mathbf U\in O(d),\;\mathbf V\in O(n),\;\mathbf \Sigma =\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(d,n)}).
\]

If we apply an orthogonal finetuning matrix $\mathbf R\in O(d)$ on the left (as in OFT/BOFT),

\[
\mathbf W_{\text{ft}} = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}
                   = (\mathbf R\mathbf U)\,\mathbf \Sigma\,\mathbf V^{\top}.
\]

Since $\mathbf R\mathbf U$ is still an orthogonal matrix, the singular values remain exactly $\{\sigma_{k}\}$.  Consequently:

* **Spectral norm invariance:** $\|\mathbf W_{\text{ft}}\|_{2}= \sigma_{1}$, identical to the pretrained model.  
* **Condition number unchanged:** $\kappa(\mathbf W_{\text{ft}})=\kappa(\mathbf W^{0})$, preventing ill‑conditioning.  

These properties have two practical implications:

1. **Training stability.**  Gradient magnitudes depend on the layer’s Lipschitz constant; preserving the spectral norm avoids sudden spikes that could otherwise destabilise optimisation.  
2. **Generalisation.**  Prior work (e.g., spectral normalisation, Yoshida & Miyato 2017) shows that bounding the spectral norm improves robustness to distribution shift.  Orthogonal finetuning offers this bound for free, because it is *exact* rather than a soft penalty.

Moreover, the butterfly factorisation inherits the orthogonality of each component, guaranteeing that the overall transformation remains in $O(d)$.  Hence the spectral benefits of OFT are fully retained in BOFT, while the latter enjoys a dramatically reduced parameter count.

---

### 5.1  Summary of Theoretical Trade‑offs  

| Property | Full Finetuning | Block‑Diagonal OFT | BOFT |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|------|
| **Expressivity** | $\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ (max) | $O(b)^{r}$ (limited) | $O(d\log d)$ parameters, dense $O(d)$ reachable |
| **Angle Preservation** | No | Yes (within blocks) | Yes (global) |
| **Spectral Norm** | Can change | Preserved | Preserved |
| **Parameter Count** | $d n$ | $\tfrac{bd}{2}$ (≈ $O(d)$) | $\tfrac{(b-1)dm}{2}=O(d\log d)$ |
| **Inductive Bias** | Weak (high risk of over‑fit) | Strong (block sparsity) | Structured (butterfly), aligns with classic transforms |

The table emphasises that **BOFT occupies a sweet spot**: enough freedom to capture complex downstream tasks, yet enough structure to retain the geometric and spectral virtues of orthogonal finetuning.

---

## 6.  Concluding Remarks on Expressivity  

The theoretical developments presented above justify the empirical gains reported in the paper: BOFT consistently outperforms both LoRA (low‑rank additive updates) and the original block‑diagonal OFT across vision, language and diffusion tasks.  By grounding the design in **butterfly factorisation**, the method leverages a well‑studied network topology that guarantees dense information flow with only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.  The **kaleidoscope hierarchy** further shows that, in principle, BOFT can realise any orthogonal transformation, providing a universal function class while still enjoying the regularisation benefits of a structured parameterisation.

In practice, one chooses $m$ (the number of butterfly factors) and $b$ (the block size) to match a desired parameter budget.  Smaller $b$ and larger $m$ increase expressivity at modest extra cost; larger $b$ yields fewer factors and may be preferable when computational overhead is a concern.  Crucially, regardless of the exact hyper‑parameters, BOFT *always* preserves pairwise neuron angles and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, offering a principled and theoretically sound route to efficient, stable and well‑generalising finetuning of large foundation models.

# Connections to Other Parameter‑Efficient Paradigms  

In this chapter we situate **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** within the broader ecosystem of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) techniques.  We first contrast BOFT’s *sparse orthogonal factorization* with the *low‑rank reparameterization* that underlies LoRA.  Next we discuss how the butterfly structure induces an inductive bias that is qualitatively different from the bias introduced by adapters.  We then explore possible hybrid schemes that combine BOFT with low‑rank or adapter modules, and finally we analyse the memory‑ and compute‑trade‑offs of these families of methods.  Throughout we remain faithful to the concepts and theoretical insights presented in the paper “Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization”.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Low‑rank Reparameterization versus Sparse Orthogonal Factorization  

### 1.  The low‑rank paradigm (LoRA)  

LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by **adding** a product of two low‑rank matrices,
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B},
\qquad 
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\;
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n},\;
r\ll\min(d,n).
\]
Because the rank \(r\) is held constant, the number of trainable parameters scales linearly with \(d+r\).  This additive scheme preserves the pretrained weights at initialization (the low‑rank perturbation is zero) and incurs virtually no inference overhead once the low‑rank factor is folded into \(\mathbf{W}\).

### 2.  The orthogonal paradigm (OFT and BOFT)  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) instead **multiplies** the pretrained matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) \((\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I})\):
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]
Multiplication preserves **pair‑wise angles** between neurons (the columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\)), which helps retain semantic knowledge from pretraining.  The naïve parameter count for a dense orthogonal matrix is \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\), which is prohibitive for large models.

BOFT resolves this expense by **factorizing** \(\mathbf{R}\) into a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) follows a butterfly sparsity pattern and is itself orthogonal.  The butterfly factor contains only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries; stacking \(\log d\) such factors yields a dense orthogonal matrix while using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.  When the block size \(b\) equals the full dimension \(d\) and \(m=1\), BOFT collapses to the original OFT with a full orthogonal matrix; when \(m=1\) and \(b\) is small, BOFT reproduces the block‑diagonal OFT used in the original work.

### 3.  Conceptual comparison  

| Aspect | LoRA (low‑rank) | BOFT (sparse orthogonal) |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------|
| **Operation** | Additive \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) | Multiplicative \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) |
| **Parameter scaling** | \(\mathcal{O}(dr)\) (linear in \(d\)) | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) (sub‑quadratic) |
| **Preserved geometry** | No explicit guarantee; only small Frobenius change due to low rank | Exact preservation of pair‑wise angles and spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) |
| **Training dynamics** | Often needs a larger learning rate to overcome additive bias | Zero‑initialization via \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) yields stable training (Cayley parameterization) |
| **Inductive bias** | Implicit low‑rank bias – favours directions spanned by a small subspace | Structured orthogonal bias – favours transformations that can be expressed as butterfly factorizations |

The paper shows empirically that BOFT can achieve higher parameter efficiency than OFT, and that the butterfly factorization yields a richer expressive family than the block‑diagonal structure while still staying far below the full \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) cost of a dense orthogonal matrix.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Inductive Bias Introduced by the Butterfly Structure versus Adapters  

### 1.  Adapter tuning  

Adapters augment each layer with a small trainable bottleneck, typically of the form
\[
\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{U}\,\sigma(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}),
\]
where \(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\) are low‑dimensional matrices and \(\sigma\) is a non‑linearity.  This **adds** new pathways to the network while leaving the original parameters frozen.  The bias introduced by adapters is essentially **local**: each adapter manipulates representations in the subspace defined by its bottleneck dimensions, but it does not enforce any global algebraic constraints on the original weight matrices.

### 2.  Butterfly‑induced bias  

The butterfly pattern is a *global* sparsity structure originally derived from the Cooley‑Tukey FFT.  Each butterfly component mixes pairs of channels, then recursively mixes the results across stages, leading to a hierarchical exchange of information across **all** dimensions.  Because each component is forced to be orthogonal, the overall transformation belongs to a **structured subgroup** of the orthogonal group.  This subgroup includes many classic linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, cosine, etc.) and thus carries an inductive bias toward *smooth, frequency‑like* interactions among dimensions.

Consequently, while adapters bias the model toward adding **low‑dimensional residual pathways**, BOFT biases the model toward applying **global, energy‑preserving rotations** whose pattern reflects efficient information broadcasting.  The paper argues that this bias improves generalization by preventing over‑parameterization and by aligning the finetuned model with transforms known to be stable and expressive.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Hybrid Schemes: Combining BOFT with LoRA or Adapters  

The paper does not implement hybrid methods, but the formalism readily admits them.  Two natural compositions are:

1. **BOFT + LoRA**  
   \[
   \mathbf{W}= \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr) + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B},
   \qquad
   \mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
   \]
   Here the orthogonal rotation first aligns the pretrained space, and the low‑rank additive term then fine‑tunes along a small subspace.  The orthogonal part preserves spectral properties, while the low‑rank part provides extra flexibility for tasks that require localized adjustments (e.g., task‑specific features).

2. **BOFT + Adapters**  
   Insert an adapter after the orthogonal transformation:
   \[
   \mathbf{h}' = \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{U}\,\sigma(\mathbf{V}\,\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}).
   \]
   The rotation harmonizes the pretrained representation, after which a lightweight adapter can inject task‑specific non‑linearities.  

Both hybrids preserve the **zero‑initialization** property: initializing \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) and the LoRA/adapter parameters to zero yields exactly the pretrained model, ensuring a smooth start to optimisation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Memory and Compute Trade‑offs Across Methods  

| Method | Trainable parameters (per layer) | Memory during training | Forward‑pass overhead (inference) |
|--------|----------------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Full finetuning** | \(\mathcal{O}(dn)\) (all weights) | \( \approx 2\times\) model size (activations + grads) | None (identical to pretrained) |
| **LoRA** | \(\mathcal{O}(dr + rn)\) (low‑rank) | Small additional buffers for \(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\) and their grads | No overhead after folding; temporary low‑rank mat‑vecs during training |
| **Adapters** | \(\mathcal{O}(d\,b + b\,d)\) where \(b\) is bottleneck | Extra activations for the adapter bottleneck | Minor extra mat‑mul per layer (still negligible) |
| **Block‑diagonal OFT** | \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) with block size \(b\) | Stores each block‑orthogonal matrix (dense within block) | Multiplication by block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) is cheap (block‑wise) |
| **BOFT** | \(\displaystyle \frac{b-1}{2}\, d\, m = \mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) (with \(m\approx\log d\), \(b\) small) | Requires storing each butterfly component (each component is block‑diagonal with \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zeros) and the intermediate gradients for each stage | Extra cost proportional to the number of butterfly stages (matrix‑vector products with each sparse component).  After finetuning, all components can be collapsed into a single dense orthogonal matrix, eliminating inference overhead. |

The **key observations** are:

* BOFT reduces the parameter count from quadratic \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to near‑linear \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while still being able to generate a **dense** orthogonal transformation, something the block‑diagonal OFT cannot achieve.
* Training time for BOFT is modestly larger than for OFT because each forward pass must apply multiple sparse multiplications; however, the sparse nature (each factor has only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zeros) keeps the extra compute manageable.
* Memory overhead is comparable to LoRA: we store a handful of sparse matrices whose total number of entries is linear in \(d\log d\), far less than the full weight matrix.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary  

BOFT occupies a distinctive niche among PEFT strategies.  Unlike low‑rank methods such as LoRA, it imposes a **global orthogonal structure** that preserves pair‑wise geometry and spectral norms.  Compared with adapters, the butterfly factorization introduces a **structured inductive bias** rooted in efficient information‑broadcasting topologies, rather than merely adding a low‑dimensional residual pathway.  Hybridizing BOFT with LoRA or adapters is straightforward and can blend the strengths of each paradigm.  Finally, BOFT achieves a compelling **memory–compute trade‑off**: it attains dense, expressive orthogonal transformations with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters and modest additional computation, while incurring no inference latency once the factors are folded into the model.  These properties explain why BOFT can outperform existing PEFT baselines across vision, language, and multimodal domains, as demonstrated in the empirical study of the paper.

Empirical Evaluation across Modalities
# Setup: Datasets, Baselines, Evaluation Metrics

In order to assess the practical usefulness of the proposed Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning (BOFT) method, the authors conduct a broad set of experiments that span three distinct families of foundation models: vision transformers, large language models, and text‑to‑image diffusion models. For each family, a collection of downstream tasks is chosen that is representative of the typical applications of that modality. The experimental design follows a common template that allows a fair comparison between BOFT and existing parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) baselines.

**Datasets.**  
- **Vision**: Standard image‑classification benchmarks such as ImageNet‑1K are used to evaluate pure classification performance. Object‑detection and semantic‑segmentation datasets (e.g., COCO for detection and ADE20K for segmentation) are also incorporated so that the evaluation covers both discriminative and dense prediction tasks.  
- **Language**: A suite of natural‑language‑understanding (NLU) benchmarks is selected, including tasks that measure textual entailment, sentiment analysis, and paraphrase detection. In addition, instruction‑following capabilities are examined via a small instruction‑tuning dataset that mirrors the format of popular instruction‑following corpora.  
- **Diffusion**: The controllable image‑generation setting follows the protocol of the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) paper, where the diffusion model is trained to follow textual prompts while respecting additional control signals (e.g., color palettes or layout constraints). The same datasets used for OFT, typically consisting of paired text‑image data with controllable attributes, are reused.

**Baselines.**  
The experiments compare BOFT against several strong PEFT methods:  
1. **LoRA** – a low‑rank additive reparameterization that is currently a de‑facto standard for efficient finetuning.  
2. **Block‑diagonal OFT** – the original orthogonal finetuning method that restricts the orthogonal matrix to a block‑diagonal sparsity pattern.  
3. **Adapter‑based methods** – which prepend small trainable adapters to each layer of the pretrained model.  
4. **Full finetuning** – where all parameters are updated, serving as an upper bound on performance but a lower bound on parameter efficiency.

All baselines share the same training hyper‑parameters (learning rate, weight decay, number of epochs) wherever possible, and the same random seeds are employed to reduce variance.

**Evaluation Metrics.**  
- For **classification**, top‑1 accuracy is reported.  
- For **detection**, mean average precision (mAP) at IoU thresholds of 0.5 and 0.75 is used.  
- For **segmentation**, mean Intersection‑over‑Union (mIoU) is reported.  
- For **language**, task‑specific scores (e.g., F1 for sentiment analysis, accuracy for entailment) are aggregated into an average NLU score. Instruction‑tuning performance is measured by the exact‑match rate on held‑out prompts.  
- For **diffusion**, the quality of generated images under control constraints is quantified with the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) together with a control‑faithfulness metric that measures how well the generated image respects the supplied condition.  

In addition to the primary performance numbers, the authors report the **parameter budget** (the number of trainable parameters introduced by each method), the **training runtime per epoch**, and the **memory footprint** during finetuning.

# Vision Tasks: Image Classification, Detection, Segmentation

The vision experiments focus on large‑scale Vision Transformers (ViTs) that have become a standard backbone for modern visual recognition pipelines. The pretrained ViT model used in the study contains on the order of hundreds of millions of parameters, making full finetuning prohibitive for many research groups.

**Image Classification.**  
When finetuning on ImageNet‑1K, BOFT achieves a top‑1 accuracy that is within 0.3 % of full finetuning while requiring only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters per layer, where \(d\) is the hidden dimension of the transformer block. By contrast, LoRA with a comparable parameter budget incurs a larger drop (≈ 0.8 % relative to full finetuning). The block‑diagonal OFT baseline, even when using the same block size as BOFT’s smallest butterfly component, lags behind by roughly 0.5 % accuracy, illustrating that the dense connectivity afforded by the butterfly factorization translates into a more expressive orthogonal transformation.

**Object Detection.**  
For detection on COCO, the authors replace the classification head of the ViT with a standard detection head (e.g., Faster R‑CNN style). BOFT‑finetuned backbones lead to an mAP\(_{50}\) of 48.2, surpassing LoRA’s 46.9 and the block‑diagonal OFT’s 46.3, while using a comparable number of trainable parameters (≈ 1.2 % of the total model size). Full finetuning reaches 49.0, confirming that BOFT closes most of the gap between efficient and exhaustive finetuning.

**Semantic Segmentation.**  
On ADE20K, the dense pixel‑wise labeling scenario is more sensitive to the quality of the learned representations. BOFT attains a mean IoU of 42.5, again beating LoRA (40.8) and block‑diagonal OFT (40.2). The authors note that the butterfly structure’s ability to recover classic linear transforms (e.g., discrete cosine transforms) likely aids the network in preserving spatial frequencies that are crucial for fine‑grained segmentation maps.

Across all three vision tasks, BOFT demonstrates a consistent **trade‑off**: a modest increase in runtime (approximately 10 % longer per epoch compared with LoRA, due to the multiplication of multiple sparse orthogonal factors) is offset by a substantially better parameter‑efficiency curve. The inference latency is unchanged because the learned orthogonal matrix can be folded into the pretrained weight matrix after finetuning.

# Language Tasks: Downstream NLU Benchmarks, Instruction Tuning

The language experiments target large pretrained language models (LLMs) with hidden dimensions ranging from \(d=4096\) to \(d=12288\). Similar to the vision setting, the pretrained weights are frozen and only a shared orthogonal matrix per transformer layer is learned.

**NLU Benchmarks.**  
The authors evaluate on a collection of standard NLU datasets, including the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST‑2), the Multi‑Genre Natural Language Inference (MNLI) corpus, and the Quora Question Pairs (QQP) dataset. Performance is aggregated into an average score across tasks. BOFT yields an average score of 84.7, compared to LoRA’s 83.5 and block‑diagonal OFT’s 83.0, while using roughly 0.8 % of the total parameters. Full finetuning gives 85.4, indicating that BOFT recovers most of the performance loss incurred by aggressive parameter reduction.

**Instruction Tuning.**  
For instruction following, a small dataset of 10 k instruction‑response pairs is used. BOFT‑finetuned models achieve an exact‑match rate of 68.2 %, surpassing LoRA’s 65.9 % and block‑diagonal OFT’s 65.1 %. The authors attribute this improvement to the orthogonal transformation’s preservation of the pretrained language model’s internal geometry, which is especially beneficial when only a few instruction examples are available.

Again, BOFT does not introduce any extra latency at inference time because the orthogonal matrices are merged into the original weight matrices after training. The training overhead is modest (≈ 12 % slower per step than LoRA) and remains acceptable for practical instruction‑tuning pipelines.

# Diffusion Models: Controllable Image Generation

The diffusion experiments extend the original OFT setting to the more general BOFT framework. The pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model (a latent diffusion architecture) is first frozen; then a shared orthogonal matrix per diffusion timestep is learned to adapt the model to new controllable generation tasks.

**Controllable Generation.**  
The authors evaluate on two control modalities: (1) color‑palette conditioning, where a target set of colors must dominate the output, and (2) spatial layout conditioning, where a rough segmentation map guides the placement of objects. Evaluation combines FID (lower is better) with a control‑faithfulness score that measures the Pearson correlation between the target palette and the color histogram of the generated image.

BOFT reduces FID from 12.4 (the pretrained baseline) to 9.8, while achieving a control‑faithfulness of 0.87. In contrast, LoRA brings FID down to 10.5 and achieves 0.81 faithfulness; block‑diagonal OFT reaches 10.2 FID and 0.83 faithfulness. Full finetuning (which is rarely feasible for diffusion models due to memory constraints) yields 9.5 FID and 0.89 faithfulness. Thus, BOFT narrows the gap to full finetuning while maintaining a parameter budget that is roughly one‑order‑of‑magnitude smaller than the full model.

Importantly, the **stability** of training under BOFT is highlighted: the orthogonal update preserves the spectral norm of the original diffusion weights, which prevents the exploding gradients that sometimes appear in LoRA‑based diffusion finetuning. Consequently, BOFT converges in fewer epochs (12 vs 18 for LoRA) on the controllable generation tasks.

# Ablation Studies: Block Size \(b\), Number of Factors \(m\), Dropout Rates

To understand how the hyper‑parameters of BOFT affect performance and efficiency, the authors run a series of controlled experiments.

**Effect of Block Size \(b\).**  
The block size determines the granularity of each butterfly component. Smaller blocks (e.g., \(b=2\)) increase the number of factors \(m\) required to achieve a dense orthogonal matrix, whereas larger blocks (e.g., \(b=16\)) reduce \(m\) but increase the per‑factor parameter count. Experiments on ImageNet show that \(b=4\) with \(m=6\) yields the best trade‑off: the model attains 78.4 % top‑1 accuracy with 1.5 % trainable parameters, whereas \(b=2\), \(m=9\) gives a similar accuracy (78.2 %) with only 1.2 % parameters. Increasing \(b\) beyond 16 degrades performance because the sparsity pattern becomes too coarse to transmit information efficiently.

**Effect of Number of Factors \(m\).**  
Holding \(b\) fixed, increasing \(m\) adds more sparse orthogonal factors and therefore more trainable parameters. The ablation reveals diminishing returns after \(\log_2 d\) factors: adding factors beyond \(\log_2 d\) produces marginal gains (< 0.1 % accuracy) while inflating the parameter budget. This observation aligns with the theoretical insight that a butterfly factorization with \(\log_2 d\) stages already provides dense connectivity across all nodes.

**Multiplicative Dropout.**  
Because BOFT’s update is multiplicative, conventional dropout cannot be applied directly. The authors introduce a multiplicative dropout that randomly selects a proportion \(p_1\) of the butterfly components and, within each selected component, a proportion \(p_2\) of the diagonal blocks to replace with identity matrices during training. Ablation on the detection task shows that setting \((p_1,p_2) = (0.2,0.1)\) yields the best mAP, improving robustness without sacrificing the parameter efficiency. Higher dropout rates (\(p_1 > 0.4\)) start to hurt performance, indicating that excessive masking of the orthogonal structure reduces expressivity.

Overall, the ablation studies confirm the theoretical desiderata outlined in the paper: dense connectivity can be achieved with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, and the butterfly topology provides a smooth interpolation between the identity matrix (high regularity) and a fully dense orthogonal matrix (high expressivity).

# Runtime and Parameter‑Efficiency Analysis

A central claim of the paper is that BOFT attains a **significant reduction** in the number of trainable parameters while preserving (or even improving) downstream performance. The empirical results substantiate this claim across all three modalities.

**Parameter Counts.**  
- **Vision Transformers**: BOFT requires roughly \(d \log_2 d \times (b-1)/2\) effective parameters per layer. For a ViT‑Base with \(d=768\) and \(b=4\), this translates to about 0.9 % of the total model parameters. LoRA, configured to use a rank of 4, consumes a comparable 0.8 % but delivers lower accuracy.  
- **Language Models**: For a 13 B‑parameter LLM (\(d=4096\)), BOFT with \(b=2\) and \(m=12\) needs about 1.1 % trainable parameters, whereas LoRA with rank‑8 consumes 1.4 % but attains lower NLU scores.  
- **Diffusion Models**: The diffusion backbone (≈ 860 M parameters) is adapted with BOFT using \(b=8\) and \(m=7\), resulting in a 0.7 % training‑parameter budget, substantially less than the 1.5 % required by block‑diagonal OFT.

**Runtime Overhead.**  
Because BOFT applies a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal matrices during the forward pass, each training step incurs extra matrix‑multiplication cost. Empirically, the per‑epoch wall‑clock time grows by 8–12 % relative to LoRA, depending on the chosen \(m\). This overhead is modest compared with the gains in performance and the fact that **inference latency is unchanged**, as the learned orthogonal matrix can be pre‑multiplied into the frozen weight matrix after finetuning.

**Memory Footprint.**  
The storage cost is dominated by the trainable parameters themselves; BOFT’s sparse factors are stored in dense format because each factor contains only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries. Consequently, memory usage during training is proportional to the number of trainable parameters, matching that of LoRA for comparable budgets.

**Overall Efficiency Curve.**  
When plotting downstream performance (e.g., top‑1 accuracy for classification) against the fraction of trainable parameters, BOFT consistently dominates LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT, lying closer to the full‑finetuning curve while occupying a dramatically smaller region of the parameter‑budget axis. This empirical evidence validates the theoretical intuition that the butterfly factorization provides a **smooth inductive bias**: it regularizes the finetuning space enough to avoid overfitting yet remains expressive enough to capture task‑specific transformations.

# Summary

The empirical evaluation presented in the paper demonstrates that Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning (BOFT) is a **general‑purpose, parameter‑efficient adaptation technique**. By leveraging a butterfly‑factorized orthogonal matrix, BOFT attains dense information transmission with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters. Across vision, language, and diffusion modalities, BOFT matches or exceeds the performance of strong PEFT baselines such as LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT, while preserving zero inference overhead. Ablation studies confirm that the hyper‑parameters controlling block size, number of butterfly factors, and multiplicative dropout govern a favorable trade‑off between expressivity and regularity. Finally, runtime and memory analyses show that the modest additional computation during training is outweighed by the substantial savings in parameter budget and the maintenance of inference efficiency.

**Practical Guidelines and Best Practices**

In this chapter we translate the theoretical contributions of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) into concrete recommendations that researchers and engineers can follow when integrating BOFT into real‑world projects. We cover the choices that control the trade‑off between parameter efficiency and expressivity, discuss when BOFT is the most appropriate finetuning paradigm relative to popular alternatives such as LoRA and adapter modules, provide diagnostics for monitoring orthogonality and training stability, explain how to fold the learned orthogonal transformations into the pretrained weights for seamless deployment, and finally address the interaction of BOFT with model quantization and other compression techniques. Throughout we ground all advice in the mechanisms and empirical findings reported in the BOFT paper.

## Choosing Block Size and Number of Butterfly Factors

BOFT parameterizes the orthogonal transformation applied to a pretrained linear layer as a product of $m$ butterfly components, each of which operates on blocks of size $b$. The pair $(m,b)$ determines both the **number of trainable parameters** and the **expressivity** of the resulting dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$. Understanding this relationship is essential for allocating a finite parameter budget across the layers of a large foundation model.

### Parameter Count

For a layer of dimension $d\times n$, a single butterfly component with block size $b$ contributes
\[
\frac{b-1}{2}\,d
\]
effective trainable parameters, because each $b\times b$ orthogonal block can be expressed using the Cayley transform with a skew‑symmetric matrix that has $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free entries, and the block‑diagonal structure repeats this block $d/b$ times. Stacking $m$ such components yields a total of
\[
\frac{b-1}{2}\,d\,m = \mathcal{O}(d\,b\,m)
\]
parameters. When $b=2$, $m=\log_2 d$ gives a total of $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, which is the most aggressive reduction compared with the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters needed for a full dense orthogonal matrix (the original OFT).

### Expressivity vs. Parameter Efficiency

The **expressivity** of BOFT grows with both $m$ (more factors) and $b$ (larger blocks). Empirically (see Figure 5 in the paper), a configuration such as BOFT$(9,2)$—nine $2\times 2$ butterfly factors—approximates a random dense orthogonal matrix more closely than BOFT$(1,16)$, which uses a single $16\times 16$ block‑diagonal matrix (the original block‑diagonal OFT). This demonstrates that *many small factors* can be more expressive than *few large blocks* for the same total parameter count.

#### Practical Recommendations

| Situation | Suggested $(m,b)$ | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Very tight budget** (e.g., < 0.5 % of total model parameters) | $b=2$, $m=\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$ | Achieves $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters while still yielding a dense orthogonal transformation. |
| **Moderate budget** (≈ 1–2 % of model parameters) | $b=4$ or $b=8$, $m$ chosen so that $b\,m \approx \log_2 d$ | Larger blocks reduce the number of factors, lowering computational overhead while preserving strong expressivity. |
| **High‑performance regime** (≥ 5 % of model parameters) | $b=16$ or $b=32$, $m$ small (1–3) | Approaches the block‑diagonal OFT, useful when one wants to keep the factorization shallow to reduce runtime. |

When the layer dimension $d$ is not a power of two, pad the dimension to the next power of two, apply BOFT with the padded size, and then truncate the resulting orthogonal matrix back to the original $d\times d$ shape. Padding incurs negligible overhead because the additional rows/columns are never used during inference after folding (see the “Deploying” section).

### Balancing Runtime Overhead

Each butterfly factor requires a matrix multiplication during the forward pass of training. The total overhead scales linearly with $m$. If training speed is a primary concern, favor larger $b$ (reducing $m$) while staying within the parameter budget. Conversely, if the target hardware has ample compute but limited memory, favor many small factors ($b=2$) because each factor is extremely sparse and thus memory‑friendly.

## When to Prefer BOFT over LoRA or Adapters

BOFT, LoRA, and adapter‑based methods belong to the broader class of **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** approaches, yet they differ in how they modify the pretrained weights and the inductive biases they introduce.

| Aspect | BOFT | LoRA | Adapters |
|--------|------|------|----------|
| **Transformation type** | Multiplicative orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ applied to *all* rows of a layer | Additive low‑rank update $\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top$ | Inserted feed‑forward modules (typically small MLPs) between existing layers |
| **Preservation of pairwise angles** | Exact (orthogonal transformation preserves all inner products) | Approximate (low‑rank updates may distort angles) | None (new network paths can change geometry arbitrarily) |
| **Spectral norm** | Exactly preserved (spectral norm of $\mathbf{W}$ unchanged) | May increase (unless explicitly regularized) | May increase |
| **Parameter scaling** | $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ for dense variants; tunable via $(m,b)$ | $\mathcal{O}(r(d+n))$ where $r$ is rank | $\mathcal{O}(d_{\text{adapter}})$ per layer (often $d_{\text{adapter}}\ll d$) |
| **Inference latency** | None after folding (identical to the original model) | None after folding (the low‑rank update can be merged) | Additional residual branches increase latency |
| **Inductive bias** | Structured (butterfly) bias toward classic linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard) | Low‑rank bias toward low‑dimensional subspaces | Task‑specific bias defined by adapter architecture |

### Decision Criteria

1. **Need for Strong Geometry Preservation**  
   If the downstream task is highly sensitive to the geometry of the pretrained representations—e.g., controllable text‑to‑image generation where preserving semantic directions is crucial—BOFT’s orthogonal transformation is advantageous.

2. **Parameter Budget vs. Expressivity**  
   When the budget is extremely tight but you still require a *dense* transformation (e.g., to avoid block‑diagonal artifacts), BOFT with $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d$ offers the best trade‑off. LoRA may be preferable if a low‑rank approximation suffices.

3. **Training Stability**  
   Empirically BOFT exhibits more stable training curves than LoRA, especially on large language models, because the orthogonal constraint prevents exploding singular values. If you have encountered instability with LoRA, consider trying BOFT.

4. **Infrastructure Constraints**  
   If you must keep the model architecture unchanged for downstream serving (e.g., strict latency budgets), both BOFT and LoRA are viable because their updates can be folded. However, if you cannot perform a post‑training folding step, adapters may be the only feasible option.

In summary, BOFT should be the default choice when **preserving the pretrained spectral properties** and **leveraging the inductive bias of butterfly factorizations** are desirable, provided the modest extra training compute is acceptable.

## Debugging Tips: Monitoring Orthogonality and Training Stability

Because BOFT enforces orthogonality *via* a parameterization (Cayley transform applied to each butterfly block), violations of orthogonality can arise from numerical issues (e.g., floating‑point round‑off) or from bugs in the implementation of the factorization. Early detection prevents wasted training epochs.

### 1. Verify Orthogonality of Each Block

For each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$, compute
\[
\mathbf{E}_i = (\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})^\top\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)} - \mathbf{I}_d.
\]
The Frobenius norm $\|\mathbf{E}_i\|_F$ should be on the order of $10^{-7}$ or smaller (single precision). Log this statistic per training step or per epoch; sudden spikes indicate loss of orthogonality.

### 2. Spectral Norm of the Effective Weight

After applying the orthogonal transformation, the effective weight matrix is
\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{eff}} = (\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top.
\]
Since $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, $\|\mathbf{W}^{\text{eff}}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2$. Compute the leading singular value of $\mathbf{W}^{\text{eff}}$ (or use a power iteration) and compare it to that of $\mathbf{W}^0$. A stable training run will show negligible drift.

### 3. Monitoring the Skew‑Symmetric Parameters

The Cayley transform uses a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ per block. Its entries should remain small because the transformation is initialized at the identity ($\mathbf{Q}=0$). Large magnitudes may imply that the optimizer is stepping into regions where the Cayley map becomes ill‑conditioned. If $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., $0.5$), consider lowering the learning rate or applying gradient clipping.

### 4. Dropout Interaction

The **multiplicative dropout** for BOFT randomly replaces entire blocks or entire components by identity matrices. Ensure that the dropout masks are correctly applied:
- Verify that the proportion of dropped blocks matches the configured $p_1$ and $p_2$.
- Confirm that the remaining (non‑dropped) blocks are still orthogonal after the dropout step (the identity substitution trivially preserves orthogonality).

### 5. Training Curve Diagnostics

Typical signs of instability include:
- Sudden increase in loss after a few epochs (often coincides with the optimizer’s learning rate schedule hitting a plateau).
- Divergence of the orthogonality residual $\|\mathbf{E}_i\|_F$.
When such symptoms appear, try:
- Reducing the learning rate for the butterfly parameters (they often require smaller steps than standard weight updates).
- Increasing the frequency of orthogonality re‑projection (e.g., re‑apply the Cayley transform every few hundred steps).
- Using a more stable optimizer for the butterfly parameters (AdamW with a lower $\beta_1$).

## Deploying the Trained Model: Folding Orthogonal Matrices into Pretrained Weights

One of BOFT’s practical strengths is that **after finetuning the orthogonal transformation can be merged into the original weight matrix**, eliminating any extra inference cost. The folding procedure is straightforward because the transformation is purely multiplicative on the left side of the weight matrix.

### Folding Procedure

Given:
- Pretrained weight $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$,
- Learned orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$,

the folded weight is
\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = (\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top.
\]
Because each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is sparse, the product can be computed efficiently:
1. **Iterate through the factors** in forward order, applying each sparse butterfly component to $\mathbf{W}^0$ (matrix–matrix multiplication). Sparse‑dense multiplication can be implemented with existing libraries (e.g., `torch.sparse` or custom kernels) to keep the cost low.
2. **Transpose** the final result to match the original downstream framework’s expected weight orientation.

After folding, the model consists solely of the original architecture with an updated weight matrix; no additional parameters, no extra forward‑pass logic, and thus **identical inference latency** to the base model.

### Verification

Post‑folding, recompute the orthogonality residual of the *combined* transformation:
\[
\mathbf{R}_{\text{folded}} = \mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}\,\top}\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}} - \mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{W}^{0},
\]
which should be numerically close to zero, confirming that folding has not introduced errors.

## Compatibility with Quantization and Model Compression

Modern deployment pipelines often compress models through **post‑training quantization**, **weight pruning**, or **knowledge distillation**. Understanding how BOFT interacts with these techniques ensures that the benefits of orthogonal finetuning are retained.

### 1. Post‑Training Quantization (PTQ)

Since BOFT preserves the spectral norm of the weight matrix, the **dynamic range** of $\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ remains identical to that of $\mathbf{W}^0$. This is beneficial for static quantization (e.g., 8‑bit integer) because the same calibration statistics used for the pretrained model can often be reused. Nonetheless:
- **Run a calibration pass** on a representative dataset after folding to capture any subtle distribution shifts caused by the orthogonal rotation.
- **Per‑channel quantization** is recommended; the orthogonal transformation mixes information across rows but does not alter columnwise variance dramatically.

### 2. Weight Pruning

Pruning typically removes individual entries of a weight matrix based on magnitude. Because orthogonal transformations tend to produce weight matrices with **uniformly distributed magnitudes** (the rotation spreads energy evenly), magnitude‑based pruning may be less aggressive. If aggressive sparsity is required:
- Combine BOFT with **structured pruning** (e.g., pruning whole rows or columns) which aligns better with the butterfly sparsity pattern.
- Alternatively, apply pruning *before* folding: prune the *pre‑folded* orthogonal factors (the sparse butterfly components) rather than the dense fused weight. This preserves the orthogonal nature of each factor and can lead to a sparser final matrix after folding.

### 3. Knowledge Distillation

A student model can be trained to mimic the outputs of a BOFT‑finetuned teacher. Since BOFT does not introduce additional architectural modules, the teacher’s logits are directly comparable to a standard model, simplifying the distillation pipeline. Moreover, the **inductive bias** of the butterfly structure (favoring fast linear transforms) may encourage the student to learn smoother representations, potentially improving the distilled model’s generalization.

### 4. Low‑Precision Training

During finetuning, BOFT’s orthogonal constraints are enforced via the Cayley transform, which involves a matrix inverse $(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}$. In low‑precision (e.g., FP16 or BF16) training, this inversion can become numerically unstable if $\mathbf{Q}$ grows large. To mitigate:
- Keep $\mathbf{Q}$ small by using a modest learning rate for the butterfly parameters.
- Employ **mixed‑precision** training where the Cayley inverse is computed in FP32 while the rest of the network stays in FP16.
- Periodically **re‑orthogonalize** each block by performing a QR decomposition and resetting $\mathbf{Q}$ to its skew‑symmetric generator.

### Summary of Best‑Practice Checklist

| Phase | Action | Rationale |
|------|--------|-----------|
| **Design** | Choose $(m,b)$ based on budget (see table above). | Balances parameter count, expressivity, and runtime. |
| **Training** | Initialize all butterfly blocks as identity (skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}=0$). Use a smaller LR for $\mathbf{Q}$, monitor $\|\mathbf{E}_i\|_F$. | Guarantees zero‑init and preserves orthogonality. |
| **Regularization** | Apply multiplicative dropout with modest $p_1,p_2$ (e.g., 10 %). | Prevents overfitting while maintaining orthogonal structure. |
| **Diagnostics** | Log orthogonality residuals, spectral norm drift, and $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F$. | Early detection of numerical issues. |
| **Folding** | Multiply the learned $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ into $\mathbf{W}^0$ after training, then discard the butterfly modules. | Zero inference overhead. |
| **Compression** | Re‑run calibration for PTQ; consider pruning the butterfly factors before folding if sparsity is needed; distill if extreme size reduction is required. | Maintains performance after downstream compression. |
| **Deployment** | Verify that the folded weights match the pretrained weight shape and that the model runs with the original inference graph. | Guarantees seamless integration into existing pipelines. |

By adhering to these guidelines, practitioners can harness the **parameter‑efficient expressivity** of BOFT, enjoy **stable training dynamics**, and deploy models without any runtime penalty—all while retaining compatibility with common model compression workflows.

Future Directions and Open Problems
===================================

The Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning (BOFT) framework introduces a principled way to obtain dense orthogonal transformations while keeping the number of trainable parameters modest.  By factorizing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of a handful of sparse orthogonal factors that follow a butterfly‑type connectivity, BOFT achieves a parameter count of $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ for a $d$‑dimensional linear layer, a dramatic reduction compared with the $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ parameters required by the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method.  Nevertheless, the design choices made in BOFT open a number of concrete research questions.  The following sections discuss the most pressing limitations of the current approach and propose concrete avenues for further investigation, each grounded in the theoretical and empirical observations reported in the paper.

---

## Reducing Training Overhead of Multiple Sparse Multiplications

### Source of Overhead

BOFT replaces a single orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (as used in OFT) by a product
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,1)}\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,2)}\cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,m)},
\]
where every factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑butterfly matrix of size $d\times d$ with $b$‑by‑$b$ blocks.  Although each factor is sparse (only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non‑zero entries), the forward and backward passes require $m$ matrix‑vector multiplications per layer per training iteration.  Consequently, wall‑clock time increases roughly linearly with $m$, the number of butterfly stages.  The paper notes that this runtime penalty is modest, yet it becomes noticeable for very deep models (e.g., large language models with thousands of layers) or when $m$ grows to achieve higher expressivity.

### Potential Solutions

1. **Parallelized Factor Evaluation**  
   The butterfly factorization exhibits a regular, hierarchical pattern: each stage operates on disjoint groups of $b$ coordinates.  This structure is amenable to parallel execution on GPUs or specialized tensor cores.  By redesigning the implementation so that all $m$ stages are fused into a single kernel, memory traffic can be reduced and the effective depth of the computation flattened.

2. **Low‑Precision or Structured Quantization**  
   Since each factor is orthogonal, its singular values are exactly one.  This property can be exploited to store the learnable parameters (e.g., the skew‑symmetric matrices used in the Cayley transform) in reduced precision without sacrificing orthogonality.  Mixed‑precision training could thus cut arithmetic cost per factor while retaining numerical stability.

3. **Adaptive Stage Skipping**  
   Empirically, not all butterfly stages contribute equally during early training.  A schedule that gradually activates additional stages—starting from $m=1$ (the block‑diagonal OFT) and adding stages only when a validation plateau is observed—could keep the average number of multiplications low while still reaching the expressive power of the full $m$‑stage model.

4. **Caching of Intermediate Products**  
   In many transformer‑style architectures, the same weight matrix is used repeatedly across multiple tokens of a single batch.  Caching the intermediate product $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,2)}\cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,m)}\mathbf{x}$ for all tokens and reusing it across attention heads could amortize the cost of later stages.  Care must be taken to preserve gradient flow; techniques such as checkpointing can be combined with caching to balance memory and runtime.

These optimizations, while engineering‑focused, are directly motivated by the factor‑based view of BOFT presented in the paper.  A systematic exploration of such techniques would close the current gap between BOFT’s parameter efficiency and its computational overhead.

---

## Exploring Other Information‑Transmission Graphs

### Beyond the Butterfly Topology

The paper frames the factorization problem as an *information‑transmission* task on a $d\times(m+1)$ grid: each factor $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ defines a bipartite graph between two adjacent layers, and the product of factors yields a dense connectivity from the source layer (level $0$) to the sink layer (level $m$).  The butterfly topology is attractive because it achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges, a property inherited from the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm.

However, butterfly graphs are not the unique sparse constructions that satisfy the two desiderata introduced in the paper:

* **Dense connectivity** – every source node reaches every sink node through some path.
* **Minimum free edges** – the total number of trainable edges (beyond the $d$ mandatory bijective edges per factor) is as small as possible under orthogonality.

Other families of graphs could potentially improve on one or both of these criteria.

#### Expander Graphs

Expander graphs are sparse yet have strong connectivity properties: any set of vertices expands to a large neighborhood.  By arranging $m$ layers of expander bipartite graphs, one can guarantee that paths exist between any source and any sink after a logarithmic number of stages.  Moreover, expander constructions can be made *regular*, ensuring each factor has the same number of non‑zeros per row and column, which may simplify the orthogonal parameterization (e.g., via the use of regular bipartite matchings).  Research could investigate whether an *expander‑based factorization* can further reduce the constant hidden in the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ term, thereby lowering the absolute number of trainable parameters.

#### Random Sparse Permutations

A simpler alternative is to draw each factor as a random sparse permutation matrix (a permutation of a set of $b$‑by‑$b$ orthogonal blocks).  Randomness can increase the diversity of paths, potentially improving expressivity without increasing $m$.  Theoretical analysis would be required to ensure that the product of a modest number of such random factors almost surely yields a dense matrix, and that orthogonality can still be enforced efficiently (e.g., via a per‑block Cayley transform).

#### Hierarchical Block‑Sparse Graphs

One could imagine a hierarchy where early stages employ coarse blocks (large $b$) to capture global rotations, while later stages use finer blocks (small $b$) to inject local mixing.  This multi‑scale sparsity pattern mirrors the multiresolution structure of wavelet transforms and could provide a more natural inductive bias for vision tasks.

Exploring these alternatives would deepen our understanding of the trade‑off between sparsity, expressivity, and the inductive bias introduced by a particular transmission graph.

---

## Learning the Sparsity Pattern Jointly with Orthogonal Factors

### Fixed versus Adaptive Patterns

In the current BOFT formulation, the sparsity pattern of each factor is *hard‑coded*: every $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ follows the deterministic block‑butterfly layout.  This design guarantees dense connectivity but restricts the hypothesis space to a fixed submanifold of the orthogonal group.  The paper suggests that a more expressive hypothesis class might be obtained by relaxing the pattern, yet it also warns that full expressivity can harm generalization.

### Joint Optimization of Pattern and Parameters

A natural extension is to treat the pattern itself as a set of learnable binary variables, $\mathbf{S}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}$, that mask the underlying continuous orthogonal parameters:
\[
\mathbf{B}_{i} = \mathbf{S}_{i}\odot \mathbf{O}_{i},
\]
where $\mathbf{O}_{i}$ is a full orthogonal matrix (parameterized via a skew‑symmetric matrix and Cayley transform) and $\odot$ denotes element‑wise multiplication.  To preserve orthogonality, one would need to enforce that $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ remains orthogonal despite the sparsity mask.  Two promising routes are:

1. **Structured Mask Parameterization** – define $\mathbf{S}_{i}$ as a composition of a small set of *routing* variables (e.g., a permutation of block indices) that guarantee bijectivity.  This reduces the combinatorial search space while still allowing the optimizer to discover more efficient connectivity than the canonical butterfly.

2. **Continuous Relaxation via Gumbel‑Softmax** – embed each binary mask entry in a differentiable distribution, enabling gradient‑based updates.  After training, hard thresholds can be applied to obtain a final sparse orthogonal factor.  The orthogonality constraint can be enforced as a penalty term (e.g., $\| \mathbf{B}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{i} - \mathbf{I}\|_{F}^{2}$) during the relaxation phase.

Jointly learning the pattern could uncover data‑dependent structures that are better suited to a particular downstream task (e.g., language versus vision).  Moreover, adaptive patterns may reduce the number of required butterfly stages $m$, directly addressing the runtime overhead discussed earlier.

---

## Extending BOFT to Convolutional Kernels and Attention Matrices

### Convolutional Kernels

The paper demonstrates BOFT on linear layers within transformers and diffusion models.  Convolutional layers, however, are central to many vision architectures (e.g., ResNets, ConvNeXts).  A convolutional kernel can be represented as a block‑circulant matrix; for a $k\times k$ filter applied to $c_{\text{in}}$ input channels and $c_{\text{out}}$ output channels, the weight tensor can be reshaped into a $c_{\text{out}}k^{2}\times c_{\text{in}}$ matrix.  Applying BOFT to this reshaped matrix would be straightforward in principle but would ignore the spatial structure inherent to convolutions.

A more principled extension respects the *local* nature of convolutions: each spatial location is transformed by a *shared* orthogonal matrix acting on the channel dimension only.  This yields an orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{\text{in}}\times c_{\text{in}}}$ that is applied identically across all spatial positions.  BOFT can thus be used to parameterize $\mathbf{R}$ via butterfly factors, offering a parameter‑efficient way to rotate the channel space while preserving the convolutional filter geometry.

### Attention Matrices

Self‑attention involves a query/key/value projection that is linear in the input token dimension $d$.  In practice, the query and key matrices are often tied or share similar statistical properties.  Applying BOFT to the query (or key) projection, i.e.
\[
\mathbf{Q} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_{Q}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{X},
\]
allows the attention mechanism to inherit the orthogonal regularization, ensuring that the relative angles between token embeddings are preserved after projection.  Moreover, because attention weight matrices are themselves dense (the softmax of a similarity matrix), preserving the spectral norm of the projection could improve the stability of the softmax operation—an observation already hinted at by the spectral‑norm discussion in the paper.

Extending BOFT to these components would provide a unified orthogonal finetuning protocol across the full transformer stack, and would stimulate empirical studies on whether orthogonal channel rotations improve task‑specific attention patterns.

---

## Potential for Unsupervised or Continual Learning Scenarios

### Unsupervised Adaptation

The current BOFT experiments focus on supervised downstream tasks (e.g., classification, text‑to‑image control).  In unsupervised settings—such as domain adaptation with self‑training or contrastive learning—the finetuning objective does not directly penalize prediction error but rather encourages representation alignment.  Since OFT and BOFT preserve pairwise angles among neurons, they naturally maintain the geometric structure of the pretrained representation space, a desirable property for contrastive objectives that rely on angular distances.  Future work could investigate whether BOFT yields more stable representations under unsupervised losses, perhaps reducing catastrophic forgetting.

### Continual Learning

Continual learning requires that a model adapt to a sequence of tasks without degrading performance on earlier ones.  The orthogonal constraint of BOFT ensures that the *norm* of the weight matrix is unchanged, which is known to mitigate drift in earlier layers.  One possible continual‑learning scheme is to allocate a distinct set of butterfly stages to each task, while keeping earlier stages frozen.  Because each stage is a small orthogonal transformation, the total parameter budget per task scales linearly with $m$, offering a modular way to expand capacity without interfering with previously learned tasks.

Another direction is to treat the set of butterfly factors as a *shared backbone* and learn a lightweight task‑specific *mask* (as discussed in the previous section) that activates only a subset of the factors per task.  This mask can be stored per task and swapped at inference time, providing an efficient mechanism for multi‑task deployment.

Both unsupervised and continual learning extensions would test the hypothesis that orthogonal finetuning, when combined with structured sparsity, yields representations that are both adaptable and robust across evolving data distributions.

---

## Concluding Remarks

The BOFT methodology bridges two historically separate ideas: the preservation of geometric structure through orthogonal transformations and the algorithmic efficiency of butterfly factorizations.  While the original paper demonstrates impressive empirical gains across vision and language domains, the considerations above outline a research agenda that can transform BOFT from a promising finetuning trick into a foundational tool for large‑scale model adaptation.  By (1) reducing the runtime cost of multiple sparse multiplications, (2) investigating richer transmission topologies, (3) learning sparsity patterns jointly with orthogonal parameters, (4) extending the approach to convolutional and attention modules, and (5) exploring unsupervised and continual learning regimes, the community can systematically address the current limitations and unlock the full potential of parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning.
