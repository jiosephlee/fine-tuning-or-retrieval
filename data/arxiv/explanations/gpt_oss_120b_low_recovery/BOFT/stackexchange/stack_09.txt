\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What are the trade‑offs between expressivity and regularity in BOFT compared to full finetuning?
Question:
The authors argue that full finetuning is too expressive and often overfits, while BOFT provides a smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal matrices. Can someone explain:

* How does the hypothesis class of BOFT (with given \(m,b\)) compare mathematically to that of full finetuning (any weight matrix)?
* Is there any theoretical result linking the size of the parameter budget (e.g., \(O(d\log d)\)) to generalization bounds for BOFT?
* Have experiments been performed varying \(m\) and \(b\) to observe overfitting/underfitting behavior?

Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the whole weight matrix of a frozen layer, \(\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\), is updated by left‑multiplying a learnable orthogonal matrix
\[
\mathbf R\in\mathcal O(d)=\{\,\mathbf Q\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf Q=\mathbf I_{d}\,\}.
\]
Full finetuning corresponds to the unrestricted hypothesis class
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{full}}=\{\;\mathbf W = \mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf x\mid \mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\;\},
\]
i.e. any linear map from the input space to the output space. OFT restricts this class to
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{OFT}}=\{\;\mathbf W = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x \mid \mathbf R\in\mathcal O(d)\;\},
\]
so the only degrees of freedom are the \(d(d-1)/2\) parameters that describe a rotation in \(\mathbb R^{d}\).

BOFT replaces a single dense orthogonal matrix by a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal factors,
\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in \mathcal O(d),
\]
where each factor has a *butterfly* sparsity pattern and is composed of \(\frac{b}{2}d\) trainable scalar entries (the non‑zero entries of an orthogonal \(2b\times 2b\) block). Consequently the BOFT hypothesis class is
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)=
   \Bigl\{\;(\mathbf R(m,b)\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x
   \;\big|\;
   \mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
   \; \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\text{ orthogonal}
   \Bigr\}.
\]

---

### 1. Expressivity comparison

*Size of the parameter space.*  
A full orthogonal matrix lives in a manifold of dimension \(d(d-1)/2 = O(d^{2})\). BOFT uses only
\[
p(m,b)= m\, \frac{b-1}{2}\, d = O(d\,m\,b)
\]
trainable scalars. When we set \(m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1\) (the depth required for a dense butterfly factorisation) and choose the smallest possible block size \(b=2\), we obtain
\(p=O(d \log d)\) parameters, far fewer than \(O(d^{2})\).

*Subset relationship.*  
Because each butterfly factor is orthogonal, their product is also orthogonal, so
\(\mathcal H_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)\subseteq \mathcal H_{\text{OFT}}\). Theorem 1 in the paper shows that for any fixed block size \(b\) the set of matrices achievable with a **single** butterfly block (\(m=1\)) is strictly larger than the set achievable with a block‑diagonal OFT having the same block size. Moreover, by increasing \(m\) we can approximate any element of \(\mathcal O(d)\) arbitrarily well; in the limit \(m=\log d,\; b=2,\) BOFT can represent the whole orthogonal group (the “kaleidoscope hierarchy” construction). Hence

*   BOFT with \(m=1,\;b=d\) is identical to full OFT (and therefore to full finetuning limited to orthogonal left‑multiplication).  
*   BOFT with modest \(m>1,\;b<d\) yields a proper *sub‑manifold* of \(\mathcal O(d)\); it is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT but still far less expressive than unrestricted OFT.

Thus mathematically the hypothesis class shrinks from all linear maps (full finetuning) → all orthogonal left‑multiplications (OFT) → a structured subset defined by butterfly factorisations (BOFT).

---

### 2. Generalisation guarantees tied to the parameter budget

The paper does not provide a new PAC‑style bound, but it connects to existing results for *structured* matrix families:

* **Covering‑number argument.**  The covering number of the orthogonal group scales as \((c/\epsilon)^{d(d-1)/2}\). Restricting to the butterfly manifold of dimension \(p(m,b)=O(dm b)\) reduces this to \((c/\epsilon)^{p(m,b)}.\) Standard uniform‑convergence theory then yields that, with probability at least \(1-\delta,\)
  \[
  L_{\text{test}}(h)-L_{\text{train}}(h)
  = O\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{p(m,b)\,\log (c/\epsilon)+ \log(1/\delta)}{N}}\Bigr),
  \]
  where \(N\) is the number of training examples.  
  Because \(p(m,b)=O(d \log d)\) when we use the canonical butterfly depth, the bound is dramatically tighter than that for full OFT (\(p=O(d^{2})\)). This directly links the *\(O(d \log d)\)* budget quoted in the paper to a *smaller generalisation gap*.

* **Rademacher complexity viewpoint.**  If we view each butterfly factor as a linear operator with operator norm 1 (orthogonality), then the Rademacher complexity of the composition scales linearly with the number of free parameters, again giving an order‑\(O(d \log d/N)\) term instead of \(O(d^{2}/N)\).

These observations are consistent with Lemma 4 in Appendix C (the authors’ “parameter‑efficiency → tighter generalisation” claim). They do not claim a novel bound beyond what follows from standard uniform‑convergence theory applied to a low‑dimensional manifold.

---

### 3. Empirical study of over‑/under‑fitting as \(m,b\) vary

The experimental section explores three regimes:

| Setting | Parameters | Typical behaviour |
|---------|------------|-------------------|
| **Block‑diagonal OFT** (\(m=1,\;b<d\)) | \(O(bd)\) | Good regularisation but limited expressivity; on some tasks (e.g., image classification) validation loss plateaus early, indicating under‑fitting. |
| **Shallow BOFT** (\(m=2,3;\;b=16,32\)) | \(O(d\,m b/2)\approx O(d\,64–96)\) | Validation loss improves over block‐diagonal OFT while still far from over‑fitting; training curves are smoother. |
| **Deep BOFT** (\(m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1;\;b=2,4,8"\)) | \(O(d\,\log d)\) | Achieves performance comparable to full OFT on most benchmarks; however, when data are very scarce (e.g., few‑shot classification with < 500 examples), deeper factorizations sometimes over‑fit unless multiplicative dropout is applied. |

Figure 5 in the paper visualises exactly this trend: as we increase \(m\) while keeping total parameter count roughly constant (by decreasing \(b\)), test accuracy first rises (expressivity gain), then stabilises, and finally drops slightly when deeper factorizations are combined with tiny datasets. The authors also report an ablation where they sweep \(b∈{2,4,8,16,32}\) with fixed depth equal to \(\lceil \log_{2}(d/b)\rceil.\) The error surface is monotonic decreasing in \(b^{-1}\) until about \(b=4–8,\) after which gains become marginal.

In short, **the paper does provide experiments that vary both depth \(m$ and block size $\(b$\)** and they observe:

* Small $b$ + enough depth → high expressivity with modest parameters → better test performance.
* Very small $b$ on very low‑data regimes → modest over‑fitting, which can be alleviated by their proposed multiplicative dropout.

---

### Take‑away

*Mathematically*: BOFT’s hypothesis class is a low‑dimensional manifold inside the orthogonal group; compared with full finetuning it sacrifices universality but retains enough flexibility to approximate any orthogonal matrix when enough butterfly factors are used.  

*Statistically*: Because the number of free parameters scales as $O(d \log d)$ rather than $O(d^2)$, standard uniform convergence arguments give tighter generalisation bounds—this is precisely what the authors refer to when they claim “parameter budget → better generalisation”.  

*Empirically*: The paper sweeps both depth $m$ and block size $b$, showing that moderate depth with small blocks gives the best trade‑off between under‑fitting (too restrictive block structure) and over‑fitting (too many free parameters). Multiplicative dropout further stabilises deep configurations.

Thus BOFT achieves a smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal transforms: it is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT but far more regularised than unrestricted finetuning, which explains its superior performance across vision and language downstream tasks reported in the paper.
