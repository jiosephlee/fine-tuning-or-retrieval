\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations of Orthogonal Transformations
=====================================================

In this chapter we develop the linear‑algebraic tools that underlie **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its parameter‑efficient extension **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**.  The methods presented in the paper rely on the ability to manipulate and reason about orthogonal (or unitary) matrices, to parameterize them in a differentiable way, and to understand the consequences of left‑multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal transformation.  We therefore introduce the necessary definitions, the Cayley transform used to enforce orthogonality during optimisation, and several geometric and spectral properties that explain why orthogonal finetuning tends to be stable and to preserve the knowledge encoded in a large foundation model.

---

## Orthogonal, Unitary, and Skew‑Symmetric Matrices

Let $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the $d$‑dimensional real vector space and $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ the space of real $d\times d$ matrices.  A matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is **orthogonal** if its columns (and rows) form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{d},
\]

where $\mathbf{I}_{d}$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix.  The set of all $d$‑dimensional orthogonal matrices forms the **orthogonal group** $\mathcal{O}(d)$; it is a compact Lie group equipped with a natural manifold structure.

When the entries are complex, the analogous notion is a **unitary** matrix $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{d}$, where $\dagger$ denotes the conjugate transpose.  All results that depend only on the algebraic conditions $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ remain valid for unitary matrices after replacing transpose with conjugate transpose.

A matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is **skew‑symmetric** if $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$.  Skew‑symmetry implies that all diagonal entries are zero and that $\mathbf{Q}$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues (or zero).  The space of skew‑symmetric matrices is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(d)$ of the orthogonal group.  Every element of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ that is not a reflection can be expressed as the exponential of a skew‑symmetric matrix; this observation motivates the **Cayley transform** used in the paper.

---

## Cayley Parameterization and Its Properties

Direct optimisation over the orthogonal group is difficult because the orthogonality constraints are nonlinear.  The paper adopts the **Cayley transform** to embed a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ into an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\qquad\text{with}\quad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}.
\tag{1}
\]

Several important properties follow from this definition:

1. **Orthogonality is guaranteed.**  
   Multiplying (1) by its transpose yields  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
   = \big[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\big]
     (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
   = \mathbf{I},
   \]
   because $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$ makes $(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top} = \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$.

2. **One‑to‑one mapping on a dense open subset.**  
   The map $\mathbf{Q}\mapsto \mathbf{R}$ is smooth and invertible wherever $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ is nonsingular (which holds for all sufficiently small $\mathbf{Q}$).  This permits a **zero‑initialisation** strategy: setting $\mathbf{Q}=0$ yields $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

3. **Parameter count.**  
   A $d\times d$ skew‑symmetric matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ independent entries, half the number of parameters of an unrestricted $d\times d$ matrix.  When additional sparsity patterns—such as block‑diagonal or butterfly structures—are imposed on $\mathbf{Q}$, the effective number of trainable parameters is reduced further, which is the core idea behind parameter‑efficient OFT and BOFT.

Because gradient‑based optimisation can be performed on the unconstrained parameters of $\mathbf{Q}$, the Cayley transform provides a practical way to learn orthogonal matrices while never leaving $\mathcal{O}(d)$ during training.

---

## Preservation of Pairwise Angles and Singular Values

OFT and BOFT rely on the geometric observation that **orthogonal transformations preserve all inner products**.  For any two column vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\rangle
= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u})^{\top}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v})
= \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}
= \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
= \langle \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle.
\]

Consequences:

* **Angles are unchanged.**  
  The cosine of the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$,
  $\cos\theta = \frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}$,
  remains identical after applying $\mathbf{R}$, because both numerator and denominator are invariant.

* **Lengths are unchanged.**  
  $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|$, a direct corollary of orthogonality.

When a pretrained linear layer is written as $\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, its columns $\{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ are precisely the “neurons’’ whose relational geometry we wish to keep.  The finetuned weight matrix in OFT/BOFT is

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) guarantees that **all pairwise angles among the columns of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ are preserved** in $\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}$.  The paper argues that this preservation helps the finetuned model retain the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining, because many learned representations are encoded in the relative orientation of neurons rather than their absolute positions.

Moreover, the singular values of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ are also preserved.  Let $\mathbf{W}^{0} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ be the singular value decomposition (SVD), where $\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}$ are orthogonal and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with non‑negative entries $\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(d,n)}$.  Multiplying on the left by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ yields

\[
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}
= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top},
\]

and $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is again orthogonal.  Hence the singular values $\sigma_{i}$ are **exactly unchanged**.  This property is central to the discussion of spectral norm invariance.

---

## Spectral Norm Invariance under Orthogonal Left‑Multiplication

The **spectral norm** of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$, denoted $\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}$, is the largest singular value of $\mathbf{A}$.  Because orthogonal left‑multiplication does not alter singular values (see previous section), we have

\[
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2} = \|\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2}.
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) has two practical implications for finetuning large models:

1. **Training stability.**  
   Gradient descent on deep networks can be sensitive to the magnitude of weight matrices; exploding or vanishing spectral norms can cause instability.  By construction, OFT and BOFT keep the spectral norm of every linear layer exactly at its pretrained value, eliminating one source of instability.

2. **Generalization.**  
   Recent theoretical and empirical work (cited in the paper) suggests that controlling the spectral norm of weight matrices improves generalization, because it bounds the Lipschitz constant of the network.  Since the orthogonal transformation does not increase the spectral norm, finetuning with OFT/BOFT inherits this regularising effect.

Thus, orthogonal finetuning can be viewed as a **norm‑preserving re‑parameterisation** of the pretrained model, offering a principled alternative to additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA, which do not guarantee spectral norm preservation.

---

## Implications for Training Stability and Generalization

Putting together the previous results, we can articulate why orthogonal finetuning—and especially the butterfly‑based, parameter‑efficient variant—tends to be robust:

* **Zero‑initialisation and smooth optimisation.**  
  The Cayley transform maps the zero matrix to the identity, so the optimisation starts exactly at the pretrained point.  Small updates to the skew‑symmetric parameters correspond to small orthogonal rotations, ensuring that each training step stays within a neighbourhood of the original model.

* **Preservation of geometry.**  
  Because all pairwise angles between neurons are unchanged, the feature space explored during pretraining is not distorted.  This alleviates catastrophic forgetting and enables the model to adapt to new tasks without “unlearning’’ useful representations.

* **Spectral norm control.**  
  Equation (3) guarantees that the Lipschitz constant of each linear layer does not increase, reducing the risk of gradient explosion and improving the smoothness of the loss landscape.  Empirically, the paper observes more stable convergence for OFT/BOFT compared with LoRA.

* **Inductive bias from structured sparsity.**  
  BOFT achieves the same geometric guarantees while drastically reducing the number of free parameters (from $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$) by factorising the orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse butterfly components.  The butterfly factorisation encodes a specific pattern of information exchange that is known to be capable of representing many classic linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.).  This structured bias limits the hypothesis space to a rich yet regular subset of $\mathcal{O}(d)$, which the paper argues improves generalisation on downstream tasks.

* **Weight interpolation property.**  
  Because the orthogonal matrix is built as a product of components, one can smoothly interpolate between the identity (pretrained) and the learned transformation by scaling the underlying skew‑symmetric parameters.  This provides a natural way to trade off between retaining pretrained knowledge and adapting to the new task, further contributing to stable training dynamics.

In summary, the mathematical foundations presented in this chapter—the definition of orthogonal matrices, the Cayley transform, and the invariance of angles and singular values—explain why orthogonal finetuning is a principled, stable, and generalizable approach.  The butterfly‑based parameterisation builds on these foundations to achieve practical parameter efficiency without sacrificing the core geometric and spectral properties that make orthogonal finetuning effective.
