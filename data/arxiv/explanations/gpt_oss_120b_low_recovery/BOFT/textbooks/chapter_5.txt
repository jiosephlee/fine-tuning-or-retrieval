\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 5: **Butterfly Structures and Fast Fourier Transform**

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) is perhaps the most celebrated algorithm in numerical computing.  Its efficiency stems from a very particular sparse factorization of the dense discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix, a factorization that can be visualized as a *butterfly graph*.  In this chapter we unpack the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly diagram, describe the algebraic form of its factor matrices, and explain why this structure provides an optimal information‑transmission topology for constructing dense orthogonal matrices with only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.  Although the FFT was originally invented for signal processing, the same butterfly factorization has been repeatedly leveraged in numerical linear algebra, matrix‑vector multiplication, and, most recently, in parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning (BOFT).

---

### Recursive FFT decomposition and butterfly diagram

The discrete Fourier transform maps a vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to its frequency representation \(\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{F}_{d}\mathbf{x}\), where \(\mathbf{F}_{d}\) is the dense \(d\times d\) Fourier matrix.  The Cooley‑Tukey algorithm observes that when \(d\) is a power of two, the DFT can be expressed recursively as two DFTs of size \(d/2\) applied to the even‑ and odd‑indexed entries of \(\mathbf{x}\).  Formally,
\[
\mathbf{F}_{d}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{I}_{d/2} & \mathbf{I}_{d/2}\\[2pt]
\mathbf{I}_{d/2} & -\mathbf{I}_{d/2}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\text{first butterfly layer}}
\;
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{F}_{d/2} & \mathbf{0}\\[2pt]
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{F}_{d/2}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\text{recursive sub‑DFTs}}
\; .
\]
The first matrix is sparse: each row contains only two non‑zero entries, a “+1’’ and a “±1’’ that connect a pair of inputs to a pair of outputs.  When this decomposition is applied recursively, the overall multiplication \(\mathbf{F}_{d}\mathbf{x}\) can be represented as a cascade of \(\log_2 d\) such sparse layers.  The wiring of these layers is what is commonly drawn as the **butterfly diagram**: nodes representing the components of the vector are placed on \(\log_2 d + 1\) vertical levels, and each layer draws edges that join pairs of nodes in a crossing pattern reminiscent of a butterfly’s wings.

From an information‑transmission viewpoint, each edge of the diagram conveys a linear combination of two source nodes to two destination nodes.  After \(\log_2 d\) layers, every source node can influence every destination node; that is, the composite matrix is dense even though each individual layer is extremely sparse.

---

### Butterfly factor definition and block‑diagonal composition

The sparse layer introduced above can be abstracted as a **butterfly factor**.  For a block size \(k\) that is itself a power of two, define
\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k) \;=\;
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\tag{1}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) is a vector of free parameters.  The four diagonal blocks are placed in the same layout as the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly; each row of \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) has exactly two non‑zero entries, one in the left half and one in the right half.  When \(k=2\), \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(2)\) reduces to a generic \(2\times2\) matrix, and the constraint that the overall factor be orthogonal can be imposed by a Cayley transform (or by a simple rotation angle).

A full **butterfly matrix** of dimension \(d=2^{N}\) is built by stacking several butterfly factors of varying block sizes in a block‑diagonal fashion.  Define the *butterfly component*
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)
\;=\;
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{1}(k),\,
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{2}(k),\dots,
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{d/k}(k)
\bigr) 
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\tag{2}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{B}^{F}_{i}(k)\) is an independent copy of the factor (1).  The component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) therefore contains exactly \(\frac{d}{k}\times 2\frac{k}{2}=d\) non‑zero entries—precisely the minimal number required for a full‑rank, orthogonal mapping between the \(d\) nodes at level \(\ell\) and those at level \(\ell+1\).

The butterfly matrix is the ordered product of such components with decreasing block sizes:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d) \;=\;
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/4)\,
\cdots\,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
\tag{3}
\]
Because each factor is orthogonal (by construction of its \(2\times2\) sub‑blocks), the product is also orthogonal.  The recursion in (3) mirrors exactly the recursive FFT decomposition: the first factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\) mixes pairs of neighboring indices, the next factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,4)\) mixes the resulting pairs across a distance of two, and so on, until the final factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\) mixes across the full width of the vector.

---

### Properties: \(O(d\log d)\) edges, full connectivity, invertibility

#### Sparse edge count

Each component \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) contains exactly \(d\) non‑zero entries, independent of \(k\).  Since the product in (3) comprises \(\log_2 d\) components, the total number of *trainable* scalar parameters is proportional to
\[
\underbrace{d}_{\text{edges per layer}}\times\underbrace{\log_2 d}_{\text{layers}} \;=\; O(d\log d).
\]
This is a dramatic reduction compared with a fully dense orthogonal matrix, which would require \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}=O(d^{2})\) independent parameters (because orthogonal matrices lie on a manifold of dimension \(d(d-1)/2\)).  The butterfly factorization therefore achieves **dense connectivity**—every input can affect every output after the full product—while only storing a number of parameters that grows quasi‑linearly in \(d\).

#### Full connectivity (dense information transmission)

The butterfly topology guarantees that for any source node at level 0 there exists a path to any destination node at level \(\log_2 d\).  This can be proved by induction on the block size: the first component mixes each node with its immediate neighbor; the second component mixes the resulting pairs across a distance of two, thereby connecting each node to every other node within a block of size four; continuing this process doubles the reachable set at each step, and after \(\log_2 d\) steps the reachable set is the entire set of \(d\) nodes.  Consequently, the composite matrix is *dense*: every entry of \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is generally non‑zero (except for pathological parameter choices).

#### Orthogonal invertibility

Because every factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is orthogonal, their product is orthogonal as well:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)^{\top}\mathbf{B}(d)=\mathbf{I}_{d}.
\]
An orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms, inner products, and singular values.  From a learning perspective, this property ensures that finetuning a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with a left‑multiplication by \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) does not perturb the spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  In the context of orthogonal finetuning, such preservation stabilizes training and mitigates catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge.

---

### Historical uses of butterfly matrices in numerical linear algebra

The butterfly factorization first emerged as a structural description of the Cooley‑Tukey FFT in the 1960s.  Soon after, researchers recognized that the same sparse product could be employed to **accelerate linear algebraic operations**:

1. **Fast matrix–vector multiplication** – By representing a dense linear transform as a butterfly product, one can multiply a vector in \(O(d\log d)\) time rather than \(O(d^{2})\).  This idea underlies fast algorithms for the DFT, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform, each of which can be written as a butterfly matrix possibly followed by a permutation.

2. **Efficient Gaussian elimination** – Parker (1995) introduced butterfly matrices as preconditioners that avoid pivoting, exploiting the fact that each factor is orthogonal and therefore numerically stable.

3. **Stabilization of recurrent networks** – Jing et al. (2017) used parametrized butterfly matrices to enforce orthogonal recurrent weights, yielding long‑term gradient flow while keeping the number of parameters modest.

4. **Kernel approximation** – Munkhoeva et al. (2018) applied butterfly factorizations to construct fast, structured random features for approximating shift‑invariant kernels.

5. **Network compression and training from scratch** – More recent works (e.g., Dao et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022) have shown that replacing dense weight layers with a butterfly product can dramatically reduce memory usage while preserving expressive power, especially when the target transform is known to have a fast algorithmic structure.

In the domain of **parameter‑efficient finetuning**, the butterfly factorization is harnessed in a novel way: instead of reparameterizing each weight matrix directly, a *single* orthogonal butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is learned to multiply the frozen pretrained matrix from the left.  This constitutes the **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** method.  BOFT inherits all the benefits listed above—dense information transmission, orthogonal invertibility, and a quasi‑linear parameter budget—while providing a principled inductive bias for downstream tasks.

---

In summary, the Cooley‑Tukey butterfly graph is more than a clever diagram for the FFT; it is a universal sparsity pattern that enables the construction of dense orthogonal matrices from a limited set of trainable edges.  By interpreting each sparse factor as a layer of information exchange on a grid‑structured graph, we see why the butterfly topology achieves full connectivity with only \(O(d\log d)\) parameters.  This insight is the cornerstone of modern parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning, where butterfly matrices serve both as efficient computational kernels and as structured regularizers that promote stability and generalization.
