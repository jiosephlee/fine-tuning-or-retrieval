\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 9: # Connections to Other Parameter‑Efficient Paradigms  

In this chapter we situate **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** within the broader ecosystem of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) techniques.  We first contrast BOFT’s *sparse orthogonal factorization* with the *low‑rank reparameterization* that underlies LoRA.  Next we discuss how the butterfly structure induces an inductive bias that is qualitatively different from the bias introduced by adapters.  We then explore possible hybrid schemes that combine BOFT with low‑rank or adapter modules, and finally we analyse the memory‑ and compute‑trade‑offs of these families of methods.  Throughout we remain faithful to the concepts and theoretical insights presented in the paper “Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization”.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Low‑rank Reparameterization versus Sparse Orthogonal Factorization  

### 1.  The low‑rank paradigm (LoRA)  

LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by **adding** a product of two low‑rank matrices,
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B},
\qquad 
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\;
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n},\;
r\ll\min(d,n).
\]
Because the rank \(r\) is held constant, the number of trainable parameters scales linearly with \(d+r\).  This additive scheme preserves the pretrained weights at initialization (the low‑rank perturbation is zero) and incurs virtually no inference overhead once the low‑rank factor is folded into \(\mathbf{W}\).

### 2.  The orthogonal paradigm (OFT and BOFT)  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) instead **multiplies** the pretrained matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) \((\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I})\):
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]
Multiplication preserves **pair‑wise angles** between neurons (the columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\)), which helps retain semantic knowledge from pretraining.  The naïve parameter count for a dense orthogonal matrix is \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\), which is prohibitive for large models.

BOFT resolves this expense by **factorizing** \(\mathbf{R}\) into a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) follows a butterfly sparsity pattern and is itself orthogonal.  The butterfly factor contains only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries; stacking \(\log d\) such factors yields a dense orthogonal matrix while using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.  When the block size \(b\) equals the full dimension \(d\) and \(m=1\), BOFT collapses to the original OFT with a full orthogonal matrix; when \(m=1\) and \(b\) is small, BOFT reproduces the block‑diagonal OFT used in the original work.

### 3.  Conceptual comparison  

| Aspect | LoRA (low‑rank) | BOFT (sparse orthogonal) |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------|
| **Operation** | Additive \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) | Multiplicative \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) |
| **Parameter scaling** | \(\mathcal{O}(dr)\) (linear in \(d\)) | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) (sub‑quadratic) |
| **Preserved geometry** | No explicit guarantee; only small Frobenius change due to low rank | Exact preservation of pair‑wise angles and spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) |
| **Training dynamics** | Often needs a larger learning rate to overcome additive bias | Zero‑initialization via \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) yields stable training (Cayley parameterization) |
| **Inductive bias** | Implicit low‑rank bias – favours directions spanned by a small subspace | Structured orthogonal bias – favours transformations that can be expressed as butterfly factorizations |

The paper shows empirically that BOFT can achieve higher parameter efficiency than OFT, and that the butterfly factorization yields a richer expressive family than the block‑diagonal structure while still staying far below the full \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) cost of a dense orthogonal matrix.

## Inductive Bias Introduced by the Butterfly Structure versus Adapters  

### 1.  Adapter tuning  

Adapters augment each layer with a small trainable bottleneck, typically of the form
\[
\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{U}\,\sigma(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}),
\]
where \(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\) are low‑dimensional matrices and \(\sigma\) is a non‑linearity.  This **adds** new pathways to the network while leaving the original parameters frozen.  The bias introduced by adapters is essentially **local**: each adapter manipulates representations in the subspace defined by its bottleneck dimensions, but it does not enforce any global algebraic constraints on the original weight matrices.

### 2.  Butterfly‑induced bias  

The butterfly pattern is a *global* sparsity structure originally derived from the Cooley‑Tukey FFT.  Each butterfly component mixes pairs of channels, then recursively mixes the results across stages, leading to a hierarchical exchange of information across **all** dimensions.  Because each component is forced to be orthogonal, the overall transformation belongs to a **structured subgroup** of the orthogonal group.  This subgroup includes many classic linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, cosine, etc.) and thus carries an inductive bias toward *smooth, frequency‑like* interactions among dimensions.

Consequently, while adapters bias the model toward adding **low‑dimensional residual pathways**, BOFT biases the model toward applying **global, energy‑preserving rotations** whose pattern reflects efficient information broadcasting.  The paper argues that this bias improves generalization by preventing over‑parameterization and by aligning the finetuned model with transforms known to be stable and expressive.

## Hybrid Schemes: Combining BOFT with LoRA or Adapters  

The paper does not implement hybrid methods, but the formalism readily admits them.  Two natural compositions are:

1. **BOFT + LoRA**  
   \[
   \mathbf{W}= \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr) + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B},
   \qquad
   \mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
   \]
   Here the orthogonal rotation first aligns the pretrained space, and the low‑rank additive term then fine‑tunes along a small subspace.  The orthogonal part preserves spectral properties, while the low‑rank part provides extra flexibility for tasks that require localized adjustments (e.g., task‑specific features).

2. **BOFT + Adapters**  
   Insert an adapter after the orthogonal transformation:
   \[
   \mathbf{h}' = \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{U}\,\sigma(\mathbf{V}\,\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}).
   \]
   The rotation harmonizes the pretrained representation, after which a lightweight adapter can inject task‑specific non‑linearities.  

Both hybrids preserve the **zero‑initialization** property: initializing \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) and the LoRA/adapter parameters to zero yields exactly the pretrained model, ensuring a smooth start to optimisation.

## Memory and Compute Trade‑offs Across Methods  

| Method | Trainable parameters (per layer) | Memory during training | Forward‑pass overhead (inference) |
|--------|----------------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Full finetuning** | \(\mathcal{O}(dn)\) (all weights) | \( \approx 2\times\) model size (activations + grads) | None (identical to pretrained) |
| **LoRA** | \(\mathcal{O}(dr + rn)\) (low‑rank) | Small additional buffers for \(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\) and their grads | No overhead after folding; temporary low‑rank mat‑vecs during training |
| **Adapters** | \(\mathcal{O}(d\,b + b\,d)\) where \(b\) is bottleneck | Extra activations for the adapter bottleneck | Minor extra mat‑mul per layer (still negligible) |
| **Block‑diagonal OFT** | \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) with block size \(b\) | Stores each block‑orthogonal matrix (dense within block) | Multiplication by block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) is cheap (block‑wise) |
| **BOFT** | \(\displaystyle \frac{b-1}{2}\, d\, m = \mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) (with \(m\approx\log d\), \(b\) small) | Requires storing each butterfly component (each component is block‑diagonal with \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zeros) and the intermediate gradients for each stage | Extra cost proportional to the number of butterfly stages (matrix‑vector products with each sparse component).  After finetuning, all components can be collapsed into a single dense orthogonal matrix, eliminating inference overhead. |

The **key observations** are:

* BOFT reduces the parameter count from quadratic \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to near‑linear \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while still being able to generate a **dense** orthogonal transformation, something the block‑diagonal OFT cannot achieve.
* Training time for BOFT is modestly larger than for OFT because each forward pass must apply multiple sparse multiplications; however, the sparse nature (each factor has only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zeros) keeps the extra compute manageable.
* Memory overhead is comparable to LoRA: we store a handful of sparse matrices whose total number of entries is linear in \(d\log d\), far less than the full weight matrix.

### Summary  

BOFT occupies a distinctive niche among PEFT strategies.  Unlike low‑rank methods such as LoRA, it imposes a **global orthogonal structure** that preserves pair‑wise geometry and spectral norms.  Compared with adapters, the butterfly factorization introduces a **structured inductive bias** rooted in efficient information‑broadcasting topologies, rather than merely adding a low‑dimensional residual pathway.  Hybridizing BOFT with LoRA or adapters is straightforward and can blend the strengths of each paradigm.  Finally, BOFT achieves a compelling **memory–compute trade‑off**: it attains dense, expressive orthogonal transformations with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters and modest additional computation, while incurring no inference latency once the factors are folded into the model.  These properties explain why BOFT can outperform existing PEFT baselines across vision, language, and multimodal domains, as demonstrated in the empirical study of the paper.
