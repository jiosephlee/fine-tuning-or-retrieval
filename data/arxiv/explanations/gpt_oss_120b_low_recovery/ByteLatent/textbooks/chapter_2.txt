\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 2: # From Bytes to Patches: Dynamic Segmentation Strategies  

In this chapter we explore the core idea that enables the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** to allocate computation where it is needed most: the conversion of a raw byte stream into *patches* of variable size.  We begin by formalising what a patching function is and why its behaviour determines the number of transformer steps – and therefore the FLOP budget – of the model.  We then examine several concrete patching schemes, ranging from naïve fixed‑stride grouping to linguistically motivated space‑based segmentation, and finally to the data‑driven entropy‑based method that lies at the heart of BLT.  Along the way we discuss how the choice of threshold (global versus relative) and a monotonicity constraint shape the resulting patches, and we clarify the crucial property of **incremental patching** that tokenizers such as BPE violate.  Understanding these concepts equips the reader to appreciate why patches “scale better than tokens’’ and how they make BLT both efficient and robust.  

---  

## Formal Definition of a Patching Function and Its Impact on FLOPs  

Consider a raw byte sequence  

\[
\mathbf{x}= \{x_1,x_2,\dots ,x_n\},
\]

where each \(x_i\) belongs to the byte vocabulary \(\mathcal V=\{0,1,\dots ,255\}\).  A *patching function*  

\[
f_p : \mathcal V^{n} \rightarrow \{0,1\}^{n}
\]

maps each position \(i\) to a binary indicator \(b_i\).  By definition  

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if a new patch starts at byte } i,\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

The locations where \(b_i=1\) delimit the patches.  If the set of start indices is  

\[
\mathcal S = \{\, s_1=1 < s_2 < \dots < s_m \,\},
\]

then the \(j\)-th patch is the subsequence  

\[
p_j = \{\, x_{s_j}, x_{s_j+1},\dots , x_{s_{j+1}-1}\,\},
\qquad
\text{with } s_{m+1}=n+1 .
\]

Thus the patching function produces a *patch sequence*  

\[
\mathbf{p}= \{p_1,p_2,\dots ,p_m\},
\qquad m < n .
\]

The **average patch size** is  

\[
\bar{L}= \frac{n}{m},
\]

and it directly controls the number of *global transformer steps* that the BLT’s latent transformer must execute: one step per patch.  Assuming the rest of the architecture (local encoder/decoder) has a cost that grows linearly with the number of bytes, the dominant term in the total FLOP count for a forward pass is  

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{global}} \propto m \,\,\times\,\, \text{cost per transformer layer}.
\]

Consequently, larger patches (larger \(\bar L\)) reduce the number of global steps and therefore the FLOP budget, while smaller patches increase flexibility because each patch corresponds to a finer‑grained computational unit.  The design of \(f_p\) is therefore a trade‑off between **efficiency** (large patches) and **adaptivity** (small patches where the data are hard to predict).

## Static (Strided) Patching and Its Drawbacks  

The simplest instantiation of \(f_p\) is *strided* or *fixed‑size* patching, also called *static patching*.  Here we choose a stride \(k\in \mathbb N\) and set  

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } (i-1) \bmod k = 0,\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

Every patch therefore contains exactly \(k\) bytes, except possibly the last one.  The average patch size \(\bar L\) is precisely \(k\), and the number of patches is \(\lceil n/k\rceil\).  

### Advantages  

* **Implementation simplicity.**  The mapping from bytes to patches is a pure arithmetic operation; it incurs no preprocessing cost.  
* **Predictable compute.**  Since \(\bar L = k\) is fixed, the FLOP budget can be set analytically before training, which simplifies scaling studies.  

### Limitations  

1. **No data‑driven allocation.**  All patches receive the same amount of model capacity, irrespective of the intrinsic difficulty of predicting the next byte.  For example, predicting a whitespace after a period is trivial, yet the global transformer still spends a full step on it; conversely, predicting the first character of a rare proper noun receives no extra attention.  
2. **Inconsistent tokenisation of identical substrings.**  The same word may be split across two patches in one context but remain within a single patch in another, depending on its alignment with the fixed stride.  This destroys the inductive bias that identical substrings should receive identical representations.  
3. **Inefficiency on highly compressible regions.**  In code or markup, long runs of predictable characters (e.g., a series of indentation spaces) still consume \(k\) bytes per global step, wasting FLOPs.  

Because the dominant FLOP cost of a large language model lies in its feed‑forward networks – which are executed for every global step – static patching cannot reap the efficiency gains that a more *adaptive* segmentation would enable.

## Space‑Based Patching and Linguistic Intuition  

A modest improvement over static patching is to use *space‑like* bytes as natural boundaries.  Define a set  

\[
\mathcal S_{\text{space}} = \{\, b\in\mathcal V \mid b\text{ is not a Latin letter, digit, or UTF‑8 continuation byte}\,\}.
\]

A *space patch* begins after every occurrence of a byte in \(\mathcal S_{\text{space}}\).  Formally,  

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } x_{i-1}\in\mathcal S_{\text{space}} \text{ and } i>1,\\[4pt]
1 & \text{if } i=1,\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

This scheme respects the intuition that **words** (or other linguistic tokens delimited by spaces) often concentrate the *prediction difficulty*: the first character of a word is usually the most uncertain, while subsequent characters become increasingly predictable due to strong intra‑word dependencies.  

### Benefits  

* **Semantic consistency.**  The same word will always be confined to a single patch, regardless of where it appears, because the patch boundaries are aligned with spaces.  
* **Targeted compute.**  Each patch corresponds to a word, so the global transformer is invoked precisely when a high‑entropy decision (the first character) must be made.  

### Shortcomings  

* **Language‑specific bias.**  Space‑based segmentation works well for languages that use visible whitespace (e.g., English) but fails for scripts without spaces (e.g., Chinese) or for domains where other delimiters dominate (e.g., source code where symbols are more informative).  
* **Inflexible patch size.**  Patch length is dictated solely by the occurrence of space‑like bytes; there is no mechanism to merge several short words into a larger patch when the surrounding context is easy, nor to split a long, information‑dense word into multiple patches.  

Hence, while space‑based patching captures a useful linguistic prior, it does not fully exploit the *dynamic* nature of information density across a document.

## Entropy‑Based Patching Using a Small Byte‑Level LM  

To overcome the heuristic limitations of the previous schemes, BLT adopts a *data‑driven* approach: patches are created wherever the **next‑byte entropy** is high.  The procedure relies on a lightweight byte‑level language model – called the *entropy model* – that estimates the distribution  

\[
p_e\bigl(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}\bigr), \qquad v\in\mathcal V .
\]

The **entropy** at position \(i\) is then  

\[
\boxed{
H(x_i)= -\sum_{v\in\mathcal V} p_e\bigl(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}\bigr)\,
\log p_e\bigl(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}\bigr)
}
\tag{1}
\]

(We omit the minus sign in the paper’s notation, but the absolute value is the conventional Shannon entropy.)  High values of \(H(x_i)\) indicate that the model is uncertain about the next byte; low values indicate that the next byte is almost deterministic given the preceding context.  

The entropy model is trained once on the same corpus that will later be used for the full BLT training, using a modest architecture (e.g., 100 M parameters, 14 layers, 512‑byte sliding window).  Because the model is tiny relative to the final BLT, its computational overhead is negligible during the preprocessing stage.  

Once we have a scalar entropy series \(\{ H(x_i) \}_{i=1}^n\), we must decide where to place patch boundaries.  Two families of thresholds are explored.  

### 1. Global Entropy Threshold  

Define a fixed scalar \(\theta_g > 0\).  A new patch starts at position \(t\) whenever  

\[
\boxed{
H(x_t) > \theta_g .
}
\tag{2}
\]

All bytes following a high‑entropy point up to the next high‑entropy point belong to the same patch.  The global threshold is simple to implement and yields roughly a target average patch size when \(\theta_g\) is tuned on a held‑out subset of the data.  

### 2. Approximate Monotonic (Relative) Constraint  

Instead of an absolute cut‑off, we can detect *local rises* in entropy relative to the previous byte:  

\[
\boxed{
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r .
}
\tag{3}
\]

Here \(\theta_r\) is a small positive constant (often much smaller than \(\theta_g\)).  This rule favours **breaks at the frontier where entropy stops decreasing**.  Within a patch, entropy typically declines as the model “fills in” the predictable tail of a word or phrase; a sudden increase signals the start of a new unpredictable region.  The monotonic constraint therefore produces patches that are more *information‑balanced*, each containing a roughly uniform amount of uncertainty.  

Both strategies are *offline*: during data loading, the byte stream is scanned once, the entropies are computed, and patch boundaries are recorded.  No additional neural component is required at inference time beyond the already‑trained entropy model.  

---  

## Global vs. Relative Entropy Thresholds, Monotonic Constraint  

Choosing between (2) and (3) involves a trade‑off between **global control** and **local adaptivity**.  

* **Global threshold** \(\theta_g\) directly controls the expected number of patches: larger \(\theta_g\) yields fewer patches (larger average patch size) because only the most uncertain bytes trigger a split.  This is convenient for FLOP budgeting – one can set \(\theta_g\) to achieve a target \(\bar L\).  However, it can be overly coarse: if a document contains a long run of moderately high entropy (e.g., a list of rare identifiers), the global rule may produce a single huge patch and waste compute on the later, still‑somewhat‑uncertain bytes.  

* **Relative threshold** \(\theta_r\) adapts to the *local shape* of the entropy curve.  By cutting whenever entropy rises sharply, the algorithm respects the natural decay of uncertainty inside a patch, leading to more evenly sized patches in terms of *information content* rather than raw byte count.  This often aligns better with the intuition that the model should spend a transformer step whenever it needs to “reset’’ its context.  The downside is that the resulting average patch size is less predictable; one must empirically tune \(\theta_r\) to hit a FLOP budget.  

In practice BLT experiments show that the monotonic constraint yields **slightly better scaling** on downstream tasks while keeping the average patch size comparable to the global‑threshold baseline, especially when the entropy model’s context is reset frequently (e.g., on newlines) to prevent *entropy drift* in long‑range repetitive content.

## Incremental Patching Property and Why BPE Fails It  

A patching function \(f_p\) is said to be **incremental** if the decision to start a new patch at position \(i\) can be made solely from the prefix \(\mathbf{x}_{<i}\), without any knowledge of future bytes.  Formally,  

\[
\boxed{
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}\quad \forall i .
}
\tag{4}
\]

This property is crucial for *autoregressive generation*: when the model is producing text one byte at a time, it must decide at each step whether the current byte completes a patch (thus invoking the global transformer) or whether it belongs to the ongoing patch (thus staying within the cheap local encoder).  The decision cannot depend on the unknown continuation.  

### BPE Tokenization Violates Incrementality  

Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE) builds a *finite* vocabulary by iteratively merging the most frequent byte pairs in a **global corpus**.  During tokenization, the algorithm greedily selects the longest matching token from the current position **given the entire remaining string**.  Consequently, the same prefix may be tokenized differently depending on the subsequent bytes:  

\[
\text{prefix } ``ab’’ \text{ could be tokenized as } \langle\text{ab}\rangle
\]
if the following bytes are ``c…’’ (forming the longer token ``abc’’), or as two separate tokens \(\langle a\rangle,\langle b\rangle\) if the continuation starts with a space.  Therefore,  

\[
f_{\text{BPE}}(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \neq f_{\text{BPE}}(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]

and BPE does **not** satisfy (4).  In a generation setting the tokenizer would require a look‑ahead into the not‑yet‑generated suffix, which is impossible without a separate beam search or a post‑hoc re‑tokenization step.  

In contrast, entropy‑based patching uses only the entropy of the *next* byte, which is computed from the already‑generated prefix via the entropy model.  The decision “Is \(H(x_i) > \theta\)?” depends exclusively on \(\mathbf{x}_{<i}\) and therefore respects (4).  This makes entropy patching naturally compatible with autoregressive decoding, while still being data‑driven.

## Comparison of BPE Tokenization and Patching  

| Aspect | BPE Tokenization | Patch‑Based Segmentation (BLT) |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Vocabulary** | Fixed, finite (e.g., 128 K tokens). Requires an embedding table of size \( |\mathcal V_{\text{BPE}}| \times d\). | No fixed vocabulary; each patch is represented by a latent vector produced on‑the‑fly by the encoder. |
| **Determinism** | Deterministic given the *full* string; not incremental. | Incremental: patch boundaries can be decided from the prefix alone. |
| **Compute Allocation** | One transformer step per token, regardless of token difficulty. | One global step per patch, with patch size adapting to predicted entropy (more steps where the data are hard). |
| **Flexibility of Patch Size** | Average token length is tied to vocabulary size; increasing it shrinks the embedding table but also enlarges the output projection. | Patch size can be arbitrarily changed (via \(\theta_g\) or \(\theta_r\)) without altering any embedding matrix; average patch length directly trades off FLOPs. |
| **Robustness to Noise** | Sensitive to byte‑level corruption because the tokenizer may map corrupted bytes to unknown or rare tokens. | Directly sees the raw bytes; the local encoder can still embed noisy bytes, leading to higher robustness. |
| **Multilingual Equity** | Vocabulary bias toward high‑frequency scripts; low‑resource scripts receive fewer tokens. | No script‑specific tokens; all byte patterns are treated uniformly, improving performance on long‑tail languages. |
| **Training Signal** | Model learns to predict the next token, which aggregates many bytes; the loss is coarse. | Model predicts the next byte distribution within each patch, providing a fine‑grained learning signal. |

The table highlights why *patches scale better*: they decouple the *computational granularity* (number of transformer steps) from a *static vocabulary* and allow an *information‑theoretic* criterion (entropy) to dictate granularity.  This yields a model that can keep the same inference FLOP budget while simultaneously expanding its global transformer capacity—precisely the scaling advantage demonstrated in the BLT experiments.

### Summary  

In this chapter we have built a rigorous understanding of how raw byte streams are transformed into variable‑size patches for BLT.  We formalised the patching function, explained how its average patch size controls FLOPs, and dissected three concrete schemes:  

1. **Static strided patching** – easy but wasteful;  
2. **Space‑based patching** – leverages linguistic boundaries but remains inflexible;  
3. **Entropy‑based patching** – data‑driven, incremental, and capable of balancing information density across patches.  

We clarified the distinction between *global* and *relative* entropy thresholds, introduced the monotonicity constraint that yields more uniformly informative patches, and argued why the incremental property is essential for autoregressive generation—something BPE tokenizers do not possess.  Armed with these concepts, the reader can now appreciate how BLT’s dynamic patching enables both **flop‑efficient scaling** and **enhanced robustness**, setting the stage for the architectural components described in the next chapter.
