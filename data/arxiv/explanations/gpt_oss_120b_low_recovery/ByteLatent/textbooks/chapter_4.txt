\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # Training and Inference Efficiency: FLOP Accounting and Patch Size Trade‑offs  

In this chapter we dissect the computational budget of the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)**.  By tracking the number of floating‑point operations (FLOPs) required by each sub‑module we can understand precisely why BLT attains its reported inference efficiency, and how the choice of **average patch size** mediates the trade‑off between model capacity and compute.  The discussion proceeds from the basic FLOP formulas of a standard transformer, through the additional cross‑attention blocks that bridge the byte‑level encoder/decoder with the patch‑level latent transformer, to the per‑byte cost model that isolates the effect of dynamically sized patches.  We then examine the auxiliary entropy model that supplies the patch‑boundary signals, the practical batching strategy that packs patches without padding, and the protocol for **context‑length equalization** when comparing models that employ different average patch sizes.  Finally we illustrate how one can hold the inference FLOP budget fixed while scaling both the global transformer and the patches themselves, exposing a new degree of freedom unavailable to conventional token‑based LLMs.

---

## 1. Transformer FLOP Formulas and Their Adaptation for BLT  

A vanilla transformer layer consists of two computational pillars: a **self‑attention** sub‑layer and a **feed‑forward network (FFN)** sub‑layer.  The classic FLOP counts (as used in the Chinchilla scaling analysis) assume dense matrix multiplications and ignore memory‑bound overhead; this is appropriate for BLT because the dominant cost resides in the arithmetic of the large linear layers.

### 1.1 Feed‑Forward Network FLOPs  

For a transformer with hidden dimension \(h\) and an FFN expansion factor \(f\) (typically \(f=4\)), the FFN consists of a linear projection from \(\mathbb{R}^{h}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{fh}\), a non‑linear activation, and a second linear projection back to \(\mathbb{R}^{h}\).  The FLOP count per token (or per patch, when the FFN is applied to a patch representation) is

\[
\text{FFN\_FLOPs}(h,f) = 2 \times h \times (f h) = 2 f h^{2}.
\]

The factor 2 accounts for the two matrix multiplications; the activation cost is negligible in comparison.

### 1.2 Self‑Attention FLOPs  

Self‑attention performs three projections to obtain queries \(\mathbf{Q}\), keys \(\mathbf{K}\), and values \(\mathbf{V}\), each of size \(h \times d_{k}\) (with \(d_{k}=h / n_{\text{heads}}\)).  For a sequence of length \(m\) the projection cost is

\[
\text{Proj\_FLOPs} = 3 \times m \times h \times d_{k}.
\]

Next, the attention scores \(\mathbf{QK}^{\top}\) are computed, costing \(2 \times m^{2} \times d_{k}\) FLOPs (a multiplication and an addition per entry).  The soft‑max is ignored in FLOP budgets because its cost is sub‑dominant.  Multiplying the normalized scores with \(\mathbf{V}\) adds another \(2 \times m^{2} \times d_{k}\) FLOPs.  Finally, the attended representation is projected back to dimension \(h\) with a matrix of size \(d_{k} \times h\) for each token, incurring

\[
\text{Output\_Proj\_FLOPs} = m \times d_{k} \times h.
\]

Summing these pieces gives the total attention FLOPs per layer:

\[
\text{Attn\_FLOPs}(h,m) = 3mh d_{k} + 4m^{2} d_{k} + mh d_{k}
= 4mh d_{k} + 4m^{2} d_{k}.
\]

When the attention mask limits the receptive field (e.g., block‑causal or sliding‑window attention) the quadratic term \(4m^{2} d_{k}\) is replaced by \(4mw d_{k}\), where \(w\) is the window size.

### 1.3 Modifications Specific to BLT  

BLT contains three distinct transformer families:

| Component | Hidden size | Sequence length | Attention mask |
|-----------|------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Global latent transformer** \(\mathcal{G}\) | \(h_{\mathcal{G}}\) | \(n_{p}\) (number of patches) | block‑causal over patches |
| **Local encoder** \(\mathcal{E}\) | \(h_{\mathcal{E}}\) | \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) (fixed window of bytes) | fixed‑window block‑causal |
| **Local decoder** \(\mathcal{D}\) | \(h_{\mathcal{D}}\) | \(w_{\mathcal{D}}\) (fixed window of bytes) | fixed‑window block‑causal |

The **global transformer** consumes a sequence of *patch* embeddings, so its length \(n_{p}\) equals the **average number of patches per context**, i.e.

\[
n_{p} = \frac{n_{\text{ctx}}}{\text{AvgPatchSize}},
\]

with \(n_{\text{ctx}}\) the byte context length (e.g. \(8\text{k}\) bytes).  Because the patch count directly scales the quadratic attention term, increasing the average patch size reduces the inference FLOPs roughly *linearly* for the global transformer.  The local encoder and decoder handle raw bytes; their sequence lengths are the *window* sizes \(w_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{D}}\) and are therefore unchanged by patching.

Putting everything together, the FLOP count for a **single forward pass through the entire BLT stack** (ignoring embedding look‑ups) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &=
\underbrace{L_{\mathcal{G}} \bigl[\text{FFN\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{G}},4) + \text{Attn\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, n_{p})\bigr]}_{\text{global latent transformer}} \\
&\;+\; \underbrace{L_{\mathcal{E}} \bigl[\text{FFN\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{E}},4) + \text{Attn\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{E}})\bigr]}_{\text{local encoder}} \\
&\;+\; \underbrace{L_{\mathcal{D}} \bigl[\text{FFN\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{D}},4) + \text{Attn\_FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, w_{\mathcal{D}})\bigr]}_{\text{local decoder}} .
\end{aligned}
\]

Because the local modules have \(L_{\mathcal{E}}, L_{\mathcal{D}} \ll L_{\mathcal{G}}\) and their windows are modest (e.g., 512 bytes), the **global latent transformer dominates the FLOP budget** for all realistic model sizes.

## 2. Cross‑Attention FLOP Cost Derivation  

BLT’s encoder and decoder contain **cross‑attention** layers that translate between byte‑level hidden states and patch‑level latent vectors.  Cross‑attention differs from self‑attention in two ways:

1. **Asymmetric sequence lengths:** queries correspond to *patches* (\(n_{p}\) queries) while keys and values correspond to the *bytes* belonging to a given patch (typically \(k\) bytes per patch on average).  
2. **Patch‑specific masks:** a query is allowed to attend only to the bytes that fall inside its own patch, never to bytes of other patches.

### 2.1 FLOPs for a Single Cross‑Attention Block  

Let \(h_{q}\) be the query dimension (the latent dimension of a patch) and \(h_{k}=h_{v}=h_{\mathcal{E}}\) be the key/value dimension (the byte‑level hidden size).  For a given patch we denote the number of constituent bytes as \(k\).  The operations are:

| Step | Operation | FLOPs |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Query projection | \(P_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{q}} \mapsto Q_{j}=W_{q}P_{j}\) | \(n_{p} \times h_{q} \times h_{q}\) |
| Key projection | each byte \(b_{i}\) → \(K_{i}=W_{k}b_{i}\) | \(k n_{p} \times h_{k} \times h_{q}\) |
| Value projection | each byte \(b_{i}\) → \(V_{i}=W_{v}b_{i}\) | \(k n_{p} \times h_{k} \times h_{v}\) |
| Score computation | \(Q_{j}K_{i}^{\top}\) for each of the \(k\) bytes in patch \(j\) | \(2 \times n_{p} \times k \times h_{q}\) |
| Weighted sum | \(\text{softmax}(QK^{\top}) V\) | \(2 \times n_{p} \times k \times h_{v}\) |
| Output projection | \(O_{j}=W_{o}(\cdot)\) | \(n_{p} \times h_{v} \times h_{q}\) |

Summing and simplifying (the quadratic terms disappear because the interaction is *patch‑by‑patch* rather than all‑pairs) yields the **cross‑attention FLOPs per block**:

\[
\text{CrossAttn\_FLOPs} = n_{p}\Bigl[ 
h_{q}^{2} + 2k h_{q} (h_{k}+h_{v}) + 4k h_{q} + h_{v} h_{q}
\Bigr].
\]

Since BLT typically chooses \(h_{q}=h_{\mathcal{G}}\) and \(h_{k}=h_{v}=h_{\mathcal{E}}\) (or \(h_{\mathcal{D}}\) for the decoder), and because the number of bytes per patch \(k\) is **by definition the average patch size**, the cross‑attention cost scales **linearly** with the average patch size.

### 2.2 Integration Into the Full FLOP Budget  

The encoder contains \(L_{\mathcal{E}}\) cross‑attention blocks, the decoder contains \(L_{\mathcal{D}}\) of them.  Adding them to the formula in Section 1 gives the complete BLT FLOP expression adopted for the experiments:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} =&\;
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Transf. FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{G}},L_{\mathcal{G}}, n_{p}, V=0)}{n_{p}}}_{\text{global per‑byte}}\\
&+ \underbrace{\text{Transf. FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{E}},L_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0)}_{\text{local encoder}}\\
&+ \underbrace{\text{Transf. FLOPs}(h_{\mathcal{D}},L_{\mathcal{D}}, w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256)}_{\text{local decoder}}\\
&+ \underbrace{\text{CrossAttn FLOPs}_{\mathcal{E}} \times \frac{k}{n_{p}}}_{\text{encoder cross‑attention per‑byte}}\\
&+ \underbrace{\text{CrossAttn FLOPs}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\text{decoder cross‑attention per‑byte}} .
\end{aligned}
\]

The division by \(n_{p}\) in the first term converts the global transformer’s FLOPs from a per‑patch to a per‑byte basis, making it easy to compare models that use different average patch sizes.

## 3. Per‑Byte FLOP Computation and the Central Role of Average Patch Size  

The **average patch size**,
\[
s \equiv \frac{n_{\text{ctx}}}{n_{p}},
\]
acts as a *knob* that simultaneously influences three components:

1. **Global transformer FLOPs** – appear divided by \(n_{p}=n_{\text{ctx}}/s\).  Larger \(s\) means fewer patches, so the quadratic attention term shrinks roughly as \(1/s^{2}\).
2. **Cross‑attention FLOPs** – scale linearly with \(k \approx s\) because each patch must attend to its constituent bytes.
3. **Memory footprint** – fewer patches reduce the activation memory needed for the global transformer, which is often the bottleneck during training.

Putting these together, the **per‑byte FLOP cost** can be expressed, after grouping constants, as

\[
\text{FLOPs per byte} \approx 
\underbrace{\frac{C_{\mathcal{G}}}{s}}_{\text{global}} 
+ \underbrace{C_{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{local encoder}} 
+ \underbrace{C_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\text{local decoder}} 
+ \underbrace{C_{\text{cross}} \, s}_{\text{cross‑attention}} .
\]

Here \(C_{\mathcal{G}}, C_{\mathcal{E}}, C_{\mathcal{D}}, C_{\text{cross}}\) are constants that bundle the hidden dimensions, layer counts, and the fixed window lengths.  Crucially, the **global term decreases with \(s\)** while the **cross‑attention term increases with \(s\)**, giving rise to a modest convex curve.  Empirically, for the hidden dimensions used in the paper, the decreasing global term dominates, which is why **inference FLOPs drop dramatically** as the average patch size grows from 4 bytes to 8 bytes (up to ≈ 50 % reduction).

## 4. Entropy Model Training and Lookup‑Table Optimization  

**Entropy‑based patching** requires a model that can estimate the next‑byte predictive entropy \(H(x_{i})\) for every position in the training corpus.  The paper trains a modest **byte‑level autoregressive LM** (100 M parameters, 14 layers, hidden size 512, sliding window of 512 bytes).  The training procedure follows the usual language‑model objective: minimize cross‑entropy over the byte vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\).

### 4.1 Computing Entropy  

Given the conditional distribution \(p_{e}(x_{i}=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})\), the entropy is

\[
H(x_{i}) = -\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_{i}=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_{i}=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

Because the byte vocabulary has only 256 symbols, the sum is cheap to evaluate.  The entropy values are stored alongside the raw bytes during the **dataloader preprocessing** step; they are not recomputed on the fly.

### 4.2 From Entropy to Patch Boundaries  

Two simple heuristics translate the entropy series \(\{H(x_{i})\}\) into binary boundary markers:

1. **Global threshold** \(\theta_{g}\): start a new patch whenever \(H(x_{i}) > \theta_{g}\).
2. **Approximate monotonicity** \(\theta_{r}\): start a new patch when the entropy rises relative to the preceding byte, i.e. \(H(x_{i}) - H(x_{i-1}) > \theta_{r}\).

The thresholds are **tuned to achieve a target average patch size** on a held‑out slice of the pre‑training data.  Because the entropy model is tiny, its parameters can be **encoded as a lookup table** once training is finished: the model’s weights are frozen, the forward pass is pre‑computed for each possible 512‑byte context (or a hashed version thereof), and the resulting entropy values are cached.  This eliminates any runtime FLOPs associated with entropy estimation at training or inference time.

## 5. Batch Packing Strategy for Patches  

During training the global latent transformer expects **a tensor of shape** \((B, n_{p}, h_{\mathcal{G}})\), where \(B\) is the batch dimension.  Because each training example can yield a different number of patches (dynamic patching), naïvely padding to the maximum \(n_{p}\) in the batch would squander compute on the massive global transformer.

BLT therefore **packs patches** across examples:

1. **Count patches** for each example after applying the chosen patching function.
2. **Accumulate patches** from successive examples until a fixed *patch batch size* \(P_{\text{batch}}\) (e.g. 4096 patches) is reached.
3. **Form a single batch** from the collected patches, discarding any leftover patches that would exceed the budget (they are deferred to the next batch).
4. **Keep the byte‑level windows** (of size \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) / \(w_{\mathcal{D}}\)) aligned with their original examples; the local encoder/decoder still see the original byte streams, but the global transformer now processes a *homogeneous* patch tensor without padding.

Because the **global transformer FLOPs are proportional to the number of patches**, this packing guarantees that **every forward pass utilizes the full capacity of the global model** and that the measured FLOPs correspond exactly to the theoretical formula from Section 1.

## 6. Context‑Length Equalization Across Different Patch Sizes  

When comparing a token‑based baseline (fixed average token size) with BLT configurations that employ different average patch sizes, we must ensure that **the number of raw bytes seen per forward pass** remains comparable; otherwise a model with larger patches would enjoy a longer effective context for free.

The paper adopts the following protocol:

1. **Fix the total number of bytes per batch** (e.g. 16 M bytes).  
2. **Adjust the byte‑level window lengths** \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(w_{\mathcal{D}}\) to keep the *memory* footprint constant; longer patches imply fewer patches, so the global transformer receives a shorter patch sequence.  
3. **Truncate or pad** each raw byte sequence to a *maximum* of 12 k or 24 k bytes (depending on dataset) before applying the patcher.  This caps the worst‑case number of patches and prevents occasional outliers from causing memory spikes.  
4. **Maintain the same *effective* context length** (bytes) across experiments: the token‑based baseline uses an 8 k‑byte context; BLT with a larger patch size may use a 16 k‑byte context while still processing the same number of bytes per batch because the batch size is measured in bytes, not patches.

By **controlling the expected byte count per batch**, the comparisons isolate the effect of patch size on FLOPs and performance, without confounding factors such as longer context windows.

## 7. Inference‑Flop‑Controlled Scaling: Fixed FLOP Budgets, Varying Model and Patch Size  

One of BLT’s most striking empirical findings is the ability to **simultaneously increase the global transformer’s parameter count and the average patch size while keeping the inference FLOP budget constant**.  This is impossible for traditional token‑based LLMs because the token vocabulary is fixed: enlarging the model inevitably raises the per‑token compute, while the token size itself is constrained by the vocabulary.

### 7.1 Fixed‑Inference‑Flop Experiment  

The authors construct *flop‑equivalent* model families:

| Model family | Global parameters | Avg. patch size | Inference FLOPs (per token) |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------|-----------------------------|
| BPE‑Llama‑2  | 400 M             | 3.7 bytes       | baseline |
| BPE‑Llama‑3  | 400 M             | 4.4 bytes       | ≈ +12 % |
| BLT‑Entropy (ps = 4.5) | 400 M | 4.5 bytes | ≈ baseline |
| BLT‑Entropy (ps = 6)   | 400 M | 6 bytes   | ≈ ‑30 % |
| BLT‑Entropy (ps = 8)   | 400 M | 8 bytes   | ≈ ‑50 % |

To **hold inference FLOPs constant** while moving from the 4.5‑byte to the 8‑byte configuration, the authors **increase the global transformer depth** (or hidden width) until the FLOP budget matches the baseline.  Because the global FLOP term scales as \(1/s\), a doubling of the average patch size roughly halves the FLOPs contributed by the global transformer, leaving room to **double the number of layers** (or increase hidden size) while staying within the same budget.

### 7.2 Scaling Trends  

When training these families across a range of **training FLOP budgets** (from compute‑optimal up to several times that optimum), the **performance curves** (bits‑per‑byte vs. training FLOPs) reveal:

- For *small* training budgets, token‑based models retain a slight edge, reflecting their more mature optimization at low compute.
- As training FLOPs increase, BLT models **overtake** token‑based curves, with the **crossover point moving closer to the compute‑optimal budget** for larger patch sizes.  
- The **slope** of the BLT curves is steeper, indicating that each additional training FLOP yields more performance gain when the model can exploit larger patches.

These observations validate the hypothesis that **patches scale better than tokens**: the flexibility to enlarge patches provides a new axis for improving the compute‑to‑performance ratio, especially in regimes where inference cost is a hard constraint (e.g., on‑device deployments or latency‑sensitive services).

---

## 8. Summary  

- **Transformer FLOPs** are derived from feed‑forward and attention operations; BLT’s global latent transformer dominates the budget because it processes *patches* rather than individual bytes.  
- **Cross‑attention** bridges bytes and patches with a FLOP cost that grows linearly with the average patch size, complementing the inverse relationship of the global transformer’s cost.  
- The **per‑byte FLOP expression** clarifies why increasing the average patch size generally reduces inference compute: the global term falls faster than the cross‑attention term rises.  
- An auxiliary **entropy model** supplies data‑driven patch boundaries; after training it can be turned into a zero‑cost lookup table.  
- **Batch packing** eliminates padding overhead in the global transformer, ensuring that measured FLOPs match the analytical formula.  
- **Context‑length equalization** guarantees fair comparisons across differing patch sizes by fixing the expected byte count per batch.  
- Finally, **inference‑flop‑controlled scaling** demonstrates a new degree of freedom: by expanding the average patch size we can reallocate saved FLOPs to enlarge the global transformer, achieving superior scaling curves while respecting a fixed inference budget.

Through these mechanisms, BLT reconciles the raw‑byte modeling paradigm with the efficiency traditionally reserved for token‑based large language models, establishing a concrete computational foundation for the claims of reduced inference cost and improved scalability presented in the original paper.
