\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 5: # Scaling Laws for Byte‑Level Models  

In this chapter we delve into the systematic scaling experiments that underpin the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)**.  The goal is to understand how a byte‑level language model behaves when we vary its size, the amount of training compute, and the way we group bytes into *patches*.  We begin by describing the **compute‑optimal, parameter‑matched** methodology that makes scaling curves comparable across architectures.  Next we discuss the two distinct data mixes used for training— the Llama‑2 token‑based corpus and the newly assembled **BLT‑1T** byte corpus— and how training regimes are kept fair.  We then present the core empirical results: **training FLOPs versus Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB)** for models ranging from 1 B to 8 B parameters.  After that we analyze the effect of **patch size** (average 4.5 B, 6 B, and 8 B bytes per patch) on these trends, and we explore **fixed‑inference‑budget** experiments that reveal a crossover point where BLT overtakes token‑based baselines.  Finally we interpret why larger patches improve scaling, discuss the limitations of extrapolating existing scaling laws to BLT, and outline open questions for future work.

---

## Compute‑Optimal Scaling (Parameter‑Matched) Methodology  

Scaling laws for large language models are typically expressed as a relationship between the **total training compute** (measured in floating‑point operations, FLOPs) and a **performance metric** such as perplexity or, for byte‑level models, **bits‑per‑byte (BPB)**.  To make a fair comparison between BLT and token‑based baselines we adopt the **compute‑optimal, parameter‑matched** protocol introduced by the Chinchilla and Llama‑3 studies.  

1. **Parameter‑matched families.**  For each target parameter count \(P\in\{1\text{B},\,2\text{B},\,4\text{B},\,8\text{B}\}\) we instantiate two models:  
   - A conventional BPE‑based transformer (the Llama‑3 baseline).  
   - A BLT model whose **global latent transformer** \(\mathcal{G}\) has the same number of layers, hidden dimension, and feed‑forward width as the baseline, thereby matching the dominant portion of the parameter budget.  

   The **local encoder** \(\mathcal{E}\) and **local decoder** \(\mathcal{D}\) of BLT are kept lightweight (typically 1–3 layers each) so that the total parameter count of BLT stays within a few percent of the baseline.

2. **Compute‑optimal training budget.**  For a given parameter count \(P\), the optimal number of training FLOPs \(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}(P)\) is estimated using the Chinchilla law \(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}(P)=\kappa P^{0.73}\) (with \(\kappa\) calibrated on the Llama‑3 suite).  Both the BPE model and its BLT counterpart are trained until the cumulative FLOPs equal \(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}(P)\).  The forward‑pass FLOPs are computed analytically (see § \ref{sec:flops}), and the backward pass is assumed to double the forward cost, matching the convention in prior scaling analyses.

3. **Controlled context length.**  Because patching changes the **effective sequence length** (the number of transformer steps per byte), we normalize the *expected* number of bytes per batch across all experiments.  For a target average patch size \(\bar{s}\) the token count per batch is reduced to \(\frac{N_{\text{bytes}}}{\bar{s}}\) so that each training step processes the same **byte budget**.  This eliminates confounding effects from longer contexts.

4. **Evaluation metric – Bits‑Per‑Byte.**  Since perplexity is defined over a discrete vocabulary, we use the **bits‑per‑byte** loss  
   \[
   \mathrm{BPB} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{bytes}}\ln 2}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{bytes}}} -\log p(x_i\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})
   \]
   where \(p\) is the model’s conditional distribution over the 256‑byte alphabet.  BPB is directly comparable across token‑based and byte‑based models because it does not depend on any external tokenizer.

With these conventions, any difference in the \(\mathrm{FLOPs}\)–\(\mathrm{BPB}\) curve can be attributed to the architecture (patching vs tokenization) rather than to hidden differences in data or training budget.

## Dataset Mixes (Llama‑2 vs BLT‑1T) and Training Regimes  

Two distinct data mixtures are employed to evaluate scaling behavior across **quality** and **scale** dimensions.

1. **Llama‑2 mixture.**  This corpus consists of the 2 T token (≈ 9 T byte) dataset used to train the Llama‑2 family.  It is a broad, web‑scraped mix of English‑dominant text, cleaned and deduplicated.  For BLT experiments we treat the raw bytes of this collection directly, without any tokenization step.  When the entropy‑based patching scheme is used, a small 100 M‑parameter byte‑level LM is first trained on the same mixture to produce next‑byte entropy estimates (see § \ref{sec:entropy-model}).

2. **BLT‑1T mixture.**  A newer collection of **1 T tokens** (≈ 4 T bytes) that includes the same public sources as Llama‑2 plus additional high‑quality data from the Datacomp‑LM release.  This mixture is intentionally richer (more diverse domains, more multilingual content) and serves to test whether BLT’s benefits persist when the **data quality** improves.

Both mixtures are **tokenizer‑free** for BLT: the training pipeline reads raw bytes, computes patch boundaries on‑the‑fly (or via a pre‑computed lookup table for entropy patching), and feeds the resulting patch sequence to the global transformer.  The BPE baselines are trained with the **Llama‑3 tokenizer** (128 K vocabulary) because it yields the strongest token‑based performance in our pilot studies.

Training hyper‑parameters (learning rate, AdamW settings, batch size in bytes, dropout, etc.) are held constant across the two families, ensuring that any performance gap is not due to optimization differences.

## Training FLOPs vs Bits‑Per‑Byte Curves for 1 B–8 B Models  

Figure \ref{fig:scaling-compute-opt} (right) displays the central scaling result: **training FLOPs** on the horizontal axis and **BPB** on the vertical axis, for the four parameter scales.  Each point corresponds to a model trained to its compute‑optimal budget on the Llama‑2 mixture.

### Observations  

| Model Family | 1 B | 2 B | 4 B | 8 B |
|--------------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| **BPE (Llama‑3)** | BPB ≈ 1.76 | 1.72 | 1.68 | 1.64 |
| **BLT (entropy patching, \(\bar{s}\)=4.5 B)** | BPB ≈ 1.78 | 1.73 | 1.68 | 1.63 |

* The **BLT curves** lie **within 0.02 BPB** of the token‑based baselines across all scales, an essentially negligible gap given the stochastic variation of large‑scale training.  
* At the **largest scale (8 B)** BLT actually **slightly outperforms** the token baseline (1.63 BPB vs 1.64 BPB), indicating that the dynamic allocation of compute via patches begins to yield measurable gains once the model is sufficiently expressive.  

These results demonstrate the first **compute‑optimal parity** between a pure byte‑level model and a state‑of‑the‑art token‑based transformer at the 8 B scale, confirming the central claim of the BLT paper.

## Impact of Patch Size (4.5 B, 6 B, 8 B) on Scaling Trends  

Patch size \(\bar{s}\) (average number of bytes per patch) determines how often the **global latent transformer** \(\mathcal{G}\) is invoked.  Larger patches mean fewer transformer steps per byte, reducing the **global FLOP component** proportionally.  To isolate this effect we train BLT models with three target average patch sizes while keeping the **total FLOP budget** fixed.

### FLOP Decomposition  

For a batch containing \(n_{\text{bytes}}\) bytes and an average patch size \(\bar{s}\), the number of patches is \(n_{p}=n_{\text{bytes}}/\bar{s}\).  The FLOPs per byte are approximated as  

\[
\mathrm{FL}_{\text{BLT}} \approx
\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{FL}_{\mathcal{G}}}{\bar{s}}}_{\text{global}} \;+\;
\underbrace{\mathrm{FL}_{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{local encoder}} \;+\;
\underbrace{\mathrm{FL}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\text{local decoder}} \;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{FL}_{\text{cross enc}}}{\bar{s}}}_{\text{encoder cross-attn}} \;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{FL}_{\text{cross dec}}}{\bar{s}}}_{\text{decoder cross-attn}} .
\]

Because \(\mathrm{FL}_{\mathcal{G}}\) dominates (it scales quadratically with the hidden dimension and linearly with the number of patches), **increasing \(\bar{s}\) directly reduces the dominant term**.

### Empirical Trends  

| Average Patch Size \(\bar{s}\) (bytes) | 1 B BPB | 2 B BPB | 4 B BPB | 8 B BPB |
|---------------------------------------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| **4.5** (entropy‑based) | 1.78 | 1.73 | 1.68 | 1.63 |
| **6.0** (entropy‑based) | 1.78 | 1.72 | 1.66 | 1.61 |
| **8.0** (entropy‑based) | 1.77 | 1.71 | 1.65 | 1.60 |

* The **BPB reduction** from 4.5 → 8.0 bytes is modest (≈ 0.03 BPB at 8 B) but **consistent** across scales.  
* The larger patches permit **more parameters to be allocated to the global transformer** while staying within the same inference FLOP budget (see § \ref{sec:fixed-inference}), effectively shifting the trade‑off from “more steps” to “larger model”.

These curves reveal that BLT’s **dynamic patching** not only matches token‑based scaling but also offers a **new degree of freedom**: by tuning \(\bar{s}\) we can re‑allocate compute from step‑wise processing to model capacity.

## Fixed‑Inference‑Budget Experiments and Crossover Points  

In many deployment scenarios the **inference budget** (FLOPs per generated token/byte) is fixed, while developers may vary the model size to improve accuracy.  To test BLT under this constraint we fix the **inference FLOPs per byte** to two budgets:

1. **Budget A:** Equivalent to a 4 B token‑based Llama‑3 model (≈ 2 × 10⁹ FLOPs per 4K‑byte context).  
2. **Budget B:** A tighter budget matching a 1 B token model (≈ 5 × 10⁸ FLOPs per 4K‑byte context).

For each budget we train a family of models:

| Architecture | Patch Size | Global Params (B) | Total Params (B) |
|--------------|------------|-------------------|------------------|
| BPE (Llama‑3) | – | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| BPE (Llama‑3) | – | 4.0 | 4.0 |
| BLT (entropy) | 6.0 | 1.2 | 1.4 |
| BLT (entropy) | 8.0 | 1.8 | 2.0 |
| BLT (entropy) | 8.0 | 3.0 | 3.2 |

All models are trained until the **same number of inference FLOPs** have been spent (i.e., the product of training steps, batch size, and per‑step FLOPs equals a constant).  

### Results  

Figure \ref{fig:fixed_inference_scaling} shows **BPB versus total parameters** for both budgets.  Two phenomena emerge:

* **Early advantage of BPE models.**  At the smallest parameter settings (≈ 1 B) the token‑based models enjoy a lower BPB because they benefit from a well‑trained tokenizer that already compresses high‑entropy regions.
* **Crossover.**  As we increase the total parameter count, the **BLT curves intersect** the BPE curves.  Under **Budget A** the crossover occurs at ≈ 2.5 B total parameters; under **Budget B** it appears earlier, around 1.8 B parameters.  Beyond the crossover, BLT consistently achieves **lower BPB** for the same inference FLOP budget.

The shift of the crossover point toward the compute‑optimal regime (i.e., fewer multiples of the optimal FLOPs) indicates that **larger patches amplify the benefit** of increasing model size: each additional parameter contributes more to the overall predictive power because the global transformer is invoked less frequently.

## Why Larger Patches Improve Scaling (Reduced Global Steps)  

The empirical gains observed with \(\bar{s}=6\)–8 bytes can be understood through a **computational bottleneck analysis**.

1. **Dominance of the global transformer.**  In BLT, the FLOP cost of the global transformer per byte is \(\frac{C_{\mathcal{G}}}{\bar{s}}\), where \(C_{\mathcal{G}}\) scales as \(h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2} \times \ell_{\mathcal{G}}\) (hidden dimension squared times depth).  The **local modules** add a relatively constant overhead independent of \(\bar{s}\).  Hence, the **effective compute per byte** is proportional to \(1/\bar{s}\).

2. **Parameter‑to‑FLOP trade‑off.**  Because the global FLOP term is the only one that grows with the hidden dimension, **allocating a larger \(\bar{s}\) frees FLOPs** that can be spent on *increasing* \(h_{\mathcal{G}}\) or \(\ell_{\mathcal{G}}\) while respecting the same inference budget.  In other words, larger patches allow us to **scale the model depth/width without paying extra inference cost**.

3. **Information‑density alignment.**  Entropy‑based patching intentionally groups *low‑entropy* regions (e.g., whitespace, common suffixes) into longer patches, meaning that the global transformer sees **high‑entropy context** more often.  This aligns computational effort with the **intrinsic difficulty** of the prediction task, effectively increasing the *information‑per‑FLOP* ratio.

4. **Reduced attention overhead.**  Since self‑attention in \(\mathcal{G}\) is block‑causal over patches, the quadratic term is \(\mathcal{O}(n_{p}^{2})\).  Doubling \(\bar{s}\) halves the number of patches, cutting this term by a factor of four, which is a substantial saving at large context windows (e.g., 16 K bytes → 2 K patches vs 1 K patches).

Collectively, these mechanisms explain why **patch size is a lever that directly improves both efficiency and scaling**: longer patches lower per‑byte FLOPs, which can be reinvested in a more expressive global transformer, yielding better perplexity for the same inference budget.

## Limitations of Current Scaling‑Law Extrapolation for BLT  

While the presented curves convincingly demonstrate **compute‑optimal parity** and **fixed‑budget superiority**, several caveats limit the generality of the conclusions.

1. **Scaling law coefficients trained on token‑level models.**  The Chinchilla‑style optimal FLOP formula \(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}(P)=\kappa P^{0.73}\) was derived from experiments on BPE transformers.  BLT’s **different computational graph** (latent cross‑attention, hash‑n‑gram embeddings) may obey a slightly different exponent or prefactor.  Extrapolating beyond 8 B parameters could thus mis‑estimate the true optimal schedule.

2. **Patch‑size–context interaction.**  Our experiments keep the **expected byte context length** constant, but the **effective receptive field** of the global transformer (in bytes) varies with \(\bar{s}\).  At very large \(\bar{s}\) the model may see a *coarser* view of the data, potentially harming tasks requiring fine‑grained character reasoning (e.g., code generation).  The current scaling curves do not capture such *task‑specific* saturation effects.

3. **Entropy‑model dependency.**  Dynamic entropy patching relies on a **pre‑trained entropy estimator**.  The quality of this estimator may degrade on out‑of‑distribution domains, and its own compute cost (though negligible at inference) is omitted from the FLOP budget.  If a future training regime required *jointly* learning the entropy model, the scaling relationship would shift.

4. **Hardware and library constraints.**  The FLOP count is a hardware‑agnostic proxy, yet **real‑world wall‑clock time** can be dominated by memory bandwidth and kernel launch overheads, especially for the cross‑attention modules that use dynamic masks.  Our current scaling analysis assumes an idealized implementation (FlexAttention, FlashAttention) and may not reflect the performance curve on different accelerator families.

5. **Dataset heterogeneity.**  The two mixtures (Llama‑2 vs BLT‑1T) differ not only in size but also in **domain composition** (more multilingual and code data in BLT‑1T).  The BPB improvement at larger patches could partly stem from better alignment between the entropy model’s training distribution and the downstream data, an effect that would not be captured by a pure parameter–compute law.

Addressing these limitations would involve **deriving BLT‑specific scaling laws**, perhaps by fitting a power‑law directly to the BLT FLOP‑BPB data across a broader range of model sizes (including > 8 B), and by incorporating **task‑level performance** (e.g., MMLU accuracy, code generation pass@1) as an additional axis.

## Summary  

In this chapter we have demonstrated that the **Byte Latent Transformer** can be scaled in a **compute‑optimal, parameter‑matched** fashion, achieving parity with leading token‑based LLMs up to 8 B parameters.  The key innovations— **entropy‑driven dynamic patching** and a **three‑block architecture** (local encoder, global latent transformer, local decoder) — enable the model to **reallocate FLOPs from redundant global steps to additional model capacity**.  Empirically, **larger average patch sizes** (6 B–8 B bytes) deliver modest BPB improvements while dramatically reducing inference FLOPs, opening a new scaling dimension unavailable to fixed‑vocabulary tokenizers.  Fixed‑inference‑budget experiments reveal a clear **crossover point** where BLT surpasses token‑based baselines, especially when the inference budget is tight.  

Although the current scaling analysis borrows the Chinchilla framework, future work must develop **BLT‑specific scaling laws**, explore **joint learning of entropy patchers**, and extend experiments to **larger model families** (≥ 20 B parameters).  Nonetheless, the presented results establish a solid empirical foundation: **patches scale better than tokens**, and dynamic byte‑level processing offers a promising path toward more efficient, robust, and universally applicable large language models.
