\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 3: # BLT Architecture: Global Latent Transformer plus Local Encoders/Decoders  

In this chapter we dissect the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** architecture, explaining how its three‑block design—**a large Global Latent Transformer flanked by two lightweight Local models**—enables dynamic allocation of compute while preserving the ability to model raw byte sequences. We begin with a high‑level view of data flow, then dive into each component, describing the underlying mechanisms (block‑causal masking, cross‑attention, hash‑n‑gram embeddings) and the reason why the Global Transformer dominates the FLOP budget.  

---  

## Overall Block Diagram and Data Flow  

The BLT model processes an input byte stream \(\mathbf{x}= \{x_{1},x_{2},\dots ,x_{n}\}\) in three stages.  

1. **Local Encoder \(\mathcal{E}\)** receives the raw bytes, embeds each byte, enriches the embedding with hash‑based n‑gram features, and then applies a shallow transformer stack. After each transformer layer a **cross‑attention** module pools the representations belonging to a single *patch* (a contiguous group of bytes) into a *latent* vector \(p_{j}\).  

2. The sequence of latent patches \(\mathbf{p}= \{p_{1},p_{2},\dots ,p_{m}\}\) is fed to the **Latent Global Transformer \(\mathcal{G}\)**. This is a deep autoregressive transformer that processes patches with a **block‑causal attention mask**, ensuring that patch \(j\) can attend only to patches \(\le j\). The output of \(\mathcal{G}\) is a sequence of *output* patch representations \(\mathbf{o}= \{o_{1},o_{2},\dots ,o_{m}\}\).  

3. The **Local Decoder \(\mathcal{D}\)** takes the output patches \(\mathbf{o}\) and, via a reverse cross‑attention operation, expands each patch back into a sequence of raw bytes \(\mathbf{y}= \{y_{1},y_{2},\dots ,y_{n}\}\). The decoder also contains a shallow transformer stack that refines the byte‑level predictions before a final softmax over the 256‑element byte vocabulary.  

Data therefore flows **byte → encoder → latent patches → global transformer → output patches → decoder → bytes**. The encoder and decoder are *local* because they operate on short byte windows; the global transformer is *global* because it aggregates information across the entire document, but it is invoked only once per patch, not per byte. This separation is the key to computational efficiency.  

---  

## Latent Global Transformer  

### Architecture and Depth  

The Global Transformer \(\mathcal{G}\) is a conventional autoregressive transformer consisting of \(l_{\mathcal{G}}\) identical layers. Each layer contains a multi‑head self‑attention (MHSA) block followed by a feed‑forward network (FFN) with SwiGLU activation, layer‑norm (RMSNorm), and residual connections. The hidden dimension \(h_{\mathcal{G}}\) is typically much larger than that of the local models (e.g., 4096 for an 8 B‑parameter BLT), enabling the model to learn rich, high‑level abstractions of the patch sequence.  

### Block‑Causal Mask  

Because patches are processed sequentially, \(\mathcal{G}\) employs a **block‑causal mask**: for a query at patch \(j\), the attention keys are limited to patches \(\{1,\dots ,j\}\). Formally, the attention weight matrix \(A\) is defined as  

\[
A_{jk}= \begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{\exp\!\bigl((Q_{j}K_{k}^{\top})/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)}{\sum_{k'\le j}\exp\!\bigl((Q_{j}K_{k'}^{\top})/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)} & k\le j,\\[4pt]
0 & k>j,
\end{cases}
\]

where \(Q_{j}\) and \(K_{k}\) are the query and key vectors for patches \(j\) and \(k\), and \(d_{k}\) is the key dimension. This mask guarantees autoregressive generation: the model never accesses information from a future patch.  

### Role in the Compute Budget  

The Global Transformer processes **one token per patch**, not per byte. If the average patch size is \(\bar{s}\) bytes, then a sequence of \(n\) bytes yields only \(m = n/\bar{s}\) patches. Since the FLOP count for a transformer layer scales quadratically with the sequence length, the reduction from \(n\) to \(m\) dramatically lowers the cost of the dominant component. In practice, the Global Transformer accounts for **≈ 70–80 % of total FLOPs** even though it contains the majority of the parameters; the remaining FLOPs belong to the lightweight local models and the cross‑attention modules.

## Local Encoder  

### Lightweight Transformer  

The encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) is deliberately shallow: \(l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}\) (often 1–3 layers). Its purpose is to map each byte to a representation that can be efficiently aggregated into a patch vector. The encoder uses the same architectural primitives as \(\mathcal{G}\) (MHSA, FFN, RMSNorm) but with a smaller hidden dimension \(h_{\mathcal{E}}\) (e.g., 768).  

### Byte Embedding and Hash‑n‑gram Augmentation  

Each raw byte \(x_{i}\) (an integer in \(\{0,\dots ,255\}\)) is first embedded via a learned matrix  

\[
\mathbf{x}_{i}=E_{\text{byte}}[x_{i}] \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]

To provide context beyond the single byte, BLT adds **hash‑based n‑gram embeddings**. For a given position \(i\) and n‑gram length \(n\in\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}\), we form the byte sequence  

\[
g_{i,n}= \{x_{i-n+1},\dots ,x_{i}\}.
\]

A fast rolling polynomial hash, \(\text{RollPolyHash}(\cdot)\), maps each n‑gram to an integer in \([0,|E_{n}^{\text{hash}}|-1]\). The resulting index selects a vector from a *fixed‑size* hash table \(E_{n}^{\text{hash}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E_{n}^{\text{hash}}|\times h_{\mathcal{E}}}\). The augmented embedding is  

\[
\mathbf{e}_{i}= \mathbf{x}_{i}+ \frac{1}{|N|+1}\sum_{n\in N}E_{n}^{\text{hash}}\!\bigl(\text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n})\bigr),
\]

where \(N=\{3,\dots ,8\}\) and the division normalizes for the number of n‑gram tables (six) plus the byte embedding. This scheme supplies a cheap approximation of the surrounding byte context without inflating the parameter count, and because it is a hash, the memory cost is bounded (e.g., 500 k entries per n‑gram size).  

### Local Block‑Causal Attention  

Within \(\mathcal{E}\), each byte can attend only to the previous \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) bytes (a fixed sliding window). This **local block causal mask** preserves autoregressive property at the byte level while limiting the attention cost to \(\mathcal{O}(w_{\mathcal{E}}n)\). The window size is typically set to a few hundred bytes, sufficient for capturing sub‑word patterns such as morphemes.  

---  

## Encoder Cross‑Attention and Patch Pooling  

### Perceiver‑Style Cross‑Attention  

After each transformer layer of \(\mathcal{E}\), a **cross‑attention** block pools the byte‑level hidden states belonging to a single patch into a latent vector. The architecture mirrors the Perceiver’s input‑cross‑attention:  

* **Queries** \(Q_{j}\) correspond to patches \(p_{j}\). They are initialized by max‑ or average‑pooling the byte hidden states that belong to patch \(j\) and then linearly projected by \(\mathcal{E}_{C}\).  
* **Keys** \(K_{i}\) and **Values** \(V_{i}\) are the byte representations \(h_{i}\) after the current transformer layer.  

The attention for patch \(j\) is constrained to the bytes that constitute that patch, i.e.,  

\[
\text{mask}_{ji}= \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if byte } i \in \text{patch } j,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

The masked attention operation  

\[
\widetilde{P}_{j}= Q_{j} + W_{o}\!\Bigl(\operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(\frac{Q_{j}K_{\text{patch }j}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\bigr) V_{\text{patch }j}\Bigr)
\]

produces the updated patch representation \(\widetilde{P}_{j}\). Multiple attention heads (\(U_{\mathcal{E}}\)) are used; each head works in the smaller byte dimension \(h_{\mathcal{E}}\) and the concatenated heads are projected to \(h_{\mathcal{G}}\) before being passed to the global transformer.  

### Patch‑Specific Masks  

Because patches have variable length, the mask must be recomputed for each patch. This dynamic masking is inexpensive: the mask is a binary vector of length equal to the patch size, and the attention routine simply skips entries with mask = 0. The locality of the mask also enables the use of **FlexAttention**, a fused kernel that speeds up variable‑length attention on modern GPUs.

## Local Decoder  

The decoder \(\mathcal{D}\) mirrors the encoder but works in the opposite direction: it receives the **output patches** \(\mathbf{o}\) from the global transformer and must reconstruct the original bytes.  

### Architecture  

\(\mathcal{D}\) contains \(l_{\mathcal{D}}\) transformer layers (often a few more than the encoder, e.g., 2–4), with hidden size \(h_{\mathcal{D}}\) that can equal \(h_{\mathcal{E}}\) or be slightly larger. Like the encoder, it uses a local block‑causal attention mask with window \(w_{\mathcal{D}}\), ensuring that byte generation remains autoregressive.  

### Cross‑Attention Direction Swap  

In the decoder, **queries** are the byte‑level representations produced by the previous decoder layer (or the final encoder output for the first layer). **Keys** and **Values** come from the *patch* vectors \(\mathbf{o}\). Formally, for layer \(l\):  

\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_{i}^{(l)} &= W_{q}\, d^{(l-1)}_{i},\\
K_{j}^{(l)} &= W_{k}\, \mathcal{D}_{C}(o_{j}),\\
V_{j}^{(l)} &= W_{v}\, \mathcal{D}_{C}(o_{j}),
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(\mathcal{D}_{C}\) is a linear projection that expands each patch to the decoder’s key/value dimension. The attention is again masked so that a byte can attend only to the *patch* that contains it, preventing leakage across patch boundaries. The result of cross‑attention is added residually to the byte query, after which a standard transformer layer refines the byte hidden state.  

### Byte Generation  

After the final decoder layer, a linear projection maps each byte hidden state to logits over the 256‑byte vocabulary, followed by a softmax to obtain the conditional probability  

\[
p(y_{i}=b\mid \mathbf{y}_{<i},\mathbf{o}) = \frac{\exp\bigl( \mathbf{w}_{b}^{\top} d^{\text{out}}_{i} \bigr)}{\sum_{b' \in \mathcal{V}} \exp\bigl( \mathbf{w}_{b'}^{\top} d^{\text{out}}_{i} \bigr)},
\]

where \(\mathcal{V}\) is the set of 256 possible byte values and \(\mathbf{w}_{b}\) is the output embedding for byte \(b\). During training the cross‑entropy loss is summed over all bytes; during inference the model samples or chooses the most probable byte at each step, updating the decoder state accordingly.  

---  

## Decoder Cross‑Attention Specifics  

* **Patch‑to‑Byte Alignment** – The decoder must know which bytes belong to which output patch. This alignment is deterministic because patches were constructed during encoding; the decoder stores the start‑index of each patch (a lightweight integer array) and uses it to construct the mask for each attention step.  

* **Multi‑Head Design** – As with the encoder, the decoder uses several heads (\(U_{\mathcal{D}}\)). Each head operates on the lower‑dimensional key/value vectors derived from patches, while the query dimension remains \(h_{\mathcal{D}}\). The heads are concatenated and projected back to the decoder hidden size.  

* **Residual Connections and Pre‑LayerNorm** – The cross‑attention output is added to the byte query before the subsequent transformer sub‑layer, and a pre‑LayerNorm is applied to queries, keys, and values. No positional embeddings are used inside the cross‑attention because the relative ordering of bytes inside a patch is already captured by the local encoder’s attention.

## Why the Global Transformer Dominates FLOPs  

The total FLOP count for a forward pass of BLT can be expressed as  

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{BLT}} = 
\underbrace{\frac{n}{\bar{s}} \;\text{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{G}}}_{\text{Global Transformer}} \;
+\;
\underbrace{n \;\text{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{Local Encoder}} \;
+\;
\underbrace{n \;\text{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\text{Local Decoder}} \;
+\;
\text{Cross‑Attention FLOPs}.
\]

Here \(n\) is the number of bytes and \(\bar{s}\) is the average patch size. The Global term scales with the **patch count** \(n/\bar{s}\), while the encoder/decoder terms scale linearly with the byte count. Since transformer FLOPs per layer are roughly quadratic in sequence length, the Global term grows as  

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{G}} \propto \left(\frac{n}{\bar{s}}\right)^{2},
\]

whereas the local terms grow as \(\mathcal{O}(n)\). For typical average patch sizes of 4–8 bytes, the reduction factor \(\bar{s}\) shrinks the quadratic term by \(\bar{s}^{2}\), making the Global Transformer the **primary consumer of compute** despite being the only component that contains the bulk of the parameters.  

Consequently, any design decision that reduces the number of global steps (e.g., larger patches, entropy‑driven merging of low‑entropy bytes) directly translates into a substantial FLOP saving, while the local modules remain inexpensive enough to be run on every byte without breaking the budget. This asymmetry is the core intuition behind BLT: allocate **most parameters** to a powerful global model, but invoke it **only when the data warrants** (i.e., at patch boundaries).  

---  

### Summary  

The BLT architecture strategically separates **fine‑grained byte processing** (handled by lightweight Local Encoder/Decoder plus cross‑attention) from **coarse‑grained, high‑capacity reasoning** (handled by the Global Latent Transformer). Patch construction—whether static, whitespace‑based, or entropy‑driven—determines how many times the Global Transformer needs to be executed. Because the Global Transformer’s FLOPs dominate, increasing average patch size yields large inference savings, while the hash‑n‑gram embeddings and Perceiver‑style cross‑attention ensure that each patch still carries enough sub‑byte information for accurate generation. This design enables a tokenizer‑free LLM that matches token‑based baselines in performance while offering superior compute‑efficiency and robustness.
