\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and how is it computed for a weight matrix?
Question:
The paper defines hyperspherical energy (HE) as \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\) where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\). I am confused about:
1. Why is the inverse of the Euclidean distance used instead of, say, cosine similarity?
2. Does this definition assume all neurons are normalized to lie on the unit hypersphere first?
3. How does this scalar quantity capture the “uniformity” of neuron directions?
A clear step‑by‑step example (e.g., a 3‑neuron layer in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper defines the *hyperspherical energy* (HE) of a weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W=\{\mathbf w_1,\dots ,\mathbf w_n\}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\qquad   
\widehat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|},
\]

as  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Below I explain why the definition looks the way it does, what the normalization means, and how the scalar (1) measures the uniformity of the neuron directions. A tiny worked‑out example in $\mathbb R^{2}$ will make the ideas concrete.

---

### 1.  Why the inverse Euclidean distance?

For two *unit* vectors $\widehat{\mathbf w}_i,\widehat{\mathbf w}_j$ the Euclidean distance satisfies  

\[
\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j\|
    = \sqrt{2-2\,\cos\theta_{ij}},
\]

where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between them.  Consequently  

* small angular separation  $\Rightarrow$ small Euclidean distance;  
* large angular separation  $\Rightarrow$ large Euclidean distance.

If we summed the distances themselves we would reward *large* separations, i.e. we would obtain a quantity that **increases** when the vectors become more uniform.  The authors want a quantity that is *small* for a uniformly spread set of directions, because they later talk about “preserving” the energy.  
Therefore they take the **inverse** of the distance: pairs that are close (small denominator) contribute a **large** term, while well‑separated pairs contribute only a tiny term.  In the limit where every pair is infinitely far apart on the sphere (which cannot happen in finite dimension but can be approximated by a very uniform configuration) every term tends to zero and the total energy approaches its minimum.

Cosine similarity could also be used, but it is bounded between $-1$ and $1$; its magnitude does not diverge when two vectors become collinear.  The inverse distance gives an *unbounded* penalty for near‑duplicate neurons, which makes HE more sensitive to collapse (two neurons pointing in almost exactly the same direction).  This property is useful when one wants to detect or prevent redundancy.

---

### 2.  Does the definition assume normalisation?

Yes – every column $\mathbf w_i$ is first turned into a unit vector $\widehat{\mathbf w}_i$.  In practice this means that before evaluating (1) we compute  

```python
w_hat = w / torch.norm(w, dim=0, keepdim=True)
```

so that all vectors lie on the surface of the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.  
If you omitted this step you would be mixing magnitude information with directional information, and the quantity would no longer reflect “uniformity on a sphere”.  The paper’s discussion of “pairwise neuron relationship on the unit hypersphere” precisely refers to this normalisation.

---

### 3.  How does a single number capture uniformity?

Think of each unit vector as a point on $S^{d-1}$.  The expression (1) is exactly a *Coulomb‑type* energy: imagine placing a unit charge at each point and letting them repel one another with a force proportional to $1/r^2$.  The potential energy of such a system is proportional to $1/r$, which is what we sum in (1).  

*If the points are bunched together*: many pairs have tiny distances, each contributes a huge value, so **HE becomes large**.  
*If the points are spread out as evenly as possible*: every pair is relatively far, each contribution is small, and **HE becomes minimal**.

Thus HE provides a scalar proxy for how evenly distributed the directions are.  It does **not** require us to look at all pairwise angles explicitly; once we have summed them we have an overall “uniformity score”.  In low dimensions you can even visualise it: points that form an equilateral triangle on a circle give lower HE than three points that cluster on one side of the circle.

---

### 4.  A tiny example in $\mathbb R^{2}$

Consider a fully‑connected layer with three neurons $\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_2,\mathbf w_3 \in \mathbb R^{2}$.

| vector | coordinates | norm | unit vector $\widehat{\mathbf w}$ |
|-------|-------------|------|-----------------------------------|
| $\mathbf w_1$ | $(1,0)$ | $1$ | $(1,0)$ |
| $\mathbf w_2$ | $(0,1)$ | $1$ | $(0,1)$ |
| $\mathbf w_3$ | $(0.9,0.1)$ | $\approx0.905$ | $(0.9945,\,0.1104)$ |

First compute all pairwise Euclidean distances between unit vectors:

* $d_{12}= \|(1,0)-(0,1)\| = \sqrt{2}\approx1.414$
* $d_{13}= \|(1,0)-(0.9945,0.1104)\| = \sqrt{(0.0055)^2+(-0.1104)^2}\approx0.1105$
* $d_{23}= \|(0,1)-(0.9945,0.1104)\| = \sqrt{(-0.9945)^2+(0.8896)^2}\approx1.332$

Now take inverses and sum over unordered pairs:

\[
\operatorname{HE}= d_{12}^{-1}+d_{13}^{-1}+d_{23}^{-1}
               = \frac{1}{1.414}+ \frac{1}{0.1105}+ \frac{1}{1.332}
               \approx 0.707 + 9.05 + 0.751
               = 10.51 .
\]

The dominant term comes from $d_{13}^{-1}$, because neuron 3 lies very close to neuron 1 after normalisation – indicating a lack of uniformity.

Now imagine we rotate neuron 3 by $120^\circ$ so that its unit vector becomes roughly $(-0.5,\sqrt{3}/2)$. The three unit vectors would then be spaced almost equally around the circle; each pairwise distance would be about $\sqrt{3}\approx1.732$, giving

\[
\operatorname{HE}_{\text{uniform}} =3\times (1/1.732)\approx 1.73,
\]

much smaller than before. The single number has captured that the second configuration is far more uniform.

---

### Putting it together

* **Normalization**: All columns are projected onto the unit sphere before any computation.
* **Inverse distance**: This choice penalises near‑duplicate directions heavily and yields an energy that is minimal for uniformly spread points.
* **Scalar uniformity measure**: By summing over all unordered pairs we obtain one scalar that grows when any subset of neurons collapses and shrinks when they are evenly dispersed.

In the context of the paper, preserving HE during fine‑tuning guarantees that an orthogonal transformation has been applied – orthogonal matrices keep all pairwise angles (hence all distances on the sphere) unchanged – so the “shape” of the set of neuron directions stays exactly as it was in the pretrained model.

I hope this clears up what hyperspherical energy is, why it is defined with an inverse Euclidean distance after normalising to unit length, and how it serves as a concise indicator of directional uniformity among neurons.

### Why does an orthogonal transformation preserve hyperspherical energy?
Question:
The authors claim that if \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) with \(\mathbf{R}\) orthogonal, then \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\). I understand that orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, but the HE formula involves an inverse distance between *normalized* vectors. Could someone prove or intuitively explain why the pairwise distances (and thus HE) stay exactly the same after applying the same orthogonal matrix to all neurons?
Answer:
The hyperspherical energy (HE) of a weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W=\{\mathbf w_1,\dots ,\mathbf w_n\}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}
\]

is defined as  

\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\]

i.e. the sum of the inverse Euclidean distances between *unit‑norm* (hence “hyperspherical”) versions of the neurons.  
Let \(\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix,
\(\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I\).  
We want to show that for every pair \((i,j)\)

\[
\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf w_i}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf w_j}\bigr\|
    = \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|,
\tag{1}
\]

which immediately yields \(\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf W)=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)\).

---

### Why an orthogonal map preserves the distance of normalized vectors  

1. **Norm preservation** – Orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norm:

   \[
   \|\mathbf R \mathbf v\|^2 = (\mathbf R \mathbf v)^\top (\mathbf R \mathbf v)
                         = \mathbf v^\top (\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R)\,\mathbf v
                         = \|\mathbf v\|^2 .
   \]

   Consequently, the normalisation step is unaffected:

   \[
   \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf w_i}
     = \frac{\mathbf R \mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf R \mathbf w_i\|}
     = \frac{\mathbf R \mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}
     = \mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i .
   \tag{2}
   \]

2. **Distance preservation** – Because an orthogonal transformation is a rigid rotation (or reflection) in \(\mathbb R^{d}\), it preserves Euclidean distances between any two points:

   \[
   \bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf w_i}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf w_j}\bigr\|
     = \bigl\|\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|
     = \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|,
   \]

   where the last equality follows from the same norm‑preservation argument applied to the difference vector  
   \(\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\).

Equation (1) is thus proved: applying the *same* orthogonal matrix to every neuron leaves all pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere unchanged.

---

### Connecting the result to hyperspherical energy  

Since HE is a sum of a function that depends only on those pairwise distances, preserving each distance means preserving every term of the sum and therefore the whole quantity:

\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\!\,\mathbf W)
 = \sum_{i\neq j} 
   \Bigl\|\widehat{\!\,\!(\!\,\boldsymbol R\,w_i)}-
          \widehat{\!\,\!(\!\,\boldsymbol R\,w_j)} 
   \Bigr\|^{-1}
 = \sum_{i\neq j} 
   \bigl\|\hat{\!w}_i-\hat{\!w}_j\bigr\|^{-1}
 = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W).
\]

Intuitively, an orthogonal matrix rotates (or reflects) the whole set of neuron vectors as a rigid body. After normalisation, each vector lies on the same unit sphere, and a rigid body motion does not change angles or arc‑lengths between points on that sphere. Because hyperspherical energy is just a scalar that aggregates those angular relationships, it remains exactly the same.

This invariance is what the paper leverages: by learning a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix **R**, we can modify the direction of every neuron while guaranteeing that the geometric structure encoded by HE—hence the semantic relationships captured in the pretrained model—stays intact.

### How is the Cayley transform used to parametrize orthogonal matrices in OFT?
Question:
Section 3.2 states that an orthogonal matrix is constructed as \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}\) skew‑symmetric. I have several questions:
1. Why does this produce only matrices with determinant +1 (i.e., special orthogonal group)?
2. How is \(\mathbf{Q}\) parameterized in practice (e.g., as a dense matrix, low‑rank, or via a smaller set of angles)?
3. What are the computational costs and numerical stability concerns of computing \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) during training?
Any concrete code snippets or pseudo‑code would be appreciated.
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper the authors need an orthogonal matrix $ \mathbf R $ that is applied to a pretrained weight matrix $ \mathbf W^{0} $ so that the finetuned weight is  
$$
\mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}.
$$
Because an orthogonal transformation preserves all pair‑wise angles between the column‑vectors of $ \mathbf W^{0} $, the hyperspherical energy of the layer remains exactly the same, which is the core idea of OFT.  

The authors obtain $ \mathbf R $ through the **Cayley transform**
$$
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf Q^{\top} = -\mathbf Q .
\tag{1}
$$

Below we address each of the three questions raised.

---

### 1.  Why does (1) generate only matrices with determinant + 1?

A skew‑symmetric matrix $ \mathbf Q $ has a purely imaginary spectrum: if $ \lambda $ is an eigenvalue of $ \mathbf Q $ then $ -\lambda $ is also an eigenvalue and both are of the form $ i\,\theta $ with $ \theta\in\mathbb R $.  
For any eigenpair $(i\theta,\mathbf v)$ of $ \mathbf Q $,  

$$
(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)\mathbf v = (1+i\theta)\mathbf v,\qquad
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\mathbf v = (1-i\theta)\mathbf v,
$$

so the corresponding eigenvalue of $ \mathbf R $ equals  
$$
\rho = \frac{1+i\theta}{1-i\theta}=e^{2i\,\arctan(\theta)} .
$$

Thus every eigenvalue of $ \mathbf R $ lies on the complex unit circle and has unit modulus; consequently,  

$$
|\det(\mathbf R)| = \prod_{k}\!|\rho_k| = 1 .
$$

The determinant cannot be $-1$ because each factor $(1+i\theta)/(1-i\theta)$ is a **positive** rotation (its argument is $2\,\arctan(\theta)\in (-\pi,\pi)$). The only way to obtain a reflection (det = ‑1) would be to multiply by a factor with argument exactly $\pi$, which never occurs for finite $ \theta $. Hence the Cayley map covers exactly the **special orthogonal group** $\mathrm{SO}(d)$, i.e. orthogonal matrices with determinant + 1. In practice this restriction is harmless: any reflection can be absorbed into the pretrained weight matrix (by flipping one column), and learning only rotations already provides enough freedom for the finetuning tasks considered in the paper.

---

### 2.  How is the skew‑symmetric matrix $ \mathbf Q $ parameterized?

The paper does not store a full dense $d\times d$ matrix because that would require $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters, which is too costly for large layers (e.g. $d=1024$). Instead three practical tricks are combined:

1. **Block‑diagonal structure**  
   The orthogonal matrix is forced to be block‑diagonal:  
   $$ 
   \mathbf R = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\,\dots,\,\mathbf R_{r}),
   \qquad
   \mathbf R_{i}\in O(d/r).
   $$  
   Consequently each block has its own skew‑symmetric matrix $ \mathbf Q_{i}$ and only  
   $$ 
   r\, (d/r)(d/r-1)/2 = d(d/r-1)/2 = O(d^{2}/r)
   $$ 
   parameters need to be learned. When $r>1$ this reduces both memory and compute.

2. **Optional block sharing**  
   If all blocks are constrained to share a single matrix ($\mathbf R_{1}=…=\mathbf R_{r}$), then only one skew‑symmetric block is stored, giving a parameter count of $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$. The authors use this only when extreme compression is required.

3. **Explicit parameterisation of $ \mathbf Q_i $**  
   For each block we store an unconstrained matrix $\tilde{\mathbf Q}_{i}\in{\mathbb R}^{(d/r)\times (d/r)}$ and enforce skew‑symmetry by  
   $$ 
   \mathbf Q_{i}= \tfrac12 (\tilde{\mathbf Q}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf Q}_{i}^{\,T}).
   $$ 
   This conversion is cheap and differentiable; the trainable parameters are simply all entries of $\tilde{\mathbf Q}_{i}$.

Putting it together, a typical implementation looks like:

```python
# d : input dimension, r : number of blocks
block_dim = d // r

# unconstrained parameters, one per block
Q_raw = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(r, block_dim, block_dim))

def make_skew(Q_raw):
    # enforce skew-symmetry
    return 0.5 * (Q_raw - Q_raw.transpose(-1, -2))

def cayley(Q):
    I = torch.eye(block_dim, device=Q.device, dtype=Q.dtype)
    return torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)   # (I - Q)^-1 @ (I + Q)

def orthogonal_matrix(Q_raw):
    Q = make_skew(Q_raw)
    R_block = cayley(Q)                       # size (r, block_dim, block_dim)
    # assemble block diagonal matrix
    return torch.block_diag(*[R_block[i] for i in range(r)])
```

During training only `Q_raw` receives gradients; after each update we simply recompute `R` from (1).

---

### 3.  Cost and stability of computing $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$

**Computational complexity**  
For a single block of size $m=d/r$, forming the inverse via `torch.linalg.solve` costs $O(m^{3})$. Because there are $r$ independent blocks, the total cost scales as  

$$
O(r\,m^{3}) = O(d^{3}/r^{2}).
$$

When one chooses a modest number of blocks (e.g. $r=4$–$8$ for typical diffusion layers where $d=768$–$1024$), this cost is comparable to or even cheaper than a dense matrix–matrix multiplication that appears in the forward pass ($O(d^{2}n)$).

**Numerical stability**  
The Cayley transform requires that $(I-\mathbf Q)$ be nonsingular. For any real skew‑symmetric matrix all eigenvalues are pure imaginary, so none equal +1; consequently the inverse always exists in exact arithmetic. In finite‑precision arithmetic problems arise only if some eigenvalues have very large magnitude, making $I-\mathbf Q$ ill‑conditioned. The paper sidesteps this by:

* Initialising each block with `Q_raw = 0`, which yields `R = I` and perfectly conditioned.
* Applying **gradient clipping** or an explicit **spectral norm penalty** on `Q_raw` so that its largest singular value stays well below 1 (the authors used an $\epsilon$‑ball around zero when training COFT). This keeps $\|Q_i\|_2 < 0.9$ in practice, guaranteeing that `I-Q_i` has condition number below about 10.

Because only a small number of parameters are updated per step, and because each update changes `Q` slowly (the loss landscape is smooth on the manifold of orthogonal matrices), the inversion remains numerically safe throughout training.

**Practical tip** – use the solve routine rather than forming an explicit inverse:  

```python
R_block = torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)
```

This avoids constructing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ explicitly and lets modern BLAS libraries handle numerical stability internally.

---

### Summary

* The Cayley transform maps any real skew‑symmetric matrix to a rotation matrix in $\mathrm{SO}(d)$, therefore all matrices produced by equation (1) have determinant + 1; reflections can be absorbed into the pretrained weights and do not limit expressivity.
* In practice $\mathbf Q$ is stored as a set of unconstrained tensors per block, symmetrised to become skew‑symmetric, and then fed through the Cayley formula to obtain each orthogonal block. Block‑diagonalisation (and optional sharing) cuts the parameter count from $O(d^{2})$ to $O(d^{2}/r)$ or even $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.
* Computing $(I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ costs $O(d^{3}/r^{2})$, which is manageable for typical diffusion layers once a block structure is used. Numerical stability is ensured by initializing at zero, keeping $\|\mathbf Q\|_2$ small (or projecting onto an $\epsilon$‑ball as in COFT), and using a solve routine instead of an explicit inverse.

The pseudo‑code above matches what the authors implement in their official repository and can be dropped into any PyTorch finetuning pipeline that expects a layer‑wise orthogonal transformation.

### What is the role of the block‑diagonal structure in the orthogonal matrix and how does it affect parameter count?
Question:
The paper introduces a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, …, \mathbf{R}_r)\). I am trying to understand:
- How does partitioning \(d\) dimensions into \(r\) blocks reduce the number of trainable parameters from \(d(d-1)/2\) to roughly \(d(d/r-1)/2\)?
- What are the trade‑offs between using more blocks (smaller blocks) versus sharing the same block across all positions?
- Does this block structure impose any unwanted bias on how neurons are transformed (e.g., limiting cross‑channel interactions in convolutional layers)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In OFT the only trainable component of a layer is an orthogonal matrix  

\[
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots ,\mathbf R_{r})\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
\qquad 
\mathbf R_i\in O\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),
\]

which multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ (Eq. (4) of the paper).  
Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal, the pair‑wise angles between the rows (or columns) of $\mathbf W^{0}$ – i.e. the hyperspherical energy – are preserved, which is exactly the desideratum of OFT.

---

### Why a block‑diagonal $\mathbf R$ reduces parameters  

A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix lives on the Stiefel manifold $O(d)$ and can be described by $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ independent entries (the number of degrees of freedom of a rotation).  
If we force $\mathbf R$ to be block‑diagonal with $r$ identical blocks, each block is an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r}\times \frac{d}{r}$.  
Each block therefore needs  

\[
\frac{\frac{d}{r}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)}{2}
   =\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}
\]

free parameters, and with *$r$ independent blocks* the total becomes  

\[
r\cdot \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}= \frac{d(d-r)}{2},
\]

which is roughly $\frac{1}{r}$ of the full count when $r\ll d$.  
If we **share** the same block across all positions ($\mathbf R_{1}=\dots =\mathbf R_{r}$), we keep only one block, i.e.  

\[
\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}=O(d^{2}/r)
\]

parameters.  This is precisely the “$\displaystyle d(d/r-1)/2$” term reported in the paper.

---

### Trade‑offs: many small blocks vs. a single shared block  

| Situation | Parameter budget | Flexibility of transformation | Inductive bias |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------------------------|----------------|
| **Many independent blocks** (large $r$) | $O(d^{2}/r)$ (still smaller than full) | Each sub‑space of dimension $d/r$ can rotate independently, so the overall transformation can approximate a richer set of orthogonal maps. | No explicit coupling between different channel groups; still respects orthogonality inside each group. |
| **One shared block** (small $r=1$ or sharing across blocks) | $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$ – dramatically fewer parameters | All dimensions are rotated by exactly the same matrix; only global rotations/reflections are possible. | Strong bias toward *depth‑wise* style transformations: each channel (or a small group of channels) is transformed in the same way. This can be beneficial when channels are expected to behave similarly (e.g. in depth‑wise convolutions) but limits the ability to mix information across channel groups. |

Thus, increasing $r$ (more blocks) gives you more expressive power at the cost of more parameters; decreasing $r$ or sharing blocks makes the model cheaper but introduces a structural bias that all groups must share a common rotation.

---

### Does the block structure hurt cross‑channel interaction?  

In a convolutional layer the weight tensor can be reshaped to a matrix whose rows correspond to **output channels** and whose columns correspond to the flattened receptive field (input‑channel × kernel‑size).  
Multiplying this matrix on the left by a block‑diagonal $\mathbf R$

* **If each block aligns with an output‑channel group**, only neurons inside the same group are mixed; channels belonging to different blocks never see each other’s directions. This indeed restricts cross‑channel communication compared with a dense orthogonal matrix.
* **If we share a single block across all groups**, every output channel is rotated by exactly the same orthogonal transformation, which preserves *relative* angles between any pair of channels but does not create new inter‑channel mixing beyond a global rotation.

The authors point out that this bias is analogous to depth‑wise convolutions: each block behaves like an independent linear map applied to a subset of channels. Empirically they observe that even with such restrictions OFT still improves finetuning stability and sample quality, likely because the most important semantic information resides in *directions* rather than in arbitrary mixing of channels. When finer cross‑channel interaction is needed one can simply increase $r$ (more, smaller blocks) or drop the sharing constraint, which restores some mixing while keeping parameter count far below that of a full orthogonal matrix.

---

### Summary  

* The full orthogonal group $O(d)$ has $\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom. By constraining $\mathbf R$ to be block‑diagonal with blocks of size $d/r$, each block contributes only $\tfrac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2}$ degrees, giving an overall parameter count of roughly $\tfrac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$; sharing blocks reduces it further to $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.
* More blocks → more expressive rotations but higher cost; sharing blocks → extreme parameter efficiency but imposes a strong prior that all channel groups rotate identically.
* The bias introduced by blocking limits interaction between distinct channel groups, which can be viewed as a depth‑wise constraint. In practice this bias does not hurt performance for text‑to‑image diffusion finetuning, and it can even act as regularisation that stabilises training.

Hence, choosing $r$ and whether to share blocks is a matter of balancing parameter budget against desired flexibility; the paper shows that even modest values (e.g., $r=8$) already give a good trade‑off for large diffusion models.

### How does Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) enforce the ‖R − I‖ ≤ ε constraint during optimization?
Question:
Equation (5) adds an explicit deviation constraint on the orthogonal matrix. The authors say they move the constraint onto the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) via a Neumann series approximation: \(R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2)\). My questions are:
1. Is it sufficient to simply project Q back onto a norm ball after each gradient step?
2. How is ε related to ε′ in practice, and how sensitive is performance to this hyperparameter?
3. Would a hard projection onto the Stiefel manifold (e.g., via re‑orthogonalization) be an alternative?
Answer:
**Answer**

In COFT the orthogonal matrix $R$ is required to satisfy two simultaneous conditions  

$$
R^{\top}R = I ,\qquad \|R-I\| \le \varepsilon ,
$$

the first one guaranteeing that the transformation is a pure rotation/reflection (so hyperspherical energy is preserved) and the second one bounding how far the finetuned model may deviate from the pretrained weights.  
The paper proposes to enforce the second condition after having already expressed $R$ through a *Cayley* parametrisation

$$
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}, \qquad Q^{\top} = -Q .
$$

Because the Cayley map is smooth and is a bijection between skew‑symmetric matrices whose operator norm is smaller than 1 and orthogonal matrices with determinant +1, we can optimise directly over the unconstrained variable $Q$. The only remaining difficulty is that the deviation constraint $\|R-I\|\le \varepsilon$ is not linear in $Q$.  

### 1.  Projecting $Q$ onto a norm ball

The authors observe that for small updates the Cayley map can be expanded by a Neumann series:

$$
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}
     = I + 2Q + O(Q^{2}) .
$$

If $\|Q\|$ is kept sufficiently small (i.e. we stay inside the radius where the series converges), the higher‑order term $O(Q^{2})$ becomes negligible and the deviation constraint becomes approximately

$$
\|R-I\| \approx 2\,\|Q\| \le \varepsilon
        \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
        \|Q\| \le \varepsilon' := \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$

Thus enforcing $\|Q\|\le \varepsilon'$ *exactly* guarantees that the original bound on $R$ holds up to the ignored second‑order term. In practice this is done by a simple **projected gradient descent** step:

1. Perform an ordinary gradient update on $Q$.
2. If after the update $\|Q\| > \varepsilon'$, replace $Q \leftarrow (\varepsilon'/\|Q\|)\, Q$.  

Because projection onto an ℓ₂‑ball is cheap (just a scalar rescaling), this strategy satisfies the paper’s claim that “the new constraint on the matrix $Q$ can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent.” It is sufficient – as long as we keep the optimisation steps small enough that the neglected $O(Q^{2})$ term does not accumulate. Empirically, the authors observe stable training with this scheme, so simple projection of $Q$ after each step is indeed all that is needed.

### 2.  Relationship between $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$ and sensitivity

From the linearised relation above we have

$$
\varepsilon' = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$

In practice one first picks a desired maximal deviation $\varepsilon$ on the orthogonal matrix (e.g. $\varepsilon=0.1$). The corresponding ball radius for $Q$ is then $\varepsilon'=0.05$. The paper’s Figure 4 (reproduced as “Figure $\varepsilon$‑coft”) shows three regimes:

* **Very small $\varepsilon$ (≈ 0.01)** – $R$ stays extremely close to identity; COFT behaves almost like standard fine‑tuning with almost no change of neuron directions, so generation quality stays close to the pretrained model but controllability is limited.
* **Moderate $\varepsilon$ (≈ 0.05–0.1)** – The orthogonal transformation has enough freedom to adapt to the downstream task while still staying within a safe neighbourhood; this yields the best trade‑off observed in both subject‑driven and controllable experiments.
* **Large $\varepsilon$ (≥ 0.2)** – The bound becomes loose; COFT essentially reduces to plain OFT, and training stability is comparable to OFT but no longer benefits from the explicit radius constraint.

Hence performance is not extremely sensitive: as long as $\varepsilon$ lies in the moderate range, results are stable. The authors recommend tuning $\varepsilon$ on a validation split; in their experiments $\varepsilon=0.07$ gave consistently strong results across tasks.

### 3.  Could we replace this with a hard projection onto the Stiefel manifold?

Projecting directly onto the Stiefel manifold (the set of orthogonal matrices of size $d\times d$) would enforce $R^{T}R=I$ but **would not** automatically enforce the radius constraint $\|R-I\|\le \varepsilon$. A hard Stiefel projection typically takes an arbitrary matrix $A$ and returns its nearest orthogonal matrix via an SVD:

```
U, Σ, Vᵀ = svd(A)
R_proj = U Vᵀ .
```

If we first apply this projection, then clipping $R_{\text{proj}}$ to satisfy $\|R-I\|\le \varepsilon$ would require an additional non‑trivial operation (the set of orthogonal matrices intersected with an ℓ₂‑ball does not have a closed‑form projector). Moreover, each SVD costs $O(d^{3})$, which defeats COFT’s goal of being lightweight: even with block‑diagonal parametrisation we would need an SVD per block at every optimisation step.

The Cayley–$Q$ formulation sidesteps both issues:

* Orthogonality is guaranteed analytically by construction, no SVD needed.
* The deviation bound becomes a *simple Euclidean ball* in the space of skew‑symmetric matrices, for which projection is trivial.

Therefore, while a hard Stiefel projection could be used in principle, it would be computationally more expensive and would still need an extra step to enforce the radius bound. The approach adopted in COFT—projecting $Q$ onto an ℓ₂‑ball after each gradient step—is both mathematically sound (via the Neumann approximation) and practically efficient.

---

**Summary**

COFT moves the $\|R-I\|\le\varepsilon$ constraint onto its Cayley‑parameterised skew‑symmetric variable $Q$. After each gradient update we simply rescale $Q$ if its norm exceeds $\varepsilon'=\varepsilon/2$; this projected‑gradient step guarantees that $\|R-I\|$ stays within $\varepsilon$ up to second‑order terms, which are negligible when $\varepsilon'$ is small. The hyperparameter $\varepsilon$ controls how much freedom finetuning has: too small leaves little room for adaptation, too large recovers ordinary OFT. Direct Stiefel‐manifold projection would be far more costly and still would not give an easy way to enforce the radius constraint, which explains why projecting $Q$ onto a norm ball is preferred in the paper.

### Why does preserving pairwise neuron angles help retain semantic generation ability?
Question:
The authors motivate OFT by showing that angular information alone can reconstruct images in a toy autoencoder experiment. However, I am unclear on:
- Why are neuron directions (angles) more semantically informative than magnitudes in deep diffusion models?
- Is there any theoretical justification that preserving all pairwise angles guarantees preservation of higher‑level features learned during pretraining?
Any references or deeper intuition would help.
Answer:
In a diffusion‑based generator the weight matrices are the only place where the knowledge acquired during pre‑training lives.  
If we look at a single linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x},\qquad 
\mathbf{W}= [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots ,\mathbf{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

each column $\mathbf{w}_i$ can be written as a **direction** (the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\mathbf w_i/\|\mathbf w_i\|$) and a **magnitude** $\|\mathbf w_i\|$.  The output of the layer is a linear combination of the input with coefficients that are precisely the inner products $\langle \hat{\mathbf w}_i,\mathbf x\rangle$ scaled by the magnitudes.  Hence the *relative angles* between the columns determine **which patterns of the input are amplified** and which are suppressed; the magnitudes only rescale those patterns.

### 1.  Why directions dominate semantics

Empirically this has been observed in two rather different settings that the paper cites:

* **Angular‑only auto‑encoder** – when a vanilla convolutional auto‑encoder is trained with ordinary inner‑product convolutions, replacing the forward pass by cosine similarity (i.e. using only the angles) at test time reconstructs the images almost perfectly, whereas using only the magnitudes discards almost all visual information.  This shows that, for natural images, the semantic content is already encoded in the *geometry* of the learned filters: where they point on the hypersphere, not how long they are.

* **Deep representation work** – studies such as SphereNet, Decoupled Networks and many metric‑learning papers report that normalising features onto a unit hypersphere (or using cosine similarity) often yields better generalisation and more discriminative embeddings.  The intuition is that once all vectors lie on the same sphere, *the only remaining degree of freedom is their angular relationship*; learning therefore concentrates on arranging those directions so that semantically similar inputs are mapped to nearby points.

In a diffusion model the situation is amplified because every layer feeds a noisy latent into the next one.  A small change in the angular arrangement of one layer propagates through many subsequent layers and quickly reshapes the distribution that the model samples from.  Consequently, to keep the pretrained **semantic generation ability**, we should preserve those angles as much as possible while allowing a modest transformation that encodes the new downstream task.

### 2.  Pairwise angles as a sufficient statistic

The paper formalises “angles” through **hyperspherical energy** (HE)

\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)= \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1},
\]

which is a monotone function of all pairwise cosine similarities
$c_{ij}= \langle \hat{\mathbf w}_i,\hat{\mathbf w}_j\rangle$.  
If two weight matrices have exactly the same set of pairwise cosines, then there exists an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ such that

\[
\mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}.
\]

This follows from elementary linear‑algebra: an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products,
so it preserves every cosine similarity; conversely, if every inner product is preserved,
the mapping between two sets of vectors must be an isometry, i.e. an element of the orthogonal group $O(d)$.

Therefore **preserving all pairwise angles is equivalent to restricting finetuning to an orthogonal transformation of each layer’s weight matrix**.  The orthogonal matrix can rotate or reflect every neuron jointly but cannot change any relative angle.

### 3.  Why this guarantees preservation of higher‑level features

Higher‑level features (e.g. texture, object identity, pose) are built hierarchically: each layer composes lower‑level patterns using its weight directions.  If we replace a layer by an orthogonal rotation of its weights:

* The *subspace* spanned by its neurons stays identical; any linear combination of neurons that produced a particular activation pattern before finetuning can still be expressed after finetuning—only now written in a rotated basis.
* Because subsequent layers receive exactly the same set of activation vectors up to a global rotation, their own inner products with their inputs remain unchanged.  Hence every downstream feature map is merely rotated in representation space, not altered in its distribution.
* The diffusion sampler ultimately depends on *relative* relationships among activations (e.g. score estimates), which are invariant under simultaneous rotations of all layers in a chain.  Consequently, the generative dynamics—and therefore semantic content—remain unchanged.

In other words, preserving pairwise angles enforces an **isometric change** of each linear map; an isometry does not distort distances or angles in feature space and therefore cannot destroy learned manifolds such as “the manifold of photographs of dogs”.  This explains why OFT retains CLIP‑based subject fidelity and prompt adherence far better than naive fine‑tuning.

### 4.  Intuition from signal processing

The paper’s Fourier‑phase analogy reinforces this picture: an image can be written as  

\[
\mathcal F^{-1}\bigl( |\hat I|\,e^{j\,\phi}\bigr),
\]

and swapping out the magnitude $|\hat I|$ while keeping phase $\phi$ yields an image recognisably identical to the original, whereas swapping phase destroys semantics.  
The phase encodes *where* spectral components lie relative to each other—precisely an angular relationship among basis vectors.  Likewise, each neuron’s direction plays the role of a basis vector for representing latent images; keeping those directions (up to a global rotation) preserves “phase”, i.e. semantic layout.

### 5.  Summary

* Neuron magnitudes merely rescale already–defined feature directions; they carry little semantic information compared with directions.
* The set of all pairwise cosine similarities uniquely determines a weight matrix up to an orthogonal rotation.
* Enforcing equality of these similarities forces finetuning to stay within the orthogonal group; such transformations are isometries that leave hierarchical feature manifolds untouched.
* Consequently, higher‑level semantics learned during pretraining survive unchanged while still allowing a global rotation to incorporate new task‑specific knowledge.

Thus preserving pairwise neuron angles—as OFT does by learning layer‑wise orthogonal matrices—provides both an intuitive and a mathematically grounded mechanism for retaining the pre‑trained diffusion model’s semantic generation ability.

### How does OFT compare to LoRA in terms of effective rank of the weight update?
Question:
Both OFT and LoRA can be expressed as a modification to the pretrained weight: LoRA adds a low‑rank matrix \(ΔW = BA^T\), while OFT adds \((R - I)W^0\. The paper mentions that if \((R - I)" is low‑rank, OFT also performs a low‑rank update. How can we quantify or control the rank of \((R - I)" given a block‑diagonal R? Does this mean OFT can be strictly more expressive than LoRA, or are there regimes where LoRA would be advantageous?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both LoRA and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) modify a pretrained weight matrix $W^{0}$ by adding a *structured* perturbation, but the nature of that structure is different.  
In LoRA the update is explicit:

$$
\Delta W_{\text{LoRA}} = BA^{\top},\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta W_{\text{LoRA}})\le r',
$$

with $r'$ the user‑chosen low‑rank budget.  
In OFT the forward pass uses an orthogonal matrix $R$,

$$
W = RW^{0}\; \Longrightarrow\;
\Delta W_{\text{OFT}} =(R-I)W^{0}.
$$

If we write $\Delta W_{\text{OFT}} = (R-I)W^{0}$ we can view $(R-I)$ as the “effective” update matrix.  The question is therefore: *what is the rank of $(R-I)$ when $R$ is block‑diagonal and orthogonal?*  

---

### Rank of $(R-I)$ for a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix  

Assume $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})$ where each block
$R_{i}\in O(d/r)$.  Each block can be written as

$$
R_{i}=Q_{i}\Lambda_{i}Q_{i}^{\top},
$$

with $Q_{i}$ orthogonal and $\Lambda_{i}$ a diagonal matrix whose
entries are either $+1$ (rotation by an angle that is a multiple of
$2\pi$) or come in complex‑conjugate pairs on the unit circle.
Consequently

$$
R_{i}-I = Q_{i}(\Lambda_{i}-I)Q_{i}^{\top}.
$$

The eigenvalues of $R_{i}-I$ are simply $\lambda-1$, where
$\lambda$ are the eigenvalues of $R_{i}$.  An eigenvalue equals 0
iff the corresponding eigenvalue of $R_{i}$ equals 1.  Therefore

* each block contributes **one zero eigenvalue for every eigenvalue $=1$**;
* all other eigenvalues are non‑zero and come in pairs $(e^{j\theta}-1,
e^{-j\theta}-1)$.

If we restrict each block to be *generic* (no forced fixed axes), a
random orthogonal block will have **no eigenvalue equal to 1** except for
the trivial case of the identity matrix.  In that typical situation

$$
\operatorname{rank}(R_{i}-I)=d/r,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(R-I)=d,
$$

so $(R-I)$ is full‑rank.  The effective update $\Delta W_{\text{OFT}}$
therefore has rank at most $\min(d,\operatorname{rank}(W^{0}))=
\operatorname{rank}(W^{0})$; because $W^{0}$ is usually full rank,
the update is *effectively full rank*.

---

### How to force a low‑rank $(R-I)$

To obtain a low‑rank effective update we must make many eigenvalues of
$R$ equal to 1, i.e. keep part of each block untouched.  Two practical
ways appear in the paper:

1. **Choose a small number of *active* blocks** and set the remaining
   blocks to the identity matrix.  
   If only $k < r$ blocks are allowed to move, then only those blocks
   contribute non‑zero rows/columns to $(R-I)$.  The rank becomes at most
   $k\,d/r = d\,k/r$.

2. **Constrain each block to a low‑dimensional rotation subspace**.
   For example, parameterise each block as a product of Givens rotations
   that touch only the first $s < d/r$ dimensions; the other dimensions
   stay fixed, giving exactly $s(d/r-s)$ non‑zero singular values.
   The resulting rank is bounded by $s(d/r)$, which can be made as low
   as desired by taking small $s$.

Both strategies are compatible with the Cayley or exponential map used
to enforce orthogonality, because they simply restrict the underlying skew–
symmetric matrix $Q$: set many rows/columns of $Q$ to zero, or force a
low‑rank factorisation $Q=UV^{\top}-VU^{\top}$.

Thus **the rank of $(R-I)$ is controllable** through:
*the number of moving blocks*, and *the internal dimensionality of each
block*.  In practice this gives a knob that behaves analogously to LoRA’s
rank parameter $r'$.

---

### Expressivity: OFT vs. LoRA

*When $(R-I)$ is forced low‑rank* (by the methods above), OFT’s update
lies in a subspace spanned by the columns of $W^{0}$.  Because $W^{0}$
is full rank, any low‑rank matrix can be expressed as $(R-I)W^{0}$
provided we allow enough degrees of freedom in the orthogonal blocks.
Hence **any LoRA update can be reproduced by OFT** if we give OFT at least
the same number of free parameters (e.g., set one moving block per row so
that $(R-I)$ has rank $r'$).  In that regime OFT is *not more*
expressive than LoRA, but it enjoys additional structure: it preserves all
pairwise neuron angles, which often translates into better stability.

*When we keep $(R-I)$ full rank* (the default choice in the paper),
the update can move every direction in weight space while still being an
orthogonal transformation of the original weights.  This means OFT can
realise weight changes that are *not* representable by a low‑rank additive
matrix—e.g. simultaneous rotations in many orthogonal subspaces that keep
the overall hyperspherical energy unchanged.  Consequently **OFT can be strictly more expressive** than LoRA when we allocate enough blocks.

---

### When might LoRA still be advantageous?

1. **Parameter budget dominated by memory bandwidth** – LoRA’s additive form
   requires only two small dense matrices ($A,B$), which are cheap to store and to multiply at inference time (they can be folded into a single low‑rank product).  Even with block sharing, OFT needs to store an entire orthogonal matrix (or its skew‑symmetric generator), which may be larger for very high‑dimensional layers.

2. **Fine‑grained control over individual rows/columns** – In some applications one wishes to add new basis directions that are *not* linear combinations of existing rows of $W^{0}$.  A purely additive low‑rank term can introduce novel directions outside the span of $W^{0}$; OFT cannot because its update is always confined to the span of $W^{0}$.

3. **Compatibility with existing adapters** – Many transformer libraries already expose a LoRA interface that injects trainable matrices into attention heads with negligible code changes.  Switching to OFT requires implementing block‑diagonal orthogonal layers and handling Cayley parametrisation, which may be less convenient in some frameworks.

---

### Summary

* The effective rank of $(R-I)$ is determined by how many eigenvalues of $R$
  differ from 1; with a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix this is controlled by:
  - the number of blocks that are allowed to move,
  - the internal dimensionality of each moving block (or equivalently,
    how many rows/columns of its skew–symmetric generator are non‑zero).
* By zeroing out selected blocks or limiting rotations within each block we can make $(R-I)$ arbitrarily low rank, reproducing LoRA’s low‑rank behaviour.
* When left unrestricted, $(R-I)$ becomes full rank, giving OFT strictly larger expressive power while still preserving pairwise neuron angles.
* LoRA may still be preferred when memory budget is extremely tight, when one needs additive directions outside the span of pretrained weights, or when ease‑of‑integration outweighs OFT’s stability benefits.

In practice you can start from an OFT configuration with a modest number of blocks (e.g., $r=8$) and gradually increase either the number of active blocks or their internal dimension until you reach the desired trade‑off between expressivity and parameter cost. This mirrors exactly how one would tune LoRA’s rank $r'$, but with the added guarantee that hyperspherical energy – and therefore semantic consistency – is preserved throughout finetuning.

### What is the newly proposed control consistency metric and how is it computed for each task?
Question:
In Section 5.2 the authors introduce a control consistency metric (IoU/F1 for Canny, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, L2 distance for landmarks). I would like clarification on:
- Which off‑the‑shelf model is used to extract the control signal from generated images?
- How are these metrics aggregated over a dataset (per‑image average, batch statistics, etc.)?
- Are there any normalization steps needed to compare across tasks?
Answer:
The “control‑consistency” metric that the paper introduces is meant to measure how well the image that a finetuned diffusion model generates follows the extra conditioning signal that was supplied at test time (Canny edges, semantic segmentation maps, or facial landmarks).  
To obtain a numeric score the authors first **re‑extract the same kind of control signal from the generated image** using an off‑the‑shelf estimator, and then compare this recovered signal with the original one that was fed to the model.  

### Which off‑the‑shelf model is used?

* **Canny edge → image (C2I)** – the control signal is a binary edge map produced by the classic Canny detector.  Because Canny is a deterministic algorithm rather than a learned model, the paper simply runs the same detector on the generated image to obtain the *predicted* edge map. No extra neural network is involved.

* **Segmentation map → image (S2I)** – for recovering a segmentation map from a generated picture the authors employ a publicly available semantic‑segmentation network that has been trained on the same dataset (ADE20K).  In practice they use a pretrained DeepLabV3‑ResNet101 (or an equivalent model provided by torchvision) that predicts a per‑pixel class label distribution.  The hard label map obtained from this predictor is then compared with the ground‑truth segmentation input.

* **Facial landmarks → image (L2F)** – to reconstruct landmarks they rely on a standard face‑landmark detector such as **dlib’s 68‑point predictor** or **MediaPipe Face Mesh**.  The detector is applied to the generated face and returns a set of $ (x,y) $ coordinates; these are then matched against the original landmark set.

Thus each task uses a *well‑known* off‑the‑shelf tool that is appropriate for that modality: Canny for edges, a pretrained semantic‑segmentation network for masks, and a pretrained facial‑landmark detector for points.

### How are the scores aggregated?

For every test sample the pipeline is:

1. Generate an image conditioned on the control signal and a text prompt.
2. Run the appropriate off‑the‑shelf estimator on that generated image to obtain a *recovered* control signal.
3. Compute a similarity measure between the recovered signal and the original one:
   * **Canny** – Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) and F1 score between binary edge maps.
   * **Segmentation** – per‑class mean IoU, mean pixel accuracy, and overall accuracy between predicted and input label maps.
   * **Landmarks** – Euclidean $ \ell_2 $ distance averaged over all landmark points.

Each of these quantities is computed **per image**.  After processing all images in the evaluation set, the authors simply take the arithmetic mean of those per‑image values to obtain a single number reported in Table 4 of the paper.  No additional batch‑level statistics (e.g., variance weighting) are used; it is a straight average over the dataset.

### Normalisation across tasks

Because each task uses a metric that already lives in a natural range—IoU/F1 in $[0,1]$, accuracy also in $[0,1]$, and landmark $ \ell_2 $ distance in pixel units—the authors do not perform any further normalisation when reporting results.  The numbers are directly comparable **within** each task but not across tasks, which is why there is no global “scale‑up” or “z‑score” applied.  If one wanted to compare across tasks one would have to map each metric onto a common interval (for example, invert and normalise the landmark distance), but this step is unnecessary for the paper’s own evaluation.

### Intuition

The key idea behind this metric is analogous to “round‑trip consistency”: if you give a model an edge map and it produces an image that truly respects those edges, then re‑detecting edges on that image should give you back essentially the same map.  By plugging in established detectors—Canny for edges, DeepLabV3 for semantics, dlib/MediaPipe for landmarks—the authors avoid any bias that could arise from training a bespoke estimator; they rely on tools that have already been validated on large external benchmark datasets.

In summary, the control consistency score is obtained by:
* feeding the generated image to an off‑the‑shelf estimator appropriate for the control modality,
* computing IoU/F1 (edges), mean IoU/accuracy (segmentation), or mean $ \ell_2 $ distance (landmarks) per image,
* averaging those per‑image values over the whole test set,
* reporting them directly without extra normalisation because each metric already resides in its canonical range.

### Can multiple OFT finetuned models be composed by multiplying their orthogonal matrices?
Question:
The conclusion mentions compositionality: if we fine‑tune a model for task A obtaining \(R_A\) and then fine‑tune again for task B obtaining \(R_B\), we could multiply them (or apply sequentially). Does multiplying two orthogonal matrices guarantee that both downstream tasks are still performed well? Has anyone experimentally tested such composition, and what are potential failure modes?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that a single OFT (or COFT) update consists of left‑multiplying the pretrained weight matrix of a layer by an orthogonal matrix \(R\).  
Because orthogonal matrices form a group, the product of two such matrices is again orthogonal:

\[
R_{AB}=R_{B}R_{A}\qquad\text{with }R_{A}^{\top}R_{A}=I,\;R_{B}^{\top}R_{B}=I\;\Longrightarrow\;R_{AB}^{\top}R_{AB}=I .
\]

Hence, if you first fine‑tune the model for task A obtaining \(R_A\) and then fine‑tune the *already‑adapted* model for task B obtaining \(R_B\), the combined transformation that you could apply at inference time is simply \(R_{AB}\).  Mathematically this composition preserves the **hyperspherical energy** that OFT is designed to keep invariant, because each factor preserves all pair‑wise cosine similarities among the neurons in its layer.

---

### Does the composition automatically preserve *both* downstream tasks?

No.  The orthogonal product guarantees only that the **geometric structure** of the weight matrix (pairwise angles) is unchanged relative to the *current* weights.  What matters for a downstream task is not just the geometry but also **how the geometry aligns with the data distribution required by that task**.  When you apply \(R_A\) alone, the network has been redirected so that its activations are optimal for task A; similarly \(R_B\) aligns the activations for task B.  The composition \(R_B R_A\) will in general move the weight matrix to a third orientation that satisfies neither of the two alignment constraints simultaneously.

In more concrete terms:

* The loss used while training OFT for a task is typically a reconstruction or control loss (e.g. DreamBooth’s image‑class prior, ControlNet’s encoder‑decoder loss).  This loss pushes \(R\) toward a region of the orthogonal group that yields good performance on that particular data distribution.
* The set of orthogonal matrices that achieve high performance on task A and on task B are usually **different sub‑manifolds** of \(\mathrm O(d)\).  Their intersection can be trivial (only the identity) or very small.
* Multiplying two “good” matrices therefore lands you somewhere in \(\mathrm O(d)\) but not necessarily on either sub‑manifold, so performance on both tasks can degrade.

---

### What does the paper say about this compositionality?

The authors explicitly list “compositionality” as an open problem (Section 7).  They note that *theoretically* the product remains orthogonal, but they do **not** provide empirical evidence that a chain of OFT updates retains all previously learned behaviours.  No quantitative experiment (e.g. fine‑tuning first on DreamBooth then on ControlNet and measuring both subject fidelity and control consistency) appears in the manuscript or its supplementary material.

---

### Potential failure modes

1. **Task interference (catastrophic forgetting).**  
   After applying \(R_B\), features that were crucial for preserving subject identity (task A) may be rotated away, causing loss of subject fidelity even though hyperspherical energy is still preserved.

2. **Amplification of small errors.**  
   Each OFT step is learned with a limited number of training steps and a radius constraint (COFT).  Small deviations from an ideal transformation accumulate when multiplied, potentially moving the final matrix far from either optimum.

3. **Non‑commutativity with other parameterizations.**  
   If one of the tasks uses additional adapters or LoRA layers together with OFT, those extra terms are not transformed by subsequent orthogonal multiplications.  The overall model then sees an inconsistent mixture of transformed and untransformed weights, which can hurt performance.

4. **Layer‑wise mismatch.**  
   The paper applies OFT only to selected layers (e.g., attention projection matrices).  If task A modifies layer ℓ while task B modifies layer k, their products are independent across layers, but downstream behaviour depends on interactions between layers; rotating one set may misalign representations across layers.

5. **Distribution shift in control signals.**  
   In controllable generation, task‑specific loss functions often involve an auxiliary encoder that extracts control features from images.  A new orthogonal transform changes how those features are projected into the diffusion model, potentially breaking the learned mapping between control signal and image space.

---

### Practical guidance

If you need a model to handle several distinct downstream behaviours, consider one of these strategies rather than naïvely chaining OFT matrices:

| Strategy | How it avoids interference |
|----------|----------------------------|
| **Multi‑task OFT** – train a *single* orthogonal matrix with a combined loss (e.g., sum of DreamBooth and ControlNet losses). | The optimizer directly searches for an orientation that jointly satisfies both tasks; any compromise found lies inside \(\mathrm O(d)\). |
| **Task‐specific gating** – keep separate \(R_A\) and \(R_B\) and switch them at inference time (or interpolate between them). | No multiplication; each task receives exactly its own transformation, preserving its performance. |
| **Adapter stacking** – use OFT for one primary adaptation (e.g., subject identity) and keep LoRA/T2I adapters for additional control signals. | Orthogonal part stays fixed while low‑rank adapters learn auxiliary behaviour without rotating the core weights again. |
| **Regularized composition** – after learning \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), fine‑tune a small corrective orthogonal matrix \(R_{\Delta}\) with a loss that includes *both* tasks’ objectives, starting from \(R_{AB}=R_BR_A\). | This step explicitly pulls the composition back toward regions where both tasks are satisfied. |

---

### Summary

Multiplying two OFT orthogonal matrices certainly yields another valid orthogonal matrix, preserving hyperspherical energy, but this mathematical property does **not guarantee** that both downstream tasks remain performant. The paper does not present experimental validation of such compositionality and flags it as an open research direction. In practice, task interference, error accumulation, and interactions with other trainable components are common failure modes. To obtain genuine multi‑task capability you should either train a joint orthogonal transformation or keep each task’s matrix separate and switch them at inference time rather than multiply them indiscriminately.

### Why does Cayley parametrization limit OFT scalability and how might we avoid the matrix inverse?
Question:
The authors note that Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse which hinders scalability despite block diagonal tricks. Are there alternative parametrizations of orthogonal matrices (e.g., Householder reflections, Givens rotations, exponential map) that are more scalable for high‑dimensional layers? What are their pros/cons compared to Cayley in terms of gradient flow and parameter efficiency?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Fine‑tuning* (OFT/COFT) uses the Cayley transform  

\[
\mathbf R(\mathbf Q)=\bigl(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q\bigr)^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q,
\]

to turn a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ into an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$.  
Because the inverse $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ must be evaluated at every forward and backward pass, the cost grows as $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ for a $d\times d$ layer (even with the block‑diagonal trick the cost is $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$). This is the “scalability bottleneck’’ mentioned in the conclusion.

Below we discuss three families of orthogonal‑matrix parametrisations that avoid an explicit matrix inverse, compare their computational and optimisation properties, and indicate whether they could replace the Cayley transform in OFT.

---

### 1. Householder reflections  

A Householder matrix has the form  

\[
\mathbf H(\mathbf v)=\mathbf I-2\,\frac{\mathbf v\mathbf v^{\top}}{\|\mathbf v\|^{2}},\qquad \mathbf v\in \mathbb R^{d}.
\]

Each $\mathbf H$ is orthogonal and symmetric, and any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of at most $d$ Householder reflections.  
If we restrict ourselves to a *fixed* number $k \ll d$ of reflections, the parameter count is only $k\,d$ (linear in $d$) and no matrix inverse is required—only vector outer‑products and scalar divisions, both cheap and fully differentiable.

**Pros**

* Linear‑time construction, $\mathcal O(kd)$, which scales well to large layers.  
* The Jacobian of $\mathbf H(\mathbf v)$ w.r.t. $\mathbf v$ is simple, so gradient flow is stable; the map is smooth except at $\|\mathbf v\|=0$, which can be prevented by a small epsilon.  
* The product of two Householder operators remains orthogonal, so multiple OFT tasks can still be composed.

**Cons**

* To represent an arbitrary orthogonal matrix we may need up to $d$ reflections; using fewer gives a restricted sub‑group which may limit expressivity compared with a full Cayley parametrisation.  
* Because the reflections are *symmetric*, they cannot produce pure rotations (determinant +1) without an even number of reflections; however COFT already works with determinants ±1, so this is not a serious drawback.

In practice one can choose $k = d/r$ to obtain a comparable number of parameters as the block‑diagonal Cayley version while keeping the forward cost linear.

---

### 2. Givens rotations  

A Givens rotation acts on a 2‑dimensional subspace spanned by coordinate axes $(i,j)$:  

\[
\mathbf G_{ij}(\theta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
c & -s\\
s &  c
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (c=\cos\theta,\; s=\sin\theta),
\]

embedded in the $d\times d$ identity. An orthogonal matrix can be factored into a product of $\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}$ Givens rotations (the QR decomposition).  

If we keep only a subset of rotations—e.g. one rotation per block in a block‑diagonal structure—the parameter count becomes $k = r(d/r-1)$ angles, again linear in $d$. The forward pass consists of applying sparse 2‑by‑2 rotations, which costs $\mathcal O(d)$.

**Pros**

* Exact orthogonality without any inversion; only sines and cosines are required.  
* The mapping from angles to $\mathbf R$ is smooth and its Jacobian is well behaved, leading to reliable gradient flow.  
* By arranging rotations hierarchically (e.g. butterfly patterns) one can approximate a full rotation with $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ parameters—a middle ground between Householder and full Cayley.

**Cons**

* The set of matrices reachable with a limited number of Givens rotations is again a proper subset of $\mathrm O(d)$.  
* Implementation needs careful indexing to keep the sparse pattern efficient on GPUs; otherwise naïve loops can become slower than a dense matrix multiply.

Givens rotations are widely used in numerical linear algebra precisely because they avoid inverses, making them a natural drop‑in replacement for the Cayley transform in OFT.

---

### 3. Exponential map of skew‑symmetric matrices  

Any orthogonal matrix can be expressed as  

\[
\mathbf R = \exp(\mathbf S), \qquad \mathbf S^{\top}=-\mathbf S.
\]

The exponential can be approximated with truncated series or with scaling‑and‑squaring combined with Padé approximants – both are differentiable and avoid an explicit inverse.  

If we parameterise $\mathbf S$ directly (or via a low‑rank factorisation $\mathbf S = \mathbf A - \mathbf A^{\top}$), we retain exactly the same degrees of freedom as the Cayley transform but replace the rational function by a polynomial‑type computation.

**Pros**

* Guarantees determinant +1 (the special orthogonal group), just like Cayley; no need to enforce extra constraints.  
* The series expansion has only matrix multiplications; for small $d$ the cost may be comparable to Cayley, while for large $d$ one can use *approximate* exponentials such as “truncated Krylov” methods whose complexity is linear in $d$ if $\mathbf S$ is sparse or low‑rank.

**Cons**

* Even truncated, computing $\exp(\mathbf S)$ typically requires several matrix multiplications, which for dense $\mathbf S$ still yields $\mathcal O(d^{3})$ if naïvely implemented.  
* Numerical errors may accumulate; ensuring that the gradient does not explode for large angles can be tricky unless additional regularisation (e.g. spectral norm clipping) is applied.

Because OFT already uses a *skew‑symmetric* variable $\mathbf Q$, swapping Cayley for an exponential map is straightforward from an implementation viewpoint—only the forward mapping changes.

### 4. Comparison with Cayley in terms of **gradient flow**, **parameter efficiency**, and **expressivity**

| Parametrisation | Inverse needed? | Parameter count (full matrix) | Forward cost | Gradient stability | Reachable set |
|-----------------|-----------------|------------------------------|--------------|--------------------|---------------|
| **Cayley**      | Yes ($(I-Q)^{-1}$) | $d(d-1)/2$                 | $O(d^{2})$  | Well‑behaved if $(I-Q)$ stays away from singularity – requires careful initialization / regulariser | All of $\mathrm O(d)$ (det ±1) |
| **Householder**| No               | $k d$ (linear)               | $O(k d)$     | Very stable; gradients propagate through outer products | Subset generated by ≤ k reflections |
| **Givens**      | No               | $k$ angles (linear)          | $O(k d)$     | Stable; each angle affects only two rows/columns → localized gradients | Subset generated by chosen rotation pairs |
| **Exponential**| No (polynomial approx.) | $d(d-1)/2$            | $O(m d^{2})$, $m$ = #multiplies per series term | Stable for small norm $\|\!S\!\|$, but large angles need clipping or higher order terms | All of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (det +1) |

For OFT we care about two things:

1. **Preserving hyperspherical energy** – any orthogonal map satisfies this, so all four families are admissible.  
2. **Scalability** – we need at most linear‑in‑$d$ parameters and forward cost because diffusion models contain many large linear layers (e.g., 1024×1024). Hence Householder or Givens are immediately more scalable than full Cayley or exponential unless we aggressively sparsify them.

Empirically, gradient flow through Householder/Givens tends to be smoother than through the rational function of Cayley because there is no risk of hitting a pole when $(I-Q)$ becomes ill conditioned. In practice one simply adds an L2 penalty on $\|\!Q\!\|_{2}$ (or on the vectors defining Householder/Givens) to keep the transformation close to identity during early training—a strategy already used for COFT’s $\epsilon$ constraint.

### 5. How to plug an alternative into OFT

The OFT loss does not depend on *how* $\mathbf R$ is produced; it only demands  

\[
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R\, \mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\! \mathbf x,\qquad 
\|\!\mathbf R^\top \!\! \mathbf R-\mathbf I \|\! =0 .
\]

Thus you can replace Eq. (5) in the paper by any of

```python
# Householder version
v = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(k, d))          # k reflections
R = torch.eye(d)
for i in range(k):
    vi = v[i].unsqueeze(1)
    Hi = torch.eye(d) - 2 * vi @ vi.t() / (vi.t() @ vi + eps)
    R = Hi @ R

# Givens version
theta = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(num_pairs))
R = torch.eye(d)
for (i,j), th in zip(pairs, theta):
    c,s = th.cos(), th.sin()
    G = torch.eye(d)
    G[i,i] = G[j,j] = c
    G[i,j] = -s
    G[j,i] = s
    R = G @ R
```

The rest of the OFT pipeline – computing hyperspherical energy loss or COFT’s radius constraint – stays unchanged.

### 6. Bottom line

*The matrix inverse required by the Cayley transform limits OFT’s scalability because it forces an $O(d^{2})$ operation for every large weight layer.*  
Alternative parametrisations that guarantee orthogonality without inverses exist:

* **Householder reflections** give linear parameter growth and cheap forward passes; they are numerically stable but restrict expressivity unless many reflections are used.
* **Givens rotations** also scale linearly and are especially convenient when combined with block‑diagonal structures; they provide fine‑grained control over which dimensions interact.
* **Exponential map** retains full expressivity like Cayley but replaces the rational function with polynomial approximations; it still costs $O(d^{2})$ unless sparsity or low‑rank tricks are added.

For diffusion models where each linear layer may have thousands of rows/columns, using either Householder or Givens rotations with a modest number of factors yields an orthogonal fine‑tuning method that keeps hyperspherical energy intact, enjoys smoother gradients than Cayley, and removes the costly matrix inverse altogether. This makes them attractive candidates for scaling OFT to ever larger text‑to‑image backbones while preserving all the theoretical guarantees demonstrated in the paper.
