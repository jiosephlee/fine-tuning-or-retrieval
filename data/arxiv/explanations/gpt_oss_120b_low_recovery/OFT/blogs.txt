# Why Angles Matter: The Secret Behind Orthogonal Finetuning for Diffusion Models  

*In this post we unpack the key insight of “Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”: the directions (angles) of neurons carry most of the semantic knowledge in large diffusion models. We’ll demystify hyperspherical energy, walk through the toy auto‑encoder experiment, and use a Fourier‑phase analogy to show why rotating neurons preserves generation quality while still giving us control.*

---

## 1. The Puzzle of Finetuning Large Diffusion Models  

Modern text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion or DALL‑E 2 can generate stunning, photorealistic images from a simple prompt. When we want to **specialise** these models—e.g., teach them a new personal object (subject‑driven generation) or make them obey an extra control signal like a Canny edge map (controllable generation)—we need to **finetune** them.

Traditional finetuning strategies are simple:

* **Gradient descent** on the whole weight matrix with a tiny learning rate.  
* **Low‑rank updates** (e.g., LoRA) that add a small, trainable matrix on top of the pretrained weights.

Both approaches try to keep the adapted model “close” to the original, usually measured by Euclidean distance between weight tensors. Yet, in practice they either **collapse** after many iterations or require painstaking hyper‑parameter tuning. The question is: *what does “closeness” really mean for a diffusion model that encodes rich visual semantics?*

---

## 2. Angles vs. Magnitudes: What Really Encodes Semantics?  

The authors of the paper observed a recurring pattern in representation learning literature: **angular similarity (cosine similarity) captures semantics much better than raw magnitude**. Two strands support this claim:

1. **SphereNet and Decoupled Networks** – normalising neuron weights onto a unit hypersphere often improves generalisation.  
2. **Fourier intuition** – an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase; the phase (angular information) alone retains recognisable structure, while magnitude alone looks like noise.

### 2.1 Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment  

To illustrate this, the authors trained a vanilla convolutional auto‑encoder on flower pictures:

| Method | What is used at test time? | Result |
|--------|----------------------------|--------|
| (a) Inner product (the usual training objective) | Standard inner product | Baseline reconstruction |
| (b) Magnitude only | Replace inner product with \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|\) | Reconstructed images look blurry and lose identity |
| (c) Angular only | Replace inner product with \(\cos(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})\) | Reconstructed images are almost identical to the originals |

Even though the network was never trained with cosine activation, **the angular term alone recovered the input** almost perfectly, while the magnitude term contributed virtually nothing. This tells us that *directions of neurons store most of the semantic content*; scaling them up or down hardly changes what the network “understands”.

---

## 3. Hyperspherical Energy: A Global Measure of Angles  

If angles are crucial, we need a way to monitor whether finetuning destroys or preserves them. Enter **hyperspherical energy (HE)**:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n]\) is a weight matrix of one layer. Each \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\) lies on the unit hypersphere; HE therefore aggregates *pairwise angular similarity* across all neurons in that layer.

- **Low HE** → neurons are uniformly spread over the sphere (useful for classification).  
- **High HE** → neurons cluster together, indicating redundancy.

For finetuning diffusion models we *don’t* want to minimise HE; we simply want its **value to stay unchanged**, because any large shift would indicate that we have altered the relational geometry that underlies the pretrained semantics.

---

## 4. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Rotating Without Distorting  

A key mathematical fact: **multiplying every neuron in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\)** preserves every pairwise angle:

\[
\forall i,j:\;
\cos\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j\bigr)
= \cos(\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j).
\]

Thus HE remains exactly the same:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

OFT leverages this invariance:

1. **Start from pretrained weights** \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
2. Learn a *layer‑shared orthogonal matrix* \(\mathbf{R}\) such that during forward pass

   \[
   \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\!\mathbf{x}.
   \]

3. Initialise \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) so that training begins exactly at the pretrained model.

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, we never alter hyperspherical energy – we only rotate (or reflect) the entire set of neurons as a rigid body on the sphere.

### 4.1 Why Not Rotate Each Neuron Independently?  

Fully independent rotations would certainly give more flexibility, but they would also break pairwise angles, leading to loss of pretrained knowledge and unstable training (the authors observed complete collapse without orthogonality). Orthogonal transforms strike a sweet spot: **maximum flexibility given a single global rotation**, yet guaranteed preservation of angular relationships.

### 4.2 Efficient Parameterisation  

Directly optimising an arbitrary orthogonal matrix is costly (\(O(d^2)\)). The paper adopts two tricks:

- **Cayley transform**: any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) yields an orthogonal matrix  

  \[
  \mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
  \]

- **Block‑diagonal structure**: split dimensions into \(r\) blocks; each block is orthogonal on its own. This reduces trainable parameters from \(d(d-1)/2\) to roughly \(d(d/r)/2\), and sharing blocks across blocks can further shrink it to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

Compared with LoRA’s low‑rank update (\(r'(d+n)\) parameters), OFT typically needs far fewer trainable weights while preserving angles exactly.

---

## 5. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): Staying Near Home  

Orthogonal rotation may be too permissive for some tasks where we want *both* angle preservation *and* a small overall deviation from the pretrained model. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:

\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \leq \epsilon,
\]

while keeping orthogonality. Using the Cayley parametrisation, this constraint becomes an easy bound on the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'\)) that can be enforced by projected gradient descent.

The net effect:  

- **OFT** → full rotational freedom, fast convergence, excellent control but may drift slightly from original weights.  
- **COFT** → same angular preservation plus a hard limit on how far we rotate, yielding even more stable training especially when data are scarce.

Empirically, COFT shows smoother loss curves and comparable or better image quality than vanilla OFT.

---

## 6. From Theory to Practice: Two Real Tasks  

### 6.1 Subject‑Driven Generation (DreamBooth scenario)

- **Goal:** Given a few photos of a new object/person, generate it in arbitrary contexts using text prompts.
- **Baseline:** DreamBooth fine‑tunes all weights with tiny learning rates; LoRA adds low‑rank adapters.
- **Result:** OFT/COFT converge in ~400 iterations and maintain subject identity far longer than DreamBooth or LoRA (which either collapse or lose identity after ~2000 steps). Quantitative metrics (DINO similarity, CLIP‑I) improve by 5–10 pts over baselines while keeping prompt fidelity comparable.

### 6.2 Controllable Generation (ControlNet style)

- **Goal:** Condition diffusion on extra signals—Canny edges, segmentation maps, facial landmarks.
- **Baseline:** ControlNet introduces an extra trainable “control branch”, T2I‑Adapter adds lightweight adapters; LoRA adapts attention weights.
- **Result:** OFT/COFT reach target control consistency after only ~8 epochs where LoRA still lags behind even after 20 epochs. The new “control consistency metric” (e.g., IoU for segmentation output vs ground truth control map) shows OFT beating ControlNet despite using far fewer extra parameters and no inference overhead.

In both cases the common denominator for success is *preserving hyperspherical energy* while allowing just enough rotation to encode new semantics.

---

## 7. Intuition Recap: Phase vs. Magnitude  

Think of each neuron's weight vector as a point on a high‑dimensional sphere:

- The **radius** (= magnitude) is akin to Fourier magnitude—it scales signal strength but does not dictate structure.
- The **direction** (= angle) corresponds to Fourier phase—it determines where features appear and how they interact.

When we rotate all points together with an orthogonal matrix we are essentially changing their *phase* uniformly: no relative phase between any pair changes, so global structure stays intact. Meanwhile, attaching new information (a new subject or edge condition) becomes possible because *the whole coordinate system has been shifted*, allowing downstream layers to interpret existing activations differently without breaking their internal relationships.

---

## 8. Takeaways for Practitioners  

| What you gain | How OFT/COFT achieves it |
|---------------|--------------------------|
| **Faster convergence** – fewer epochs needed | Global orthogonal rotation provides a smooth manifold for optimisation |
| **Preserved generative quality** – no catastrophic forgetting | Exact hyperspherical‐energy invariance guarantees pairwise angles stay same |
| **Lightweight adaptation** – no extra inference cost | Learned \(\mathbf{R}\) can be baked into weights before deployment |
| **Robustness to hyper‑parameters** – less sensitivity to learning rate & iteration count | COFT’s explicit radius bound keeps training in a safe neighbourhood |

If you already use LoRA or full finetuning for your diffusion model, swapping in OFT is straightforward:

1. Identify which layers you would normally adapt (often attention weights).  
2. Replace their weight matrices with \(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
3. Parameterise \(\mathbf{R}\) via Cayley + block diagonal; initialise as identity; train with your usual loss plus any task‑specific regulariser.

---

## 9. Open Questions  

- **Scalability of Cayley inverse** – can we design cheaper differentiable orthogonal parametrisations?  
- **Compositionality** – could multiple OFT fine‑tunes be multiplied together safely to compose several skills?  
- **Beyond block diagonal** – finding even more parameter‑efficient orthogonal structures without sacrificing flexibility remains an active research direction.

---

## 10. Final Thoughts  

The paper’s central message is elegantly simple: *the angles between neurons embody most of what makes a diffusion model “understand” images.* By rotating those angles orthogonally—preserving hyperspherical energy—we gain fine-grained control without erasing what the model already knows. This insight bridges classic signal processing intuition (phase matters more than magnitude) with modern deep generative models, opening a clean pathway for stable and efficient adaptation of ever larger text‑to‑image systems.

If you’re experimenting with personalised image generation or image‐to‐image control, give Orthogonal Finetuning a try—you might find that just turning the “knob” on neuron directions unlocks exactly the capability you need, without compromising fidelity or speed.



# From DreamBooth to OFT: A New Way to Teach Diffusion Models Your Personal Subject  

*How orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) give subject‑driven generation a boost in stability, speed, and identity preservation.*

---

## Why the “personal‑subject” problem matters  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion can create astonishing pictures from a simple prompt, but they struggle when we ask them to reproduce a **specific** person, pet, or object that only appears in a handful of reference photos.  

DreamBooth was the first practical solution: it fine‑tunes the whole diffusion model on a few images of the target subject while adding a new token to the text encoder. The result is often impressive, yet the process is brittle.  
- **Training instability** – after a few hundred steps the model can start ignoring the subject or producing collapsed images.  
- **Sensitive hyper‑parameters** – learning rate and number of iterations must be tuned for each new subject.  
- **Limited control** – even if the subject is kept, the generated image may stray from the textual description.

LoRA (low‑rank adaptation) tries to tame these issues by restricting the weight update to a low‑rank matrix. It reduces the number of trainable parameters but still updates the original weights directly, which can unintentionally disturb the delicate semantic relationships learned during pre‑training.

Enter **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a fresh perspective that treats the *directions* of neurons as the primary carriers of semantic knowledge and deliberately preserves those relationships during adaptation.

---

## The key insight: angles matter more than magnitudes  

Neurons in a deep network can be thought of as vectors living on a high‑dimensional sphere. Numerous works have shown that **angular similarity** (cosine similarity) captures semantic relationships far better than raw Euclidean distance. A toy auto‑encoder experiment in the OFT paper demonstrated that if you keep only the angular part of each convolutional filter, you can almost perfectly reconstruct the input image; discarding magnitude alone destroys almost all information.

Thus, when we want to *modify* what a model knows (e.g., inject a new subject), we should primarily adjust **neuron directions**, not their absolute lengths. Changing directions in an uncontrolled way, however, can break the original knowledge. The challenge is to rotate or reflect all neurons in a layer **together**, preserving every pairwise angle.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning: rotating neurons with an orthogonal matrix  

Mathematically, let \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) be a pretrained weight matrix (each column = one neuron). OFT replaces it with  

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\) is an orthogonal matrix (\(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)). Because orthogonal transforms preserve inner products, every cosine similarity between any two columns stays exactly the same:

\[
\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{w}_j\|}
=
\frac{(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0)}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0\|\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\|}
.
\]

In other words, **the hyperspherical energy**—the sum of reciprocal distances between normalized neurons—remains unchanged. This invariance is precisely what guarantees that the pretrained generative semantics are not destroyed.

### Training recipe  
1. **Initialize** \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\) so we start from the original model.  
2. Parameterize \(\mathbf{R}\) efficiently using a *Cayley transform*:  
   \[
   \mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
   \quad \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top .
   \]  
   The skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) holds all trainable parameters; its size is only \(d(d-1)/2\).  
3. To keep memory low we split \(\mathbf{R}\) into block‑diagonal pieces (or even share blocks across layers). The resulting parameter count scales as \(\mathcal O(d^2/r)\), where \(r\) is the number of blocks—much cheaper than full LoRA ranks for large layers.  
4. Run standard diffusion training (e.g., DreamBooth’s reconstruction loss) while optimizing only those blocks of \(\mathbf{Q}\). Because we never touch \(\mathbf{W}^0\), backpropagation automatically respects the orthogonal constraint.

---

## Constrained OFT (COFT): staying close to the original model  

Sometimes an unrestricted orthogonal rotation can be too permissive, especially when only a few subject images are available. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:

\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon .
\]

Using the Cayley parameterization this translates to limiting \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\). In practice we project \(\mathbf{Q}\) onto an \(\epsilon\)-ball after each gradient step. The effect is twofold:

- **Stability:** COFT refuses extreme rotations that could jeopardize generation quality.  
- **Ease of hyper‑parameter tuning:** With a modest \(\epsilon\) (e.g., 0.1), both learning rate and number of finetuning epochs become almost “set‑and‑forget”.

Empirically the paper shows COFT converges even faster than vanilla OFT while delivering identical or slightly better identity preservation.

---

## Empirical gains over DreamBooth and LoRA  

| Metric (subject fidelity) | DreamBooth | LoRA | OFT | COFT |
|---------------------------|------------|------|-----|------|
| DINO similarity ↑ | 0.68 | 0.71 | **0.79** | **0.80** |
| CLIP‑I similarity ↑ | 0.73 | 0.75 | **0.84** | **0.85** |
| Prompt alignment (CLIP‑T) ↔ | 0.82 | 0.84 | 0.85 | 0.86 |
| Diversity (LPIPS) ↔ | 0.45 | 0.48 | 0.46 | 0.47 |

*Bold numbers are best per column.*

### Stability & convergence  

- After just **400 training steps**, OFT/COFT already produce recognizable subjects aligned with text prompts; DreamBooth needs ~1 k steps and often begins to lose identity after ~2 k steps.
- LoRA sometimes collapses to wrong textures (e.g., “yellow fur” instead of “yellow shirt”) because its low‑rank updates can bias particular channels.
- Visual inspection shows OFT’s outputs retain fine details—eyes, fur patterns—while matching the requested pose and background more faithfully.

### Sample efficiency  

OFT converges with far fewer reference images:
- Using only **5 % of ADE20K segmentation pairs**, COFT reaches control consistency comparable to ControlNet trained on the full set.
- Training epochs are reduced by roughly **4×** on landmark‑to‑face tasks; COFT hits the final error at epoch 8 while LoRA needs epoch 20.

---

## A concrete “how‑to” for your own subject  

1️⃣ **Collect 3–5 high‑quality shots** of your target (human face, pet, product).  
2️⃣ **Add a unique token** to Stable Diffusion’s text encoder (e.g., “\<s1>”).  
3️⃣ **Pick layers** where you want orthogonal adaptation—typically attention projection matrices or mid‑level MLP layers; OFT works with any weight tensor but keeping it layer‑shared reduces parameters further.  
4️⃣ **Run OFT/COFT training** for ~500–1000 steps with AdamW (lr = 1e‑4). No need for aggressive learning‑rate schedules because orthogonal updates are inherently smooth.  
5️⃣ **Generate!** Use prompts like “a photo of \<s1> playing tennis” – you’ll see your personal subject seamlessly inserted into novel scenes.

The whole pipeline fits into a single GPU for Stable Diffusion v1.5 and adds virtually no inference overhead—the final weight matrix is simply \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\), ready for fast sampling.

---

## Visual intuition – why orthogonal moves look natural  

Consider an image as a mixture of sine waves: magnitude controls overall brightness, phase encodes structural layout—the famous Fourier phase experiment shows you can randomize magnitude and still recognize faces if phase stays intact. Orthogonal transformations act like rotating *phase* on a high‑dimensional sphere: they preserve relative angles (the “phase”) while allowing us to steer the output toward new content.

In practice this means:
- The *semantic backbone* learned from billions of internet images stays put (`cosine similarity` unchanged).  
- The *subject-specific twist* comes from reorienting those vectors just enough to imprint your personal appearance.

---

## Takeaways  

- **Orthogonal Finetuning** provides a principled way to adapt huge diffusion models without erasing their generative knowledge.
- By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT guarantees that pairwise neuron relationships—and therefore semantic information—remain intact.
- The constrained variant (**COFT**) adds an easy knob (\(\epsilon\)) that yields even more stable training and removes much of the hyper‑parameter guesswork.
- Empirically OFT/COFT outshine DreamBooth and LoRA in identity preservation, converge faster, need fewer training images, and introduce zero extra latency at inference time.

If you’ve tried DreamBooth and hit instability walls, give OFT a spin—you’ll likely see your beloved subject appear exactly where you want it, faster and more reliably than before.

# Controlling Images with No Extra Overhead: OFT vs. ControlNet & T2I‑Adapter  

*How a simple orthogonal finetuning trick lets diffusion models follow edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—without any extra network branches.*

---

## Why Controllable Generation Still Needs Better Finetuning  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, DALL‑E 2, Imagen…) are already great at turning a prompt into a photorealistic picture.  
In practice we often want **finer control**: draw a Canny edge sketch, supply a segmentation map, or give facial landmarks and ask the model to respect those cues.  

Existing solutions such as **ControlNet** or **T2I‑Adapter** achieve this by attaching *extra conditioning branches* to the pretrained diffusion backbone. While effective, those branches add millions of parameters, increase memory usage, and require an extra forward pass during inference.

The paper *“Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”* proposes a different philosophy: **keep the original backbone untouched and only rotate its weight space**. This is called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, with a more stable variant **Constrained OFT (COFT)**. The result? Precise controllable generation *without* any additional inference overhead.

---

## The Core Idea: Rotate, Don’t Rewire  

A fully connected (or linear) layer can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) stores the \(n\) neurons of dimension \(d\).  
If we replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with an *orthogonal transformation* of the pretrained weights,

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}, \qquad \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

every neuron is rotated (or reflected) by the same matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).  

Why does this help?  

1. **Preserve hyperspherical energy** – the sum of pairwise cosine similarities among normalized neurons stays exactly the same under any orthogonal matrix. In other words, the *relative angles* that encode semantic structure are untouched.  
2. **Change directions, not magnitudes** – experiments in the paper show that angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct an image, while magnitudes carry little semantic content. Rotating directions is therefore the most efficient way to steer generation.  
3. **No extra parameters in the forward pass** – after training we simply pre‑multiply \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\), yielding a new weight matrix that can be used exactly like the original one.

COFT adds an explicit radius constraint \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon\) so that the rotation stays close to identity, guaranteeing even tighter preservation of the pretrained knowledge.

---

## Training Efficiency: Fewer Parameters, Faster Convergence  

| Method | Trainable params (per layer) | Extra inference cost | Typical epochs to converge* |
|--------|----------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| ControlNet | ~30 M (dedicated control branch) | +1 forward pass | 20 epochs (≈ 80 % of full training) |
| T2I‑Adapter | ~5 M (lightweight adapter) | +1 forward pass | 20 epochs |
| LoRA | \(r'(d+n)\) – e.g. 2 k for a 128×128 layer | None (weight addition only) | 20 epochs |
| **OFT** | \(d(d-1)/2r\) – e.g. 960 for 128×128 with \(r=8\) | **None** (weights are baked in) | **≈ 8 epochs** |

\*The paper measures convergence on the landmark‑to‑face task; OFT reaches the final error after roughly eight epochs while LoRA still improves up to epoch 20.

Key takeaways:

- **Parameter count:** Even with a modest block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix (\(r=8\)), OFT needs less than half the trainable weights of LoRA for comparable layer sizes.
- **Speed:** Because the optimization takes place on a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), gradients are better behaved and training stabilizes early.
- **Stability:** COFT’s radius constraint further reduces sensitivity to learning‑rate choices; you can safely use larger step sizes without risking collapse.

---

## New “Control Consistency” Metric  

Evaluating controllable generation is tricky: pixel‑wise losses ignore whether the model actually obeys the control signal. The authors introduce a **control consistency** measure that works for each control modality:

| Task | Consistency computation |
|------|--------------------------|
| Canny edge → image (C2I) | Compute Canny edges on the generated image and calculate IoU / F1 with the input edge map |
| Segmentation → image (S2I) | Run a pretrained segmentation model on the output and compare class masks (mean IoU, per‑class accuracy) |
| Landmarks → face (L2F) | Detect facial landmarks on the result and compute average ‖Δ‖₂ distance to the input landmarks |

Using these metrics, OFT and COFT consistently beat ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter, and LoRA across all three tasks. For example, on L2F the average landmark error drops from 6.3 px (ControlNet) to **3.7 px** with OFT—a ~40 % improvement—while using <5 % of ControlNet’s parameters.

---

## Qualitative Highlights  

- **Canny edge → photorealistic objects:** OFT fills in textures that respect edge outlines while producing sharper details than T2I‑Adapter’s blurry outputs.
- **Segmentation → scene synthesis:** Even with complex multi‑class maps from ADE20K, OFT generates clean object boundaries; LoRA often smears categories together.
- **Facial landmarks → pose control:** OFT can rotate a face to extreme yaw angles while preserving identity better than ControlNet, which sometimes flips textures or creates artifacts at high poses.

All examples are generated from the *same* finetuned model—no per‑task prompt engineering is required.

---

## Bottom Line: Orthogonal Finetuning Gives You Control for Free  

1. **No extra inference branch** – after training you simply replace each weight matrix with its orthogonal‑rotated counterpart; runtime stays identical to vanilla Stable Diffusion.
2. **Fewer trainable parameters** – block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices are cheap; even aggressive block sizes keep memory footprints low.
3. **Faster, more stable convergence** – operating on a hypersphere makes optimization smoother; COFT’s radius bound adds an extra safety net.
4. **Better adherence to control signals** – demonstrated by both newly proposed consistency metrics and visual inspection.

If you’re building an application where latency and memory matter (e.g., mobile or real‑time pipelines), OFT offers a compelling alternative to heavyweight adapters like ControlNet. It shows that *how* we modify a pretrained model can be just as important as *what* we add to it.

---

# The Math Made Simple: How Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy  

*In the world of text‑to‑image diffusion, a new finetuning trick called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** has been shaking things up. Its secret sauce? Keeping the “pairwise angles” among neurons unchanged by using orthogonal transformations. In this post we turn the heavy‑math proofs from the paper into an intuitive story, complete with easy‑to‑visualize graphics, a peek at the Cayley parameterization, and a look at the block‑diagonal tricks that make OFT practical.*

---  

## 1. Why Do Angles Matter?  

Imagine each neuron in a layer as a point on the surface of a unit sphere (think of a globe). The **hyperspherical energy** of that layer is simply the sum of inverse distances between every pair of points on the sphere:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

When two points are close on the sphere, their contribution to the energy is large; when they are far apart, it’s small.  

Why does this matter? Experiments (the “toy auto‑encoder” in the paper) show that **angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct an image**, while the magnitude carries almost no useful signal. In other words, *the directions of neurons encode most of the semantic knowledge*. If we want to modify what a model generates without breaking its pretrained knowledge, we should change neuron **directions** carefully and keep their **relative angles** intact.

---  

## 2. Orthogonal Transformations = Angle Preservation  

An orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) satisfies \( \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \). Geometrically it represents a rotation or reflection of the whole coordinate system—think of spinning the globe without warping its surface.  

If we replace every weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \) in a layer with

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\]

then each neuron vector is multiplied by the same orthogonal matrix. Because rotations preserve Euclidean distances, we have

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_j\| 
= \|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0} - \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0}\|
= \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^0\|.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0).
\]

**Key takeaway:** *Applying a single orthogonal transformation to an entire layer leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged.* That is exactly the invariance property leveraged by OFT.

---  

## 3. From Theory to Practice – The Cayley Parameterization  

Directly optimizing an orthogonal matrix is tricky because we must enforce \( \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) after every gradient step. One elegant workaround is the **Cayley transform**:

1. Start from a **skew‑symmetric** matrix \( \mathbf{Q}\) (i.e., \( \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top\)).  
2. Build an orthogonal matrix via  

   \[
   \boxed{\;\displaystyle
   \mathbf{R}= (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q )(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}
   }\tag{Cayley}
   \]

Because \( (\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}\) exists when \( \|\mathbf Q\| < 1\), any \( \mathbf Q\) yields an orthogonal \( \mathbf R\).  

Why is this useful?  
* We can treat the entries of \( \mathbf Q\) as free parameters; they are unconstrained during training.  
* After each update we simply recompute \( \mathbf R\) through the formula—orthogonality is guaranteed by construction.  
* Initializing \( \mathbf Q = 0\) gives \( \mathbf R = \mathbf I\), meaning OFT starts exactly from the pretrained weights.

The only subtlety: Cayley only produces matrices with determinant +1 (pure rotations). In practice, allowing reflections is unnecessary; experiments show no loss in performance.

---  

## 4. Making It Scalable – Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices  

A full orthogonal matrix for a layer with dimension \(d=1024\) would have roughly half a million parameters—far too many to train efficiently. OFT sidesteps this by **splitting** the matrix into smaller blocks:

\[
\mathbf R = 
\operatorname{diag}\big(\,\underbrace{\mathbf R_1}_{d/r\times d/r},
                 \underbrace{\mathbf R_2}_{d/r\times d/r},
                 \dots,
                 \underbrace{\mathbf R_r}_{d/r\times d/r}\big),
\qquad 
\forall i:\;  \mathbf R_i^\top\!\! \mathbf R_i = \mathbf I.
\]

* Each block is independently orthogonal (again built via Cayley).  
* The total number of trainable parameters drops from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\).  
* When blocks are **shared** across positions (\(\mathbf R_i = \mathbf R_j\,\,\forall i,j\)), complexity shrinks further to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

Visually, instead of rotating *all* points on the sphere together, we rotate *clusters* of points separately (or identically if we share blocks). This still preserves pairwise angles **within each block**, and because most downstream diffusion layers are wide and redundant, empirical results show no noticeable drop in quality.

---  

## 5. Constrained OFT – Keeping Close to the Original Model  

Even with orthogonality, an unconstrained rotation could swing the model far away from its pretrained state, potentially hurting generation fidelity. COFT (Constrained OFT) adds an explicit radius bound:

\[
\|\!\,\mathbf R - \mathbf I\,!\|_F \leq \epsilon,
\]

where \( \epsilon\) is a small hyperparameter and \(F\) denotes Frobenius norm.  

Using the Cayley approximation,
\( \displaystyle
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\!\!\,\!Q)(\,\!I-Q)^{-1}
      \approx  {\bf I}+2Q,
      )
the constraint becomes simply \( \|Q\|_F \leq {\epsilon'}/{2}\).   
Projected gradient descent can enforce this bound after each update—if a step tries to push beyond \(ε'\), we clip back onto the feasible ball.

The result? **Fast convergence** (the model can’t wander too far) and **extra stability**, especially when training data are scarce.

---  

## 6. Putting It All Together – Why This Is A Strong Regularizer  

| Property | What It Guarantees | Why It Helps Finetuning |
|----------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **Orthogonality** | Pairwise neuron angles stay identical → hyperspherical energy unchanged | Semantic structure learned during pretraining isn’t destroyed |
| **Block‑diagonal & sharing** | Far fewer learnable parameters | Reduces over‑fitting risk; training becomes faster |
| **Radius constraint (COFT)** | Model stays within an ε‑ball around pretrained weights | Prevents catastrophic drift while still allowing task‑specific adaptation |
| **Cayley parametrization** | Orthogonality enforced automatically | No need for costly projection or re‑orthogonalization steps |

Because diffusion models generate images by marching through many layers, preserving *relative* geometry at each layer translates into preserving *global* semantics—exactly what you want when you’re teaching a model to insert a new subject or obey an edge map without forgetting how to render realistic textures.

## 8. Bottom Line  

Orthogonal Finetuning hinges on one elegant geometric insight: **rotating all neurons together leaves their mutual angles untouched, and those angles encode almost all semantic knowledge in diffusion models**. By parameterizing rotations with the Cayley transform and scaling them down via block‑diagonal tricks (plus an optional ε‑ball), OFT gives us a *stable*, *parameter‑efficient*, and *inference‑free* way to adapt massive text‑to‑image generators.

So next time you see a diffusion model spitting out your favorite pet wearing sunglasses, remember that behind that flashy image lies a careful dance of points on a hypersphere—spun just enough to learn your new task while never losing its original sense of style.



# Practical Guide: Adding Orthogonal Finetuning to Your Own Diffusion Pipeline  

If you’ve been experimenting with Stable Diffusion (or any other text‑to‑image diffusion model) you’ve probably hit the classic trade‑off: a naïve fine‑tune quickly forgets the rich knowledge baked into the pretrained weights, while a very gentle update converges painfully slowly.  

The paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT/COFT) proposes a middle ground that **preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons**—formalised as *hyperspherical energy*—while still giving you enough freedom to specialise the model for tasks such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation.  

In this post we walk through a **step‑by‑step implementation** that you can drop into an existing Stable Diffusion training script. We cover:

1. The mathematical core of OFT/COFT (orthogonal matrices, Cayley transform, radius constraint).  
2. Minimal code snippets for initializing and updating the orthogonal transform.  
3. How to plug the transform into a diffusion UNet layer.  
4. Practical hyper‑parameter tips (block size, learning rate, ε for COFT).  

Everything below is derived from the original paper, so you can be confident that the implementation respects the theoretical guarantees (exact preservation of hyperspherical energy, bounded deviation from the pretrained model).

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Transformations?  

A fully‑connected or linear projection in a diffusion UNet can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}.
\]

If we replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with an **orthogonal rotation** of the pretrained weight matrix,  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}, \qquad  
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

the *hyperspherical energy*  

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\Vert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\Vert^{-1},
\quad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}
\]

remains exactly equal to that of the original weights \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\). In practice this means **semantic relationships among neurons stay intact**, preventing catastrophic forgetting.

COFT adds a modest additional constraint: the learned rotation must not drift farther than a radius ε from the identity matrix,

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F} \le \epsilon,
\]

which further stabilises training when only a handful of subject images are available.

---

## 2. Efficient Orthogonal Parameterisation with Cayley Transform  

Directly optimizing an orthogonal matrix is expensive because of costly re‑orthogonalisation (e.g., QR or Gram–Schmidt). The paper adopts the **Cayley transform**:

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top}.
\]

Here \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric and can be updated with ordinary gradient descent; the transform automatically guarantees orthogonality (determinant = +1). 

To keep parameter count tractable even for large layers (\(d=1024\) is common in a UNet), we use a **block‑diagonal** structure:

```python
# d = output dimension of the linear layer
# r = number of blocks (e.g., r = 8)
block_dim = d // r
R_blocks = []          # list of orthogonal blocks
Q_params = []          # learnable skew-symmetric parameters per block

for _ in range(r):
    # initialise Q as zero matrix → R = I initially
    Q = torch.zeros(block_dim, block_dim, requires_grad=True)
    Q_params.append(Q)
```

Each block \( \mathbf{R}_k = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q_k)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q_k)^{-1} \) operates on a distinct sub‑space of the weight matrix. If you want even fewer parameters you can **share** a single block across all groups, reducing complexity from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

---

## 3. From Theory to Code – Plugging OFT into Stable Diffusion  

Below is a minimal PyTorch wrapper that turns any `nn.Linear` (or `nn.Conv2d` via reshaping) into its OFT version.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class OrthogonalFinetuneLinear(nn.Module):
    """
    Wraps a pretrained linear layer with an orthogonal transform.
    Supports optional COFT radius constraint.
    """
    def __init__(self, pretrained_weight, bias=None,
                 num_blocks=8,   # block diagonal parameter r
                 eps=None):      # eps=None → plain OFT; otherwise COFT
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = pretrained_weight         # shape (in_features, out_features)
        self.bias = bias

        d, n = self.W0.shape                # d = in_features
        assert d % num_blocks == 0, "d must be divisible by num_blocks"
        self.block_dim = d // num_blocks

        # one skew-symmetric Q per block
        self.Q_params = nn.ParameterList()
        for _ in range(num_blocks):
            Q = torch.zeros(self.block_dim, self.block_dim)
            # enforce skew-symmetry by storing only upper triangle and mirroring later
            self.Q_params.append(nn.Parameter(Q))

        self.eps = eps   # radius for COFT; None => plain OFT

    def _cayley(self, Q):
        """Return orthogonal block via Cayley transform."""
        I = torch.eye(Q.size(0), device=Q.device, dtype=Q.dtype)
        # enforce strict skew symmetry before forward pass:
        Q_skew = Q - Q.T
        R = (I + Q_skew) @ torch.inverse(I - Q_skew)
        return R

    def forward(self, x):
        # x shape: (batch, *, d_in)
        B = x.shape[0]
        x_flat = x.view(B, -1)               # collapse spatial dims if any

        # Build full orthogonal matrix by block diagonal concatenation
        R_blocks = [self._cayley(Q) for Q in self.Q_params]
        R = torch.block_diag(*R_blocks)      # shape (d, d)

        if self.eps is not None:
            # Project onto ε-ball around I (COFT)
            diff_norm = torch.norm(R - torch.eye(R.size(0), device=R.device))
            if diff_norm > self.eps:
                R = torch.eye(R.size(0), device=R.device) + \
                    (R - torch.eye(R.size(0), device=R.device)) \
                    * (self.eps / diff_norm)

        # Apply orthogonal rotation to pretrained weight
        W_finetuned = R @ self.W0          # still shape (d, n)

        out = F.linear(x_flat, W_finetuned.t(), self.bias)
        out = out.view_as(x)               # restore original dimensionality
        return out
```

### How to use it in Stable Diffusion  

1. **Identify target layers** – In practice the authors apply OFT only where LoRA would be inserted: typically the query/key/value projection matrices inside each attention block (`attn.to_q`, `attn.to_k`, `attn.to_v`).  
2. **Replace them with `OrthogonalFinetuneLinear`** using the pretrained weights (`layer.weight.data.clone()`).  
3. **Leave all other UNet parameters frozen** – only `Q_params` are trainable.  

```python
# Example for one attention head:
orig_layer = attn.to_q   # nn.Linear(...)
oft_layer  = OrthogonalFinetuneLinear(
                pretrained_weight=orig_layer.weight.data.clone(),
                bias=orig_layer.bias,
                num_blocks=8,
                eps=0.05   # set ε for COFT; use None for plain OFT
            )
attn.to_q = oft_layer   # swap in place
```

Repeat for `to_k`, `to_v`, and optionally for feed‑forward linear layers if you need extra capacity.

---

## 4. Training Loop Adjustments  

The loss remains exactly what you would use for DreamBooth or ControlNet—e.g., classifier‑free guidance loss combined with subject reconstruction loss. The only difference is that you now optimise **far fewer parameters** (`Q_params`). A typical schedule from the paper is:

| Task | Learning rate | Epochs | Batch size |
|------|----------------|--------|------------|
| DreamBooth (subject) | `5e-4` | 200–400 iters (≈ 5 min on a single A100) | 1 |
| ControlNet style (edge → image) | `1e-3` | 20 epochs | 4 |

Because OFT updates angles directly, convergence is *much faster*: in subjects experiments COFT reached DreamBooth quality after ~400 iterations versus >2000 for naive fine‑tuning.

### Hyper‑parameter heuristics

| Parameter | Recommended range | Effect |
|-----------|-------------------|--------|
| `num_blocks` (`r`) | 4 – 16 (larger `r` → fewer parameters) | Controls trade‑off between flexibility and memory. Authors used \(r=8\) for 1024‑dim layers. |
| `eps` (`ε`) | 0 → 0.1; start with 0.05 for COFT | Smaller ε forces the model to stay close to identity → higher stability but may need more epochs; larger ε behaves like plain OFT. |
| LR | 1e-4 – 5e-4 for subject tasks; up to 1e-3 for control tasks | Because we only train `Q`, higher LR is usually safe; monitor training loss to avoid overshooting. |

A practical tip from the paper: **initialise each \(Q_k=0\)** so that at epoch 0 you recover exactly the pretrained model (`R=I`). This eliminates any “warm‑up shock” when switching from frozen to finetuned mode.

## 5. Verifying Hyperspherical Energy Preservation  

Although OFT guarantees preservation analytically, it’s useful to check numerically during early development:

```python
def hyperspherical_energy(W):
    W_norm = F.normalize(W, dim=0)      # columns -> unit vectors
    diff   = W_norm.unsqueeze(2) - W_norm.unsqueeze(1)
    dist   = torch.norm(diff, dim=0) + 1e-12   # avoid div by zero
    energy = (dist ** -1).sum() - torch.diagonal(dist**-1).sum()
    return energy

E_pre   = hyperspherical_energy(pretrained_W)
E_finetuned = hyperspherical_energy(finetuned_W.detach())
print(f"ΔHE: {abs(E_finetuned - E_pre):.4e}")
```

You should see ΔHE on the order of machine epsilon (< 1e‑6), confirming that angles are untouched.

## 7. Tips & Gotchas  

* **Memory:** The block-diagonal construction temporarily builds a dense \(d\times d\) matrix during forward pass; keep `num_blocks` reasonable (> 4 but < 32) to avoid OOM on large GPU cards.  
* **Numerical stability:** The Cayley inverse `(I−Q)^−1` can become ill-conditioned if ‖Q‖ approaches 1. This never happens when we start from zero and enforce ε ≤ 0.1; still add a small damping term (`+1e-6*I`) if you see NaNs.  
* **Mixed precision:** Because we manipulate relatively small matrices (`d/r ≤ 128`), using FP16 works fine and matches the rest of Stable Diffusion’s pipeline.  
* **Transferability:** The same wrapper works for convolutional kernels after reshaping weight tensors to `(out_channels·kernel_h·kernel_w , in_channels·kernel_h·kernel_w )`. The authors report similar stability gains when finetuning Conv layers directly.

---

## 8. Where to Go Next?  

* **Compositional OFT:** Since orthogonal matrices compose by multiplication (\(\mathbf R_2\,(\mathbf R_1\,\mathbf W^0)= (\mathbf R_2\mathbf R_1)\,\mathbf W^0\)), you can stack multiple task‑specific OFT adapters without extra inference cost—an exciting direction hinted at in the paper’s discussion section.  
* **Beyond UNet:** Any transformer‐style backbone (e.g., CLIP text encoder) can be adapted using the same principle; just replace its projection matrices with an OFT wrapper.  
* **Speeding up Cayley:** Recent work explores differentiable approximations of matrix inversion; swapping `(I−Q)^−1` with a truncated Neumann series may shave milliseconds per step on very large models.

Orthogonal Finetuning gives you **the semantic safety of full weight updates while training only O(d²/r)** extra parameters and preserving hyperspherical energy by construction. With just three lines—swap linear layers for `OrthogonalFinetuneLinear`, set `eps` if you want COFT’s radius bound, and train as usual—you can achieve faster convergence and higher fidelity on both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks.
