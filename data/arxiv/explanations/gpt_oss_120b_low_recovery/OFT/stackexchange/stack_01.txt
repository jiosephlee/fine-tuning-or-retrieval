\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and how is it computed for a weight matrix?
Question:
The paper defines hyperspherical energy (HE) as \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\) where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\). I am confused about:
1. Why is the inverse of the Euclidean distance used instead of, say, cosine similarity?
2. Does this definition assume all neurons are normalized to lie on the unit hypersphere first?
3. How does this scalar quantity capture the “uniformity” of neuron directions?
A clear step‑by‑step example (e.g., a 3‑neuron layer in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper defines the *hyperspherical energy* (HE) of a weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W=\{\mathbf w_1,\dots ,\mathbf w_n\}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\qquad   
\widehat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|},
\]

as  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Below I explain why the definition looks the way it does, what the normalization means, and how the scalar (1) measures the uniformity of the neuron directions. A tiny worked‑out example in $\mathbb R^{2}$ will make the ideas concrete.

---

### 1.  Why the inverse Euclidean distance?

For two *unit* vectors $\widehat{\mathbf w}_i,\widehat{\mathbf w}_j$ the Euclidean distance satisfies  

\[
\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j\|
    = \sqrt{2-2\,\cos\theta_{ij}},
\]

where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between them.  Consequently  

* small angular separation  $\Rightarrow$ small Euclidean distance;  
* large angular separation  $\Rightarrow$ large Euclidean distance.

If we summed the distances themselves we would reward *large* separations, i.e. we would obtain a quantity that **increases** when the vectors become more uniform.  The authors want a quantity that is *small* for a uniformly spread set of directions, because they later talk about “preserving” the energy.  
Therefore they take the **inverse** of the distance: pairs that are close (small denominator) contribute a **large** term, while well‑separated pairs contribute only a tiny term.  In the limit where every pair is infinitely far apart on the sphere (which cannot happen in finite dimension but can be approximated by a very uniform configuration) every term tends to zero and the total energy approaches its minimum.

Cosine similarity could also be used, but it is bounded between $-1$ and $1$; its magnitude does not diverge when two vectors become collinear.  The inverse distance gives an *unbounded* penalty for near‑duplicate neurons, which makes HE more sensitive to collapse (two neurons pointing in almost exactly the same direction).  This property is useful when one wants to detect or prevent redundancy.

---

### 2.  Does the definition assume normalisation?

Yes – every column $\mathbf w_i$ is first turned into a unit vector $\widehat{\mathbf w}_i$.  In practice this means that before evaluating (1) we compute  

```python
w_hat = w / torch.norm(w, dim=0, keepdim=True)
```

so that all vectors lie on the surface of the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.  
If you omitted this step you would be mixing magnitude information with directional information, and the quantity would no longer reflect “uniformity on a sphere”.  The paper’s discussion of “pairwise neuron relationship on the unit hypersphere” precisely refers to this normalisation.

---

### 3.  How does a single number capture uniformity?

Think of each unit vector as a point on $S^{d-1}$.  The expression (1) is exactly a *Coulomb‑type* energy: imagine placing a unit charge at each point and letting them repel one another with a force proportional to $1/r^2$.  The potential energy of such a system is proportional to $1/r$, which is what we sum in (1).  

*If the points are bunched together*: many pairs have tiny distances, each contributes a huge value, so **HE becomes large**.  
*If the points are spread out as evenly as possible*: every pair is relatively far, each contribution is small, and **HE becomes minimal**.

Thus HE provides a scalar proxy for how evenly distributed the directions are.  It does **not** require us to look at all pairwise angles explicitly; once we have summed them we have an overall “uniformity score”.  In low dimensions you can even visualise it: points that form an equilateral triangle on a circle give lower HE than three points that cluster on one side of the circle.

---

### 4.  A tiny example in $\mathbb R^{2}$

Consider a fully‑connected layer with three neurons $\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_2,\mathbf w_3 \in \mathbb R^{2}$.

| vector | coordinates | norm | unit vector $\widehat{\mathbf w}$ |
|-------|-------------|------|-----------------------------------|
| $\mathbf w_1$ | $(1,0)$ | $1$ | $(1,0)$ |
| $\mathbf w_2$ | $(0,1)$ | $1$ | $(0,1)$ |
| $\mathbf w_3$ | $(0.9,0.1)$ | $\approx0.905$ | $(0.9945,\,0.1104)$ |

First compute all pairwise Euclidean distances between unit vectors:

* $d_{12}= \|(1,0)-(0,1)\| = \sqrt{2}\approx1.414$
* $d_{13}= \|(1,0)-(0.9945,0.1104)\| = \sqrt{(0.0055)^2+(-0.1104)^2}\approx0.1105$
* $d_{23}= \|(0,1)-(0.9945,0.1104)\| = \sqrt{(-0.9945)^2+(0.8896)^2}\approx1.332$

Now take inverses and sum over unordered pairs:

\[
\operatorname{HE}= d_{12}^{-1}+d_{13}^{-1}+d_{23}^{-1}
               = \frac{1}{1.414}+ \frac{1}{0.1105}+ \frac{1}{1.332}
               \approx 0.707 + 9.05 + 0.751
               = 10.51 .
\]

The dominant term comes from $d_{13}^{-1}$, because neuron 3 lies very close to neuron 1 after normalisation – indicating a lack of uniformity.

Now imagine we rotate neuron 3 by $120^\circ$ so that its unit vector becomes roughly $(-0.5,\sqrt{3}/2)$. The three unit vectors would then be spaced almost equally around the circle; each pairwise distance would be about $\sqrt{3}\approx1.732$, giving

\[
\operatorname{HE}_{\text{uniform}} =3\times (1/1.732)\approx 1.73,
\]

much smaller than before. The single number has captured that the second configuration is far more uniform.

---

### Putting it together

* **Normalization**: All columns are projected onto the unit sphere before any computation.
* **Inverse distance**: This choice penalises near‑duplicate directions heavily and yields an energy that is minimal for uniformly spread points.
* **Scalar uniformity measure**: By summing over all unordered pairs we obtain one scalar that grows when any subset of neurons collapses and shrinks when they are evenly dispersed.

In the context of the paper, preserving HE during fine‑tuning guarantees that an orthogonal transformation has been applied – orthogonal matrices keep all pairwise angles (hence all distances on the sphere) unchanged – so the “shape” of the set of neuron directions stays exactly as it was in the pretrained model.

I hope this clears up what hyperspherical energy is, why it is defined with an inverse Euclidean distance after normalising to unit length, and how it serves as a concise indicator of directional uniformity among neurons.
