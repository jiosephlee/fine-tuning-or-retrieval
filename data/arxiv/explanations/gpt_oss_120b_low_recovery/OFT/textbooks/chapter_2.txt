\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: # Geometric Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Neuron Angles  

In this chapter we develop the geometric intuition that underlies **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**.  The central idea is that the *directions* of the neurons in a neural network—once they are projected onto a unit hypersphere—encode most of the semantic information learned during pre‑training.  By quantifying the relationships among these directions with **hyperspherical similarity** and **hyperspherical energy**, we obtain a rigorous measure of how much of the pre‑trained knowledge is retained after finetuning.  The chapter proceeds in four parts:  

1. **Definition of hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy** – we formalize the pairwise angular relationship among neurons and introduce the energy functional that aggregates these relationships.  
2. **Interpretation of neuron directions on the unit hypersphere** – we explain why normalizing neurons to unit length is natural, how their positions on the hypersphere reflect learned features, and why preserving these positions is desirable.  
3. **Empirical evidence: angular features vs. magnitude in auto‑encoders** – a concrete experiment from the paper demonstrates that the angular component of neuron responses alone can almost perfectly reconstruct visual data, whereas the magnitude component carries little useful signal.  
4. **Mathematical invariance of hyperspherical energy under orthogonal transforms** – we prove that applying a common orthogonal matrix to all neurons in a layer leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged, which motivates the orthogonal transformation used in OFT.  

Throughout we use the notation of the original paper.  All mathematical expressions are rendered in LaTeX.

## Definition of Hyperspherical Similarity and Hyperspherical Energy  

Consider a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \{\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{w}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the *neuron* (or filter) that maps an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to the scalar output \(z_{i}= \mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\).  To study the *angular* relationships among neurons we first normalise every neuron to unit length:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},\qquad i=1,\dots ,n .
\]

The normalized vectors \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\) lie on the unit hypersphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid \|\mathbf{u}\|=1\}\).  

**Hyperspherical similarity** between two neurons is defined as the Euclidean distance between their normalized representations, or equivalently the cosine of the angle they span:

\[
\text{sim}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}) \;=\; \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|^{-1}
           \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\rangle }} .
\]

This quantity grows when the two neurons become more aligned (small angular separation) and shrinks when they are far apart on the hypersphere.

Aggregating the pairwise similarities over a layer yields the **hyperspherical energy** (HE):

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|^{-1}
}\tag{1}
\]

The sum runs over all unordered pairs \((i,j)\) with \(i\neq j\).  Intuitively, \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\) measures how *clustered* the neurons are on the hypersphere: a large value indicates many neurons pointing in similar directions, whereas a small value suggests a more uniform spread.  In the literature on deep classification \([\,\text{Liu et al., 2018, 2021}\,]\) small hyperspherical energy has been linked to better generalisation; here we *preserve* the energy of a pretrained model because it already encodes a useful semantic structure.

## Interpretation of Neuron Directions on the Unit Hypersphere  

Normalising neurons to unit length removes the scale (or magnitude) component, isolating the *direction* of each weight vector.  Several theoretical and empirical observations justify focusing on these directions:

1. **Capacity of angular features** – Prior work on *SphereNet* \([\,\text{Liu et al., 2017}\,]\) demonstrated that a network whose weights are constrained to lie on \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) retains comparable expressive power to an unconstrained network, while often enjoying improved generalisation.  This suggests that the *direction* of a weight vector is sufficient to capture the feature it extracts.

2. **Semantic meaning of angles** – In many vision‑language models, the angle between two weight vectors correlates with the semantic similarity of the corresponding features.  For example, two filters whose vectors are close on the hypersphere tend to respond to similar visual patterns, whereas orthogonal filters capture distinct aspects of the input.

3. **Preservation of pairwise relationships** – The set of pairwise angles \(\{\angle(\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j})\}\) forms a geometric fingerprint of a layer.  When these angles are unchanged, the *relative* arrangement of neurons—their collaborative coding of the input space—remains the same, even if the absolute coordinates rotate in the ambient space.

Therefore, if a finetuning procedure can modify the network while **keeping the hyperspherical energy unchanged**, it will retain the essential relational geometry that the pretrained model learned.  This is precisely the desideratum of OFT: we seek a transformation that re‑orients the neurons but does not alter the set of angles among them.

## Empirical Evidence: Angular Features vs. Magnitude in Auto‑Encoders  

To illustrate the dominance of angular information, the authors performed a toy experiment with a convolutional auto‑encoder trained on a small flower dataset.  The encoder uses standard inner‑product convolutions; that is, for a sliding window input \(\mathbf{x}\) and kernel \(\mathbf{w}\), the activation is  

\[
z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

After training, three alternatives for constructing the feature map at test time were examined:

1. **Standard inner product** (the same operation used during training).  
2. **Magnitude‑only**: replace each convolution output with \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|\), discarding the cosine angle.  
3. **Angular‑only**: compute the cosine similarity \(\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}\) and multiply by the input magnitude.

Figure 1 (referenced in the paper) shows that the *angular‑only* reconstruction recovers the original flower images almost perfectly, whereas the *magnitude‑only* reconstruction yields noise‑like outputs devoid of recognizable structure.  Importantly, the network was never trained with explicit cosine activations; the angular information emerged implicitly from the learned weights.  This experiment substantiates the claim that **the direction of neurons encodes the bulk of semantic content**, while the absolute magnitude contributes little to reconstructing visual data.

Consequently, a finetuning method that directly manipulates neuron directions—rather than scaling magnitudes—will be more effective at altering semantics (e.g., adapting a subject’s appearance) while preserving the pre‑training knowledge embedded in the angular geometry.

## Mathematical Invariance of Hyperspherical Energy Under Orthogonal Transforms  

Let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I},\qquad \det(\mathbf{R})=\pm 1 .
\]

Applying the same orthogonal transformation to every neuron in a layer yields a new weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) denotes the pretrained weights.  Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, for any pair \(i\neq j\),

\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}, \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\rangle
= \left\langle
\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|},
\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}
\right\rangle
= \left\langle
\frac{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|},
\frac{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}
\right\rangle
= \langle \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\rangle .
\]

The equality follows because orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean norms (\(\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|=\|\mathbf{w}\|\)) and inner products.  Consequently, the Euclidean distance between any two normalized neurons is unchanged:

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\|.
\]

Plugging this into the definition of hyperspherical energy (1) gives

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}') = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\|^{-1}
                     = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\|^{-1}
                     = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}).
\]

Thus **hyperspherical energy is invariant under a common orthogonal transformation of all neurons in a layer**.  This invariance is the core theoretical justification for Orthogonal Finetuning: by learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) we can rotate (or reflect) the entire set of weight vectors without altering any pairwise angle, thereby guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy—and consequently the relational geometry of the layer—remains exactly as in the pretrained model.

The finetuning objective of OFT can therefore be expressed as

\[
\min_{\mathbf{R}} \; \bigl\| \text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \bigr\|
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

whose optimum is trivially achieved when \(\mathbf{R}\) is any orthogonal matrix, with the identity \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) providing a natural initialization.  By restricting to **layer‑shared** orthogonal matrices, OFT achieves a balance: sufficient flexibility to adapt the model to a downstream task, yet enough structure to preserve the high‑fidelity semantic representation encoded by the original hyperspherical energy.

### Summary  

In this chapter we have:

* Formalised **hyperspherical similarity** and **hyperspherical energy** as geometric descriptors of neuron directions on the unit hypersphere.  
* Interpreted why the *directions* of neurons, rather than their magnitudes, carry the semantic weight of a pretrained diffusion model.  
* Presented an empirical auto‑encoder study that demonstrates the near‑perfect reconstruction capability of angular features alone.  
* Proven that a common orthogonal transformation leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged, providing the theoretical foundation for **Orthogonal Finetuning**.

These geometric foundations motivate the design of OFT and its constrained variant COFT, which leverage the invariance of hyperspherical energy to finetune large text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving their pretrained generative abilities.
