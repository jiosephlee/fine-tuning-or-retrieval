\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: **Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT): Theory and Formulation**

In this chapter we develop the mathematical foundations of Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT), the finetuning paradigm introduced for large text‑to‑image diffusion models.  We begin by formalising the principle that drives OFT, then show how minimising a hyperspherical‑energy discrepancy inevitably leads to an orthogonal transformation of the weight matrix in each layer.  Next we describe how this transformation is inserted into the forward computation, and finally we contrast OFT with the more common strategies of naïve gradient‑based finetuning and the low‑rank LoRA approach.

---

### Formal statement of the OFT principle

Let \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) denote the weight matrix of a pretrained layer (fully‑connected, convolutional, or attention‑projection).  Its columns \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) are the \(i\)-th neurons of the layer.  During finetuning we obtain a new weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) with columns \(\mathbf{w}_{i}\).  To preserve the semantic structure that the pretrained model has learned, OFT requires that the **hyperspherical energy** of the finetuned layer remain identical to that of the pretrained layer:

\[
\boxed{\ \bigl\|\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\bigr\|=0\ }.
\tag{1}
\]

The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix is defined as the sum of inverse Euclidean distances between every pair of **unit‑normed** neurons:

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;:=\;
\sum_{i\neq j}\Bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\Bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (1) therefore states that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons—captured entirely by the set \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\}\)—must be unchanged after finetuning.

---

### Derivation: minimizing the hyperspherical‑energy difference ⇒ orthogonal matrix

Consider the optimisation problem

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\;\bigl\|\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\bigr\|.
\tag{3}
\]

Because \(\operatorname{HE}(\cdot)\) depends only on the directions of the columns, any transformation that preserves all pairwise angles will leave the energy unchanged.  A **global orthogonal transformation** applied to every neuron in the same layer satisfies precisely this condition.  Formally, let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I}.
\tag{4}
\]

Define the finetuned weight matrix as

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\tag{5}
\]

For any pair \((i,j)\),

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
      {\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
      {\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \mathbf{R}\,\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i},
\]
where the second equality uses the fact that orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norm.  Consequently

\[
\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|
= \bigl\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}
      -\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\bigr\|
= \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}
      -\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\bigr\|,
\]
so each term in (2) is invariant under the map (5).  Hence

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}),
\]
and the objective (3) attains its minimum value zero.  Importantly, the converse also holds: if two sets of unit‑norm vectors have identical pairwise distances, there exists an orthogonal matrix relating them (up to a possible reflection).  Therefore, the **necessary and sufficient** condition for preserving hyperspherical energy is that the finetuned weight matrix be obtained from the pretrained one by a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation.

Thus the OFT principle reduces to learning, for each layer, an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix on the left.

---

### Forward‑pass modification with a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix

Let a layer receive an input activation vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) and produce an output \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) through a linear map.  In the pretrained model the computation is

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\tag{6}
\]

After applying OFT, we replace \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{\ \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}
      = (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\ }.
\tag{7}
\]

Equation (7) shows that finetuning is achieved by **rotating the column space of the weight matrix** while leaving the input activations unchanged (or equivalently, by rotating the input features before applying the original weights).  The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is shared across all neurons of the layer, which dramatically reduces the number of free parameters relative to learning an arbitrary full‑rank update.

Training proceeds by gradient descent on a task‑specific loss (e.g., reconstruction loss for DreamBooth, or a control‑consistency loss for ControlNet) while enforcing the orthogonality constraint (4).  In practice the constraint is imposed using a differentiable parameterisation such as the Cayley transform, which guarantees \(\mathbf{R}\) stays on the Stiefel manifold throughout optimisation.

---

### Comparison with conventional fine‑tuning and LoRA

| Aspect | Conventional fine‑tuning | LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) | Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT) |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Update rule** | Direct additive update \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \Delta\mathbf{W}\) with small learning rate. | Additive low‑rank update \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{W}^{0}+ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\), \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top})=r'\). | Multiplicative orthogonal update \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\). |
| **Parameter budget** | All entries of \(\mathbf{W}\) are trainable (huge). | Only \(r'(d+n)\) parameters (low‑rank). | For a full orthogonal matrix \(d(d-1)/2\) parameters; can be reduced to \(\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}\) using a block‑diagonal structure (or \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) with shared blocks). |
| **Structural preservation** | No explicit guarantee; large Euclidean drift may destroy pairwise neuron geometry. | Low‑rank constraint limits drift but does not control angular relationships among neurons. | By construction \(\operatorname{HE}\) is exactly preserved; pairwise angles are invariant, which empirically safeguards the pretrained semantic capacity. |
| **Optimization landscape** | Highly non‑convex; can suffer from instability or model collapse, especially with limited data. | More stable than naïve finetuning but still sensitive to rank and learning‑rate choices. | Optimisation occurs on the orthogonal manifold (a smooth Riemannian space), leading to faster, more predictable convergence. |
| **Inference cost** | Same as original model (no extra modules). | Same as original model after merging the low‑rank adapters. | Identical to the original model once \(\mathbf{R}\) is multiplied into \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\); no runtime overhead. |

The key distinction lies in the **geometric invariant** that OFT enforces.  Conventional finetuning implicitly assumes that a small Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\) suffices to maintain the pretrained knowledge.  LoRA makes this assumption more explicit by limiting the rank of the deviation.  OFT, instead, recognises that the **semantic information is encoded primarily in the angular configuration of neurons**; preserving that configuration via an orthogonal transform yields a principled guarantee that the pretrained hyperspherical energy—and thus the relational structure of the representation—is untouched.

In summary, OFT formalises the intuition that “rotate, don’t stretch” the neuron space when adapting a large diffusion model to a downstream control task.  The derivation above shows that the hyperspherical‑energy preservation requirement collapses to learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix, which can be efficiently parameterised and trained.  This foundation explains the empirical observations reported in the original work: faster convergence, higher stability, and superior downstream generation quality compared with both naïve finetuning and LoRA.
