\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion and Fine‑Tuning Challenges  

## Overview of Large Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have become the leading paradigm for generating photorealistic images conditioned on natural‑language prompts.  The core idea is to train a denoising network \( \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}) \) that predicts the noise component of a latent image \( \mathbf{x}_t \) at diffusion step \(t\) given a conditioning signal \( \mathbf{c} \) derived from a text encoder.  Training proceeds by minimizing a simple mean‑squared error between the predicted and true noise, which implicitly learns the score function of the data distribution.  

Three families of models dominate the recent literature:

* **Imagen** \cite{saharia2022photoreactive} and **GLIDE** \cite{nichol2022glide} operate directly in pixel space.  Imagen adopts a frozen large language model (e.g. T5) for the text encoder, while GLIDE jointly trains a diffusion prior and a text encoder on paired image‑text data.  

* **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} and **DALL‑E 2** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} perform diffusion in a compressed latent space.  Stable Diffusion learns a VQ‑GAN codebook that maps images to a lower‑dimensional latent \( \mathbf{z} \) and trains the diffusion model to generate \( \mathbf{z} \) rather than raw pixels.  DALL‑E 2 exploits a CLIP‑based joint visual‑language embedding space, allowing the diffusion network to be conditioned on the same embedding used for image‑text similarity.  

Despite architectural differences, all of these systems share two crucial ingredients: a **large pretrained diffusion backbone** that has absorbed massive visual‑semantic knowledge, and a **text encoder** that projects a prompt into a conditioning vector.  Once pretrained, the diffusion backbone can produce high‑fidelity images that respect the semantics expressed by the prompt.  

## Downstream Tasks: Subject‑Driven Generation and Controllable Generation  

The pretrained models excel at generating images that roughly match the supplied prompt, yet many practical applications demand finer‑grained control.  Two representative downstream tasks illustrate this need.  

### Subject‑Driven Generation  

In the subject‑driven setting (often called **personalization**), the user supplies a handful of images of a particular object or person—e.g. five photos of a specific cat.  The goal is to generate novel images that retain the visual identity of that subject while placing it in new contexts dictated by a textual description.  Formally, given a small image set \( \{\mathbf{I}_k\}_{k=1}^{K} \) and a prompt \( \mathbf{p} \), we wish to obtain a conditional distribution  

\[
p_{\theta^\star}(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{p},\text{subject}) \approx p_{\text{pretrained}}(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{p}) 
\]

where the distribution is altered just enough to inject the subject’s appearance.  Methods such as DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} achieve this by fine‑tuning the entire diffusion model on the few subject images together with a reconstruction loss.  

### Controllable Generation  

Controllable generation expands the conditioning space beyond pure text.  An external **control signal**—for instance a Canny edge map, a semantic segmentation mask, or facial landmarks—is supplied alongside the text prompt.  The diffusion process must now obey two constraints:

\[
\mathbf{x} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{x}} \, p_{\theta^\star}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{s}),
\]

where \( \mathbf{s} \) denotes the control modality.  Pioneering systems such as ControlNet \cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter \cite{mou2023t2i} augment the pretrained backbone with additional trainable branches that ingest \( \mathbf{s} \) and steer the generation.  

Both tasks share a common challenge: **adapting the pretrained diffusion model without erasing the semantic knowledge it has already acquired**.  The model must learn a new mapping (subject identity or control alignment) while preserving the ability to generate diverse, high‑quality images for arbitrary prompts.  

## Desiderata for Fine‑Tuning: Efficiency vs. Preservation of Pretrained Knowledge  

A fine‑tuning method for diffusion models should satisfy two sometimes competing requirements.  

1. **Training Efficiency** – The number of trainable parameters should be modest, and the number of optimisation steps should be small.  Efficient fine‑tuning reduces GPU memory consumption, shortens wall‑clock time, and makes it feasible to specialise a large model for many subjects or control signals.  

2. **Generalizability Preservation** – After adaptation, the model must still retain the broad generative capabilities of the original pretrained backbone.  In practice this means that (i) generated images should remain photorealistic and diverse, and (ii) the model should not catastrophically forget how to follow arbitrary textual prompts.  

Achieving both simultaneously is non‑trivial.  Naïve strategies either over‑parameterise the adaptation (e.g. fine‑tuning all weights) and consequently degrade the original knowledge, or under‑parameterise (e.g. freezing most weights) and fail to capture the new task.  

## Why Simple Euclidean‑Distance Based Fine‑Tuning Is Insufficient  

A common intuition in transfer learning is that **small Euclidean changes** to the weight tensor \( \mathbf{M} \) (the collection of all parameters) should preserve the original functionality:

\[
\| \mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{0} \|_{2} \;\text{small} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{behaviour close to pretrained}.
\]

Here \( \mathbf{M}^{0} \) denotes the pretrained weights.  Existing fine‑tuning approaches, such as DreamBooth’s direct gradient descent with a tiny learning rate, implicitly rely on this assumption, as does LoRA’s low‑rank additive update.  However, Euclidean proximity alone does **not** capture the *relational* geometry among neurons, which is essential for preserving the semantics encoded in the network.

### The Role of Pairwise Neuronal Relationships  

Consider a fully‑connected layer \( \mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \).  Each column \( \mathbf{w}_i \) can be interpreted as a neuron direction in a \(d\)‑dimensional feature space.  The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) of this layer is defined as  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j}\big\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\big\rVert^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i} \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]

which aggregates the inverse distances between all **normalized** neurons.  HE measures how uniformly the neurons are spread on the unit hypersphere; low energy corresponds to a highly redundant configuration, while high energy indicates that neurons occupy diverse directions.  

Empirical studies in the paper show that **hyperspherical similarity (e.g. cosine similarity) correlates strongly with semantic information**.  A toy auto‑encoder experiment demonstrates that reconstructing an image using only the angular component of the convolution kernels retains almost all visual detail, whereas the magnitude component alone is essentially useless.  Consequently, preserving the *pairwise angles* among neurons is a more faithful proxy for retaining pretrained semantics than preserving raw Euclidean distances.

### Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy  

A crucial mathematical property is that **any orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) (i.e. \( \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \)) leaves all pairwise angular relationships unchanged**:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j \rangle 
= \langle \mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_j \rangle 
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

Thus, if a fine‑tuning step is restricted to applying a *layer‑shared* orthogonal transformation, the hyperspherical energy remains exactly the same as in the pretrained model.  This guarantees that the relational geometry of the neurons is untouched, even though the absolute weight values may change substantially.  By contrast, a naïve Euclidean regularizer (e.g. penalising \( \| \mathbf{W} - \mathbf{W}^{0}\| \)) does not enforce preservation of these angles and can easily collapse the geometric structure, leading to semantic drift.

In summary, Euclidean‑distance‑only regularisation is **insufficient** because it ignores the *directional* structure that carries most of the model’s semantic knowledge.  The paper’s proposed Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT) therefore shifts focus from preserving raw weight magnitude to preserving the **pairwise hyperspherical relationships**, which the authors empirically demonstrate to be essential for both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks.
