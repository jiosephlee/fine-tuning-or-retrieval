\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 5: # Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (COFT)

In this chapter we elaborate on **Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (COFT)**, the extension of Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT) that explicitly limits how far the finetuned model may stray from its pretrained counterpart.  COFT introduces an ε‑ball around the identity matrix, thereby improving training stability and guaranteeing that the finetuned parameters remain close to the original ones while still preserving the hyperspherical energy of each layer.  We develop the mathematical formulation of the ε‑constraint, show how it can be enforced efficiently through projected gradient descent on a skew‑symmetric matrix, and discuss how the magnitude of ε governs the trade‑off between flexibility (the ability to adapt to a downstream task) and preservation (the ability to retain the generative knowledge of the pretrained diffusion model).  All derivations follow directly from the definitions and arguments presented in the original paper.

---

## 1. Motivation for an ε‑Ball Around the Identity

Standard OFT updates the weight matrix of a layer \(\mathbf{W}\) by left‑multiplying the pretrained matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\):
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]
Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise cosine similarities among the column vectors (i.e., the neurons), the **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\big\|^{-1},\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}
\]
remains exactly the same as that of the pretrained model.  This guarantees that the relational geometry of the neurons does not collapse during finetuning.

However, orthogonality alone does **not** bound how far the transformed weight matrix can deviate from the original one.  An orthogonal matrix may be any rotation or reflection, including those that turn the canonical basis into a completely different orientation.  In practice such large rotations can destabilize training, especially when the downstream task supplies only a few supervision examples (e.g., a handful of subject images for DreamBooth).  Empirically the authors observed that naïve OFT without any additional regularization sometimes fails to generate realistic images (see Figure 2 of the paper).

A natural remedy is to **restrict the orthogonal transformation to lie within a small neighbourhood of the identity matrix**.  Geometrically this amounts to confining \(\mathbf{R}\) to an ε‑ball on the orthogonal group:
\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \varepsilon,
\]
where the matrix norm may be any operator norm (the Frobenius norm is used in the experiments).  The parameter \(\varepsilon\) directly controls the *allowed angular deviation* of every neuron from its pretrained direction.  When \(\varepsilon=0\) we recover the exact pretrained model; when \(\varepsilon\) grows large the constraint becomes inactive and COFT reduces to ordinary OFT.

Thus, the **motivation** behind introducing an ε‑ball is twofold:

1. **Stability** – By limiting the magnitude of the orthogonal update, the optimizer cannot take steps that would drastically rotate the neuron space, reducing the risk of divergence or mode collapse.
2. **Preservation of pretrained knowledge** – A small ε ensures that the Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\) remains low, complementing the hyperspherical‑energy preservation already guaranteed by orthogonality.

---

## 2. Deriving the ε‑Constraint via the Neumann Series Approximation

The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is parametrized in the paper by the **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}) (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a skew‑symmetric matrix.  This parameterization guarantees \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal (and with determinant +1, i.e., a rotation).  Directly imposing \(\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\le\varepsilon\) on \(\mathbf{R}\) would require projecting onto a non‑trivial manifold after each gradient step.  To obtain a tractable constraint we linearize the Cayley transform using the **Neumann series**.

Recall the matrix identity
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{Q}^{k},
\]
which converges when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) is smaller than 1 in the chosen operator norm.  Truncating after the first‑order term yields the approximation
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}\approx \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}.
\]
Substituting into the Cayley formula gives
\[
\mathbf{R}
\approx (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})
 = \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^{2}).
\]
Dropping the higher‑order term \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})\) (justified when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) is small) leads to the **first‑order relation**
\[
\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \;\approx\; 2\mathbf{Q}.
\]
Consequently the ε‑constraint on \(\mathbf{R}\) can be rewritten as an equivalent bound on \(\mathbf{Q}\):
\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
\|2\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon' \quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon'=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]
Thus, **instead of projecting \(\mathbf{R}\) directly**, we can enforce a simple norm ball on the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\).  Since \(\mathbf{Q}\) is the *only* learnable parameter in the Cayley transform, this constraint is straightforward to maintain during gradient‑based optimization.

The approximated relation also clarifies why **identity initialization** is natural: setting \(\mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{0}\) yields \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) exactly, so the finetuning starts from the pretrained weights.

## 3. Projected Gradient Enforcement on the Skew‑Symmetric Matrix

During training we maintain the following variables for each layer that we wish to finetune:

* the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) (fixed);
* a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (learnable);
* an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) implicitly defined by the Cayley transform.

The forward pass of the layer under COFT is
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\quad
\mathbf{Q}= -\mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\quad
\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'.
\]

The **training loop** proceeds as follows:

1. **Gradient step** – Compute the gradient of the task loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{Q}\) (the chain rule naturally propagates through the Cayley transform).  Perform an unconstrained update:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}} = \mathbf{Q} - \eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathcal{L},
   \]
   where \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

2. **Skew‑symmetrization** – Numerical errors may break exact skew‑symmetry.  Enforce it by averaging with its negative transpose:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}} - (\mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}})^{\top}\bigr).
   \]

3. **Projection onto the ε‑ball** – If the norm of the updated matrix exceeds the radius, scale it back:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q} \leftarrow
   \begin{cases}
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}, & \text{if } \|\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}\|\le\varepsilon',\\[4pt]
   \displaystyle \varepsilon'\,\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}}{\|\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}\|}, & \text{otherwise}.
   \end{cases}
   \]
   The scaling operation preserves skew‑symmetry because the scaling factor is a scalar.

4. **Recompute \(\mathbf{R}\)** – Using the projected \(\mathbf{Q}\), evaluate the Cayley transform (now with a guaranteed small \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) thus the inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) is well‑conditioned).

The above procedure is a **projected gradient descent (PGD)** step on the feasible set
\[
\mathcal{C}= \bigl\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\;\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'\bigr\},
\]
which is convex (intersection of a linear subspace and a Euclidean ball).  Consequently the projection is unique and cheap to compute.

*Implementation note*: In practice the authors employ a block‑diagonal parameterization of \(\mathbf{Q}\) (Section 3.1 of the paper).  Each block is treated independently, allowing the same PGD steps to be applied block‑wise, and further reducing the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) where \(r\) is the number of blocks.

## 4. Effect of ε on Flexibility vs. Preservation

The hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\) governs the **size of the admissible orthogonal transformation**.  Intuitively:

* **Small ε** → \(\mathbf{R}\) stays close to \(\mathbf{I}\).  The finetuned model changes neuron directions only slightly, thus preserving the pretrained generative distribution almost unchanged.  Training is highly stable, but the model may lack sufficient capacity to adapt to a demanding downstream task (e.g., learning a highly distinctive new subject).

* **Large ε** → \(\mathbf{R}\) may approach any rotation matrix, effectively recovering the full expressive power of OFT.  The model can more aggressively align its internal geometry with the new task, but the risk of destabilizing the diffusion dynamics increases.

### 4.1 Illustrative Plot (Conceptual)

Consider a 2‑dimensional toy layer with two unit‑norm neurons \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{0}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{2}^{0}\).  The set of admissible orthogonal matrices corresponds to rotations by an angle \(\theta\).  The ε‑constraint translates into a bound on \(|\theta|\):
\[
\|\mathbf{R}(\theta)-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}= 2\sqrt{2}\,\big|\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\big|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
|\theta|\le 2\arcsin\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr).
\]
A plot of **maximum allowable rotation** versus \(\varepsilon\) is a monotonically increasing curve that saturates at \(\pi\) when \(\varepsilon = 2\sqrt{2}\) (the diameter of the orthogonal group in Frobenius norm).  In higher dimensions the relationship is analogous: ε controls the *geodesic distance* on the orthogonal manifold.

### 4.2 Empirical Observations from the Paper

Figure 5 of the original work (reproduced as “ε‑effect on COFT”) shows three regimes:

| ε value | Observed Behaviour |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Very small (≈0.01)** | The finetuned model behaves almost exactly like the pretrained diffusion model; downstream task performance is modest but training never diverges. |
| **Moderate (≈0.1‑0.2)** | The model quickly attains high fidelity on the new task while still preserving image quality; convergence is smooth and the loss curve is monotonic. |
| **Large (≈0.5‑1.0)** | Training dynamics resemble unrestricted OFT: rapid adaptation but occasional spikes in loss, and a slight increase in artifacts when the rotation exceeds what the diffusion scheduler can accommodate. |

Thus, **ε acts as a knob** for practitioners: choose a moderate value to enjoy the best trade‑off—fast convergence with strong controllability yet without sacrificing the semantic richness of the pretrained model.

## 5. Summary

Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning enriches the original OFT framework by **explicitly bounding the orthogonal update** with an ε‑ball around the identity.  The key steps are:

1. **Motivation** – guaranteeing training stability and limiting Euclidean drift from the pretrained weights.
2. **Derivation** – using a first‑order Neumann series approximation of the Cayley transform to convert the ε‑constraint on \(\mathbf{R}\) into a simple norm bound on the skew‑symmetric parameter \(\mathbf{Q}\).
3. **Optimization** – applying projected gradient descent on \(\mathbf{Q}\), with an inexpensive projection that simultaneously enforces skew‑symmetry and the radius constraint.
4. **Interpretation of ε** – understanding how the radius regulates the balance between flexibility (ability to fit new data) and preservation (retaining pretrained generative knowledge), both analytically (via rotation angle bounds) and empirically (through convergence curves and qualitative results).

By preserving hyperspherical energy **and** restricting the magnitude of the orthogonal transformation, COFT achieves **more stable and sample‑efficient finetuning** of large text‑to‑image diffusion models for downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation and controllable generation, all without any extra computational burden at inference time.
