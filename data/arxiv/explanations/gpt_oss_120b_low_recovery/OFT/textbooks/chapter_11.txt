\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 11: # Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions  

In this chapter we critically examine the constraints that accompany Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT), and we outline promising avenues for further research. The discussion is anchored in the observations and open questions raised by the original work, and each subsection expands on a specific limitation, drawing out its implications for the stability, scalability, and generality of OFT‑based finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.

---

## Scalability of Matrix Inverse in the Cayley Transform  

A central component of OFT is the enforcement of orthogonality via the Cayley parameterization  
\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\)). This construction guarantees \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) while allowing the orthogonal matrix to be expressed in terms of unconstrained parameters (the entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\)). However, the matrix inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) must be computed at every optimization step, and its cost grows as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3})\) for a dense \(d\times d\) matrix.  

When the dimensionality \(d\) of a weight matrix in a diffusion model reaches several thousand (as is typical for attention projections in transformer blocks), the cubic cost can become a bottleneck, especially on commodity GPUs where memory bandwidth limits the size of intermediate matrices. Moreover, the inverse operation is numerically sensitive: if eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{Q}\) approach \(1\), the denominator matrix becomes ill‑conditioned, potentially destabilizing gradient propagation.  

A practical remedy employed in the original work is block‑diagonal factorization of \(\mathbf{R}\), thereby reducing the effective dimension of each sub‑matrix and lowering the computational burden to \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr)\) for \(r\) blocks. While this improves scalability, it introduces other concerns (discussed later). Future research may explore alternative orthogonal parameterizations that avoid explicit matrix inversion, such as:

* **Householder reflections** – representing \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of a small number of Householder matrices, each of which is cheap to apply and differentiate;
* **Exponential map** – parameterizing \(\mathbf{R}= \exp(\mathbf{S})\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{S}\). Approximating the matrix exponential with truncated series or scaling‑and‑squaring can be more efficient than a full inverse;
* **Iterative orthogonalization** – using gradient‑based updates that directly enforce \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) via a penalty term, possibly combined with a few corrective projection steps per iteration.

Developing such alternatives that retain the provable preservation of hyperspherical energy while being computationally lightweight would significantly broaden the applicability of OFT to larger models and longer training pipelines.

## Compositionality of Multiple Orthogonal Transforms  

One of the attractive theoretical properties of orthogonal matrices is closure under multiplication: if \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{2}\) are orthogonal, then \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2}\) is also orthogonal. This suggests a potential for **compositional finetuning**, where several downstream tasks—each solved by learning an orthogonal transformation—could be combined simply by multiplying their learned \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices.  

In practice, however, the composition raises several questions that the original paper identifies but does not resolve:

1. **Inter‑task interference** – Each orthogonal transform reshapes the coordinate system of the pretrained weight matrix. When multiple transforms are multiplied, the resulting rotation may no longer align with any single downstream objective, possibly degrading performance on all tasks.  
2. **Order dependence** – Matrix multiplication is not commutative, so the order in which task‑specific transforms are applied matters. Determining a principled ordering, or learning a task‑specific routing mechanism, remains open.  
3. **Capacity limits** – While an orthogonal matrix can represent any rotation or reflection, the space of useful transformations for a given set of tasks may be sparsely populated. Understanding whether the union of tasks can be captured by a low‑dimensional subspace of the orthogonal group could inform more efficient compositional schemes.

A concrete research direction is **task‑aware composition**, where a meta‑learner predicts a weighted combination of task‑specific \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices conditioned on a task identifier or on the current input. Alternatively, one could enforce a **shared subspace constraint**, requiring all task matrices to share a common factor (e.g., \(\mathbf{R}_{k}= \mathbf{R}_{\text{shared}}\mathbf{R}_{k}^{\text{private}}\)). This would preserve orthogonality while limiting the degree of interference among tasks.

## Bias Introduced by Block‑Diagonal Parameterization  

The block‑diagonal structure of \(\mathbf{R}\) reduces the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\), where \(r\) denotes the number of diagonal blocks. Though efficient, this simplification imposes an **inductive bias**: neurons belonging to different blocks are never mixed by the orthogonal transformation. Consequently, inter‑block angular relationships are fixed, and only intra‑block pairwise angles can be altered.  

Empirically, the original experiments demonstrate that a modest number of blocks (e.g., \(r=8\) for a \(128\times128\) weight matrix) yields satisfactory results. Nonetheless, the bias can manifest as:

* **Limited expressivity** – Certain downstream tasks may require coordinated rotations across all neurons, which a block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) cannot provide.  
* **Uneven adaptation** – If the distribution of semantic information is not aligned with the predefined block boundaries (e.g., some semantic features are spread across multiple blocks), the finetuning may over‑emphasize some features while neglecting others.  

Addressing this bias motivates **dynamic or learned block structures**. Instead of fixing the block partition a priori, one could introduce a lightweight gating network that assigns each neuron to a block during training, allowing the block delineation to adapt to the data. Alternatively, a **low‑rank plus block‑diagonal hybrid** could capture both global rotations (via a low‑rank component) and fine‑grained per‑block adjustments. Research into such adaptive architectures would clarify the trade‑off between parameter efficiency and representational flexibility.

## Potential for Dynamic/Learned Block Structures  

Building on the previous point, a promising avenue is to let the model **learn the decomposition** of \(\mathbf{R}\) into blocks rather than hand‑designing it. One concrete scheme is:

1. Initialize a set of **anchor orthogonal matrices** \(\{\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{K}\}\) with full size \(d\times d\).  
2. For each neuron \(i\), learn a soft assignment vector \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\in\Delta^{K-1}\) (the probability simplex) that weights the contribution of each anchor matrix.  
3. Construct the effective transformation for neuron \(i\) as \(\mathbf{R}_{i}= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{i,k}\mathbf{R}_{k}\).  

Because each \(\mathbf{R}_{k}\) remains orthogonal, convex combinations of orthogonal matrices are not orthogonal in general. However, one can enforce orthogonality after the weighted sum by applying a **projection onto the orthogonal group** (e.g., via singular value decomposition) at each update step. This yields a **soft, data‑driven block structure** that can capture complex rotations while preserving the overall orthogonal constraint.

Such a framework would also enable **task‑conditional block selection**: for a new control signal, the model could adapt the assignment vectors \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) on the fly, effectively reconfiguring the orthogonal transform without retraining the full set of anchor matrices.

## Extending OFT to Other Modalities and Architectures  

While the current study focuses on text‑to‑image diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) and primarily on fully‑connected or attention projection layers, the underlying principle—preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal transformations—is modality‑agnostic. Extending OFT could proceed along several lines:

### 1. Video Diffusion Models  
Video generation models often augment image‑space diffusion with temporal conditioning (e.g., 3D convolutions or cross‑frame attention). OFT can be applied to the temporal attention matrices, ensuring that the relational geometry of latent tokens across time remains intact. An additional challenge is the **larger hidden dimension** due to stacked frame embeddings, which intensifies the matrix‑inverse scalability issue discussed earlier; thus, more efficient orthogonal parameterizations become crucial.

### 2. Audio Diffusion  
Audio synthesis models such as diffusion‑based waveform generators or spectrogram‑based pipelines contain 1‑D convolutional stacks. Here the block‑diagonal orthogonal transform can be interpreted as **channel‑wise rotations** of convolution kernels, potentially preserving phase‑like information that is vital for timbre. Evaluating hyperspherical energy on 1‑D kernels may require adapting the definition of pairwise similarity to account for stride and receptive field differences.

### 3. Vision Transformers (ViT) and ConvNeXt  
Both ViT and ConvNeXt rely heavily on linear projections (e.g., query/key/value matrices, feed‑forward layers). Applying OFT to these projections is straightforward: each projection matrix can be left‑multiplied by a learned orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). However, the **layer‑wise sharing** of \(\mathbf{R}\) must be reconsidered. In ViT, each attention head could have its own orthogonal transform, or a shared transform could be imposed across heads to reduce parameters. For ConvNeXt, the block‑diagonal structure aligns naturally with **group convolutions**, suggesting a synergy between OFT and existing architectural bottlenecks.

### 4. Multi‑Modal Fusion Layers  
Models that jointly process text, image, and other modalities often employ cross‑modal attention or joint embedding spaces. Orthogonal finetuning of the **cross‑modal projection matrices** could preserve the delicate balance between modalities, potentially improving downstream tasks such as image captioning or video‑guided generation.

In each extension, the **hyperspherical energy** must be evaluated in the appropriate latent space (e.g., token embeddings for ViT, spectrogram frames for audio). Moreover, the impact of preserving pairwise angular relationships on the semantic fidelity of generated outputs should be empirically validated, ideally through task‑specific metrics analogous to the control consistency metric introduced for image control signals.

---

## Summary  

The Orthogonal Finetuning framework offers a principled route to adapt powerful text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving their pretrained semantic structure. Nonetheless, several concrete limitations remain:

* The cubic cost of the Cayley‑based matrix inverse limits scalability; alternative orthogonal parameterizations are needed.  
* The compositional nature of orthogonal transforms invites multi‑task finetuning, but interference and ordering effects require systematic study.  
* Block‑diagonal parameterization, while efficient, imposes a bias that may restrict expressivity; dynamic or learned block structures could mitigate this.  
* Extending OFT beyond image diffusion—to video, audio, and newer architectures—promises broader impact but also introduces new challenges related to dimensionality, modality‑specific similarity measures, and architecture‑aware design.

Addressing these open problems will not only make OFT more practical for ever‑larger generative models but also deepen our theoretical understanding of how angular relationships among neurons encode semantic knowledge across modalities.
