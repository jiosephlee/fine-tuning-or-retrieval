\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 9: # Comparison with Related Fine‑Tuning Techniques  

In recent years the community has proposed a variety of strategies for adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks.  While many of these methods were originally developed for language models or classification networks, they share a common goal: to modify a model’s parameters so that it can accomplish a new task while retaining the knowledge acquired during pre‑training.  This chapter situates **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—the method introduced in the paper—within this broader landscape.  We begin by reviewing the most widely used additive low‑rank approach, **LoRA**, and then discuss **adapter‑style methods** and **prompt‑tuning**, both of which modify a model’s behavior by inserting lightweight modules.  After that we examine **orthogonal over‑parameterized training** (the work of Liu et al.) and explain how its motivations and theoretical guarantees differ from those of OFT.  Finally, we compare the techniques on three practical axes that matter to a practitioner: **parameter efficiency**, **inference overhead**, and **scalability** to very large diffusion models.

---  

## LoRA: Low‑Rank Additive Updates  

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) became a de‑facto baseline for finetuning large generative and language models because it requires only a small number of additional parameters.  The core idea is to decompose the weight update for a given layer into a product of two low‑rank matrices,  
\[
\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top ,\qquad 
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'},\; \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'},
\]  
where \(r'\ll \min(d,n)\) is a hyper‑parameter that controls the rank of the update.  During finetuning only \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are learned; the original pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) remains frozen.  The forward pass of a LoRA‑adapted linear layer therefore becomes  
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0}+\Delta\mathbf{W})^\top\mathbf{x}
      = (\mathbf{W}^{0}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top)^\top\mathbf{x}.
\]  

Because \(\Delta\mathbf{W}\) is low‑rank, the number of trainable parameters scales as \(r'(d+n)\).  In practice this yields a dramatic reduction compared with updating the full weight matrix, which would require \(dn\) parameters.  The low‑rank restriction also acts as a regularizer: it constrains how far the finetuned model can drift from the pretrained one, implicitly encouraging a small Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\).  

Despite these advantages, LoRA does **not** directly constrain the geometric relationship among the rows (or columns) of \(\mathbf{W}\).  Consequently, the pairwise angles between neurons—captured by hyperspherical energy—may change arbitrarily as training proceeds.  The paper argues that such uncontrolled angular drift can degrade the semantic generation ability of text‑to‑image diffusion models, especially when only a few examples are available for a downstream task.  OFT addresses this shortcoming by enforcing an orthogonal transformation that **preserves all pairwise angles** by construction, as discussed in the next sections.

## Adapter‑Style Methods and Prompt‑Tuning  

Adapter‑style techniques—originally popular in natural‑language processing—introduce small bottleneck modules (often two linear layers with a non‑linear activation) into each layer of a pretrained network.  The base model’s parameters stay frozen; only the adapters are learned.  Prompt‑tuning follows a similar philosophy but modifies the **input embeddings** (or the text encoder) rather than the backbone weights, thereby steering the model toward a target domain with a learned “soft prompt”.  

Both families share two practical traits: (1) they require dramatically fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning, and (2) they leave the bulk of the pretrained network untouched, which empirically helps preserve the original generative qualities.  However, the **parameterization** of adapters and prompts is additive rather than multiplicative.  The adapted representation can be written as  
\[
\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{A}_{\text{adapter}}(\mathbf{h}),
\]  
where \(\mathbf{A}_{\text{adapter}}\) is a small neural function.  This additive form does not guarantee any invariance of the hyperspherical energy of the underlying weight matrices.  In other words, the **relative orientations** of neurons may still be altered in an uncontrolled fashion, which—as the paper’s experiments demonstrate—can lead to slower convergence or a loss of subject identity in few‑shot generation.  

OFT, by contrast, imposes a **global orthogonal rotation** (or reflection) on the entire weight matrix of a layer: \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  This transformation is multiplicative and **exactly preserves** the pairwise cosine similarities among the rows of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  Consequently, OFT provides a principled guarantee that the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained model remains intact, something that adapters and prompt‑tuning cannot claim.

## Orthogonal Over‑Parameterized Training (Liu et al.) – Similarities and Differences  

The orthogonal over‑parameterized training framework introduced by Liu et al. (2021) explores the idea of **training a network from scratch while constraining all weight matrices to be orthogonal**.  Their motivation stems from observations that a small hyperspherical energy—meaning neurons are uniformly spread on the unit sphere—correlates with good generalization in classification tasks.  To achieve this, they parameterize each weight matrix as an orthogonal matrix and train the network directly on the target task, thereby keeping the hyperspherical energy low throughout training.

While the mathematical machinery (Cayley parameterization, block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices) is shared with OFT, the **objective** differs fundamentally:

1. **Energy Direction** – Liu et al. **minimize** hyperspherical energy, pushing neurons toward a uniform configuration.  OFT **preserves** the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, keeping the original angular relationships intact.  Minimizing the energy during finetuning could unintentionally erase the semantic structure that the diffusion model has already learned.

2. **Training Regime** – The orthogonal over‑parameterized approach starts from a random initialization and trains **from scratch**.  OFT operates in a **finetuning** regime: the pretrained weights \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) already encode a rich multimodal distribution of images and text.  OFT’s orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is learned **on top of** these weights, acting as a *coordinate change* that re‑orients the existing representation without destroying it.

3. **Task Domain** – The original orthogonal training is evaluated on **classification** benchmarks where uniformity of neurons correlates with margin‑based generalization.  OFT is applied to **text‑to‑image diffusion** models, where preserving the delicate balance between visual fidelity and textual alignment is crucial.  The paper’s empirical evidence indicates that preserving angular structure, rather than minimizing it, is more beneficial for generative tasks.

Thus, although both lines of work exploit the invariance of pairwise angles under orthogonal transformations, OFT repurposes this invariance for **stable, efficient finetuning** of diffusion models, whereas Liu et al. use it to **regularize training from scratch**.

## Parameter Efficiency, Inference Overhead, and Scalability  

### Parameter Efficiency  

| Method | Trainable Parameters (per layer) | Typical Scaling |
|--------|-----------------------------------|-----------------|
| Full finetuning | \(d\times n\) | \(\mathcal{O}(dn)\) |
| LoRA | \(r'(d+n)\) | \(\mathcal{O}(r' d)\) if \(r'\ll d,n\) |
| Adapter / Prompt | \(\mathcal{O}(r_{\text{ad}}\cdot d)\) (bottleneck dim.) | linear in \(d\) |
| OFT (full orthogonal) | \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) |
| OFT (block‑diagonal, \(r\) blocks) | \(\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)\frac{r}{2}\) | \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^{2}}{r}\bigr)\) |
| OFT (shared blocks) | \(\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)/2\) | \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^{2}}{r^{2}}\bigr)\) |

In practice the diffusion model’s linear layers often have modest hidden dimensions (e.g., 128, 256).  The paper’s concrete example shows that for a \(128\times128\) weight matrix, LoRA with rank \(r'=8\) needs 2,048 trainable parameters, while a block‑diagonal OFT with \(r=8\) uses only 960 parameters.  Therefore, **OFT can be more parameter‑efficient than LoRA** when an appropriate block size is chosen, while still preserving the full‑rank nature of the transformation (the orthogonal matrix remains invertible).

### Inference Overhead  

A critical practical consideration for any adaptation technique is whether it adds latency or memory consumption at test time.  

* **LoRA** and **adapter** methods require the additional low‑rank or bottleneck modules to be executed alongside the original forward pass, which modestly increases the number of matrix multiplications.  
* **ControlNet**, a contemporaneous controllable‑generation approach, injects extra conditional branches and consequently incurs a noticeable inference cost.  

In contrast, **OFT introduces no extra computation at inference**.  Once training finishes, the learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be **pre‑multiplied** into the pretrained weight matrix:
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]  
The resulting \(\mathbf{W}\) is a standard weight matrix; the forward pass proceeds exactly as in the original diffusion model.  Hence the inference speed and memory footprint are identical to those of the unmodified pretrained model.

### Scalability  

Scalability concerns both the **size of the orthogonal parameterization** and the **cost of ensuring orthogonality** during training.  

* The naïve Cayley transform requires an inversion of \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\), which scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3})\) for a full \(d\times d\) matrix.  For very large hidden dimensions this operation could become a bottleneck.  
* The **block‑diagonal** strategy mitigates this by decomposing \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) smaller orthogonal blocks, each of size \(d/r\).  The computational cost of each block’s Cayley transform drops to \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl((d/r)^{3}\bigr)\), and the total cost becomes \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{3}/r^{2}\bigr)\).  Empirically the paper finds that even modest block numbers (e.g., \(r=8\) or \(r=16\)) retain enough flexibility for diffusion finetuning while keeping the overhead manageable.  
* **Shared‑block** variants further reduce the number of unique parameters and the associated matrix‑inverse operations, at the expense of introducing a bias toward a common rotation across blocks.  The experiments demonstrate that this bias does not materially harm generation quality for the tasks considered.  

Overall, OFT scales to the hidden sizes typical of modern text‑to‑image diffusion models (e.g., the 768‑dimensional transformer layers in Stable Diffusion) when an appropriate block‑diagonal factorization is used.  In comparison, LoRA’s low‑rank factorization scales linearly with \(d\) and is therefore also scalable, but it lacks the **geometric invariance** guarantees that OFT provides.

---  

### Synthesis  

The comparative analysis highlights that OFT occupies a middle ground between the **extreme flexibility** of full finetuning and the **parameter frugality** of low‑rank or adapter‑based methods.  Its distinguishing feature is the **preservation of hyperspherical energy**, which the paper empirically links to the retention of semantic generation ability in diffusion models.  By learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix—implemented efficiently via block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization—OFT can achieve:

* **Stronger stability** and faster convergence on both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks, because the optimization occurs on a smooth hyperspherical manifold.  
* **Zero inference overhead**, as the orthogonal transform can be folded into the original weight matrix after training.  
* **Competitive or superior parameter efficiency** relative to LoRA when the block size is chosen wisely.  

At the same time, OFT inherits some of the practical challenges of orthogonal training, namely the need for matrix inverses and the careful tuning of block granularity.  Future work may explore faster differentiable orthogonalizations or more expressive yet still angle‑preserving parameterizations, but the present formulation already demonstrates that a principled geometric constraint can dramatically improve the finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.
