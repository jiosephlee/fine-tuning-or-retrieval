\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 6: # Re‑Scaled OFT and Further Extensions  

In the preceding sections we introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** as a principled way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained network. OFT achieves this by learning a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that rotates (or reflects) the entire set of neurons in a given layer, thereby keeping every pairwise angular relationship unchanged. Although this formulation already yields remarkable stability and controllability, it can be enriched by allowing modest adjustments of the individual neuron magnitudes. This chapter presents the **re‑scaled OFT** variant, explains why such scaling does not interfere with the hyperspherical energy invariant, summarizes the empirical benefits observed in the paper’s appendix, and outlines promising avenues for extending the framework, such as multi‑task composition and adaptive block structures.

---

## Formulation of Re‑scaled OFT with Learnable Diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\)

The original OFT modifies a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by a single orthogonal transformation:
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]
Every output feature \(\mathbf{z}\) in a fully‑connected layer is then computed as
\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the input activation.

To increase the expressive capacity without breaking the orthogonal invariance, the authors propose **re‑scaled OFT**. In this variant a diagonal scaling matrix \(\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is introduced:
\[
\mathbf{D}= \operatorname{diag}(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}),\qquad s_{i}>0\;\; \forall i .
\]
The forward pass becomes
\[
\mathbf{z}= \bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}
       = \mathbf{D}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]
Thus each neuron \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\) is first rotated by the same orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) and then multiplied by a scalar \(s_{i}\). The set \(\{s_{i}\}\) is learned jointly with \(\mathbf{R}\) during finetuning. Importantly, the orthogonal matrix is still initialized as the identity and remains orthogonal throughout training, while the diagonal entries start from one, guaranteeing that the model initially coincides with the pretrained weights.

---

## Why Scaling Does Not Affect Hyperspherical Energy

Recall that the **hyperspherical energy (HE)** of a layer is defined as the sum over inverse pairwise distances of the **normalized** neuron vectors:
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})=
\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}.
\]
HE is *invariant* under any transformation that preserves the *directions* of the neurons, because the normalization removes the effect of magnitude. An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates every vector without changing its norm, so \(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_{i}} = \widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\) and consequently \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\).

When we additionally multiply each neuron by a positive scalar \(s_{i}\), the direction of the vector is still unchanged:
\[
\widehat{\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\text{new}}}
= \frac{s_{i}\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
       {\|s_{i}\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
       {\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}} .
\]
Since the scalar factor cancels in the normalization, the set of normalized vectors remains exactly the same as under pure orthogonal transformation. Hence **the hyperspherical energy of re‑scaled OFT equals that of the original OFT**, and both match the pretrained value. This property guarantees that the central theoretical premise of OFT—preserving pairwise angular relationships—remains intact even after introducing per‑neuron scaling.

## Empirical Gains from Re‑scaled OFT  

Although the scaling does not alter the HE, allowing individual magnitude adjustments can improve the model’s ability to fit downstream data, especially when the downstream task demands subtle changes in intensity or contrast that are not captured solely by rotating the weight space. The original paper reports a brief but informative set of experiments in the appendix (Section C). The key findings are:

1. **Faster Convergence:** On the controllable generation tasks (Canny‑edge‑to‑image, segmentation‑to‑image, landmark‑to‑face), re‑scaled OFT reaches the same control‑consistency metrics a few epochs earlier than vanilla OFT. The added degrees of freedom help the optimizer locate a suitable region of the loss landscape more rapidly.

2. **Higher Final Quality:** Quantitative scores such as IoU for edge‑to‑image and landmark \(\ell_{2}\) error for face pose control improve by roughly \(2\!-\!4\%\) when scaling is enabled, indicating that the model can fine‑tune finer details without sacrificing the overall semantic structure.

3. **Stability Across Seeds:** The variance of performance over 30 random seeds decreases when using re‑scaled OFT, suggesting that the extra scaling parameters act as a regularizer that mitigates sensitivity to initialization.

4. **Negligible Parameter Overhead:** The diagonal matrix \(\mathbf{D}\) introduces only \(n\) additional trainable scalar parameters per layer (the number of output neurons). In typical diffusion UNet layers \(n\) is a few hundred, which is orders of magnitude smaller than the full weight matrix and comparable to the parameter count of LoRA’s low‑rank adapters.

Overall, the empirical evidence confirms that **re‑scaled OFT preserves the theoretical guarantees of OFT while delivering modest but consistent performance improvements**.

## Potential Directions for Extending OFT  

The success of OFT and its re‑scaled variant opens several research avenues. Below we sketch two promising directions that stay faithful to the paper’s underlying principles.

### 1. Multi‑Task Composition via Orthogonal Matrices  

An orthogonal matrix forms a group under multiplication: if \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{2}\) are orthogonal, so is \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2}\). Suppose we finetune a diffusion model on two distinct downstream tasks, obtaining orthogonal transforms \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\) and \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\). Applying them sequentially yields a composite transform
\[
\mathbf{R}^{(AB)} = \mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)},
\]
which is still orthogonal and therefore continues to preserve hyperspherical energy. This property suggests a **compositional finetuning** scheme: a base model could be equipped with a library of orthogonal adapters, each encoding a specific capability (e.g., a particular style, a subject identity, or a control modality). At inference time, the desired set of capabilities can be combined simply by matrix multiplication, without any additional training or inference overhead. Investigating how well such compositions retain the individual task performances, and whether interference arises, is an open problem highlighted by the authors.

### 2. Adaptive Block Structures for Greater Parameter Efficiency  

The current OFT implementation reduces parameter cost by imposing a **block‑diagonal** structure on \(\mathbf{R}\), optionally sharing blocks across layers. While effective, this design introduces a bias: neurons belonging to different blocks cannot interact through the orthogonal transformation. An **adaptive block scheme** could allocate block sizes dynamically based on the statistical properties of each layer (e.g., variance of activations, redundancy measured by pairwise cosine similarity). One could learn a gating matrix that decides whether two neurons should belong to the same orthogonal subspace, effectively learning a sparse orthogonal matrix. Such a scheme would retain the HE invariance—since each connected component remains orthogonal—but would allocate parameters where they are most needed, potentially improving expressivity on very large layers (e.g., the attention projection matrices in transformer blocks) while still keeping the total parameter count low.

Other extensions naturally follow from these ideas, such as:
- **Layer‑wise or attention‑specific scaling** where diagonal matrices are attached only to key/query/value projections,
- **Regularizing the spectrum of \(\mathbf{R}\)** to encourage near‑identity transformations for certain layers while allowing larger rotations in others,
- **Combining re‑scaled OFT with low‑rank adapters** (LoRA) to capture both directional and low‑dimensional additive changes.

All of these directions preserve the core principle of OFT: **maintaining the pairwise angular geometry of neurons** while granting controlled flexibility to adapt to new tasks.

---

### Concluding Remarks  

Re‑scaled OFT enriches the original orthogonal finetuning framework by permitting per‑neuron magnitude adjustments without compromising the hyperspherical energy invariant. The empirical gains reported in the appendix demonstrate that this modest extension yields faster convergence, higher final quality, and more robust training. Looking forward, the algebraic structure of orthogonal transformations invites compositional multi‑task adaptation, and adaptive block parameterizations promise even greater efficiency. These extensions keep the method fully **model‑agnostic** and free of inference overhead, reinforcing OFT’s appeal as a principled, scalable tool for controlling text‑to‑image diffusion models.
