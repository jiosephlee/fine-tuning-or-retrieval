Chapter 4: # Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices  

In this chapter we develop a practical understanding of how orthogonal matrices can be represented and learned with modest computational resources.  The discussion follows directly from the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework introduced for text‑to‑image diffusion models.  We begin by describing the Cayley transform, which provides a differentiable way to generate orthogonal matrices from a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \).  Next we explain how a block‑diagonal structure and optional block sharing dramatically reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving the essential orthogonal property.  We then compare the resulting parameter count to that of low‑rank approaches such as LoRA, and finally we outline concrete implementation considerations—including initialization, gradient computation, and numerical stability—required for robust training.  

---

## Cayley Transform and Skew‑Symmetric Matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \)

The central requirement of OFT is that the transformation applied to a pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \) be orthogonal, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}.
\]

Directly optimizing over the set of orthogonal matrices, denoted \( \operatorname{O}(d) \), is difficult because the orthogonality constraints are nonlinear and would typically require expensive projection steps after each gradient update.  The Cayley transform offers a compact, analytically differentiable parametrization that automatically enforces orthogonality.

### Definition  

Let \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) be a **skew‑symmetric** matrix, meaning  

\[
\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}.
\]

The Cayley transform maps \( \mathbf{Q} \) to an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) as

\[
\boxed{\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}} \tag{1}
\]

where \( \mathbf{I} \) is the \( d\times d \) identity matrix.  The inverse \( (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) exists as long as \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) is nonsingular, which holds for sufficiently small \( \mathbf{Q} \).  By construction, \( \mathbf{R} \) satisfies  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \big[(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\big]
                       \big[(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\big]
                     =\mathbf{I}.
\]

Thus any skew‑symmetric \( \mathbf{Q} \) yields a valid orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).  

### Parameter Count  

A skew‑symmetric matrix has only \( d(d-1)/2 \) independent entries (the upper triangular part, with the lower triangular part determined by the sign).  Consequently, the Cayley transform reduces the number of free parameters from \( d^{2} \) (for an unconstrained matrix) to exactly the dimension of the Lie algebra \( \mathfrak{so}(d) \).  This reduction is the first source of efficiency in OFT.

### Practical Remarks  

* **Determinant Restriction** – The Cayley transform can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant \( +1 \) (the special orthogonal group \( \operatorname{SO}(d) \)), because reflections (det \( -1 \)) cannot be expressed as a rational function of \( \mathbf{Q} \).  Empirically, this restriction does not hinder performance in diffusion‑model finetuning.  
* **Small‑\( \mathbf{Q} \) Regime** – When \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) is small, a first‑order Taylor expansion of (1) yields  

\[
\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\mathbf{Q}^{2}),
\]  

which is convenient for analysis (see the Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning variant) and for initializing \( \mathbf{R} \) close to the identity.

## Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices and Block‑Sharing Strategies  

Even with the reduction to \( d(d-1)/2 \) parameters, a full‑size orthogonal matrix can be costly to store and to multiply during training, especially for high‑dimensional layers (e.g., transformer attention projections).  A **block‑diagonal** construction further lowers both storage and computational demands while preserving orthogonality.

### Block‑Diagonal Construction  

We partition the dimensionality \( d \) into \( r \) equal blocks of size \( \frac{d}{r} \) (assume \( r \) divides \( d \)).  The orthogonal matrix is then defined as  

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}\right),\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\operatorname{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),\; i=1,\dots ,r. \tag{2}
\]

Each block \( \mathbf{R}_{i} \) is itself orthogonal, and because the blocks do not interact, the overall matrix remains orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{r}\right)=\mathbf{I}.
\]

### Parameter Savings  

A full orthogonal matrix of size \( d\times d \) requires  

\[
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
\]  

independent parameters.  With the block‑diagonal form, the total number of free parameters becomes  

\[
\frac{r\,\frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)}{2}
= \frac{d\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2},
\]  

which scales as \( \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr) \).  Larger \( r \) yields a stronger reduction.  

#### Block Sharing  

An even more aggressive reduction is achieved by **sharing** the same block across all positions, i.e.,  

\[
\mathbf{R}_{1}= \mathbf{R}_{2}= \dots = \mathbf{R}_{r}\equiv\mathbf{R}_{\text{share}}.
\]  

Now the orthogonal matrix is fully determined by a single block, and the parameter count drops to  

\[
\frac{\bigl(\frac{d}{r}\bigr)\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}= \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr).
\]  

In practice, modest values of \( r \) (e.g., \( r=4 \) or \( r=8 \)) already provide a substantial compression while retaining enough flexibility to adapt the model to downstream tasks.

### Interpretation for Convolutional Layers  

When applying OFT to convolutional weight tensors, each block can be aligned with a group of input channels.  If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block acts like a depth‑wise orthogonal transformation, analogous to a depth‑wise convolution kernel.  Sharing blocks across channels yields a uniform rotation applied to all channels, which can be viewed as a learned color‑space rotation.  These interpretations explain why the block‑diagonal parameterization remains effective for both fully‑connected and convolutional layers.

## Parameter‑Count Analysis vs. LoRA and Full‑Rank Updates  

It is instructive to place the block‑diagonal OFT parameter budget next to that of existing low‑rank adaptation methods such as LoRA.  Consider a weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) (e.g., the projection matrix of a transformer attention head).  

### LoRA  

LoRA represents the weight update as a low‑rank product  

\[
\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top},
\]  

where \( \mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'} \) and \( \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'} \) with a small rank \( r' \).  The number of trainable parameters is  

\[
\underbrace{dr'}_{\mathbf{A}} + \underbrace{nr'}_{\mathbf{B}} = r'(d+n). \tag{3}
\]  

If \( r' \) is taken as a constant fraction of \( d \) (e.g., \( r'=\alpha d \) with \( \alpha\le 1 \)), the complexity becomes  

\[
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}+dn).
\]  

### OFT (Block‑Diagonal)  

For a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with \( r \) blocks (no sharing) the trainable parameters are  

\[
\frac{d\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}
  = \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr). \tag{4}
\]  

If block sharing is employed, the count reduces to \( \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr) \).  

### Concrete Example  

Suppose \( d=n=128 \) (a typical transformer head size).  

| Method | Parameter Count | Example Setting |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|
| LoRA   | \( 2,048 \) (with \( r'=8 \)) | \( r'=8 \) |
| OFT (no sharing, \( r=8 \)) | \( 960 \) | \( r=8 \) |
| OFT (sharing, \( r=8 \)) | \( 120 \) | \( r=8 \) (shared blocks) |

Even the most expressive block‑diagonal OFT (no sharing) uses fewer parameters than LoRA while guaranteeing orthogonality and preserving hyperspherical energy.  This parameter efficiency, together with the structural regularization induced by orthogonality, explains the observed faster convergence and higher stability in downstream diffusion‑model finetuning.

## Implementation Details (Initialization, Gradient Handling, Numerical Stability)

A theoretically sound parametrization is only useful if it can be implemented robustly in modern deep‑learning frameworks.  Below we outline the critical steps required to make Cayley‑based orthogonal finetuning practical.

### 1. Initialization  

* **Skew‑Symmetric Matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \)** – Initialize all entries of the upper‑triangular part of \( \mathbf{Q} \) to zero, and set the lower‑triangular part to the negative of the upper part.  This yields the all‑zero matrix, which in turn gives  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{0})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{0})^{-1}= \mathbf{I}.
\]  

Thus the OFT‑finetuned weight starts exactly at the pretrained weight \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \).

* **Block‑Diagonal Structure** – When constructing \( \mathbf{Q} \) for each block, allocate a separate skew‑symmetric parameter tensor of size \( \frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r} \) for each block (or a single tensor if block sharing is used).  The same zero‑initialization rule applies.

### 2. Forward Pass Computation  

Given a pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \), the OFT forward transformation for a layer is  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W})^{\top}\mathbf{x}. \tag{5}
\]  

In practice:
1. Construct each block \( \mathbf{R}_{i} \) via the Cayley transform (1) applied to its block‑specific \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \).  
2. Assemble the block‑diagonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) (or the shared block).  
3. Multiply \( \mathbf{R} \) with \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \).  
Because \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal, the multiplication does not increase the dynamic range of the weights, which helps preserve numerical stability.

### 3. Gradient Computation  

Automatic differentiation in frameworks such as PyTorch or JAX handles the Cayley transform automatically, provided the inverse \( (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) is differentiable.  However, care must be taken:

* **Avoiding Singularities** – Ensure that \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) stays well‑conditioned.  Practically, this is achieved by using a modest learning rate for \( \mathbf{Q} \) (or equivalently for the orthogonal matrix) and by initializing \( \mathbf{Q}=0 \).  During training, the norm of \( \mathbf{Q} \) typically remains far below 1, preventing the denominator from approaching singularity.

* **Efficient Inverse** – The block‑diagonal form allows the inverse to be computed independently per block.  Since each block is small (size \( d/r \)), a direct matrix inversion incurs negligible overhead.  For very large blocks one may replace the exact inverse with a few steps of the Newton–Schulz iteration, which is differentiable and numerically stable.

* **Projected Gradient for COFT** – In Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) an additional norm constraint \( \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon \) is imposed.  Using the first‑order approximation \( \mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q} \), the constraint translates to \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|\le \epsilon' \).  After each gradient update, one can simply clip the Frobenius norm of each \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \) to \( \epsilon' \).  This projection is cheap and preserves differentiability.

### 4. Numerical Stability Tips  

* **Floating‑Point Precision** – When \( d \) is large (> 1024), the intermediate matrices \( \mathbf{I}\pm\mathbf{Q} \) can become ill‑conditioned if \( \mathbf{Q} \) grows.  Using 32‑bit floating point (FP32) is sufficient for typical diffusion‑model layers; if needed, switch to FP16 with loss‑scaling to maintain stability.  

* **Regularizer for Skew‑Symmetry** – While the construction enforces skew‑symmetry analytically, small numerical drift may occur after many updates.  A lightweight regularizer \( \| \mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{Q}^{\top} \|^{2} \) can be added to the loss to keep the matrix exactly skew‑symmetric.

* **Batching Across Blocks** – For GPU efficiency, concatenate all block‑specific \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \) tensors along a new batch dimension and perform the Cayley transform in parallel.  This vectorized implementation maximizes throughput and leverages existing linear‑algebra kernels.

## Summary  

The Cayley transform provides a mathematically elegant bridge from unrestricted skew‑symmetric parameters to guaranteed orthogonal matrices.  By exploiting a block‑diagonal layout—optionally with shared blocks—OFT dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters from \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}) \) to \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r) \) or even \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2}) \), while still preserving the hyperspherical energy that underlies semantic stability in diffusion models.  Compared with low‑rank schemes such as LoRA, the orthogonal approach offers a different trade‑off: it retains full‑rank structure (through orthogonal rotations) but compresses the representation via groupwise rotations.  Implementing this scheme reliably involves zero‑initialization of the skew‑symmetric matrix, careful handling of the matrix inverse (facilitated by the block structure), and optional norm clipping for the constrained variant.  With these techniques, orthogonal finetuning becomes a practical, efficient, and theoretically grounded tool for adapting large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream generation tasks.
