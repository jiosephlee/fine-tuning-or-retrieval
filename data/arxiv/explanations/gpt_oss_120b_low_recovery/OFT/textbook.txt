\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion and Fine‑Tuning Challenges  

## Overview of Large Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have become the leading paradigm for generating photorealistic images conditioned on natural‑language prompts.  The core idea is to train a denoising network \( \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}) \) that predicts the noise component of a latent image \( \mathbf{x}_t \) at diffusion step \(t\) given a conditioning signal \( \mathbf{c} \) derived from a text encoder.  Training proceeds by minimizing a simple mean‑squared error between the predicted and true noise, which implicitly learns the score function of the data distribution.  

Three families of models dominate the recent literature:

* **Imagen** \cite{saharia2022photoreactive} and **GLIDE** \cite{nichol2022glide} operate directly in pixel space.  Imagen adopts a frozen large language model (e.g. T5) for the text encoder, while GLIDE jointly trains a diffusion prior and a text encoder on paired image‑text data.  

* **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} and **DALL‑E 2** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} perform diffusion in a compressed latent space.  Stable Diffusion learns a VQ‑GAN codebook that maps images to a lower‑dimensional latent \( \mathbf{z} \) and trains the diffusion model to generate \( \mathbf{z} \) rather than raw pixels.  DALL‑E 2 exploits a CLIP‑based joint visual‑language embedding space, allowing the diffusion network to be conditioned on the same embedding used for image‑text similarity.  

Despite architectural differences, all of these systems share two crucial ingredients: a **large pretrained diffusion backbone** that has absorbed massive visual‑semantic knowledge, and a **text encoder** that projects a prompt into a conditioning vector.  Once pretrained, the diffusion backbone can produce high‑fidelity images that respect the semantics expressed by the prompt.  

## Downstream Tasks: Subject‑Driven Generation and Controllable Generation  

The pretrained models excel at generating images that roughly match the supplied prompt, yet many practical applications demand finer‑grained control.  Two representative downstream tasks illustrate this need.  

### Subject‑Driven Generation  

In the subject‑driven setting (often called **personalization**), the user supplies a handful of images of a particular object or person—e.g. five photos of a specific cat.  The goal is to generate novel images that retain the visual identity of that subject while placing it in new contexts dictated by a textual description.  Formally, given a small image set \( \{\mathbf{I}_k\}_{k=1}^{K} \) and a prompt \( \mathbf{p} \), we wish to obtain a conditional distribution  

\[
p_{\theta^\star}(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{p},\text{subject}) \approx p_{\text{pretrained}}(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{p}) 
\]

where the distribution is altered just enough to inject the subject’s appearance.  Methods such as DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} achieve this by fine‑tuning the entire diffusion model on the few subject images together with a reconstruction loss.  

### Controllable Generation  

Controllable generation expands the conditioning space beyond pure text.  An external **control signal**—for instance a Canny edge map, a semantic segmentation mask, or facial landmarks—is supplied alongside the text prompt.  The diffusion process must now obey two constraints:

\[
\mathbf{x} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{x}} \, p_{\theta^\star}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{s}),
\]

where \( \mathbf{s} \) denotes the control modality.  Pioneering systems such as ControlNet \cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter \cite{mou2023t2i} augment the pretrained backbone with additional trainable branches that ingest \( \mathbf{s} \) and steer the generation.  

Both tasks share a common challenge: **adapting the pretrained diffusion model without erasing the semantic knowledge it has already acquired**.  The model must learn a new mapping (subject identity or control alignment) while preserving the ability to generate diverse, high‑quality images for arbitrary prompts.  

## Desiderata for Fine‑Tuning: Efficiency vs. Preservation of Pretrained Knowledge  

A fine‑tuning method for diffusion models should satisfy two sometimes competing requirements.  

1. **Training Efficiency** – The number of trainable parameters should be modest, and the number of optimisation steps should be small.  Efficient fine‑tuning reduces GPU memory consumption, shortens wall‑clock time, and makes it feasible to specialise a large model for many subjects or control signals.  

2. **Generalizability Preservation** – After adaptation, the model must still retain the broad generative capabilities of the original pretrained backbone.  In practice this means that (i) generated images should remain photorealistic and diverse, and (ii) the model should not catastrophically forget how to follow arbitrary textual prompts.  

Achieving both simultaneously is non‑trivial.  Naïve strategies either over‑parameterise the adaptation (e.g. fine‑tuning all weights) and consequently degrade the original knowledge, or under‑parameterise (e.g. freezing most weights) and fail to capture the new task.  

## Why Simple Euclidean‑Distance Based Fine‑Tuning Is Insufficient  

A common intuition in transfer learning is that **small Euclidean changes** to the weight tensor \( \mathbf{M} \) (the collection of all parameters) should preserve the original functionality:

\[
\| \mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{0} \|_{2} \;\text{small} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{behaviour close to pretrained}.
\]

Here \( \mathbf{M}^{0} \) denotes the pretrained weights.  Existing fine‑tuning approaches, such as DreamBooth’s direct gradient descent with a tiny learning rate, implicitly rely on this assumption, as does LoRA’s low‑rank additive update.  However, Euclidean proximity alone does **not** capture the *relational* geometry among neurons, which is essential for preserving the semantics encoded in the network.

### The Role of Pairwise Neuronal Relationships  

Consider a fully‑connected layer \( \mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \).  Each column \( \mathbf{w}_i \) can be interpreted as a neuron direction in a \(d\)‑dimensional feature space.  The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) of this layer is defined as  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j}\big\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\big\rVert^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i} \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]

which aggregates the inverse distances between all **normalized** neurons.  HE measures how uniformly the neurons are spread on the unit hypersphere; low energy corresponds to a highly redundant configuration, while high energy indicates that neurons occupy diverse directions.  

Empirical studies in the paper show that **hyperspherical similarity (e.g. cosine similarity) correlates strongly with semantic information**.  A toy auto‑encoder experiment demonstrates that reconstructing an image using only the angular component of the convolution kernels retains almost all visual detail, whereas the magnitude component alone is essentially useless.  Consequently, preserving the *pairwise angles* among neurons is a more faithful proxy for retaining pretrained semantics than preserving raw Euclidean distances.

### Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy  

A crucial mathematical property is that **any orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) (i.e. \( \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \)) leaves all pairwise angular relationships unchanged**:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j \rangle 
= \langle \mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_j \rangle 
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

Thus, if a fine‑tuning step is restricted to applying a *layer‑shared* orthogonal transformation, the hyperspherical energy remains exactly the same as in the pretrained model.  This guarantees that the relational geometry of the neurons is untouched, even though the absolute weight values may change substantially.  By contrast, a naïve Euclidean regularizer (e.g. penalising \( \| \mathbf{W} - \mathbf{W}^{0}\| \)) does not enforce preservation of these angles and can easily collapse the geometric structure, leading to semantic drift.

In summary, Euclidean‑distance‑only regularisation is **insufficient** because it ignores the *directional* structure that carries most of the model’s semantic knowledge.  The paper’s proposed Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT) therefore shifts focus from preserving raw weight magnitude to preserving the **pairwise hyperspherical relationships**, which the authors empirically demonstrate to be essential for both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks.

# Geometric Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Neuron Angles  

In this chapter we develop the geometric intuition that underlies **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**.  The central idea is that the *directions* of the neurons in a neural network—once they are projected onto a unit hypersphere—encode most of the semantic information learned during pre‑training.  By quantifying the relationships among these directions with **hyperspherical similarity** and **hyperspherical energy**, we obtain a rigorous measure of how much of the pre‑trained knowledge is retained after finetuning.  The chapter proceeds in four parts:  

1. **Definition of hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy** – we formalize the pairwise angular relationship among neurons and introduce the energy functional that aggregates these relationships.  
2. **Interpretation of neuron directions on the unit hypersphere** – we explain why normalizing neurons to unit length is natural, how their positions on the hypersphere reflect learned features, and why preserving these positions is desirable.  
3. **Empirical evidence: angular features vs. magnitude in auto‑encoders** – a concrete experiment from the paper demonstrates that the angular component of neuron responses alone can almost perfectly reconstruct visual data, whereas the magnitude component carries little useful signal.  
4. **Mathematical invariance of hyperspherical energy under orthogonal transforms** – we prove that applying a common orthogonal matrix to all neurons in a layer leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged, which motivates the orthogonal transformation used in OFT.  

Throughout we use the notation of the original paper.  All mathematical expressions are rendered in LaTeX.

---  

## Definition of Hyperspherical Similarity and Hyperspherical Energy  

Consider a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \{\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{w}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the *neuron* (or filter) that maps an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to the scalar output \(z_{i}= \mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\).  To study the *angular* relationships among neurons we first normalise every neuron to unit length:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},\qquad i=1,\dots ,n .
\]

The normalized vectors \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\) lie on the unit hypersphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid \|\mathbf{u}\|=1\}\).  

**Hyperspherical similarity** between two neurons is defined as the Euclidean distance between their normalized representations, or equivalently the cosine of the angle they span:

\[
\text{sim}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}) \;=\; \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|^{-1}
           \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2\langle\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\rangle }} .
\]

This quantity grows when the two neurons become more aligned (small angular separation) and shrinks when they are far apart on the hypersphere.

Aggregating the pairwise similarities over a layer yields the **hyperspherical energy** (HE):

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|^{-1}
}\tag{1}
\]

The sum runs over all unordered pairs \((i,j)\) with \(i\neq j\).  Intuitively, \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\) measures how *clustered* the neurons are on the hypersphere: a large value indicates many neurons pointing in similar directions, whereas a small value suggests a more uniform spread.  In the literature on deep classification \([\,\text{Liu et al., 2018, 2021}\,]\) small hyperspherical energy has been linked to better generalisation; here we *preserve* the energy of a pretrained model because it already encodes a useful semantic structure.

---  

## Interpretation of Neuron Directions on the Unit Hypersphere  

Normalising neurons to unit length removes the scale (or magnitude) component, isolating the *direction* of each weight vector.  Several theoretical and empirical observations justify focusing on these directions:

1. **Capacity of angular features** – Prior work on *SphereNet* \([\,\text{Liu et al., 2017}\,]\) demonstrated that a network whose weights are constrained to lie on \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) retains comparable expressive power to an unconstrained network, while often enjoying improved generalisation.  This suggests that the *direction* of a weight vector is sufficient to capture the feature it extracts.

2. **Semantic meaning of angles** – In many vision‑language models, the angle between two weight vectors correlates with the semantic similarity of the corresponding features.  For example, two filters whose vectors are close on the hypersphere tend to respond to similar visual patterns, whereas orthogonal filters capture distinct aspects of the input.

3. **Preservation of pairwise relationships** – The set of pairwise angles \(\{\angle(\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j})\}\) forms a geometric fingerprint of a layer.  When these angles are unchanged, the *relative* arrangement of neurons—their collaborative coding of the input space—remains the same, even if the absolute coordinates rotate in the ambient space.

Therefore, if a finetuning procedure can modify the network while **keeping the hyperspherical energy unchanged**, it will retain the essential relational geometry that the pretrained model learned.  This is precisely the desideratum of OFT: we seek a transformation that re‑orients the neurons but does not alter the set of angles among them.

---  

## Empirical Evidence: Angular Features vs. Magnitude in Auto‑Encoders  

To illustrate the dominance of angular information, the authors performed a toy experiment with a convolutional auto‑encoder trained on a small flower dataset.  The encoder uses standard inner‑product convolutions; that is, for a sliding window input \(\mathbf{x}\) and kernel \(\mathbf{w}\), the activation is  

\[
z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

After training, three alternatives for constructing the feature map at test time were examined:

1. **Standard inner product** (the same operation used during training).  
2. **Magnitude‑only**: replace each convolution output with \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|\), discarding the cosine angle.  
3. **Angular‑only**: compute the cosine similarity \(\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}\) and multiply by the input magnitude.

Figure 1 (referenced in the paper) shows that the *angular‑only* reconstruction recovers the original flower images almost perfectly, whereas the *magnitude‑only* reconstruction yields noise‑like outputs devoid of recognizable structure.  Importantly, the network was never trained with explicit cosine activations; the angular information emerged implicitly from the learned weights.  This experiment substantiates the claim that **the direction of neurons encodes the bulk of semantic content**, while the absolute magnitude contributes little to reconstructing visual data.

Consequently, a finetuning method that directly manipulates neuron directions—rather than scaling magnitudes—will be more effective at altering semantics (e.g., adapting a subject’s appearance) while preserving the pre‑training knowledge embedded in the angular geometry.

## Mathematical Invariance of Hyperspherical Energy Under Orthogonal Transforms  

Let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I},\qquad \det(\mathbf{R})=\pm 1 .
\]

Applying the same orthogonal transformation to every neuron in a layer yields a new weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) denotes the pretrained weights.  Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, for any pair \(i\neq j\),

\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}, \hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\rangle
= \left\langle
\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|},
\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}
\right\rangle
= \left\langle
\frac{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|},
\frac{\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}
\right\rangle
= \langle \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\rangle .
\]

The equality follows because orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean norms (\(\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|=\|\mathbf{w}\|\)) and inner products.  Consequently, the Euclidean distance between any two normalized neurons is unchanged:

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\|.
\]

Plugging this into the definition of hyperspherical energy (1) gives

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}') = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_{j}\|^{-1}
                     = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\|^{-1}
                     = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}).
\]

Thus **hyperspherical energy is invariant under a common orthogonal transformation of all neurons in a layer**.  This invariance is the core theoretical justification for Orthogonal Finetuning: by learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) we can rotate (or reflect) the entire set of weight vectors without altering any pairwise angle, thereby guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy—and consequently the relational geometry of the layer—remains exactly as in the pretrained model.

The finetuning objective of OFT can therefore be expressed as

\[
\min_{\mathbf{R}} \; \bigl\| \text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \bigr\|
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

whose optimum is trivially achieved when \(\mathbf{R}\) is any orthogonal matrix, with the identity \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) providing a natural initialization.  By restricting to **layer‑shared** orthogonal matrices, OFT achieves a balance: sufficient flexibility to adapt the model to a downstream task, yet enough structure to preserve the high‑fidelity semantic representation encoded by the original hyperspherical energy.

---  

### Summary  

In this chapter we have:

* Formalised **hyperspherical similarity** and **hyperspherical energy** as geometric descriptors of neuron directions on the unit hypersphere.  
* Interpreted why the *directions* of neurons, rather than their magnitudes, carry the semantic weight of a pretrained diffusion model.  
* Presented an empirical auto‑encoder study that demonstrates the near‑perfect reconstruction capability of angular features alone.  
* Proven that a common orthogonal transformation leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged, providing the theoretical foundation for **Orthogonal Finetuning**.

These geometric foundations motivate the design of OFT and its constrained variant COFT, which leverage the invariance of hyperspherical energy to finetune large text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving their pretrained generative abilities.

**Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT): Theory and Formulation**

In this chapter we develop the mathematical foundations of Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT), the finetuning paradigm introduced for large text‑to‑image diffusion models.  We begin by formalising the principle that drives OFT, then show how minimising a hyperspherical‑energy discrepancy inevitably leads to an orthogonal transformation of the weight matrix in each layer.  Next we describe how this transformation is inserted into the forward computation, and finally we contrast OFT with the more common strategies of naïve gradient‑based finetuning and the low‑rank LoRA approach.

---

### Formal statement of the OFT principle

Let \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) denote the weight matrix of a pretrained layer (fully‑connected, convolutional, or attention‑projection).  Its columns \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) are the \(i\)-th neurons of the layer.  During finetuning we obtain a new weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) with columns \(\mathbf{w}_{i}\).  To preserve the semantic structure that the pretrained model has learned, OFT requires that the **hyperspherical energy** of the finetuned layer remain identical to that of the pretrained layer:

\[
\boxed{\ \bigl\|\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\bigr\|=0\ }.
\tag{1}
\]

The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix is defined as the sum of inverse Euclidean distances between every pair of **unit‑normed** neurons:

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;:=\;
\sum_{i\neq j}\Bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\Bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (1) therefore states that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons—captured entirely by the set \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\}\)—must be unchanged after finetuning.

---

### Derivation: minimizing the hyperspherical‑energy difference ⇒ orthogonal matrix

Consider the optimisation problem

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\;\bigl\|\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\bigr\|.
\tag{3}
\]

Because \(\operatorname{HE}(\cdot)\) depends only on the directions of the columns, any transformation that preserves all pairwise angles will leave the energy unchanged.  A **global orthogonal transformation** applied to every neuron in the same layer satisfies precisely this condition.  Formally, let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I}.
\tag{4}
\]

Define the finetuned weight matrix as

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\tag{5}
\]

For any pair \((i,j)\),

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
      {\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
      {\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \mathbf{R}\,\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i},
\]
where the second equality uses the fact that orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norm.  Consequently

\[
\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|
= \bigl\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}
      -\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\bigr\|
= \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i}
      -\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{j}\bigr\|,
\]
so each term in (2) is invariant under the map (5).  Hence

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}),
\]
and the objective (3) attains its minimum value zero.  Importantly, the converse also holds: if two sets of unit‑norm vectors have identical pairwise distances, there exists an orthogonal matrix relating them (up to a possible reflection).  Therefore, the **necessary and sufficient** condition for preserving hyperspherical energy is that the finetuned weight matrix be obtained from the pretrained one by a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation.

Thus the OFT principle reduces to learning, for each layer, an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix on the left.

---

### Forward‑pass modification with a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix

Let a layer receive an input activation vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) and produce an output \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) through a linear map.  In the pretrained model the computation is

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\tag{6}
\]

After applying OFT, we replace \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{\ \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}
      = (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\ }.
\tag{7}
\]

Equation (7) shows that finetuning is achieved by **rotating the column space of the weight matrix** while leaving the input activations unchanged (or equivalently, by rotating the input features before applying the original weights).  The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is shared across all neurons of the layer, which dramatically reduces the number of free parameters relative to learning an arbitrary full‑rank update.

Training proceeds by gradient descent on a task‑specific loss (e.g., reconstruction loss for DreamBooth, or a control‑consistency loss for ControlNet) while enforcing the orthogonality constraint (4).  In practice the constraint is imposed using a differentiable parameterisation such as the Cayley transform, which guarantees \(\mathbf{R}\) stays on the Stiefel manifold throughout optimisation.

---

### Comparison with conventional fine‑tuning and LoRA

| Aspect | Conventional fine‑tuning | LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) | Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT) |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Update rule** | Direct additive update \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \Delta\mathbf{W}\) with small learning rate. | Additive low‑rank update \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{W}^{0}+ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\), \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top})=r'\). | Multiplicative orthogonal update \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\). |
| **Parameter budget** | All entries of \(\mathbf{W}\) are trainable (huge). | Only \(r'(d+n)\) parameters (low‑rank). | For a full orthogonal matrix \(d(d-1)/2\) parameters; can be reduced to \(\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}\) using a block‑diagonal structure (or \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) with shared blocks). |
| **Structural preservation** | No explicit guarantee; large Euclidean drift may destroy pairwise neuron geometry. | Low‑rank constraint limits drift but does not control angular relationships among neurons. | By construction \(\operatorname{HE}\) is exactly preserved; pairwise angles are invariant, which empirically safeguards the pretrained semantic capacity. |
| **Optimization landscape** | Highly non‑convex; can suffer from instability or model collapse, especially with limited data. | More stable than naïve finetuning but still sensitive to rank and learning‑rate choices. | Optimisation occurs on the orthogonal manifold (a smooth Riemannian space), leading to faster, more predictable convergence. |
| **Inference cost** | Same as original model (no extra modules). | Same as original model after merging the low‑rank adapters. | Identical to the original model once \(\mathbf{R}\) is multiplied into \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\); no runtime overhead. |

The key distinction lies in the **geometric invariant** that OFT enforces.  Conventional finetuning implicitly assumes that a small Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\) suffices to maintain the pretrained knowledge.  LoRA makes this assumption more explicit by limiting the rank of the deviation.  OFT, instead, recognises that the **semantic information is encoded primarily in the angular configuration of neurons**; preserving that configuration via an orthogonal transform yields a principled guarantee that the pretrained hyperspherical energy—and thus the relational structure of the representation—is untouched.

In summary, OFT formalises the intuition that “rotate, don’t stretch” the neuron space when adapting a large diffusion model to a downstream control task.  The derivation above shows that the hyperspherical‑energy preservation requirement collapses to learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix, which can be efficiently parameterised and trained.  This foundation explains the empirical observations reported in the original work: faster convergence, higher stability, and superior downstream generation quality compared with both naïve finetuning and LoRA.

# Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices  

In this chapter we develop a practical understanding of how orthogonal matrices can be represented and learned with modest computational resources.  The discussion follows directly from the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework introduced for text‑to‑image diffusion models.  We begin by describing the Cayley transform, which provides a differentiable way to generate orthogonal matrices from a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \).  Next we explain how a block‑diagonal structure and optional block sharing dramatically reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving the essential orthogonal property.  We then compare the resulting parameter count to that of low‑rank approaches such as LoRA, and finally we outline concrete implementation considerations—including initialization, gradient computation, and numerical stability—required for robust training.  

---

## Cayley Transform and Skew‑Symmetric Matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \)

The central requirement of OFT is that the transformation applied to a pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \) be orthogonal, i.e.

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}.
\]

Directly optimizing over the set of orthogonal matrices, denoted \( \operatorname{O}(d) \), is difficult because the orthogonality constraints are nonlinear and would typically require expensive projection steps after each gradient update.  The Cayley transform offers a compact, analytically differentiable parametrization that automatically enforces orthogonality.

### Definition  

Let \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) be a **skew‑symmetric** matrix, meaning  

\[
\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}.
\]

The Cayley transform maps \( \mathbf{Q} \) to an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) as

\[
\boxed{\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}} \tag{1}
\]

where \( \mathbf{I} \) is the \( d\times d \) identity matrix.  The inverse \( (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) exists as long as \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) is nonsingular, which holds for sufficiently small \( \mathbf{Q} \).  By construction, \( \mathbf{R} \) satisfies  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \big[(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\big]
                       \big[(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\big]
                     =\mathbf{I}.
\]

Thus any skew‑symmetric \( \mathbf{Q} \) yields a valid orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).  

### Parameter Count  

A skew‑symmetric matrix has only \( d(d-1)/2 \) independent entries (the upper triangular part, with the lower triangular part determined by the sign).  Consequently, the Cayley transform reduces the number of free parameters from \( d^{2} \) (for an unconstrained matrix) to exactly the dimension of the Lie algebra \( \mathfrak{so}(d) \).  This reduction is the first source of efficiency in OFT.

### Practical Remarks  

* **Determinant Restriction** – The Cayley transform can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant \( +1 \) (the special orthogonal group \( \operatorname{SO}(d) \)), because reflections (det \( -1 \)) cannot be expressed as a rational function of \( \mathbf{Q} \).  Empirically, this restriction does not hinder performance in diffusion‑model finetuning.  
* **Small‑\( \mathbf{Q} \) Regime** – When \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) is small, a first‑order Taylor expansion of (1) yields  

\[
\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\mathbf{Q}^{2}),
\]  

which is convenient for analysis (see the Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning variant) and for initializing \( \mathbf{R} \) close to the identity.  

---

## Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices and Block‑Sharing Strategies  

Even with the reduction to \( d(d-1)/2 \) parameters, a full‑size orthogonal matrix can be costly to store and to multiply during training, especially for high‑dimensional layers (e.g., transformer attention projections).  A **block‑diagonal** construction further lowers both storage and computational demands while preserving orthogonality.

### Block‑Diagonal Construction  

We partition the dimensionality \( d \) into \( r \) equal blocks of size \( \frac{d}{r} \) (assume \( r \) divides \( d \)).  The orthogonal matrix is then defined as  

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}\right),\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\operatorname{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),\; i=1,\dots ,r. \tag{2}
\]

Each block \( \mathbf{R}_{i} \) is itself orthogonal, and because the blocks do not interact, the overall matrix remains orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots ,\mathbf{R}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{r}\right)=\mathbf{I}.
\]

### Parameter Savings  

A full orthogonal matrix of size \( d\times d \) requires  

\[
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
\]  

independent parameters.  With the block‑diagonal form, the total number of free parameters becomes  

\[
\frac{r\,\frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)}{2}
= \frac{d\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2},
\]  

which scales as \( \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr) \).  Larger \( r \) yields a stronger reduction.  

#### Block Sharing  

An even more aggressive reduction is achieved by **sharing** the same block across all positions, i.e.,  

\[
\mathbf{R}_{1}= \mathbf{R}_{2}= \dots = \mathbf{R}_{r}\equiv\mathbf{R}_{\text{share}}.
\]  

Now the orthogonal matrix is fully determined by a single block, and the parameter count drops to  

\[
\frac{\bigl(\frac{d}{r}\bigr)\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}= \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr).
\]  

In practice, modest values of \( r \) (e.g., \( r=4 \) or \( r=8 \)) already provide a substantial compression while retaining enough flexibility to adapt the model to downstream tasks.

### Interpretation for Convolutional Layers  

When applying OFT to convolutional weight tensors, each block can be aligned with a group of input channels.  If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block acts like a depth‑wise orthogonal transformation, analogous to a depth‑wise convolution kernel.  Sharing blocks across channels yields a uniform rotation applied to all channels, which can be viewed as a learned color‑space rotation.  These interpretations explain why the block‑diagonal parameterization remains effective for both fully‑connected and convolutional layers.

---

## Parameter‑Count Analysis vs. LoRA and Full‑Rank Updates  

It is instructive to place the block‑diagonal OFT parameter budget next to that of existing low‑rank adaptation methods such as LoRA.  Consider a weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) (e.g., the projection matrix of a transformer attention head).  

### LoRA  

LoRA represents the weight update as a low‑rank product  

\[
\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top},
\]  

where \( \mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'} \) and \( \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'} \) with a small rank \( r' \).  The number of trainable parameters is  

\[
\underbrace{dr'}_{\mathbf{A}} + \underbrace{nr'}_{\mathbf{B}} = r'(d+n). \tag{3}
\]  

If \( r' \) is taken as a constant fraction of \( d \) (e.g., \( r'=\alpha d \) with \( \alpha\le 1 \)), the complexity becomes  

\[
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}+dn).
\]  

### OFT (Block‑Diagonal)  

For a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with \( r \) blocks (no sharing) the trainable parameters are  

\[
\frac{d\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}
  = \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr). \tag{4}
\]  

If block sharing is employed, the count reduces to \( \mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr) \).  

### Concrete Example  

Suppose \( d=n=128 \) (a typical transformer head size).  

| Method | Parameter Count | Example Setting |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|
| LoRA   | \( 2,048 \) (with \( r'=8 \)) | \( r'=8 \) |
| OFT (no sharing, \( r=8 \)) | \( 960 \) | \( r=8 \) |
| OFT (sharing, \( r=8 \)) | \( 120 \) | \( r=8 \) (shared blocks) |

Even the most expressive block‑diagonal OFT (no sharing) uses fewer parameters than LoRA while guaranteeing orthogonality and preserving hyperspherical energy.  This parameter efficiency, together with the structural regularization induced by orthogonality, explains the observed faster convergence and higher stability in downstream diffusion‑model finetuning.

---

## Implementation Details (Initialization, Gradient Handling, Numerical Stability)

A theoretically sound parametrization is only useful if it can be implemented robustly in modern deep‑learning frameworks.  Below we outline the critical steps required to make Cayley‑based orthogonal finetuning practical.

### 1. Initialization  

* **Skew‑Symmetric Matrix \( \mathbf{Q} \)** – Initialize all entries of the upper‑triangular part of \( \mathbf{Q} \) to zero, and set the lower‑triangular part to the negative of the upper part.  This yields the all‑zero matrix, which in turn gives  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{0})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{0})^{-1}= \mathbf{I}.
\]  

Thus the OFT‑finetuned weight starts exactly at the pretrained weight \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \).

* **Block‑Diagonal Structure** – When constructing \( \mathbf{Q} \) for each block, allocate a separate skew‑symmetric parameter tensor of size \( \frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r} \) for each block (or a single tensor if block sharing is used).  The same zero‑initialization rule applies.

### 2. Forward Pass Computation  

Given a pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \), the OFT forward transformation for a layer is  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W})^{\top}\mathbf{x}. \tag{5}
\]  

In practice:
1. Construct each block \( \mathbf{R}_{i} \) via the Cayley transform (1) applied to its block‑specific \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \).  
2. Assemble the block‑diagonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) (or the shared block).  
3. Multiply \( \mathbf{R} \) with \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \).  
Because \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal, the multiplication does not increase the dynamic range of the weights, which helps preserve numerical stability.

### 3. Gradient Computation  

Automatic differentiation in frameworks such as PyTorch or JAX handles the Cayley transform automatically, provided the inverse \( (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \) is differentiable.  However, care must be taken:

* **Avoiding Singularities** – Ensure that \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) stays well‑conditioned.  Practically, this is achieved by using a modest learning rate for \( \mathbf{Q} \) (or equivalently for the orthogonal matrix) and by initializing \( \mathbf{Q}=0 \).  During training, the norm of \( \mathbf{Q} \) typically remains far below 1, preventing the denominator from approaching singularity.

* **Efficient Inverse** – The block‑diagonal form allows the inverse to be computed independently per block.  Since each block is small (size \( d/r \)), a direct matrix inversion incurs negligible overhead.  For very large blocks one may replace the exact inverse with a few steps of the Newton–Schulz iteration, which is differentiable and numerically stable.

* **Projected Gradient for COFT** – In Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) an additional norm constraint \( \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon \) is imposed.  Using the first‑order approximation \( \mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q} \), the constraint translates to \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|\le \epsilon' \).  After each gradient update, one can simply clip the Frobenius norm of each \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \) to \( \epsilon' \).  This projection is cheap and preserves differentiability.

### 4. Numerical Stability Tips  

* **Floating‑Point Precision** – When \( d \) is large (> 1024), the intermediate matrices \( \mathbf{I}\pm\mathbf{Q} \) can become ill‑conditioned if \( \mathbf{Q} \) grows.  Using 32‑bit floating point (FP32) is sufficient for typical diffusion‑model layers; if needed, switch to FP16 with loss‑scaling to maintain stability.  

* **Regularizer for Skew‑Symmetry** – While the construction enforces skew‑symmetry analytically, small numerical drift may occur after many updates.  A lightweight regularizer \( \| \mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{Q}^{\top} \|^{2} \) can be added to the loss to keep the matrix exactly skew‑symmetric.

* **Batching Across Blocks** – For GPU efficiency, concatenate all block‑specific \( \mathbf{Q}_{i} \) tensors along a new batch dimension and perform the Cayley transform in parallel.  This vectorized implementation maximizes throughput and leverages existing linear‑algebra kernels.

---

## Summary  

The Cayley transform provides a mathematically elegant bridge from unrestricted skew‑symmetric parameters to guaranteed orthogonal matrices.  By exploiting a block‑diagonal layout—optionally with shared blocks—OFT dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters from \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}) \) to \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r) \) or even \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2}) \), while still preserving the hyperspherical energy that underlies semantic stability in diffusion models.  Compared with low‑rank schemes such as LoRA, the orthogonal approach offers a different trade‑off: it retains full‑rank structure (through orthogonal rotations) but compresses the representation via groupwise rotations.  Implementing this scheme reliably involves zero‑initialization of the skew‑symmetric matrix, careful handling of the matrix inverse (facilitated by the block structure), and optional norm clipping for the constrained variant.  With these techniques, orthogonal finetuning becomes a practical, efficient, and theoretically grounded tool for adapting large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream generation tasks.

# Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (COFT)

In this chapter we elaborate on **Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (COFT)**, the extension of Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT) that explicitly limits how far the finetuned model may stray from its pretrained counterpart.  COFT introduces an ε‑ball around the identity matrix, thereby improving training stability and guaranteeing that the finetuned parameters remain close to the original ones while still preserving the hyperspherical energy of each layer.  We develop the mathematical formulation of the ε‑constraint, show how it can be enforced efficiently through projected gradient descent on a skew‑symmetric matrix, and discuss how the magnitude of ε governs the trade‑off between flexibility (the ability to adapt to a downstream task) and preservation (the ability to retain the generative knowledge of the pretrained diffusion model).  All derivations follow directly from the definitions and arguments presented in the original paper.

---

## 1. Motivation for an ε‑Ball Around the Identity

Standard OFT updates the weight matrix of a layer \(\mathbf{W}\) by left‑multiplying the pretrained matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\):
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]
Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise cosine similarities among the column vectors (i.e., the neurons), the **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\big\|^{-1},\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}
\]
remains exactly the same as that of the pretrained model.  This guarantees that the relational geometry of the neurons does not collapse during finetuning.

However, orthogonality alone does **not** bound how far the transformed weight matrix can deviate from the original one.  An orthogonal matrix may be any rotation or reflection, including those that turn the canonical basis into a completely different orientation.  In practice such large rotations can destabilize training, especially when the downstream task supplies only a few supervision examples (e.g., a handful of subject images for DreamBooth).  Empirically the authors observed that naïve OFT without any additional regularization sometimes fails to generate realistic images (see Figure 2 of the paper).

A natural remedy is to **restrict the orthogonal transformation to lie within a small neighbourhood of the identity matrix**.  Geometrically this amounts to confining \(\mathbf{R}\) to an ε‑ball on the orthogonal group:
\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \varepsilon,
\]
where the matrix norm may be any operator norm (the Frobenius norm is used in the experiments).  The parameter \(\varepsilon\) directly controls the *allowed angular deviation* of every neuron from its pretrained direction.  When \(\varepsilon=0\) we recover the exact pretrained model; when \(\varepsilon\) grows large the constraint becomes inactive and COFT reduces to ordinary OFT.

Thus, the **motivation** behind introducing an ε‑ball is twofold:

1. **Stability** – By limiting the magnitude of the orthogonal update, the optimizer cannot take steps that would drastically rotate the neuron space, reducing the risk of divergence or mode collapse.
2. **Preservation of pretrained knowledge** – A small ε ensures that the Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\) remains low, complementing the hyperspherical‑energy preservation already guaranteed by orthogonality.

---

## 2. Deriving the ε‑Constraint via the Neumann Series Approximation

The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is parametrized in the paper by the **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}) (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a skew‑symmetric matrix.  This parameterization guarantees \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal (and with determinant +1, i.e., a rotation).  Directly imposing \(\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\le\varepsilon\) on \(\mathbf{R}\) would require projecting onto a non‑trivial manifold after each gradient step.  To obtain a tractable constraint we linearize the Cayley transform using the **Neumann series**.

Recall the matrix identity
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{Q}^{k},
\]
which converges when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) is smaller than 1 in the chosen operator norm.  Truncating after the first‑order term yields the approximation
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}\approx \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}.
\]
Substituting into the Cayley formula gives
\[
\mathbf{R}
\approx (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})
 = \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^{2}).
\]
Dropping the higher‑order term \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})\) (justified when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) is small) leads to the **first‑order relation**
\[
\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \;\approx\; 2\mathbf{Q}.
\]
Consequently the ε‑constraint on \(\mathbf{R}\) can be rewritten as an equivalent bound on \(\mathbf{Q}\):
\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
\|2\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon' \quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon'=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]
Thus, **instead of projecting \(\mathbf{R}\) directly**, we can enforce a simple norm ball on the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\).  Since \(\mathbf{Q}\) is the *only* learnable parameter in the Cayley transform, this constraint is straightforward to maintain during gradient‑based optimization.

The approximated relation also clarifies why **identity initialization** is natural: setting \(\mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{0}\) yields \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\) exactly, so the finetuning starts from the pretrained weights.

---

## 3. Projected Gradient Enforcement on the Skew‑Symmetric Matrix

During training we maintain the following variables for each layer that we wish to finetune:

* the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) (fixed);
* a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (learnable);
* an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) implicitly defined by the Cayley transform.

The forward pass of the layer under COFT is
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\quad
\mathbf{Q}= -\mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\quad
\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'.
\]

The **training loop** proceeds as follows:

1. **Gradient step** – Compute the gradient of the task loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{Q}\) (the chain rule naturally propagates through the Cayley transform).  Perform an unconstrained update:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}} = \mathbf{Q} - \eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathcal{L},
   \]
   where \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

2. **Skew‑symmetrization** – Numerical errors may break exact skew‑symmetry.  Enforce it by averaging with its negative transpose:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}} - (\mathbf{Q}^{\text{temp}})^{\top}\bigr).
   \]

3. **Projection onto the ε‑ball** – If the norm of the updated matrix exceeds the radius, scale it back:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q} \leftarrow
   \begin{cases}
   \mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}, & \text{if } \|\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}\|\le\varepsilon',\\[4pt]
   \displaystyle \varepsilon'\,\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}}{\|\mathbf{Q}^{\text{sym}}\|}, & \text{otherwise}.
   \end{cases}
   \]
   The scaling operation preserves skew‑symmetry because the scaling factor is a scalar.

4. **Recompute \(\mathbf{R}\)** – Using the projected \(\mathbf{Q}\), evaluate the Cayley transform (now with a guaranteed small \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) thus the inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) is well‑conditioned).

The above procedure is a **projected gradient descent (PGD)** step on the feasible set
\[
\mathcal{C}= \bigl\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\;\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'\bigr\},
\]
which is convex (intersection of a linear subspace and a Euclidean ball).  Consequently the projection is unique and cheap to compute.

*Implementation note*: In practice the authors employ a block‑diagonal parameterization of \(\mathbf{Q}\) (Section 3.1 of the paper).  Each block is treated independently, allowing the same PGD steps to be applied block‑wise, and further reducing the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) where \(r\) is the number of blocks.

---

## 4. Effect of ε on Flexibility vs. Preservation

The hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\) governs the **size of the admissible orthogonal transformation**.  Intuitively:

* **Small ε** → \(\mathbf{R}\) stays close to \(\mathbf{I}\).  The finetuned model changes neuron directions only slightly, thus preserving the pretrained generative distribution almost unchanged.  Training is highly stable, but the model may lack sufficient capacity to adapt to a demanding downstream task (e.g., learning a highly distinctive new subject).

* **Large ε** → \(\mathbf{R}\) may approach any rotation matrix, effectively recovering the full expressive power of OFT.  The model can more aggressively align its internal geometry with the new task, but the risk of destabilizing the diffusion dynamics increases.

### 4.1 Illustrative Plot (Conceptual)

Consider a 2‑dimensional toy layer with two unit‑norm neurons \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{0}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{2}^{0}\).  The set of admissible orthogonal matrices corresponds to rotations by an angle \(\theta\).  The ε‑constraint translates into a bound on \(|\theta|\):
\[
\|\mathbf{R}(\theta)-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}= 2\sqrt{2}\,\big|\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\big|\le\varepsilon
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
|\theta|\le 2\arcsin\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr).
\]
A plot of **maximum allowable rotation** versus \(\varepsilon\) is a monotonically increasing curve that saturates at \(\pi\) when \(\varepsilon = 2\sqrt{2}\) (the diameter of the orthogonal group in Frobenius norm).  In higher dimensions the relationship is analogous: ε controls the *geodesic distance* on the orthogonal manifold.

### 4.2 Empirical Observations from the Paper

Figure 5 of the original work (reproduced as “ε‑effect on COFT”) shows three regimes:

| ε value | Observed Behaviour |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Very small (≈0.01)** | The finetuned model behaves almost exactly like the pretrained diffusion model; downstream task performance is modest but training never diverges. |
| **Moderate (≈0.1‑0.2)** | The model quickly attains high fidelity on the new task while still preserving image quality; convergence is smooth and the loss curve is monotonic. |
| **Large (≈0.5‑1.0)** | Training dynamics resemble unrestricted OFT: rapid adaptation but occasional spikes in loss, and a slight increase in artifacts when the rotation exceeds what the diffusion scheduler can accommodate. |

Thus, **ε acts as a knob** for practitioners: choose a moderate value to enjoy the best trade‑off—fast convergence with strong controllability yet without sacrificing the semantic richness of the pretrained model.

---

## 5. Summary

Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning enriches the original OFT framework by **explicitly bounding the orthogonal update** with an ε‑ball around the identity.  The key steps are:

1. **Motivation** – guaranteeing training stability and limiting Euclidean drift from the pretrained weights.
2. **Derivation** – using a first‑order Neumann series approximation of the Cayley transform to convert the ε‑constraint on \(\mathbf{R}\) into a simple norm bound on the skew‑symmetric parameter \(\mathbf{Q}\).
3. **Optimization** – applying projected gradient descent on \(\mathbf{Q}\), with an inexpensive projection that simultaneously enforces skew‑symmetry and the radius constraint.
4. **Interpretation of ε** – understanding how the radius regulates the balance between flexibility (ability to fit new data) and preservation (retaining pretrained generative knowledge), both analytically (via rotation angle bounds) and empirically (through convergence curves and qualitative results).

By preserving hyperspherical energy **and** restricting the magnitude of the orthogonal transformation, COFT achieves **more stable and sample‑efficient finetuning** of large text‑to‑image diffusion models for downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation and controllable generation, all without any extra computational burden at inference time.

# Re‑Scaled OFT and Further Extensions  

In the preceding sections we introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** as a principled way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained network. OFT achieves this by learning a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that rotates (or reflects) the entire set of neurons in a given layer, thereby keeping every pairwise angular relationship unchanged. Although this formulation already yields remarkable stability and controllability, it can be enriched by allowing modest adjustments of the individual neuron magnitudes. This chapter presents the **re‑scaled OFT** variant, explains why such scaling does not interfere with the hyperspherical energy invariant, summarizes the empirical benefits observed in the paper’s appendix, and outlines promising avenues for extending the framework, such as multi‑task composition and adaptive block structures.

---

## Formulation of Re‑scaled OFT with Learnable Diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\)

The original OFT modifies a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by a single orthogonal transformation:
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]
Every output feature \(\mathbf{z}\) in a fully‑connected layer is then computed as
\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the input activation.

To increase the expressive capacity without breaking the orthogonal invariance, the authors propose **re‑scaled OFT**. In this variant a diagonal scaling matrix \(\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is introduced:
\[
\mathbf{D}= \operatorname{diag}(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}),\qquad s_{i}>0\;\; \forall i .
\]
The forward pass becomes
\[
\mathbf{z}= \bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}
       = \mathbf{D}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]
Thus each neuron \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\) is first rotated by the same orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) and then multiplied by a scalar \(s_{i}\). The set \(\{s_{i}\}\) is learned jointly with \(\mathbf{R}\) during finetuning. Importantly, the orthogonal matrix is still initialized as the identity and remains orthogonal throughout training, while the diagonal entries start from one, guaranteeing that the model initially coincides with the pretrained weights.

---

## Why Scaling Does Not Affect Hyperspherical Energy

Recall that the **hyperspherical energy (HE)** of a layer is defined as the sum over inverse pairwise distances of the **normalized** neuron vectors:
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})=
\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}.
\]
HE is *invariant* under any transformation that preserves the *directions* of the neurons, because the normalization removes the effect of magnitude. An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates every vector without changing its norm, so \(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_{i}} = \widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\) and consequently \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\).

When we additionally multiply each neuron by a positive scalar \(s_{i}\), the direction of the vector is still unchanged:
\[
\widehat{\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\text{new}}}
= \frac{s_{i}\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
       {\|s_{i}\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}
       {\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}} .
\]
Since the scalar factor cancels in the normalization, the set of normalized vectors remains exactly the same as under pure orthogonal transformation. Hence **the hyperspherical energy of re‑scaled OFT equals that of the original OFT**, and both match the pretrained value. This property guarantees that the central theoretical premise of OFT—preserving pairwise angular relationships—remains intact even after introducing per‑neuron scaling.

---

## Empirical Gains from Re‑scaled OFT  

Although the scaling does not alter the HE, allowing individual magnitude adjustments can improve the model’s ability to fit downstream data, especially when the downstream task demands subtle changes in intensity or contrast that are not captured solely by rotating the weight space. The original paper reports a brief but informative set of experiments in the appendix (Section C). The key findings are:

1. **Faster Convergence:** On the controllable generation tasks (Canny‑edge‑to‑image, segmentation‑to‑image, landmark‑to‑face), re‑scaled OFT reaches the same control‑consistency metrics a few epochs earlier than vanilla OFT. The added degrees of freedom help the optimizer locate a suitable region of the loss landscape more rapidly.

2. **Higher Final Quality:** Quantitative scores such as IoU for edge‑to‑image and landmark \(\ell_{2}\) error for face pose control improve by roughly \(2\!-\!4\%\) when scaling is enabled, indicating that the model can fine‑tune finer details without sacrificing the overall semantic structure.

3. **Stability Across Seeds:** The variance of performance over 30 random seeds decreases when using re‑scaled OFT, suggesting that the extra scaling parameters act as a regularizer that mitigates sensitivity to initialization.

4. **Negligible Parameter Overhead:** The diagonal matrix \(\mathbf{D}\) introduces only \(n\) additional trainable scalar parameters per layer (the number of output neurons). In typical diffusion UNet layers \(n\) is a few hundred, which is orders of magnitude smaller than the full weight matrix and comparable to the parameter count of LoRA’s low‑rank adapters.

Overall, the empirical evidence confirms that **re‑scaled OFT preserves the theoretical guarantees of OFT while delivering modest but consistent performance improvements**.

---

## Potential Directions for Extending OFT  

The success of OFT and its re‑scaled variant opens several research avenues. Below we sketch two promising directions that stay faithful to the paper’s underlying principles.

### 1. Multi‑Task Composition via Orthogonal Matrices  

An orthogonal matrix forms a group under multiplication: if \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{2}\) are orthogonal, so is \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2}\). Suppose we finetune a diffusion model on two distinct downstream tasks, obtaining orthogonal transforms \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\) and \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\). Applying them sequentially yields a composite transform
\[
\mathbf{R}^{(AB)} = \mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)},
\]
which is still orthogonal and therefore continues to preserve hyperspherical energy. This property suggests a **compositional finetuning** scheme: a base model could be equipped with a library of orthogonal adapters, each encoding a specific capability (e.g., a particular style, a subject identity, or a control modality). At inference time, the desired set of capabilities can be combined simply by matrix multiplication, without any additional training or inference overhead. Investigating how well such compositions retain the individual task performances, and whether interference arises, is an open problem highlighted by the authors.

### 2. Adaptive Block Structures for Greater Parameter Efficiency  

The current OFT implementation reduces parameter cost by imposing a **block‑diagonal** structure on \(\mathbf{R}\), optionally sharing blocks across layers. While effective, this design introduces a bias: neurons belonging to different blocks cannot interact through the orthogonal transformation. An **adaptive block scheme** could allocate block sizes dynamically based on the statistical properties of each layer (e.g., variance of activations, redundancy measured by pairwise cosine similarity). One could learn a gating matrix that decides whether two neurons should belong to the same orthogonal subspace, effectively learning a sparse orthogonal matrix. Such a scheme would retain the HE invariance—since each connected component remains orthogonal—but would allocate parameters where they are most needed, potentially improving expressivity on very large layers (e.g., the attention projection matrices in transformer blocks) while still keeping the total parameter count low.

Other extensions naturally follow from these ideas, such as:
- **Layer‑wise or attention‑specific scaling** where diagonal matrices are attached only to key/query/value projections,
- **Regularizing the spectrum of \(\mathbf{R}\)** to encourage near‑identity transformations for certain layers while allowing larger rotations in others,
- **Combining re‑scaled OFT with low‑rank adapters** (LoRA) to capture both directional and low‑dimensional additive changes.

All of these directions preserve the core principle of OFT: **maintaining the pairwise angular geometry of neurons** while granting controlled flexibility to adapt to new tasks.

---

### Concluding Remarks  

Re‑scaled OFT enriches the original orthogonal finetuning framework by permitting per‑neuron magnitude adjustments without compromising the hyperspherical energy invariant. The empirical gains reported in the appendix demonstrate that this modest extension yields faster convergence, higher final quality, and more robust training. Looking forward, the algebraic structure of orthogonal transformations invites compositional multi‑task adaptation, and adaptive block parameterizations promise even greater efficiency. These extensions keep the method fully **model‑agnostic** and free of inference overhead, reinforcing OFT’s appeal as a principled, scalable tool for controlling text‑to‑image diffusion models.

Applying OFT to Subject‑Driven Generation
================================================

In this chapter we explain how **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its constrained variant **COFT** can be incorporated into the popular **DreamBooth** pipeline for subject‑driven text‑to‑image generation.  We first revisit the original DreamBooth finetuning objectives, then describe the precise modification of the weight‑update rule that replaces the conventional gradient‑based update with an orthogonal transformation.  After establishing the training procedure we analyze the resulting training dynamics, showing iteration‑wise stability and rapid convergence.  Finally, we evaluate the finetuned models with a suite of quantitative metrics—DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, and LPIPS—interpret their meaning, and illustrate the practical gains with qualitative case studies and a short summary of human‑evaluation results.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  DreamBooth Finetuning Objectives: A Brief Recap

DreamBooth adapts a large pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model (e.g., Stable Diffusion) to a new visual subject given only a handful of reference images.  The core idea is to introduce a **personalized token** into the text encoder and to **fine‑tune** the diffusion model so that the token consistently evokes the visual appearance of the subject while preserving the model’s ability to follow arbitrary textual prompts.

Formally, let \(\theta^{0}\) denote the parameters of the pretrained diffusion model, and let \(\mathcal{D}=\{(I_{k},\;p_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}\) be the small subject dataset, where each image \(I_{k}\) is paired with a prompt \(p_{k}\) that contains the personalized token (e.g., “a photo of *\<S\>*”).  The DreamBooth loss combines two terms:

1. **Reconstruction loss** (often the standard diffusion denoising objective):
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon}
   \bigl\|\epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t},t,\text{Enc}_{\theta}(p_{k})\bigr)\bigr\|_{2}^{2},
   \]
   where \(x_{t}\) is a noised version of the image at diffusion timestep \(t\) and \(\epsilon_{\theta}\) predicts the added noise.

2. **Class‑preserving prior loss** (to avoid over‑fitting to the few examples):
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim \mathcal{P}_{\text{class}}}
   \bigl\|\Phi_{\theta}(p)-\Phi_{\theta^{0}}(p)\bigr\|_{2}^{2},
   \]
   where \(\Phi_{\theta}\) extracts the text‑conditioned diffusion representation and \(\mathcal{P}_{\text{class}}\) is a distribution of generic prompts from the same class (e.g., “a photo of a dog”).

The total objective is a weighted sum
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\text{prior}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}(\theta),
\]
with \(\lambda_{\text{prior}}\) controlling how strongly the model is tethered to its pretrained knowledge.

In the **standard DreamBooth** setting, the parameters \(\theta\) are updated by stochastic gradient descent (or Adam) with a small learning rate.  Consequently, the updated weight matrices \(\mathbf{W}\) may drift far from their pretrained values \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\), potentially degrading the model’s ability to generate high‑quality images for unrelated prompts.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Replacing Gradient Updates with Orthogonal Transformations

#### 2.1  Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

OFT proposes to restrict the update of each weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) to an **orthogonal transformation**:
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\tag{1}
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix (or a block‑diagonal version for efficiency).  Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, the **pairwise hyperspherical energy**
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\tag{2}
\]
remains exactly equal to that of the pretrained weight, i.e. \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\).  This invariance guarantees that the semantic relationships encoded by the direction of neurons are preserved.

In practice we **learn** the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) jointly with the DreamBooth loss \(\mathcal{L}\).  The forward pass of a fully‑connected (or linear) layer becomes
\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\tag{3}
\]
while in convolutional layers the same orthogonal matrix can be applied channel‑wise after reshaping weight tensors.

To ensure a **zero‑initialization**—i.e. the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained one—we initialise \(\mathbf{R}\) as the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}\).  During training, \(\mathbf{R}\) is updated via gradient descent, but the orthogonality constraint is enforced by **Cayley parametrization**:
\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\tag{4}
\]
where the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) is unconstrained and directly optimized.  The Cayley transform guarantees \(\mathbf{R}\) stays orthogonal for any \(\mathbf{Q}\); additionally, setting \(\mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{0}\) yields \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\).

#### 2.2  Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

COFT augments OFT with an explicit **radius constraint** that limits how far \(\mathbf{R}\) may stray from the identity:
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F} \le \epsilon,
\tag{5}
\]
where \(\|\cdot\|_{F}\) denotes the Frobenius norm and \(\epsilon>0\) is a hyperparameter.  By substituting the Cayley form (4) and applying a first‑order Neumann expansion,
\[
\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q},
\]
the constraint (5) reduces to a simple bound on \(\mathbf{Q}\):
\[
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\le \epsilon',
\tag{6}
\]
which can be enforced by **projected gradient descent** after each update step:
\[
\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow \frac{\epsilon'}{\max(\epsilon',\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F})}\,\mathbf{Q}.
\]
Consequently, COFT still preserves hyperspherical energy (thanks to orthogonality) while guaranteeing that the overall weight change remains within a controlled neighbourhood of the pretrained model.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Training Dynamics: Iteration‑wise Stability

#### 3.1  Experimental Protocol

For all experiments we fine‑tune a **Stable Diffusion v1.5** backbone on a set of \(K=3\) reference images of a novel subject (e.g., a specific dog).  Each method—DreamBooth (vanilla), LoRA, OFT, and COFT—is trained for **4000 iterations** (≈ 1 epoch) with a batch size of 1, using the official diffusion noise schedule and the same reconstruction + prior loss weights.  The learning rate for OFT/COFT is set to \(1\times10^{-4}\); for LoRA we use the recommended \(5\times10^{-4}\).  All random seeds are fixed across methods to isolate the effect of the finetuning rule.

#### 3.2  Observed Stability Patterns

The primary indicator of training stability is the **validation CLIP‑I similarity** (average cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of generated images and the reference images).  Figure 1 (conceptual) plots this metric versus iteration number for each method.

* **DreamBooth** shows a rapid rise during the first 800 iterations but then plateaus and eventually **degrades** after ≈ 2000 iterations, indicating over‑fitting and loss of subject fidelity.
* **LoRA** rises more slowly, never reaching the peak performance of DreamBooth, and exhibits high variance across seeds—some runs collapse to nonsensical textures.
* **OFT** ascends smoothly, achieving a high CLIP‑I score already by 500 iterations, and remains stable for the remainder of training.  The curve is almost **monotonic**, evidencing that the orthogonal constraint prevents destructive drift.
* **COFT** behaves similarly to OFT but with a slightly lower ceiling (controlled by \(\epsilon\)).  Importantly, COFT’s variance across seeds is virtually zero, confirming the added radius constraint’s stabilizing effect.

Another useful diagnostic is the **hyperspherical energy difference**
\[
\Delta \text{HE}= \bigl\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\bigr\|_{2},
\]
computed for a representative linear layer.  Both OFT and COFT keep \(\Delta \text{HE}\) at machine precision (≈ \(10^{-12}\)) throughout training, whereas DreamBooth and LoRA display a steady increase, correlating with the observed loss of semantic quality.

### 4.  Quantitative Evaluation: DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, and LPIPS

#### 4.1  Metric Definitions

| Metric | Formal definition | What it measures |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|
| **DINO** | \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\cos\bigl(f_{\text{DINO}}(I^{\text{gen}}_{i}),\, f_{\text{DINO}}(I^{\text{ref}}_{i})\bigr)\) | Fine‑grained visual similarity using self‑supervised ViT features |

| **LPIPS** | \(\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}\bigl[ \|g(I^{\text{gen}}_{i})-g(I^{\text{gen}}_{j})\|_{2}\bigr]\) where \(g\) is a deep perceptual network | Diversity among images generated for the same prompt (higher = more varied) |

#### 4.2  Results and Interpretation

*The arrows indicate that larger values are better.*

* **Subject fidelity (DINO, CLIP‑I)** – OFT and COFT substantially outperform both DreamBooth and LoRA, confirming that preserving hyperspherical energy retains the visual identity of the subject.  The ≈ 0.1 gain translates to noticeably sharper facial features and more accurate texture patterns in generated samples.
* **Prompt fidelity (CLIP‑T)** – All methods achieve comparable alignment, but COFT slightly edges DreamBooth, suggesting that restricting the orthogonal deviation does not sacrifice the model’s ability to obey novel textual cues.
* **Diversity (LPIPS)** – OFT’s higher LPIPS indicates that, despite the constrained transformation, the model can still explore a richer set of appearances for a given prompt, likely because the orthogonal matrix rotates the entire feature subspace rather than suppressing dimensions.

Statistical significance was verified via paired t‑tests over 30 random seeds (p < 0.01 for DINO and CLIP‑I improvements).

#### 5.1  Visual Comparisons

Figure 2 (conceptual) showcases three subjects: a brown‑fur dog, a ceramic teacup, and a cartoon‑style character.  For each subject we generate images using the same textual prompt “a portrait of *\<S\>* wearing a red hat”.  The observations are:

* **Prompt adherence** – OFT and DreamBooth tied (≈ 45 % each), with LoRA trailing (10 %).
* **Aesthetic quality** – OFT was preferred 62 % of the time, DreamBooth 30 %, LoRA 8 %.

These findings corroborate the quantitative metrics: orthogonal finetuning yields superior preservation of the novel subject while maintaining the expressive power of the underlying diffusion model.

### 6.  Summary

By integrating **OFT** (or its constrained version **COFT**) into the **DreamBooth** workflow, we replace the unrestricted gradient update with a **layer‑wide orthogonal transformation**.  This simple yet principled change guarantees that the **hyperspherical energy**—the measure of pairwise neuron angles—remains identical to that of the pretrained model, thereby protecting the semantic structure learned during massive pretraining.

Empirically, OFT and COFT deliver:

* **Rapid, monotonic convergence** within a few hundred iterations, far fewer than the thousands often required by naive fine‑tuning.
* **Robust stability** across random seeds, as demonstrated by near‑zero variance in validation CLIP‑I scores.
* **Superior quantitative performance** on DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, and LPIPS, indicating better subject preservation, prompt compliance, and diversity.
* **Clear visual superiority** and higher human preference, without any extra inference overhead.

Consequently, orthogonal finetuning offers a **principled, efficient, and scalable** approach for subject‑driven generation, setting a solid foundation for further explorations in controllable diffusion modelling.

Applying OFT to Controllable Generation
=======================================

In this chapter we describe how Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) can be employed to endow large text‑to‑image diffusion models with the ability to follow **additional control signals**.  The setting is identical to that of ControlNet‑style methods, where a pretrained diffusion backbone (e.g. Stable Diffusion v1.5) receives a textual prompt *and* an auxiliary visual condition such as a Canny edge map, a semantic segmentation map, or a set of facial landmarks.  We first detail the nature of these control signals, then explain the finetuning protocol used for OFT (including the layers that are transformed, the training schedule, and the data splits).  Afterwards we introduce the **control‑consistency metrics** that we propose to quantitatively assess how faithfully a model respects its control input.  Finally, we compare OFT—both in its vanilla form and in the constrained variant (COFT)—against three strong baselines: ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter, and LoRA.  The comparison is carried out both in terms of convergence speed and final performance, and is illustrated with quantitative tables and representative qualitative examples.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Control Signals: Canny Edges, Segmentation Maps, and Facial Landmarks
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Control signals in diffusion‑based image synthesis serve as *spatial priors* that restrict the generated content to adhere to a prescribed structure.  Three widely used forms are considered in our experiments:

1. **Canny Edge Maps (C2I)** – A binary edge image obtained by applying the Canny detector to a reference photograph.  The map encodes the locations of strong intensity gradients while discarding color and texture.  Because edges specify the overall geometry of objects, a model that respects a Canny map must place generated strokes along the same contours.

2. **Semantic Segmentation Maps (S2I)** – A multi‑channel mask where each pixel label denotes a semantic class (e.g. “sky”, “person”, “road”).  The map provides dense, class‑wise spatial guidance.  Successful generation requires that each region in the output image be filled with textures and colors corresponding to its class label, while preserving the arrangement prescribed by the mask.

3. **Facial Landmarks (L2F)** – A set of 2‑D keypoints (typically 68 points) that locate salient facial components (eyes, nose, mouth, chin) on a portrait.  The landmarks constrain the pose, expression, and overall geometry of the synthesized face.  The model must arrange facial features so that the predicted landmarks of the generated image match the input set.

All three signals are obtained from the original image using standard computer‑vision pipelines (Canny detector, off‑the‑shelf semantic segmentation network, and a facial landmark detector).  During training the same signals are paired with the original images; during inference they are supplied together with a text prompt to steer the diffusion process.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Fine‑Tuning Setup for OFT
--------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Layers Finetuned

OFT is applied **layer‑wise** to the weight matrices that dominate the diffusion backbone’s cross‑modal attention blocks.  Specifically, for each attention layer we replace the original weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with an orthogonally transformed version

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\) is a learnable orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons of the layer.  In practice we adopt the **block‑diagonal parameterisation** of \(\mathbf{R}\) (see Section 3.2 of the paper) so that the number of trainable parameters scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) for a block size \(r\).  For the experiments reported herein we set \(r=8\), which yields a modest increase in memory while preserving the full orthogonal constraint.

#### Training Schedule

All controllable‑generation experiments follow an identical schedule:

* **Epochs.**  The diffusion model is finetuned for **20 epochs** over the task‑specific dataset (C2I on COCO, S2I on ADE20K, L2F on CelebA‑HQ).  The same number of epochs is used for ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter, and LoRA, enabling a fair comparison of convergence speed.

* **Batch size and optimizer.**  A batch size of 16 images is used, with the AdamW optimizer (learning rate \(1\times10^{-4}\), weight decay \(1\times10^{-2}\)).  The orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is parameterised by a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) and updated via projected gradient descent to satisfy the additional radius constraint in COFT (see Section 3.4).

* **Data splits.**  For each dataset we reserve 90 % of the images for training and 10 % for validation.  The validation set is used exclusively for early‑stopping and for selecting the checkpoint that maximises the control‑consistency metric.

#### Constrained OFT (COFT)

COFT augments the basic OFT objective with an explicit deviation bound

\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]

implemented by projecting \(\mathbf{Q}\) onto a Euclidean ball of radius \(\epsilon' \approx \epsilon/2\) after each gradient step.  Empirically we set \(\epsilon = 0.1\), which yields a tight enough neighbourhood around the pretrained weights to improve stability while still allowing substantial rotation of the neuron directions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Control‑Consistency Metrics
--------------------------------------------------------------------

A central challenge in evaluating controllable generation is to **measure how well the output image reproduces the supplied control signal**.  Existing works often rely on visual inspection, which is subjective and non‑reproducible.  We therefore propose a family of *control‑consistency* metrics that are computed automatically from the generated images and the original control signals.

| Task | Metric | Formal Definition |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Canny Edge → Image (C2I) | **Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU)** | \(\displaystyle \mathrm{IoU} = \frac{|E_{\text{gen}} \cap E_{\text{ref}}|}{|E_{\text{gen}} \cup E_{\text{ref}}|}\), where \(E_{\text{gen}}\) is the Canny edge map extracted from the generated image and \(E_{\text{ref}}\) is the input edge map. |
|   | **F1‑Score** | \(\displaystyle \mathrm{F1} = \frac{2\,|E_{\text{gen}} \cap E_{\text{ref}}|}{|E_{\text{gen}}|+|E_{\text{ref}}|}\). |
| Segmentation → Image (S2I) | **Mean IoU (mIoU)** | Average IoU over all semantic classes: \(\displaystyle \mathrm{mIoU}= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{|S_{\text{gen}}^{c}\cap S_{\text{ref}}^{c}|}{|S_{\text{gen}}^{c}\cup S_{\text{ref}}^{c}|}\). |
|   | **Pixel Accuracy** | \(\displaystyle \mathrm{Acc}= \frac{\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{S_{\text{gen}}(i)=S_{\text{ref}}(i)\}}{N}\). |
| Facial Landmarks → Image (L2F) | **Mean \(\ell_{2}\) Landmark Error** | \(\displaystyle \mathrm{Err}= \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\mathbf{l}^{\text{gen}}_{k}-\mathbf{l}^{\text{ref}}_{k}\|_{2}\), where \(K\) is the number of landmarks. |

**Rationale.**  All three metrics compare a *re‑extracted* signal from the generated image (using the same detector that produced the original control) with the original signal.  Because the extraction pipeline is deterministic, the metric purely reflects how faithfully the diffusion model respects the spatial prior, independent of perceptual quality.  High IoU/F1 indicates that the edges of the output align with the input; high mIoU and pixel accuracy indicate that semantic regions are filled correctly; low landmark error signals that facial geometry has been reproduced.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Convergence Speed Analysis
--------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Quantitative Convergence Curves

We track the evolution of the control‑consistency metric on the **validation split** after each training epoch.  Figure 1 (reproduced from the paper) shows the mean landmark error for the L2F task as a function of epoch number for four methods:

* **ControlNet** – exhibits a sudden drop in error around epoch 8, after which the error plateaus.
* **T2I‑Adapter** – reduces the error gradually but remains above the OFT curve even after 20 epochs.
* **LoRA** – converges very slowly; at epoch 20 the error is still roughly twice that of OFT.
* **COFT / OFT** – start decreasing the error from the first epoch and achieve the lowest error by epoch 8.  COFT’s curve lies slightly below OFT’s because the radius constraint prevents excessive deviation from the pretrained weights, stabilising early updates.

The **area‑under‑the‑curve (AUC)** for COFT is reduced by roughly 55 % relative to LoRA, confirming that fewer training steps are needed to reach a given level of control fidelity.

#### Why OFT Converges Faster

The speed advantage stems from two complementary properties:

1. **Directional finetuning.**  By rotating neuron directions on the unit hypersphere, OFT directly manipulates the *angular* component that encodes semantic information (cf. Section 3.1 of the paper).  This aligns the optimisation trajectory with the most semantically impactful degrees of freedom, thereby requiring fewer gradient updates.

2. **Manifold‑aware optimisation.**  The orthogonal constraint forces updates to stay on the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices, which yields a smoother loss landscape and avoids the pathological plateaus often observed when unconstrained parameters wander far from the pretrained configuration.

### Summary
--------------------------------------------------------------------

In this chapter we have detailed how OFT can be harnessed for **controllable text‑to‑image diffusion**.  By learning a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation (or its constrained version COFT) we preserve the pairwise angular relations among neurons, which empirical evidence shows to be a reliable proxy for semantic stability.  The proposed control‑consistency metrics provide an objective way to evaluate how faithfully a model obeys its auxiliary input, and the experiments demonstrate that OFT achieves **faster convergence**, **higher control fidelity**, and **better visual quality** than leading baselines while using only a modest number of additional parameters and imposing **no extra inference cost**.  This makes OFT a practical and theoretically grounded tool for extending large diffusion models to any downstream task that requires precise spatial conditioning.

# Comparison with Related Fine‑Tuning Techniques  

In recent years the community has proposed a variety of strategies for adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks.  While many of these methods were originally developed for language models or classification networks, they share a common goal: to modify a model’s parameters so that it can accomplish a new task while retaining the knowledge acquired during pre‑training.  This chapter situates **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—the method introduced in the paper—within this broader landscape.  We begin by reviewing the most widely used additive low‑rank approach, **LoRA**, and then discuss **adapter‑style methods** and **prompt‑tuning**, both of which modify a model’s behavior by inserting lightweight modules.  After that we examine **orthogonal over‑parameterized training** (the work of Liu et al.) and explain how its motivations and theoretical guarantees differ from those of OFT.  Finally, we compare the techniques on three practical axes that matter to a practitioner: **parameter efficiency**, **inference overhead**, and **scalability** to very large diffusion models.

---  

## LoRA: Low‑Rank Additive Updates  

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) became a de‑facto baseline for finetuning large generative and language models because it requires only a small number of additional parameters.  The core idea is to decompose the weight update for a given layer into a product of two low‑rank matrices,  
\[
\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top ,\qquad 
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'},\; \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'},
\]  
where \(r'\ll \min(d,n)\) is a hyper‑parameter that controls the rank of the update.  During finetuning only \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are learned; the original pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) remains frozen.  The forward pass of a LoRA‑adapted linear layer therefore becomes  
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0}+\Delta\mathbf{W})^\top\mathbf{x}
      = (\mathbf{W}^{0}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top)^\top\mathbf{x}.
\]  

Because \(\Delta\mathbf{W}\) is low‑rank, the number of trainable parameters scales as \(r'(d+n)\).  In practice this yields a dramatic reduction compared with updating the full weight matrix, which would require \(dn\) parameters.  The low‑rank restriction also acts as a regularizer: it constrains how far the finetuned model can drift from the pretrained one, implicitly encouraging a small Euclidean distance \(\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|\).  

Despite these advantages, LoRA does **not** directly constrain the geometric relationship among the rows (or columns) of \(\mathbf{W}\).  Consequently, the pairwise angles between neurons—captured by hyperspherical energy—may change arbitrarily as training proceeds.  The paper argues that such uncontrolled angular drift can degrade the semantic generation ability of text‑to‑image diffusion models, especially when only a few examples are available for a downstream task.  OFT addresses this shortcoming by enforcing an orthogonal transformation that **preserves all pairwise angles** by construction, as discussed in the next sections.

---  

## Adapter‑Style Methods and Prompt‑Tuning  

Adapter‑style techniques—originally popular in natural‑language processing—introduce small bottleneck modules (often two linear layers with a non‑linear activation) into each layer of a pretrained network.  The base model’s parameters stay frozen; only the adapters are learned.  Prompt‑tuning follows a similar philosophy but modifies the **input embeddings** (or the text encoder) rather than the backbone weights, thereby steering the model toward a target domain with a learned “soft prompt”.  

Both families share two practical traits: (1) they require dramatically fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning, and (2) they leave the bulk of the pretrained network untouched, which empirically helps preserve the original generative qualities.  However, the **parameterization** of adapters and prompts is additive rather than multiplicative.  The adapted representation can be written as  
\[
\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{A}_{\text{adapter}}(\mathbf{h}),
\]  
where \(\mathbf{A}_{\text{adapter}}\) is a small neural function.  This additive form does not guarantee any invariance of the hyperspherical energy of the underlying weight matrices.  In other words, the **relative orientations** of neurons may still be altered in an uncontrolled fashion, which—as the paper’s experiments demonstrate—can lead to slower convergence or a loss of subject identity in few‑shot generation.  

OFT, by contrast, imposes a **global orthogonal rotation** (or reflection) on the entire weight matrix of a layer: \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) with \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  This transformation is multiplicative and **exactly preserves** the pairwise cosine similarities among the rows of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  Consequently, OFT provides a principled guarantee that the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained model remains intact, something that adapters and prompt‑tuning cannot claim.

---  

## Orthogonal Over‑Parameterized Training (Liu et al.) – Similarities and Differences  

The orthogonal over‑parameterized training framework introduced by Liu et al. (2021) explores the idea of **training a network from scratch while constraining all weight matrices to be orthogonal**.  Their motivation stems from observations that a small hyperspherical energy—meaning neurons are uniformly spread on the unit sphere—correlates with good generalization in classification tasks.  To achieve this, they parameterize each weight matrix as an orthogonal matrix and train the network directly on the target task, thereby keeping the hyperspherical energy low throughout training.

While the mathematical machinery (Cayley parameterization, block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices) is shared with OFT, the **objective** differs fundamentally:

1. **Energy Direction** – Liu et al. **minimize** hyperspherical energy, pushing neurons toward a uniform configuration.  OFT **preserves** the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, keeping the original angular relationships intact.  Minimizing the energy during finetuning could unintentionally erase the semantic structure that the diffusion model has already learned.

2. **Training Regime** – The orthogonal over‑parameterized approach starts from a random initialization and trains **from scratch**.  OFT operates in a **finetuning** regime: the pretrained weights \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) already encode a rich multimodal distribution of images and text.  OFT’s orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is learned **on top of** these weights, acting as a *coordinate change* that re‑orients the existing representation without destroying it.

3. **Task Domain** – The original orthogonal training is evaluated on **classification** benchmarks where uniformity of neurons correlates with margin‑based generalization.  OFT is applied to **text‑to‑image diffusion** models, where preserving the delicate balance between visual fidelity and textual alignment is crucial.  The paper’s empirical evidence indicates that preserving angular structure, rather than minimizing it, is more beneficial for generative tasks.

Thus, although both lines of work exploit the invariance of pairwise angles under orthogonal transformations, OFT repurposes this invariance for **stable, efficient finetuning** of diffusion models, whereas Liu et al. use it to **regularize training from scratch**.

## Parameter Efficiency, Inference Overhead, and Scalability  

### Parameter Efficiency  

| Method | Trainable Parameters (per layer) | Typical Scaling |
|--------|-----------------------------------|-----------------|
| Full finetuning | \(d\times n\) | \(\mathcal{O}(dn)\) |
| LoRA | \(r'(d+n)\) | \(\mathcal{O}(r' d)\) if \(r'\ll d,n\) |
| Adapter / Prompt | \(\mathcal{O}(r_{\text{ad}}\cdot d)\) (bottleneck dim.) | linear in \(d\) |
| OFT (full orthogonal) | \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) |
| OFT (block‑diagonal, \(r\) blocks) | \(\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)\frac{r}{2}\) | \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^{2}}{r}\bigr)\) |
| OFT (shared blocks) | \(\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)/2\) | \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^{2}}{r^{2}}\bigr)\) |

In practice the diffusion model’s linear layers often have modest hidden dimensions (e.g., 128, 256).  The paper’s concrete example shows that for a \(128\times128\) weight matrix, LoRA with rank \(r'=8\) needs 2,048 trainable parameters, while a block‑diagonal OFT with \(r=8\) uses only 960 parameters.  Therefore, **OFT can be more parameter‑efficient than LoRA** when an appropriate block size is chosen, while still preserving the full‑rank nature of the transformation (the orthogonal matrix remains invertible).

### Inference Overhead  

A critical practical consideration for any adaptation technique is whether it adds latency or memory consumption at test time.  

* **LoRA** and **adapter** methods require the additional low‑rank or bottleneck modules to be executed alongside the original forward pass, which modestly increases the number of matrix multiplications.  
* **ControlNet**, a contemporaneous controllable‑generation approach, injects extra conditional branches and consequently incurs a noticeable inference cost.  

In contrast, **OFT introduces no extra computation at inference**.  Once training finishes, the learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) can be **pre‑multiplied** into the pretrained weight matrix:
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]  
The resulting \(\mathbf{W}\) is a standard weight matrix; the forward pass proceeds exactly as in the original diffusion model.  Hence the inference speed and memory footprint are identical to those of the unmodified pretrained model.

### Scalability  

Scalability concerns both the **size of the orthogonal parameterization** and the **cost of ensuring orthogonality** during training.  

* The naïve Cayley transform requires an inversion of \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\), which scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3})\) for a full \(d\times d\) matrix.  For very large hidden dimensions this operation could become a bottleneck.  
* The **block‑diagonal** strategy mitigates this by decomposing \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) smaller orthogonal blocks, each of size \(d/r\).  The computational cost of each block’s Cayley transform drops to \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl((d/r)^{3}\bigr)\), and the total cost becomes \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{3}/r^{2}\bigr)\).  Empirically the paper finds that even modest block numbers (e.g., \(r=8\) or \(r=16\)) retain enough flexibility for diffusion finetuning while keeping the overhead manageable.  
* **Shared‑block** variants further reduce the number of unique parameters and the associated matrix‑inverse operations, at the expense of introducing a bias toward a common rotation across blocks.  The experiments demonstrate that this bias does not materially harm generation quality for the tasks considered.  

Overall, OFT scales to the hidden sizes typical of modern text‑to‑image diffusion models (e.g., the 768‑dimensional transformer layers in Stable Diffusion) when an appropriate block‑diagonal factorization is used.  In comparison, LoRA’s low‑rank factorization scales linearly with \(d\) and is therefore also scalable, but it lacks the **geometric invariance** guarantees that OFT provides.

---  

### Synthesis  

The comparative analysis highlights that OFT occupies a middle ground between the **extreme flexibility** of full finetuning and the **parameter frugality** of low‑rank or adapter‑based methods.  Its distinguishing feature is the **preservation of hyperspherical energy**, which the paper empirically links to the retention of semantic generation ability in diffusion models.  By learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix—implemented efficiently via block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization—OFT can achieve:

* **Stronger stability** and faster convergence on both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks, because the optimization occurs on a smooth hyperspherical manifold.  
* **Zero inference overhead**, as the orthogonal transform can be folded into the original weight matrix after training.  
* **Competitive or superior parameter efficiency** relative to LoRA when the block size is chosen wisely.  

At the same time, OFT inherits some of the practical challenges of orthogonal training, namely the need for matrix inverses and the careful tuning of block granularity.  Future work may explore faster differentiable orthogonalizations or more expressive yet still angle‑preserving parameterizations, but the present formulation already demonstrates that a principled geometric constraint can dramatically improve the finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.

Practical Implementation Guide
# Code Skeleton (PyTorch Pseudo‑code) for Cayley‑Parameterized OFT  

In this section we present a minimal yet complete PyTorch‑style implementation that follows the formalism introduced in the paper.  Recall that a single OFT‑enabled layer replaces the original weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad   \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I},
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\) is an orthogonal matrix generated through the Cayley transform.  Let \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be a skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\)).  The Cayley map  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})\bigl(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\bigr)^{-1}
\tag{1}
\]

produces an orthogonal matrix with \(\det(\mathbf{R})=1\); this satisfies the invariance property of hyperspherical energy described in the paper.  

Below is a concise skeleton that can be inserted into any diffusion model where a linear layer, a convolution, or an attention projection is to be finetuned.  The code is deliberately written to expose the three major steps: (i) construction of a skew‑symmetric matrix from a free‑parameter tensor, (ii) Cayley conversion, and (iii) forward substitution of the transformed weight.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class OFTLiner(nn.Module):
    """
    Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) wrapper for a linear projection.
    The pretrained weight W0 is frozen; an orthogonal matrix R,
    parameterized by a skew‑symmetric Q, is learned.
    """
    def __init__(self, weight_pretrained: torch.Tensor,
                 block_size: int = None,
                 share_blocks: bool = False):
        """
        Args:
            weight_pretrained: Tensor of shape (d, n) – the frozen
                               pretrained weights W0.
            block_size:       Size of each block in the block‑diagonal
                               decomposition of R (see paper).  If None,
                               a full d×d matrix is used.
            share_blocks:     If True, all blocks share the same
                               parameters, further reducing the
                               number of learnable parameters.
        """
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = weight_pretrained  # frozen, register as buffer
        self.register_buffer('W0', self.W0.clone())

        d, _ = self.W0.shape
        # ---------- Determine block structure ----------
        if block_size is None:
            block_size = d                     # full matrix
        self.num_blocks = d // block_size
        assert d % block_size == 0, "d must be divisible by block_size"

        # ---------- Allocate raw parameters for Q ----------
        # For each block we store the upper‑triangular entries of a
        # skew‑symmetric matrix.  A skew‑symmetric matrix of size b has
        # b(b‑1)/2 independent elements.
        b = block_size
        self.q_shape = (self.num_blocks, b, b)
        # store only the strict upper‑triangular part, flattened
        self.raw_q = nn.Parameter(
            torch.zeros(self.num_blocks, b, b)
        )  # we will enforce skew‑symmetry later

        self.block_size = block_size
        self.share_blocks = share_blocks

    def _build_Q(self) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Construct a block‑diagonal skew‑symmetric matrix Q from the raw
        learnable parameters.
        """
        # Enforce skew symmetry: Q = (raw_q - raw_q.T) / 2
        Q_blocks = (self.raw_q - self.raw_q.transpose(-2, -1)) * 0.5

        if self.share_blocks:
            # Broadcast a single block to all positions
            Q_blocks = Q_blocks[0:1].expand(self.num_blocks, -1, -1)

        # Assemble block‑diagonal matrix
        Q = torch.block_diag(*[Q_blocks[i] for i in range(self.num_blocks)])
        return Q  # shape (d, d)

    def _cayley(self, Q: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Apply the Cayley transform (1) to obtain an orthogonal matrix R.
        """
        I = torch.eye(Q.shape[0], device=Q.device, dtype=Q.dtype)
        # (I + Q) @ (I - Q)^{-1}
        # Use torch.solve or torch.linalg.solve for stability
        R = (I + Q) @ torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I)
        return R  # shape (d, d), orthogonal and det(R)=1

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Replace the original linear projection by (R W0)^T x.
        """
        Q = self._build_Q()                # (d, d)
        R = self._cayley(Q)                # (d, d)
        # Compute transformed weight once per forward pass
        # Note: we keep W0 frozen, thus the gradient flows only
        # through R (hence through raw_q).
        W = R @ self.W0                     # (d, n)
        # Linear projection (the diffusion model expects (W^T x))
        out = F.linear(x, W.t())            # shape (..., n)
        return out
```

Key points to notice:

* **Skew‑symmetry enforcement** – we store an unrestricted tensor `raw_q` and symmetrize it on the fly, guaranteeing \(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\).  
* **Block‑diagonal parameterization** – the `block_size` argument corresponds to the \(r\) blocks described in the paper.  When `share_blocks=True` the memory footprint drops from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2})\).  
* **Cayley transform** – the implementation uses `torch.linalg.solve` to avoid explicit matrix inversion while preserving differentiability.  The resulting matrix fulfills \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) and, because of the determinant constraint, belongs to the special orthogonal group.  

The same wrapper can be applied to convolutional kernels (by reshaping the kernel to a 2‑D matrix) or to the query/key/value projection matrices of a transformer block.  In all cases the semantics are identical: the pretrained parameters are rotated by an orthogonal matrix that is learned jointly with the downstream loss.

# Choosing Block Size \(r\) and \(\epsilon\) for COFT  

## Block Size \(r\)  

The hyper‑parameter \(r\) controls how many square blocks appear on the diagonal of \(\mathbf{R}\).  According to the paper, a full matrix (\(r=1\)) enjoys maximal expressive power at a cost of \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) learnable parameters.  When memory is limited, increasing \(r\) reduces the number of free parameters to  

\[
\#\text{params}( \mathbf{R}) = \frac{d\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}.
\]

A practical rule of thumb, validated empirically in the experiments, is:

| Model dimension \(d\) | Recommended \(r\) | Approx. trainable params |
|-----------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| \(d \le 256\)         | \(r=1\) (full)    | \(\approx 32\text{k}\)   |
| \(256 < d \le 1024\)  | \(r = 4\)         | \(\approx 0.5d^{2}\)     |
| \(d > 1024\)          | \(r = 8\) or larger (share blocks) | \(\approx 0.125d^{2}\) |

When the same block is shared across all positions (`share_blocks=True`), the parameter count shrinks further to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2})\).  In the diffusion backbone of Stable Diffusion (U‑Net with hidden dimension 1024), the authors report using \(r=8\) with shared blocks, which reduces each orthogonal matrix to roughly \(16\,\text{k}\) parameters while preserving the hyperspherical energy invariance.

## Radius Constraint \(\epsilon\) for COFT  

COFT augments the OFT objective with an explicit deviation bound  

\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\tag{2}
\]

ensuring that the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained weights.  Because the Cayley transform linearises around the identity as  

\[
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^{2}),
\]

the bound (2) can be equivalently imposed on \(\mathbf{Q}\) as  

\[
\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'.
\tag{3}
\]

In practice one enforces (3) after each gradient step by a simple projection:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    norm = torch.norm(self.raw_q, p='fro')
    if norm > eps_prime:
        self.raw_q.mul_(eps_prime / norm)
```

Empirical observations in the paper (Figure 7) suggest that:

* **Large \(\epsilon\) (e.g. \(\epsilon \approx 0.5\))** – COFT behaves similarly to unconstrained OFT, providing maximal flexibility but with a slightly higher risk of over‑adjusting the direction of neurons.  
* **Medium \(\epsilon\) (0.1 – 0.2)** – offers the best trade‑off: the model converges quickly, retains most of the pretrained semantic capacity, and is robust to random seeds.  
* **Very small \(\epsilon\) (< 0.05)** – the finetuned model remains almost identical to the pretrained one, which may be insufficient to capture the downstream control signal.  

A recommended initial setting for Stable Diffusion’s 1024‑dimensional layers is \(\epsilon = 0.15\) (corresponding to \(\epsilon' \approx 0.075\)).  One can later perform a grid search around this value; the training curves are usually monotonic with respect to \(\epsilon\), making the selection straightforward.

# Memory and Compute Considerations for Large Models  

Even though OFT replaces the conventional additive weight‑update (e.g., LoRA) with a multiplicative orthogonal transformation, the computational overhead is modest:

| Operation | Complexity | Memory Impact |
|-----------|------------|---------------|
| Construction of \(\mathbf{Q}\) (block‑diagonal) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) | Only raw parameters are stored (no full \(d\times d\) matrix). |
| Cayley transform (solve linear system) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3})\) per forward if naive, but practical implementations use the block‑diagonal structure, reducing to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3}/r)\). | The intermediate inverse is never materialised; we solve \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}\) via `torch.linalg.solve`, which reuses memory. |
| Multiplication \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}n/r)\) | The product can be cached for several steps if the downstream loss does not modify \(\mathbf{R}\) dramatically. |

**Practical memory tips**

1. **Cache the transformed weight per training step** – after the first forward pass of a batch, store \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) in a buffer and reuse it for the remaining micro‑batches of the same iteration.  This avoids recomputing the matrix product for each micro‑batch.  
2. **Mixed‑precision training** – the orthogonal matrix is well‑conditioned so FP16 does not degrade the orthogonality enforcement, and it halves the memory consumption of `raw_q`.  
3. **Gradient checkpointing** – for very deep U‑Net blocks one can checkpoint the computation of \(\mathbf{R}\) and re‑compute it during the backward pass, swapping a few extra FLOPs for a large memory saving.  

By adopting a block‑diagonal \(r\) of 8 or larger, the additional memory required on a 24 GB GPU for a full 1024‑dimensional layer stays below 30 MB, which is negligible compared with the activation memory of the diffusion backbone.

# Tips for Hyper‑Parameter Tuning (Learning Rate, Epochs, Seed Robustness)  

The empirical studies in the paper show that OFT (and COFT) are considerably less sensitive to the number of fine‑tuning epochs than traditional DreamBooth or LoRA.  Nevertheless, sensible defaults accelerate convergence and improve reproducibility.

| Hyper‑parameter | Recommended Range | Rationale |
|-----------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Learning rate (for `raw_q`) | \(1\!\times\!10^{-4}\) – \(5\!\times\!10^{-4}\) (AdamW) | The orthogonal manifold is smooth; a moderate step size keeps \(\mathbf{R}\) close to identity while allowing enough rotation to capture the downstream signal. |
| Weight decay | 0 (or \(1\!\times\!10^{-5}\) for very large \(r\)) | The orthogonal constraint already regularises the model; additional decay can unnecessarily shrink the rotation magnitude. |
| Number of epochs | 5 – 30 (subject‑driven) / 10 – 20 (controllable) | Figures 4‑5 in the paper demonstrate that stable performance is achieved after ≈200 – 400 iterations (≈5 epochs on a modest dataset).  Extending beyond 20 epochs rarely improves metrics but may increase the risk of over‑rotating when \(\epsilon\) is large. |
| Random seed | Test at least three seeds; use the median of metrics. | The paper reports variance across seeds; OFT’s variance is smaller than DreamBooth’s, but evaluating a few seeds guards against pathological initializations of the skew‑symmetric matrix. |
| Block size \(r\) | 1 (full) for low‑dimensional layers, 4–8 for hidden dimensions ≥ 512 | Larger \(r\) reduces parameters and stabilises training while preserving enough expressive power. |
| \(\epsilon\) (COFT) | 0.1 – 0.2 (default 0.15) | Controls the trade‑off between faithful preservation of the pretrained knowledge (small \(\epsilon\)) and ability to learn new control signals (larger \(\epsilon\)). |

**Tuning workflow**

1. **Start with the default \(\epsilon=0.15\) and \(r=8\)** (shared blocks) on a single layer (e.g., the text‑conditioned cross‑attention projection).  
2. **Run a short 5‑epoch probe**; monitor the validation hyperspherical energy difference \(\| \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\|\).  If the difference remains near zero, the orthogonal constraint is correctly enforced.  
3. **If downstream loss plateaus early**, increase \(\epsilon\) by 0.05 increments while keeping the learning rate fixed.  Observe whether validation metrics (e.g., CLIP‑I for DreamBooth) improve.  
4. **If training becomes unstable (loss spikes or generated images collapse)**, either shrink \(\epsilon\) or increase \(r\) (more blocks → fewer degrees of freedom per block).  
5. **Finalize** by training the full set of selected layers for the target number of epochs; evaluate on a held‑out validation set and keep the checkpoint with the best combined metric (e.g., DINO + CLIP‑T).  

Because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, the usual early‑stopping based on Euclidean weight drift is unnecessary; the model’s semantic capacity remains intact as long as the \(\epsilon\) bound is respected.

# Deploying the Fine‑Tuned Model with Zero Inference Overhead  

One of the most attractive properties of OFT (and COFT) is that the orthogonal transformation can be *absorbed* into the pretrained weight matrix after training, yielding a standard weight tensor that can be used by any existing diffusion pipeline without modification.

The post‑training step is straightforward:

```python
def bake_orthogonal_transform(original_weight, raw_q, block_size, share_blocks):
    # Build Q as in the training wrapper
    Q = build_Q(raw_q, block_size, share_blocks)          # (d, d)
    R = cayley_transform(Q)                               # (d, d)
    # Compute the baked weight
    baked_weight = R @ original_weight                     # (d, n)
    return baked_weight
```

Once `baked_weight` replaces the original \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) in the model checkpoint, the inference path becomes exactly the same as the pretrained network:

* **No extra matrix multiplication** – the orthogonal‐matrix multiplication is performed once offline.  
* **Identical memory layout** – the baked weight is a dense tensor, compatible with any accelerator‑optimized kernel.  
* **Zero latency impact** – the diffusion timestep loop, classifier‑free guidance, and other components remain untouched.  

The same “baking” procedure can be applied to every OFT‑wrapped layer (e.g., all cross‑attention projections, the up‑sampling convolutions, or any custom linear module).  After baking, the checkpoint can be exported to the standard `diffusers` or `CompVis` format and loaded by downstream services without any code change.

In summary, the deployment pipeline is:

1. **Finetune** using the OFT/COFT wrapper (training time only).  
2. **Bake** orthogonal matrices into the frozen weights.  
3. **Save** the resulting checkpoint as a standard diffusion model.  
4. **Load** the checkpoint in any existing inference script – the model behaves exactly as a vanilla diffusion model, but now embodies the learned controllability.  

This zero‑overhead property makes OFT especially appealing for production environments where latency and memory budgets are strict, and it aligns with the paper’s claim that “no additional inference overhead” is introduced.

# Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions  

In this chapter we critically examine the constraints that accompany Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT), and we outline promising avenues for further research. The discussion is anchored in the observations and open questions raised by the original work, and each subsection expands on a specific limitation, drawing out its implications for the stability, scalability, and generality of OFT‑based finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.

---

## Scalability of Matrix Inverse in the Cayley Transform  

A central component of OFT is the enforcement of orthogonality via the Cayley parameterization  
\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\)). This construction guarantees \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) while allowing the orthogonal matrix to be expressed in terms of unconstrained parameters (the entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\)). However, the matrix inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) must be computed at every optimization step, and its cost grows as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{3})\) for a dense \(d\times d\) matrix.  

When the dimensionality \(d\) of a weight matrix in a diffusion model reaches several thousand (as is typical for attention projections in transformer blocks), the cubic cost can become a bottleneck, especially on commodity GPUs where memory bandwidth limits the size of intermediate matrices. Moreover, the inverse operation is numerically sensitive: if eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{Q}\) approach \(1\), the denominator matrix becomes ill‑conditioned, potentially destabilizing gradient propagation.  

A practical remedy employed in the original work is block‑diagonal factorization of \(\mathbf{R}\), thereby reducing the effective dimension of each sub‑matrix and lowering the computational burden to \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr)\) for \(r\) blocks. While this improves scalability, it introduces other concerns (discussed later). Future research may explore alternative orthogonal parameterizations that avoid explicit matrix inversion, such as:

* **Householder reflections** – representing \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of a small number of Householder matrices, each of which is cheap to apply and differentiate;
* **Exponential map** – parameterizing \(\mathbf{R}= \exp(\mathbf{S})\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{S}\). Approximating the matrix exponential with truncated series or scaling‑and‑squaring can be more efficient than a full inverse;
* **Iterative orthogonalization** – using gradient‑based updates that directly enforce \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) via a penalty term, possibly combined with a few corrective projection steps per iteration.

Developing such alternatives that retain the provable preservation of hyperspherical energy while being computationally lightweight would significantly broaden the applicability of OFT to larger models and longer training pipelines.

---

## Compositionality of Multiple Orthogonal Transforms  

One of the attractive theoretical properties of orthogonal matrices is closure under multiplication: if \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{2}\) are orthogonal, then \(\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2}\) is also orthogonal. This suggests a potential for **compositional finetuning**, where several downstream tasks—each solved by learning an orthogonal transformation—could be combined simply by multiplying their learned \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices.  

In practice, however, the composition raises several questions that the original paper identifies but does not resolve:

1. **Inter‑task interference** – Each orthogonal transform reshapes the coordinate system of the pretrained weight matrix. When multiple transforms are multiplied, the resulting rotation may no longer align with any single downstream objective, possibly degrading performance on all tasks.  
2. **Order dependence** – Matrix multiplication is not commutative, so the order in which task‑specific transforms are applied matters. Determining a principled ordering, or learning a task‑specific routing mechanism, remains open.  
3. **Capacity limits** – While an orthogonal matrix can represent any rotation or reflection, the space of useful transformations for a given set of tasks may be sparsely populated. Understanding whether the union of tasks can be captured by a low‑dimensional subspace of the orthogonal group could inform more efficient compositional schemes.

A concrete research direction is **task‑aware composition**, where a meta‑learner predicts a weighted combination of task‑specific \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices conditioned on a task identifier or on the current input. Alternatively, one could enforce a **shared subspace constraint**, requiring all task matrices to share a common factor (e.g., \(\mathbf{R}_{k}= \mathbf{R}_{\text{shared}}\mathbf{R}_{k}^{\text{private}}\)). This would preserve orthogonality while limiting the degree of interference among tasks.

---

## Bias Introduced by Block‑Diagonal Parameterization  

The block‑diagonal structure of \(\mathbf{R}\) reduces the number of trainable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\), where \(r\) denotes the number of diagonal blocks. Though efficient, this simplification imposes an **inductive bias**: neurons belonging to different blocks are never mixed by the orthogonal transformation. Consequently, inter‑block angular relationships are fixed, and only intra‑block pairwise angles can be altered.  

Empirically, the original experiments demonstrate that a modest number of blocks (e.g., \(r=8\) for a \(128\times128\) weight matrix) yields satisfactory results. Nonetheless, the bias can manifest as:

* **Limited expressivity** – Certain downstream tasks may require coordinated rotations across all neurons, which a block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) cannot provide.  
* **Uneven adaptation** – If the distribution of semantic information is not aligned with the predefined block boundaries (e.g., some semantic features are spread across multiple blocks), the finetuning may over‑emphasize some features while neglecting others.  

Addressing this bias motivates **dynamic or learned block structures**. Instead of fixing the block partition a priori, one could introduce a lightweight gating network that assigns each neuron to a block during training, allowing the block delineation to adapt to the data. Alternatively, a **low‑rank plus block‑diagonal hybrid** could capture both global rotations (via a low‑rank component) and fine‑grained per‑block adjustments. Research into such adaptive architectures would clarify the trade‑off between parameter efficiency and representational flexibility.

---

## Potential for Dynamic/Learned Block Structures  

Building on the previous point, a promising avenue is to let the model **learn the decomposition** of \(\mathbf{R}\) into blocks rather than hand‑designing it. One concrete scheme is:

1. Initialize a set of **anchor orthogonal matrices** \(\{\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{K}\}\) with full size \(d\times d\).  
2. For each neuron \(i\), learn a soft assignment vector \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\in\Delta^{K-1}\) (the probability simplex) that weights the contribution of each anchor matrix.  
3. Construct the effective transformation for neuron \(i\) as \(\mathbf{R}_{i}= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{i,k}\mathbf{R}_{k}\).  

Because each \(\mathbf{R}_{k}\) remains orthogonal, convex combinations of orthogonal matrices are not orthogonal in general. However, one can enforce orthogonality after the weighted sum by applying a **projection onto the orthogonal group** (e.g., via singular value decomposition) at each update step. This yields a **soft, data‑driven block structure** that can capture complex rotations while preserving the overall orthogonal constraint.

Such a framework would also enable **task‑conditional block selection**: for a new control signal, the model could adapt the assignment vectors \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) on the fly, effectively reconfiguring the orthogonal transform without retraining the full set of anchor matrices.

---

## Extending OFT to Other Modalities and Architectures  

While the current study focuses on text‑to‑image diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) and primarily on fully‑connected or attention projection layers, the underlying principle—preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal transformations—is modality‑agnostic. Extending OFT could proceed along several lines:

### 1. Video Diffusion Models  
Video generation models often augment image‑space diffusion with temporal conditioning (e.g., 3D convolutions or cross‑frame attention). OFT can be applied to the temporal attention matrices, ensuring that the relational geometry of latent tokens across time remains intact. An additional challenge is the **larger hidden dimension** due to stacked frame embeddings, which intensifies the matrix‑inverse scalability issue discussed earlier; thus, more efficient orthogonal parameterizations become crucial.

### 2. Audio Diffusion  
Audio synthesis models such as diffusion‑based waveform generators or spectrogram‑based pipelines contain 1‑D convolutional stacks. Here the block‑diagonal orthogonal transform can be interpreted as **channel‑wise rotations** of convolution kernels, potentially preserving phase‑like information that is vital for timbre. Evaluating hyperspherical energy on 1‑D kernels may require adapting the definition of pairwise similarity to account for stride and receptive field differences.

### 3. Vision Transformers (ViT) and ConvNeXt  
Both ViT and ConvNeXt rely heavily on linear projections (e.g., query/key/value matrices, feed‑forward layers). Applying OFT to these projections is straightforward: each projection matrix can be left‑multiplied by a learned orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). However, the **layer‑wise sharing** of \(\mathbf{R}\) must be reconsidered. In ViT, each attention head could have its own orthogonal transform, or a shared transform could be imposed across heads to reduce parameters. For ConvNeXt, the block‑diagonal structure aligns naturally with **group convolutions**, suggesting a synergy between OFT and existing architectural bottlenecks.

### 4. Multi‑Modal Fusion Layers  
Models that jointly process text, image, and other modalities often employ cross‑modal attention or joint embedding spaces. Orthogonal finetuning of the **cross‑modal projection matrices** could preserve the delicate balance between modalities, potentially improving downstream tasks such as image captioning or video‑guided generation.

In each extension, the **hyperspherical energy** must be evaluated in the appropriate latent space (e.g., token embeddings for ViT, spectrogram frames for audio). Moreover, the impact of preserving pairwise angular relationships on the semantic fidelity of generated outputs should be empirically validated, ideally through task‑specific metrics analogous to the control consistency metric introduced for image control signals.

---

## Summary  

The Orthogonal Finetuning framework offers a principled route to adapt powerful text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving their pretrained semantic structure. Nonetheless, several concrete limitations remain:

* The cubic cost of the Cayley‑based matrix inverse limits scalability; alternative orthogonal parameterizations are needed.  
* The compositional nature of orthogonal transforms invites multi‑task finetuning, but interference and ordering effects require systematic study.  
* Block‑diagonal parameterization, while efficient, imposes a bias that may restrict expressivity; dynamic or learned block structures could mitigate this.  
* Extending OFT beyond image diffusion—to video, audio, and newer architectures—promises broader impact but also introduces new challenges related to dimensionality, modality‑specific similarity measures, and architecture‑aware design.

Addressing these open problems will not only make OFT more practical for ever‑larger generative models but also deepen our theoretical understanding of how angular relationships among neurons encode semantic knowledge across modalities.

## 1. Key Theoretical Insight – Preserving Pairwise Neuron Angles  

### 1.1. Hyperspherical Energy as a Structural Measure  

A central obstacle when finetuning diffusion models is the lack of a quantitative proxy for *semantic* preservation.  Traditional approaches rely on Euclidean distance between the finetuned weight matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) and the pretrained matrix \(\mathbf{M}^{0}\), i.e. \(\|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^{0}\|\).  This metric captures how much each entry moves, but it is blind to **how the relative geometry of the neurons changes**.  The paper proposes to use **hyperspherical energy (HE)**:

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\big\|^{-1},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n}]\) denotes the weight matrix of a fully connected (or linear) layer.  Each \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\) lives on the unit hypersphere in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\).  The energy aggregates the inverse Euclidean distances of all pairs of normalized neurons, which is an **angular similarity** measure because for unit vectors \(\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\| = \sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}\).  Consequently, **minimizing the change in hyperspherical energy** directly forces the pairwise angles \(\theta_{ij}\) to stay the same.

### 1.2. Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy  

A fundamental property of the hyperspherical energy is its **invariance under orthogonal transformations**.  If an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\), then for any weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\),

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) \;=\; \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}),
\]

because \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates (or reflects) all neurons **together**, preserving all pairwise inner products and thus all angles.  This invariance provides a **guaranteed method** to keep HE unchanged while still allowing the model to change its behavior: we simply learn a per‑layer orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) and replace the original weights by \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  The finetuning problem becomes:

\[
\min_{\mathbf{R}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]

Here \(\mathcal{L}\) denotes the task‑specific loss (e.g. reconstruction loss for DreamBooth, control‑signal alignment loss for ControlNet).  Because the orthogonal constraint guarantees \(\operatorname{HE}\) preservation, **no explicit regularizer is required**; the structural preservation follows automatically from the parametrization.

### 1.3. Why Angles Matter More Than Magnitudes  

The paper presents a toy auto‑encoder experiment: when features are computed using the inner product during training, **reconstructing an image using only the angular component of the neurons recovers the input almost perfectly**, whereas the magnitude component carries almost no recognizable information.  This mirrors the classical observation that in Fourier analysis the **phase spectrum** (angular information) encodes most semantic content, while the magnitude mainly affects low‑level texture.  Therefore, **changing neuron directions is the most effective lever** for altering the semantics of the generated image, and orthogonal transformations provide a *controlled* way of doing exactly that.

## 2. Empirical Advantages of OFT / COFT  

### 2.1. Training Stability  

When the orthogonal constraint is omitted (i.e., learning an unconstrained matrix \(\mathbf{R}\)), the model quickly diverges and fails to generate realistic images, as demonstrated in the controllable‑generation ablation.  Enforcing orthogonality eliminates drift in pairwise angles, which prevents catastrophic forgetting of the pretrained diffusion prior.  The constrained variant **COFT** adds an explicit bound \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon\), guaranteeing that the finetuned model remains **close** to the original in the Euclidean sense as well.  Empirically, COFT yields smoother loss curves and a reduced sensitivity to learning‑rate hyperparameters.

### 2.2. Faster Convergence  

Because the optimization operates on a **manifold (the orthogonal group)** rather than the full Euclidean space of weight matrices, gradients point directly toward directions that respect the desired geometry.  In both subject‑driven generation (DreamBooth setting) and controllable generation (Canny, segmentation, landmark tasks), OFT‑finetuned models achieve comparable or superior performance **after far fewer training epochs**.  For example, on the landmark‑to‑face task, OFT reaches the final quality at epoch 8 whereas LoRA needs about epoch 20, despite using a similar parameter budget.

### 2.3. Sample Quality and Diversity  

Quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I for subject fidelity; CLIP‑T for prompt fidelity; LPIPS for diversity) consistently show that OFT/COFT **outperform DreamBooth and LoRA** on subject fidelity while maintaining or slightly improving prompt alignment and diversity.  In controllable generation, the newly introduced **control‑consistency metrics** (IoU/F1 for Canny, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, \(\ell_{2}\) landmark error for faces) reveal that OFT’s generated images adhere more tightly to the conditioning signal.

### 2.4. Parameter‑Efficiency  

A naïve orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) contains \(d(d-1)/2\) free parameters.  The paper introduces two tricks to reduce this cost:

1. **Cayley parameterization** – represent \(\mathbf{R}\) as \((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\).  This enforces orthogonality automatically while allowing gradient‑based learning.
2. **Block‑diagonal factorization** – split \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) independent blocks, each of size \((d/r)\times(d/r)\).  The number of trainable parameters becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\); sharing the same block across all positions reduces it further to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2})\).

Comparisons with LoRA (low‑rank additive updates) show that for a typical linear layer of size \(128\times128\), OFT can be trained with **~960 parameters** versus **~2 048 parameters** for LoRA (rank 8).  Moreover, OFT does **not add any inference overhead**: after training the orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weight once, yielding a standard weight matrix for downstream sampling.

## 3. When to Choose OFT / COFT Over Other Finetuning Methods  

| Scenario | Desired Property | Recommended Method | Rationale |
|----------|------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| **Limited finetuning data (few‑shot subject images)** | Preserve subject identity, avoid over‑fitting | **OFT** (or **COFT** with modest \(\epsilon\)) | Orthogonal transformation respects the pretrained relational geometry, leading to stable updates even with only a handful of examples. |
| **Strong control signal (edges, segmentation, landmarks)** | Accurate alignment with control, fast convergence | **COFT** (tight \(\epsilon\) for smoother training) | The extra radius constraint stabilizes the optimization on noisy control signals while still allowing enough flexibility to learn the mapping. |
| **Very large models where additional parameters are prohibitive** | Minimal extra parameters, zero inference cost | **OFT with block‑diagonal sharing** | Parameter count scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r^{2})\); inference remains unchanged because the orthogonal matrix can be baked into the original weights. |
| **Need to combine several downstream adaptations (e.g., subject‑driven + style control)** | Compositionality of adaptations | **COFT** (or sequential OFT with careful \(\epsilon\) scheduling) | Orthogonal matrices compose multiplicatively; keeping each update close to identity helps avoid destructive interference. |
| **When the downstream task is purely discriminative (e.g., classification finetuning)** | Reduce redundancy, encourage uniformity | **Minimize hyperspherical energy** (as in Liu 2021) rather than preserve it | The goal differs: uniform angular spread improves generalization; preservation would keep irrelevant pretraining bias. |
| **When a low‑rank additive change is sufficient and memory is abundant** | Simpler implementation, easy integration with existing adapters | **LoRA** | LoRA’s additive low‑rank updates are straightforward and work well if angular preservation is not critical. |

In practice, **COFT** is often the safest default because it inherits the theoretical guarantees of OFT while explicitly limiting drift from the pretrained model, yielding the most robust training dynamics across diverse downstream tasks.

## 4. Checklist for Applying OFT / COFT to a New Downstream Task  

1. **Define the Target Layer(s)**  
   - Identify which linear (or attention‑weight) matrices will be adapted.  
   - For transformer‑based diffusion models, the query/key/value projection matrices are natural candidates.

2. **Choose Orthogonal Parameterization**  
   - Use Cayley transform with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\).  
   - Decide on block‑diagonal factorization: choose block count \(r\) based on the dimension \(d\) (e.g., \(r = \sqrt{d}\) is a common trade‑off).  
   - If memory permits, start with a full‑matrix (\(r=1\)) to assess maximal flexibility.

3. **Initialize Properly**  
   - Set \(\mathbf{Q}=0\) so that \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\) at the start of training; this guarantees identical behavior to the pretrained model before any update.

4. **Specify the Loss Function**  
   - For **subject‑driven generation**, combine reconstruction loss (e.g., pixel‑wise or perceptual) with a class‑preservation prior (as in DreamBooth).  
   - For **controllable generation**, add a control‑consistency term that measures the distance between the generated image’s extracted control signal and the provided one (IoU, landmark \(\ell_{2}\), etc.).

5. **Set Hyper‑parameters**  
   - **Learning rate**: OFT tolerates larger rates than naive finetuning; start with \(1\!\times\!10^{-4}\) and adjust only if loss plateaus.  
   - **\(\epsilon\) (COFT only)**: choose a small value (e.g., \(0.05\)–\(0.1\)) to keep \(\mathbf{R}\) near identity; verify that the training loss still decreases.  
   - **Number of epochs / iterations**: OFT is less sensitive; you can overshoot (e.g., 2000 iterations) without harming stability, unlike DreamBooth which may collapse.

6. **Optimization Details**  
   - Use standard Adam or AdamW; the orthogonal constraint is already enforced by the Cayley representation, so no projection step is needed.  
   - If you adopt block sharing, ensure the shared block’s gradients are accumulated correctly across all appearances.

7. **Monitoring Metrics**  
   - Track **hyperspherical energy difference** \(\|\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\|\) as a sanity check (should stay near zero).  
   - Record task‑specific metrics (subject fidelity, control consistency) every few hundred steps to detect early divergence.

8. **Post‑Training Consolidation**  
   - Multiply the learned orthogonal matrix into the pretrained weight once: \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
   - Discard \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{Q}\) to obtain a model that can be used with the original diffusion inference pipeline without extra parameters.

9. **Evaluation**  
   - Run the **control‑consistency** pipeline (re‑extract control signal from generated images) to obtain a quantitative score.  
   - For subject‑driven tasks, compute DINO/CLIP‑I and LPIPS as in the paper to compare against baselines.  
   - Conduct a **human preference** study if possible; the paper reports that users consistently rank OFT outputs higher.

10. **Iterate or Compose**  
    - If you need multiple adaptations (e.g., subject plus style), sequentially apply OFT/COFT, each time initializing from the previously finetuned weights.  
    - Because orthogonal matrices compose, the overall transformation remains orthogonal, preserving the theoretical guarantees.

Following this checklist will help you reproduce the strong results reported in the paper and adapt the methodology to novel conditioning signals (e.g., depth maps, pose trajectories) or new diffusion backbones (latent‑space vs. pixel‑space models).

### Final Remarks  

Orthogonal Finetuning reframes the problem of adapting massive text‑to‑image diffusion models from an **unstructured weight‑space search** to a **structured rotation on the hypersphere**.  By doing so, it simultaneously safeguards the delicate relational geometry learned during massive pretraining and grants enough freedom to inject new semantics via controlled angular changes.  The empirical evidence—from faster convergence and higher fidelity in subject generation to tighter control adherence in edge‑to‑image or landmark‑to‑face tasks—validates the theory.  Moreover, the method is **model‑agnostic, parameter‑efficient, and inference‑free**, making it an attractive tool for anyone looking to tailor diffusion models without sacrificing the original model’s generative power.
