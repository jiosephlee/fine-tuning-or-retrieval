\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why is softmax computation a bottleneck despite its low FLOP count?
Question:
The paper states that the exponential unit on H100 provides only ~3.9 TFLOPS while FP16 matmul offers ~989 TFLOPS, leading to softmax taking up to 50% of cycle time. However, softmax only processes N² values which seems negligible compared to the GEMM work. Can someone explain why the hardware throughput disparity makes softmax a bottleneck in practice, and how the ping‑pong scheduling mitigates this issue?
Answer:
**Answer**

The soft‑max that follows the first GEMM in a Transformer attention head looks cheap – it only touches the $N^{2}$ scores that have just been produced.  
What makes it a *real* bottleneck on an H100 is not the amount of data but the *throughput* of the hardware unit that implements the exponential (and the surrounding add‑multiply‑divide) operations.

### Why the FLOP count is misleading

On Hopper the Tensor‑Core (exposed through the WGMMA instruction) can sustain about **989 TFLOP/s** of FP16 matrix‑multiply work.  
In contrast, the special‑function unit (SFU) that evaluates $\exp(\cdot)$ (and the subsequent scaling needed for a stable soft‑max) can deliver only **≈3.9 TFLOP/s**.  
These numbers are derived from the device specifications: each SM can issue 16 SFU operations per clock, and with 132 SMs running at 1.83 GHz this yields roughly $3.9\times10^{12}$ floating‑point “special‑function” operations per second.

Consider a typical configuration: head dimension $d=128$, sequence length $N=8192$.  
The forward pass does two GEMMs of size $B_{r}\times d$ by $d\times B_{c}$. The total FP16 FLOP count for those GEMMs is on the order of  

$$
2\,B_{r}B_{c}d \;\approx\; 2\,(8192^{2}) \times 128 \;\approx\; 1.7\times10^{12}\;\text{FLOP}.
$$

The soft‑max needs, per block, one exponentiation and one reduction per element of the same $N^{2}$ matrix, i.e. about  

$$
2\,N^{2}\;\approx\;1.3\times10^{8}\;\text{operations}.
$$

Even though this is two orders of magnitude fewer *operations*, each exponentiation runs on a unit that is **256 × slower** in terms of FLOP‑rate than the Tensor‑Core.  
If we naïvely divide the work by the respective peak throughputs we obtain roughly

$$
\frac{1.7\times10^{12}}{989\times10^{12}}\;\approx\;1.7\text{ ms}
\qquad
\frac{1.3\times10^{8}}{3.9\times10^{12}}\;\approx\;0.033\text{ ms}.
$$

Because the two kernels cannot be perfectly overlapped in a naïve implementation, the exponentiation stage occupies about half of the total wall‑clock time – exactly what the authors observe (“softmax takes up to 50 % of cycle time”). The effect becomes even worse with FP8: tensor‑core throughput doubles while the SFU stays at 3.9 TFLOP/s, so softmax can dominate an even larger fraction of the runtime.

### How “ping‑pong” (producer–consumer) scheduling helps

The key insight behind FlashAttention‑3 is that the exponential does **not** have to wait for *all* GEMM work to finish; it only needs the scores from *one* block of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$. Hopper’s Tensor Cores are **asynchronous**: once a WGMMA instruction is issued, execution proceeds in parallel with later instructions as long as data dependencies are respected.

FlashAttention‑3 splits each CTA into two warp‑groups:

| Warp‑group | Role |
|------------|------|
| Producer   | Issues TMA loads (move tiles of $\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{K},\,\mathbf{V}$ from HBM to shared memory). |
| Consumer   | Issues WGMMA GEMMs and performs softmax on the already‑available block. |

Using a circular shared‑memory buffer with depth $s$, the producer continuously streams tiles ahead while the consumer works on previously loaded ones. Crucially, after computing $\mathbf{S}_{j}= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}_{j}^{\top}$ (GEMM 0 for block $j$), the consumer **does not stall** waiting for softmax to finish before launching GEMM 1 for block $j+1$. Instead:

1. **Issue GEMM 0** (`Q × K_j^T`) and immediately start loading `K_{j+1}` and `V_j` (still in parallel because TMA is async).
2. **While GEMM 0 is in flight**, start computing `exp(S_j - m_j)` and updating the running sums $\ell, m$. This uses the SFU but does not block the tensor cores.
3. **When GEMM 0 completes**, launch GEMM 1 (`P_j × V_j`) which consumes the just‑computed softmax probabilities.
4. Meanwhile, launch GEMM 0 for block `j+1`.  

Visually this looks like two warps “ping‑ponging”: while warp‑group A is busy with softmax on block $j$, warp‑group B is busy feeding tensor cores with GEMM for block $j+1$; then they swap roles on the next iteration.

Because both warp groups run concurrently on different functional units (SFU vs Tensor Core), their latencies overlap almost perfectly: every clock tick that would have been idle while waiting for exponentials now carries useful matrix‑multiply work. In practice this raises effective tensor‑core utilization from ~570 TFLOP/s (softmax serialized) to ~620–640 TFLOP/s, i.e. a **~10–15 % speedup** even before low‑precision tricks are applied.

### Summary

* The softmax kernel processes far fewer arithmetic operations than a GEMM, but it runs on a hardware unit whose peak throughput is two orders of magnitude lower than that of Tensor Cores.
* On Hopper this mismatch means that, if executed sequentially, softmax can consume ~50 % of total cycle time despite its small FLOP count.
* FlashAttention‑3’s *producer–consumer* (ping‑pong) scheduling separates data movement from computation, lets two warp groups operate on different pipeline stages simultaneously, and overlaps low‑throughput exponentials with high‑throughput GEMMs.
* The result is a much higher overall TFLOP/s utilization (up to 740 TFLOP/s for FP16), and when FP8 tensor cores are used—doubling matrix throughput while keeping SFU throughput unchanged—the overlap becomes even more critical.

Thus, “softmax is a bottleneck” is not about how many numbers it touches; it’s about **where those numbers are processed** inside the GPU architecture and how efficiently we can hide its slow unit behind faster tensor‑core work. The ping‑pong schedule achieves exactly that hiding.
