### 1.  Multi‑Head Attention: Formal Definition  

The attention mechanism is the heart of the Transformer architecture.  For a single head we are given three matrices  

\[
\mathbf{Q},\;\mathbf{K},\;\mathbf{V}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},
\]

where  

* \(N\) is the sequence length (the number of tokens processed in parallel),  
* \(d\) is the head dimension (the length of the vector that each token is projected onto).  

From these matrices we compute a similarity score matrix  

\[
\mathbf{S}= \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},
\qquad\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} .
\]

The scaling factor \(\alpha\) prevents the dot‑product from growing too large as \(d\) increases, which would otherwise cause the soft‑max to saturate.  

Because \(\mathbf{S}\) contains exponentials, numerical overflow is a risk.  The standard trick is to subtract the maximum value in each row before applying the exponential:

\[
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}_{i,:}= \mathbf{S}_{i,:} - \max_{j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j},
\qquad
\mathbf{P}= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S})_{i,j}
      =\frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}^{\prime}_{i,j})}{\sum_{k}\exp(\mathbf{S}^{\prime}_{i,k})}.
\]

The resulting probability matrix \(\mathbf{P}\) is row‑stochastic (each row sums to one).  The final output for the head is the weighted sum of the value vectors:

\[
\mathbf{O}= \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}.
\]

For a full **multi‑head attention** (MHA) layer we repeat the above computation with independent projection matrices for each head, i.e. we have \(\{\mathbf{Q}^{(h)},\mathbf{K}^{(h)},\mathbf{V}^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^{H}\) where \(H\) is the number of heads.  Each head produces \(\mathbf{O}^{(h)}\) of shape \((N,d)\); these are concatenated along the head dimension (producing \((N, H\!\times\! d)\)) and finally projected back to the model dimension with another learned linear layer.  The description above therefore applies to a single head; the extension to multiple heads is embarrassingly parallel across heads and batches.

### 2.  Backward Pass: Gradients of Q, K, V  

Training a Transformer requires the gradients of the loss \(\phi\) with respect to the three inputs of the attention head.  Using the notation  

\[
\mathbf{d}(\cdot)=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial (\cdot)} ,
\qquad 
\mathbf{dO}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
\]

to denote the upstream gradient flowing into the output \(\mathbf{O}\), the chain of derivatives is:

1. **Gradient w.r.t. V**  
   \[
   \mathbf{dV}= \mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{dO}.
   \]

   This follows directly from \(\mathbf{O}= \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\) and the fact that \(\partial (\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V})/\partial \mathbf{V}= \mathbf{P}^{\top}\).

2. **Gradient w.r.t. the soft‑max probabilities**  
   \[
   \mathbf{dP}= \mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top}.
   \]

   Here we differentiate the matrix‑multiply \(\mathbf{O}= \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{P}\).

3. **Gradient through the soft‑max**  
   The soft‑max is applied row‑wise, so for a single row vector \(\mathbf{p}= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{s})\) we have  

   \[
   \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial \mathbf{s}}
   =\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\top}.
   \]

   Multiplying this Jacobian by \(\mathbf{dP}\) row‑wise yields  

   \[
   \mathbf{dS}= \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})
            = (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{P}) - \mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{\top})\odot \mathbf{dP},
   \]

   where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication, applied independently to each row.

4. **Gradients w.r.t. Q and K**  
   Recall \(\mathbf{S}= \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\).  Differentiating gives  

   \[
   \mathbf{dQ}= \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}\,\mathbf{K},
   \qquad
   \mathbf{dK}= \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}^{\top}\,\mathbf{Q}.
   \]

These formulas expose a crucial pattern: all backward‑pass operations are again matrix multiplications (GEMMs) of the same shape as in the forward pass, plus an element‑wise soft‑max Jacobian.  Because the forward pass already requires two GEMMs (\(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\) and \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\)), the backward pass needs three GEMMs (for \(\mathbf{dV}\), \(\mathbf{dQ}\), \(\mathbf{dK}\)) and a few cheap element‑wise kernels.  This symmetry is what enables the **FlashAttention** family to reuse the same low‑level tiling and scheduling ideas for both forward and backward phases.

### 3.  GPU Memory Hierarchy  

Understanding how data moves on a modern NVIDIA GPU is essential for appreciating why FlashAttention‑3 can attain high utilization.  The hierarchy can be visualized as a pyramid of decreasing capacity but increasing bandwidth:

| Level | Name | Capacity | Typical Bandwidth | Access Pattern |
|------|------|----------|-------------------|----------------|
| 0 | **Registers (RMEM)** | ~256 KB per SM (private to each thread) | ~ on‑chip, effectively limitless for a thread’s own data | Register‑to‑register, low‑latency |
| 1 | **Shared Memory (SMEM)** | 64–128 KB per SM, programmer‑managed, banking | ~128 bytes per clock per SM (≈ 184 GB/s on H100) | Cooperative among threads of a CTA; enables low‑latency data reuse |
| 2 | **L2 Cache** | ~40 MB (device‑wide) | ~1.5 TB/s on H100 | Transparent caching of global memory reads/writes |
| 3 | **Global Memory (GMEM / HBM)** | Tens of GB (off‑chip DRAM) | ~2 TB/s on H100 (HBM2e) | Main storage of model parameters, activations, intermediate tensors |

**Why the hierarchy matters:**  
* The *standard* attention implementation materializes the full score matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) (size \(N\times N\)) and the soft‑max probabilities \(\mathbf{P}\) in GMEM, incurring two expensive global‑memory passes.  
* FlashAttention‑2 eliminated those passes by keeping each block of \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{P}\) in SMEM, doing a *local* soft‑max, and writing only the final output \(\mathbf{O}\) back to GMEM.  
* FlashAttention‑3 pushes the idea further: by overlapping data movement (via the **Tensor Memory Accelerator**, TMA) with computation (via asynchronous **Tensor Cores**, WGMMA) the kernel can keep the SMEM pipeline full, achieving near‑peak GEMM throughput.

### 4.  Thread Hierarchy on Hopper  

NVIDIA GPUs expose a nested parallelism model that maps software concepts (threads, warps, threadblocks) onto hardware resources (SMs, GPCs, clusters).  On the Hopper architecture the relevant levels are:

1. **Thread** – the smallest unit of execution; each executes its own instruction stream and has its own registers.  
2. **Warp** – a group of 32 threads that always execute the same instruction (SIMT).  Warps are scheduled together and share a program counter.  
3. **Warp‑Group** – a collection of 4 *contiguous* warps (i.e., 128 threads).  Hopper introduces **warp‑group‑wide matrix‑multiply‑accumulate (WGMMA)** instructions that operate collectively across the 4 warps, delivering the performance of a Tensor Core cluster to a single CTA.  
4. **Cooperative Thread Array (CTA) / Threadblock** – a set of warps that cooperate via shared memory and barrier synchronization.  All warps of a CTA reside on the same SM; they can synchronize with `__syncthreads()` or, in Hopper, with `bar.sync`.  
5. **Threadblock Cluster** – a higher‑level grouping of CTAs that are scheduled on the same **Graphics Processing Cluster (GPC)**, enabling low‑latency inter‑CTA communication on Hopper.  

The *warp‑specialization* technique used in FlashAttention‑3 deliberately assigns different logical roles to the warps inside a CTA:

* **Producer warps** issue TMA loads (global → shared) for the next block of \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\).  
* **Consumer warps** perform the GEMMs (via WGMMA) and the soft‑max on the data that is already resident in SMEM.

Because the TMA engine and the Tensor Cores are *asynchronous*—they can progress independently of the regular CUDA core pipeline—this division allows the two groups of warps to run in parallel, hiding the latency of global‑memory traffic behind compute.

### 5.  Asynchrony Primitives on Hopper  

#### 5.1 Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)  

TMA is a dedicated hardware block that moves data between GMEM (HBM) and SMEM without involving the scalar CUDA cores.  Its salient properties:

* **Non‑blocking:** a warp can issue a TMA load and immediately continue issuing other instructions; the actual data transfer proceeds in the background.  
* **Fine‑grained completion notification:** via `membar.cuda.async` or `bar.sync` the programmer can query whether a particular tile of data has arrived in SMEM.  
* **Zero‑copy to registers:** WGMMA can read its input directly from SMEM, eliminating an extra copy step.

In FlashAttention‑3 the producer warpgroup repeatedly issues TMA loads for successive key/value blocks (\(\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j\)), each placed into a *circular* SMEM buffer with \(s\) stages.  As soon as a stage is filled, a small flag is set so that the consumer warpgroup may start computing on that stage.

#### 5.2 Asynchronous Tensor Cores: WGMMA  

The **warp‑group‑wide matrix‑multiply‑accumulate (WGMMA)** instruction is Hopper’s low‑latency, high‑throughput GEMM primitive.  It differs from earlier Tensor‑Core instructions in two ways:

1. **Asynchrony:** WGMMA does not stall the issuing warp; it launches a micro‑kernel on the Tensor Core array and returns immediately.  The result becomes visible only after a later `bar.sync` or a dependent instruction.  
2. **Warp‑Group Scope:** The instruction implicitly gathers data from the *entire* warp group (four warps) and deposits the accumulator into registers distributed across the warps.  This matches the natural tiling of FlashAttention, where a block of \(\mathbf{Q}\) (size \(B_r \times d\)) multiplies a block of \(\mathbf{K}^{\top}\) (size \(d \times B_c\)).

Because the Tensor Core pipeline can be kept busy while the producer warps are still fetching the next block, the overall *occupancy*—the fraction of time the hardware is doing useful work—increases dramatically.  The paper reports utilization climbing from roughly 35 % (FlashAttention‑2) to about 75 % for FP16 on an H100.

### 6.  Low‑Precision Number Formats and Hardware Support  

#### 6.1  FP16 and BF16  

* **FP16** (half‑precision) uses 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, and 10 mantissa bits.  It became widely used after the Pascal generation (2017) because Tensor Cores provided a 2× speedup over FP32.  
* **BF16** (brain float) keeps the 8‑bit exponent of FP32 but drops mantissa precision to 7 bits.  Ampere (2020) added native BF16 Tensor‑Core support, giving the same throughput as FP16 while preserving a larger dynamic range—useful for training stability.

Both formats are *FP32‑accumulating*: the GEMM result is accumulated in an FP32 register to avoid overflow, then optionally cast back to the source precision.

#### 6.2  FP8 (e4m3)  

Hopper introduced native **FP8** Tensor‑Core support.  The paper focuses on the *e4m3* variant:

* 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits.  
* Dynamic range is comparable to BF16 (thanks to the 4‑bit exponent) but the mantissa is much coarser, leading to higher quantization error.

FP8 GEMM is executed by the same WGMMA micro‑kernel, but with a **layout restriction**:

* The operand matrices must be stored in **k‑major** order (contiguous along the reduction dimension \(K\)).  This is stricter than FP16, where both *mn‑major* and *k‑major* layouts are accepted.  
* The accumulator remains in FP32, so a conversion from the FP8 operand layout to the FP32 accumulator layout is required.  FlashAttention‑3 solves this by a lightweight register permutation (byte‑permute) after the first GEMM, and by performing an **in‑kernel transpose** of the value tiles with LDSM/STSM instructions.

#### 6.3  Accuracy Techniques for FP8  

Because FP8 has only 3 mantissa bits, outlier values (common in large language models) can dominate the quantization error.  FlashAttention‑3 introduces two mitigations:

1. **Block Quantization** – instead of a single scaling factor per tensor, each tile (e.g., a \(B_r\times d\) block of \(\mathbf{Q}\)) receives its own scale.  This significantly reduces the relative error for tiles that contain large values, while incurring negligible extra memory traffic (the scales are kept in registers).  

2. **Incoherent Processing** – multiply \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{K}\) by a *random orthogonal matrix* \(\mathbf{M}\) before quantization.  Since \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}=I\), the product \((\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^{\top}\) equals the original \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\).  The orthogonal transformation spreads large entries across many positions, making the distribution within each block more uniform and thus reducing the per‑block quantization error.  In practice \(\mathbf{M}\) is realized as a product of a random diagonal of \(\pm 1\) and a Hadamard matrix, which can be applied in \(O(d\log d)\) time and fused with other linear operations (e.g., rotary embeddings) at no extra cost.

Empirically, these two tricks lower the FP8 root‑mean‑square error by **2.6 ×** compared with a naïve per‑tensor FP8 quantization baseline.

### 7.  Summary  

The mathematical core of attention—the two GEMMs surrounding a row‑wise soft‑max—maps naturally onto the GPU execution model: the heavy matrix multiplications benefit from the high‑throughput Tensor Cores (WGMMA), while the light element‑wise soft‑max (exponential, reduction) can be hidden behind the GEMM pipeline using **asynchronous execution**.  By splitting warps into **producer** (TMA‑driven data loading) and **consumer** (WGMMA‑driven computation) groups and by employing a circular SMEM buffer, FlashAttention‑3 keeps the Tensor Cores busy and achieves up to **75 %** of the theoretical TFLOPs on an H100.

Furthermore, Hopper’s native FP8 support offers a theoretical **2 ×** increase in arithmetic throughput.  The strict k‑major layout and the limited mantissa are obstacles, but they can be surmounted with in‑kernel transposes, register permutations, **block quantization**, and **incoherent processing**.  The resulting FP8 implementation reaches close to **1.2 PFLOPs/s** while maintaining numerical fidelity that is markedly better than a naïve FP8 baseline.

Understanding the interplay between the mathematical structure of multi‑head attention and the hierarchical, asynchronous hardware of modern GPUs is therefore essential for appreciating why FlashAttention‑3 constitutes a significant step forward in both speed and accuracy.  The rest of this textbook will build on these foundations to explore the detailed algorithmic design, implementation tricks, and experimental evaluation presented in the paper.
