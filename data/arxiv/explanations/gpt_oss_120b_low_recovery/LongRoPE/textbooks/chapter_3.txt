\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: Empirical Analysis of Non‑Uniform Positional Interpolation
================================================================

In this chapter we detail the experimental investigations that uncovered two distinct sources of non‑uniformity in Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) – a non‑uniformity across the individual RoPE dimensions and a non‑uniformity across token positions within a sequence.  By explicitly modelling these two forms of non‑uniformity, LongRoPE is able to obtain substantially better perplexity scores both when the model is *not* fine‑tuned on longer sequences and when a brief fine‑tuning stage is employed.  The chapter is organized around the design of the evolutionary search that discovers per‑dimension scaling factors, followed by three empirical findings that illustrate the impact of each non‑uniformity, and ends with a concise synthesis of the implications for context‑window extension strategies.

Design of the Evolutionary Search for Optimal Per‑Dimension Scaling
-------------------------------------------------------------------

The objective of the search is to determine a set of rescaling factors \(\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\) – one for each RoPE dimension – together with a token‑position threshold \(\hat n\) that governs which early tokens retain their original RoPE angles.  Formally, the modified RoPE for a token at position \(n\) and dimension \(i\) is

\[
\bigl[\cos\!\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr),
\;\sin\!\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr)\bigr],
\]

where \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\) (with the default base \(\theta = 10{,}000\)), and

\[
\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1, & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n\ge \hat n .
\end{cases}
\]

Thus \(\lambda_i\) controls how strongly the original rotary angle is compressed for positions beyond \(\hat n\).  The search space is deliberately large: each \(\lambda_i\) may take any value in the interval \([1.0,\,1.25\,s]\) with a step size of \(0.01\) (where \(s\) is the target extension ratio), and \(\hat n\) is chosen from the discrete set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\).  For a 4‑k token base window and an extension ratio \(s=8\), the raw combinatorial space exceeds \(10^{167}\) possibilities, making exhaustive enumeration infeasible.

To explore this space efficiently, LongRoPE adopts an evolutionary algorithm (Algorithm 1).  The key innovations are:

1. **Optimized Initial Population** – three well‑known interpolation schemes (linear Position Interpolation, NTK‑based interpolation, and YaRN) are inserted as seed individuals.  The remaining members of the initial population are generated by mutating these seeds with probability \(p\), ensuring that the early search region already contains promising baselines.

2. **Monotonically Non‑Decreasing Constraint** – a theoretical insight from the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) literature suggests that higher‑frequency (low‑index) dimensions should undergo less compression than lower‑frequency (high‑index) dimensions.  Consequently, we enforce \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) for all \(i\).  This reduces the number of candidate vectors dramatically while preserving the search’s expressive power.

During each generation, the perplexity of the target LLM on a small validation subset (five random PG19 samples of at least the target length) is computed for every individual that satisfies the monotonicity constraint.  The top‑\(k\) individuals become parents for the next generation; mutation and crossover are performed under the same monotonicity rule, and the process repeats for \(\mathcal T\) iterations.  The individual with the lowest validation perplexity is returned as the optimal set of \(\{\lambda_i\},\hat n\).

Finding 1: Dimension‑wise Non‑Uniformity Improves 8× Extension Without Fine‑Tuning
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first empirical investigation asked whether a single global scaling factor (as in classic linear PI) is sufficient for extreme extensions.  By allowing each RoPE dimension to adopt its own \(\lambda_i\), the evolutionary search discovered highly non‑uniform patterns: low‑frequency dimensions (large \(i\)) tended to receive larger \(\lambda_i\) (more compression), whereas high‑frequency dimensions (small \(i\)) retained values close to 1 (minimal compression).  Table 1 (referenced in the paper) shows perplexities on PG19 and Proof‑Pile for several baselines and for the searched non‑uniform configuration, all *without* any fine‑tuning.

The searched configuration reduced perplexity by roughly **30 %** relative to linear PI at an 8× extension (from 4 k to 32 k tokens) and outperformed both NTK‑based and YaRN methods across all evaluated lengths.  Notably, YaRN’s performance degraded sharply after 7 k tokens because its hand‑crafted grouping does not adapt to the target extension ratio; the learned per‑dimension scaling, in contrast, preserves the information content of the most critical dimensions, thereby mitigating the “crowding” of positions that plagues uniform scaling.  This result establishes that *dimension‑wise non‑uniformity is essential* for achieving large extensions without any additional training.

Finding 2: Preserving Early Token Positions Reduces Interpolation Loss
---------------------------------------------------------------------

The second set of experiments focused on the token‑position non‑uniformity encoded by \(\hat n\).  The hypothesis was that the first \(\hat n\) tokens receive disproportionately large attention scores (as observed in prior work on streaming LLMs and LM‑Infinite), and thus should retain their original rotary angles.  To test this, the authors extended the context window to 8 k and 16 k using PI and NTK while systematically varying \(\hat n\) among \(\{0,2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256\}\).

Results (Table 2 in the paper) reveal two systematic trends:

1. **Performance Improves When Early Tokens Are Unchanged.**  For any fixed extension ratio, perplexity drops as \(\hat n\) increases from 0 to a modest value (typically between 16 and 64) because the model can rely on exact positional information for the most attended tokens.

2. **The Optimal \(\hat n\) Depends on the Target Length.**  When extending to 8 k, the best \(\hat n\) lies around 32–64, whereas for 16 k the optimum shifts toward larger values (≈ 128).  This aligns with the intuition that longer extensions increase the proportion of new positions, so preserving a larger prefix becomes beneficial.

These observations confirm that *position‑wise non‑uniformity* – i.e., applying interpolation selectively to later tokens – is a simple yet powerful lever for reducing the catastrophic loss of information introduced by naïve global scaling.

Finding 3: Non‑Uniform Interpolation Benefits Both Fine‑Tuned and Non‑Fine‑Tuned Regimes
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Having demonstrated improvements in the pure inference setting, the authors examined whether the same non‑uniform scaling also facilitates fine‑tuning on longer sequences.  They fine‑tuned LLaMA2‑7B on a 64 k context window using the per‑dimension and per‑position scaling discovered for that length.  Perplexities were measured both **before** and **after** fine‑tuning, and compared against baselines that use PI or YaRN scaling (Table 3).

Key observations include:

* **Before Fine‑Tuning** the searched scaling already yields lower perplexity than PI or YaRN, confirming the benefit of a better initialization.
* **After Fine‑Tuning** the gap widens: the non‑uniformly initialized model attains perplexities that are up to 20 % lower than those of the baseline‑initialized models.  The fine‑tuned model thus requires fewer updates to adapt to the newly introduced positions because the initial RoPE already respects the underlying information entropy of the transformer.

These results demonstrate that the *same non‑uniform interpolation scheme plays a dual role*: it serves as an effective out‑of‑the‑box extension method, and it provides a superior starting point for subsequent fine‑tuning when extremely long contexts (e.g., > 100 k tokens) are required.

Summary of Insights and Their Implications for Extension Strategies
-------------------------------------------------------------------

The empirical studies in this chapter converge on a coherent picture:

1. **RoPE dimensions are not equally informative.**  High‑frequency dimensions should experience minimal interpolation, while low‑frequency dimensions can tolerate stronger compression.  Allowing each dimension to adopt its own rescaling factor dramatically reduces information loss and enables 8× extensions without any fine‑tuning.

2. **Early token positions carry disproportionate importance.**  By leaving the first \(\hat n\) positions untouched, the model retains precise positional cues for the tokens that dominate attention, further mitigating the degradation caused by position scaling.

3. **A well‑chosen non‑uniform interpolation provides a strong initialization for fine‑tuning.**  When a modest amount of fine‑tuning (e.g., 400–600 steps) is affordable, starting from the searched scaling yields faster convergence and superior final perplexity compared with naïve baselines.

These insights directly motivate the three pillars of the LongRoPE methodology:

* **Comprehensive Evolutionary Search** – to discover the optimal \(\{\lambda_i\},\hat n\) pair for any target extension ratio.
* **Progressive Extension** – first apply the search to reach a medium‑size window (e.g., 256 k), fine‑tune briefly, then search again to leap to the final ultra‑long window (2048 k) without further training.
* **Short‑Window Recovery** – after extreme extensions, a second, constrained search on short lengths (4 k–8 k) restores performance for traditional tasks.

In sum, the empirical evidence establishes non‑uniformity across both dimensions and token positions as the cornerstone for reliable, high‑quality context‑window extension in RoPE‑based LLMs.
