\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # Problem Formulation: Optimizing RoPE Rescaling

In order to push the context window of large language models (LLMs) far beyond the limits imposed by their original training regime, we must rethink how the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) is applied to token positions that were never seen during pre‑training.  The **LongRoPE** framework treats this redesign as a well‑defined optimization problem: find a set of rescaling factors that modifies the RoPE angles so that the model’s next‑token prediction loss—measured by perplexity—remains as low as possible even when the model processes sequences that are hundreds of times longer than those used during its initial training.  

This chapter develops the formalism underlying that problem.  We start from the canonical RoPE formulation, introduce the two control variables—per‑dimension scaling factors \( \lambda_i \) and an early‑token threshold \( \hat{n} \)—and then articulate the objective, the constraints that make the search tractable, and the combinatorial nature of the space that must be explored.

---

## 1. RoPE with Non‑Uniform Rescale Factors

Rotary positional encoding injects a deterministic sinusoidal rotation into each query–key pair in the self‑attention mechanism.  For a token occupying absolute position \( n \) (counting from zero), the traditional RoPE vector of dimension \( d \) can be written as

\[
\underbrace{\bigl[\,\cos (n\theta_0),\; \sin (n\theta_0),\; \cos (n\theta_1),\; \sin (n\theta_1),\;\dots,\; \cos (n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin (n\theta_{d/2-1}) \,\bigr]}_{\text{Eq.\;(1)}}
\]

where the *rotation frequencies* obey  

\[
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},\qquad \beta = \theta^{2/d},
\]

and the default base \( \theta \) is \(10\,000\).  The term \( \beta \) conveniently expresses the frequency scaling across dimensions: the angle contributed by dimension \( i \) is simply \( n / \beta^{\,i} \).

When extending the context window, we cannot directly use the raw angles for positions \( n > L \) (the original maximum length).  Instead, we apply a **rescaling function** \( \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \) that modifies the angle before the sinusoid is evaluated:

\[
\text{RoPE}^{(i)}_n \;=\;
\bigl[\,\cos\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \, \tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\bigr),
\;\sin\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \, \tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\bigr) \bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

The function \( \mathbb{I}(\cdot) \) encodes two **non‑uniformities** discovered empirically in the LongRoPE analysis:

| Variable | Meaning | Effect on Eq. (2) |
|---|---|---|
| \( \hat{\lambda}_i \) (or equivalently \( \lambda_i = 1/\hat{\lambda}_i \)) | Per‑dimension scaling factor that determines how much the angle for dimension \( i \) is *compressed* (if \( \lambda_i>1 \)) or *expanded* (if \( \lambda_i<1 \)). | For positions \( n \ge \hat{n} \), the angle becomes \( \tfrac{n}{\lambda_i \,\beta^{\,i}} \). |
| \( \hat{n} \) | Early‑token threshold; the first \( \hat{n} \) token positions are left **unaltered** (i.e., \( \mathbb{I}=1\)). | For \( n < \hat{n} \), the original RoPE angle \( \tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} \) is kept, preserving the high‑attention signal of the initial tokens. |

Thus, the complete piecewise definition of \( \mathbb{I} \) reads

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1, & n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
\tag{3}
\]

When \( \hat{n}=0 \), every token is subject to a scaling factor; when \( \lambda_i = 1\) for all \(i\), the embedding reduces to the original RoPE (no interpolation).  The classic **linear positional interpolation (PI)** corresponds to a uniform choice \( \lambda_i = s\) (the desired extension ratio) for all dimensions and \( \hat{n}=0\).  LongRoPE generalizes PI by allowing **dimension‑wise** and **position‑wise** non‑uniformities.

## 2. Objective: Minimizing Next‑Token Loss on Ultra‑Long Sequences

The ultimate purpose of any positional modification is to preserve the model’s ability to predict the next token accurately.  In language modeling, the standard scalar loss is the *negative log‑likelihood* (NLL) of the true next token; perplexity \( \mathcal{P} \) is \( \exp(\text{NLL}) \).  Denote the LLM (including its attention layers and output head) as a black‑box function  

\[
\mathcal{M}_{\!\text{LLM}}: (\mathbf{x},\;\text{RoPE}) \mapsto \bigl\{ p_{\theta}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t}) \bigr\}_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{x}|},
\]

where \( \mathbf{x} \) is a tokenized document of length \( |\mathbf{x}| \).  The loss incurred on a single document is  

\[
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x};\lambda,\hat{n}) = 
- \frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{x}|} \log p_{\theta}\!\bigl(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t};\lambda,\hat{n}\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

Our **optimization problem** is then to select the collection of per‑dimension scalings \( \{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1} \) and the early‑token threshold \( \hat{n} \) that minimize the expected loss over a representative set of ultra‑long documents \( \mathbf{X} \).  Formally,

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\underset{\lambda,\;\hat{n}}{\text{minimize}} \quad &
\frac{1}{|\mathbf{X}|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in \mathbf{X}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x};\lambda,\hat{n})\\[4pt]
\text{subject to}\quad &
\lambda_i \in [1,\; s\!\times\!1.25]\quad\forall i,\\
&\hat{n}\in \mathcal{N},\qquad \mathcal{N}= \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{5}
\]

*Key elements of the objective*:

* **Dataset \( \mathbf{X} \)** – The documents must be at least as long as the target context window \( L' \) (e.g., 2048 k tokens).  In practice the authors use a few randomly sampled validation documents from large corpora such as PG‑19 or the Pile.
* **Extension ratio \( s = L'/L \)** – Determines the upper bound of the search interval for each \( \lambda_i \).  The factor \(1.25\) provides a modest safety margin so that the algorithm can explore slightly more aggressive interpolation if it proves beneficial.
* **Per‑dimension bounds** – The lower bound \(1\) corresponds to *pure extrapolation* (no compression), while the upper bound reflects the most aggressive uniform compression (the same as linear PI, multiplied by 1.25 to tolerate slight over‑compression.
* **Early‑token set \( \mathcal{N} \)** – These are discrete choices for how many initial tokens retain the original RoPE; values are chosen based on empirical curves that show performance improvements when a modest prefix is left untouched.

Because we evaluate the loss on a **finite validation subset**, the optimization is effectively a **black‑box function evaluation** problem: for each candidate \((\lambda,\hat{n})\) we must instantiate the modified RoPE inside the LLM, run forward passes over the selected documents, and compute perplexity.  This is computationally expensive, especially when \(L'\) reaches the order of millions of tokens, which is why a sophisticated search strategy (evolutionary search with monotonicity constraints) is required.

## 3. Constraints and Design Choices

The raw combinatorial space defined by Eq. (5) is astronomically large.  For an extension ratio of \( s=4 \) and a model with \( d=128 \) (hence \(d/2=64\) rotary pairs), a naïve grid with step size \(0.01\) yields

\[
\underbrace{400^{64}}_{\text{per‑dimension choices}} 
\times \underbrace{14}_{text{possible }\hat{n}\text{ values}}
\;\approx\; 4\times10^{167}
\]

possible configurations.  To render the problem tractable, LongRoPE imposes **two disciplined constraints**:

1. **Monotonicity across dimensions**  
   The scaling factors must satisfy  

   \[
   \lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}.
   \tag{6}
   \]

   This mirrors the intuition behind the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis: lower‑frequency dimensions (larger \(i\)) are less sensitive to position crowding and can tolerate larger compression, whereas high‑frequency dimensions (small \(i\)) should be changed more conservatively.  Enforcing monotonicity prunes the search space dramatically; only sequences that respect (6) are evaluated.

2. **Discrete early‑token thresholds**  
   Rather than treating \( \hat{n} \) as a continuous variable, the authors restrict it to a short list of powers‑of‑two and small multiples thereof (see the set \( \mathcal{N} \) above).  Empirical studies indicate that performance exhibits diminishing returns beyond a few hundred initial tokens, so a coarse discretization suffices.

Additional practical design choices include:

* **Population‑based evolutionary search** – A modest population (e.g., 64 candidates) is evolved over a fixed number of generations (≈ 40).  The algorithm evaluates perplexity for each individual, retains the top‑k, and applies mutation/crossover while respecting the monotonicity constraint.
* **Warm‑starting the population** – Individuals corresponding to the classical PI, NTK, and YaRN configurations are explicitly inserted into the initial population, ensuring that the search never performs worse than these baselines.
* **Early stopping of evaluation** – If during a forward pass the accumulated loss already exceeds the best known perplexity, the inference can be aborted, saving time.

These constraints shape the search into a **structured combinatorial optimization** problem: the goal is to discover a *sequence* of scalars (the \( \lambda_i \)’s) together with a small integer (\( \hat{n} \)) that yields the lowest possible language‑model loss under the expensive black‑box evaluation.

---

## 4. Why This Is a Combinatorial Optimization Problem

At its core, the task is to find the *best* point in a discretized high‑dimensional space.  The difficulty stems from three intertwined factors:

1. **Exponential combinatorics** – The number of possible assignments grows exponentially with the number of rotary dimensions. Even after imposing monotonicity, the space remains combinatorial because each dimension can independently adopt any value within a bounded interval, subject only to ordering constraints.

2. **Non‑convex, black‑box objective** – The perplexity resulting from a particular RoPE configuration cannot be expressed analytically; it is obtained by running the full LLM on long texts.  The loss surface is highly non‑convex, riddled with plateaus and rapid spikes when a sampled configuration creates severe “crowding” of positional angles.

3. **Expensive function evaluations** – Computing perplexity on millions of tokens is time‑consuming and memory‑intensive.  Hence, we cannot afford exhaustive search or high‑resolution grid sweeps.  Instead, we rely on **evolutionary heuristics** that balance exploration (via mutation and crossover) with exploitation (by always preserving the current best individuals).

The combination of exponential search space, black‑box evaluation, and high computational cost classifies the problem squarely within the realm of **combinatorial optimization**.  Evolutionary algorithms are well‑suited because they need only relative ranking (better vs. worse perplexities) rather than gradients, and they naturally respect structural constraints (like monotonicity) by rejecting or repairing infeasible offspring.

### Summary

In this chapter we have:

* Re‑derived the RoPE formulation and introduced a piecewise scaling function that captures two empirically observed non‑uniformities: per‑dimension scaling \( \lambda_i \) and an early‑token threshold \( \hat{n} \).
* Formalized the objective of minimizing the next‑token loss (perplexity) across ultra‑long validation documents.
* Defined concrete bounds for \( \lambda_i \) and discrete choices for \( \hat{n} \), and explained why monotonicity across dimensions is imposed.
* Highlighted that the resulting search problem is a high‑dimensional, combinatorial optimization task, motivating the use of an evolutionary search algorithm with carefully designed initialization and constraint handling.

These foundations enable the **LongRoPE** pipeline to discover highly effective, non‑uniform positional interpolations that allow modern LLMs to process sequences of up to two million tokens while retaining performance on their original short‑context workloads.
