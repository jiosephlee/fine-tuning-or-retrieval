\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What are the limitations of LongRoPE when applied to architectures that do not use RoPE?
Question:
LongRoPE is described as applicable to any LLM based on RoPE. For models using absolute sinusoidal embeddings or learned positional embeddings, could a similar evolutionary search be performed, or are there fundamental obstacles? How would one adapt the search space and constraints for non‑rotary positional schemes?
Answer:
**Answer**

LongRoPE works by re‑scaling the rotary angles that appear in the *Rotary Positional Embedding* (RoPE) used by many recent LLMs (LLaMA‑2, Mistral, etc.).  
The whole pipeline—evolutionary search for a per‑dimension scale $ \lambda_i $ together with a “no‑interpolation” prefix length $ \hat n $—relies on the fact that the positional information is expressed as a set of trigonometric rotations (Eq. (1)–(2) in the paper).  

If a model does **not** use RoPE, but instead adopts absolute sinusoidal embeddings (the classic “sinusoid” of Vaswani et al.) or learned absolute embeddings, the same mathematical object is *not* present, so the exact formulation of LongRoPE cannot be applied.  Below we detail why this is a *fundamental* obstacle and what would be required to obtain an analogue of LongRoPE for those schemes.

---

### Why LongRoPE cannot be used unchanged on non‑rotary models  

1. **Different functional form**  
   In RoPE each token’s key and query vectors are multiplied by a rotation matrix whose angle is proportional to the token index $n$ and a frequency $\theta_i$.  The interpolation step is simply a scalar rescaling of the argument of $\sin$ and $\cos$:   
   $$ 
   \cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}\bigr),\qquad 
   \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}\bigr).
   $$  
   By varying $\lambda_i$ we preserve the *shape* of the original embedding while stretching or compressing it along the position axis.  

   For absolute sinusoidal embeddings the vectors are  
   $$ 
   p_{n,i}= \sin (n\,\omega_i),\;\; p_{n,i+d/2}= \cos (n\,\omega_i),
   $$  
   where each dimension has its own frequency $\omega_i$.  Changing the effective range of positions therefore requires **changing the frequencies themselves**, not merely scaling an argument that already appears inside a rotation matrix.  The interaction between frequencies and the attention kernel is different, so the monotonic‑non‑decreasing constraint that LongRoPE imposes on $\lambda_i$ (derived from NTK theory for RoPE) no longer holds.

2. **No clean “interpolation‐only” operation**  
   In RoPE you can keep the original angles for the first $\hat n$ tokens (the “no‑interpolation” region) and apply a different scaling for later tokens without touching any other part of the model.  With absolute embeddings you would have to replace **all** position vectors after $ \hat n $ with a new set that is *not* simply a scaled version of the original ones; otherwise you would introduce discontinuities in the embedding space that break the continuity assumptions used by attention.

3. **Loss landscape changes**  
   The evolutionary search in LongRoPE evaluates candidate rescaling vectors by feeding them to the *unchanged* model and measuring perplexity.  Because RoPE preserves orthogonality, any rescaling retains most of the original geometry, keeping perplexity gradients relatively smooth.  Learned absolute embeddings, however, are already tuned to a specific distribution of position indices; arbitrary alterations can create severe out‑of‑distribution shifts that cause gradients to explode or collapse, making the search noisy and unreliable.

Because of these three reasons, simply plugging a non‑uniform scaling vector into a sinusoidal or learned absolute embedding does not give you an “extension” that preserves the model’s knowledge; it effectively creates a new positional encoding that would require full re‑training.

---

### What would be required to obtain an analogue for non‑rotary schemes  

Even though we cannot reuse LongRoPE verbatim, one can still adopt its *high‑level idea*: treat positional interpolation as an **optimization problem** over a large but structured search space and use evolutionary search (or any black‑box optimiser) to find parameters that minimise perplexity on a small validation set.  The concrete components would have to be altered:

1. **Search space design**  
   *For absolute sinusoidal embeddings*: let each dimension’s frequency be multiplied by a factor $ \alpha_i >0 $.  The embedding for token $n$ becomes  
   $$ 
   p'_{n,i}= \sin (n\,\alpha_i\,\omega_i),\qquad
   p'_{n,i+d/2}= \cos (n\,\alpha_i\,\omega_i).
   $$  
   The search variables are therefore $ \alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{d/2-1} $ together with an optional prefix length $ \hat n $ after which all frequencies are multiplied.  Bounds similar to those used in LongRoPE ($1.0 \le \alpha_i \le s\times 1.25$) can be kept, where $s = L'/L$ is the desired extension ratio.

   *For learned absolute embeddings*: you have a matrix $E\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{\max}\times d}$.  Extending beyond $L_{\max}$ means constructing rows for indices $n>L_{\max}$.  One pragmatic parametrisation is to fit a low‑order polynomial or spline to the existing rows and then extrapolate; the coefficients of that extrapolation become searchable parameters.  Alternatively, you can keep the first $L_{\max}$ rows unchanged and learn a small set of “extension vectors” that are added linearly with position index.

2. **Constraints analogous to monotonicity**  
   Empirically, higher‑frequency dimensions tolerate less stretch than low‑frequency ones (the NTK insight).  For sinusoidal frequencies this translates into requiring  
   $$ 
   \alpha_0 \le \alpha_1 \le \dots \le \alpha_{d/2-1},
   $$  
   which can be enforced during mutation/crossover exactly as LongRoPE does for $ \lambda_i $.

3. **Evaluation metric**  
   The paper uses perplexity on five PG19 samples as a cheap proxy for quality.  The same proxy can be used for any embedding scheme: replace RoPE with the candidate embedding, run a forward pass on those samples and record loss.  Because learned absolute embeddings often have much larger variance across positions, it may be necessary to average over more samples (e.g., ten instead of five) to obtain stable fitness signals.

4. **Progressive fine‑tuning**  
   Once an optimal set of frequency scalings has been found for, say, an intermediate length (128 k), you can fine‑tune on that length exactly as in LongRoPE.  The fine‑tuning step will let the model adjust its attention weights to the new geometry, which mitigates catastrophic forgetting caused by newly introduced positions.

5. **Short‑context recovery**  
   After extending to millions of tokens you will again observe crowding of embeddings in the original range; re‑run the evolutionary search with tighter bounds on $ \alpha_i $ for short lengths (4 k–8 k).  This mirrors LongRoPE’s “readjustment” phase.

---

### Practical limitations

Even after redesigning the search space as above, several practical hurdles remain:

| Issue | Why it matters | Expected impact |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Lack of rotational invariance** | RoPE’s rotation guarantees that dot products between queries and keys depend only on relative position differences; absolute sinusoidals do not enjoy this property once frequencies are altered arbitrarily. | Perplexity may degrade more sharply when extending beyond ~4× without fine‑tuning. |
| **Training data scarcity** | The paper stresses that very long documents are rare; for non‑rotary models you would need longer examples anyway because each new position now carries *new* raw values rather than scaled versions of existing ones. | Evolutionary search may converge to sub‑optimal solutions because validation loss becomes noisy at extreme lengths. |
| **Memory footprint** | Learned absolute embeddings require storing a full table up to $L'$; extending to millions of tokens means allocating gigabytes just for positional vectors, which defeats one advantage of RoPE (no extra parameters). | This makes pure extrapolation unattractive; hybrid schemes (e.g., trainable linear projection plus sinusoidal base) become necessary. |
| **Compatibility with existing optimisations** | Many kernels (FlashAttention, xformers) are tuned assuming RoPE’s element‑wise rotation; swapping in arbitrary sinusoidals may break fused kernels and thus slow down inference dramatically. | You lose one of LongRoPE’s selling points—the ability to retain most pre‑existing optimisations. |

---

### Bottom line

LongRoPE’s core contribution—*effective non‑uniform positional interpolation discovered by an evolutionary search*—is specific to rotary embeddings because those embeddings admit a simple scalar rescaling per dimension while preserving orthogonal structure.  For models that use absolute sinusoidal or learned positional vectors there is no mathematically equivalent “rescale factor” that leaves the rest of the network untouched; consequently you must redesign both the parametrisation of new positions and possibly retrain part of the model.

Nevertheless, nothing prevents you from borrowing LongRoPE’s methodology: define a searchable family of frequency scalings (or extrapolation functions), enforce monotonicity constraints derived from NTK intuition, evaluate candidates via perplexity on short validation slices, and then fine‑tune progressively.  The expected outcome will likely be *less impressive* than the 8× non‑fine‑tuned extension reported for RoPE because absolute schemes lack rotational invariance, but such an approach could still push current limits beyond the ≈128 k barrier observed in prior work.

In short: **the fundamental obstacle is that non‑rotary positional encodings do not support simple per‑dimension scaling without breaking their internal geometry**, so LongRoPE cannot be directly applied.  To adapt it you must redesign both the representation (frequency scaling or parametric extrapolation) and the search space while keeping in mind memory and kernel constraints—expecting modest gains compared with what roped­based models achieve.
