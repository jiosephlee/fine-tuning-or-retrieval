\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What exactly is being fine‑tuned in the progressive extension steps?
Question:
The method first searches for RoPE factors for 128k, fine‑tunes 400 steps, then swaps to 256k factors and fine‑tunes another 600 steps. Are only the RoPE parameters updated during these steps, or are all model weights also trained? How does this limited fine‑tuning (1k steps total) avoid overfitting given the massive increase in context length?
Answer:
LongRoPE does **not** keep the bulk of the model frozen while it “only” updates the RoPE factors.  
During each of the two short fine‑tuning phases (400 steps at 128 k tokens and then 600 steps at 256 k tokens) the whole‑model set of parameters is allowed to receive gradients – the same way as in any ordinary language‑model fine‑tuning run. What changes between the two phases is only the **positional‑embedding configuration** that is supplied to the model: after the first 400 steps the RoPE rescaling that was found for a 128 k window is swapped for the one that was searched for a 256 k window, and training continues for another 600 steps with that new configuration.

Why does such a tiny amount of optimisation (1 000 steps total) not lead to catastrophic over‑fitting, even though the extension ratio is huge (up to $512\times$)?

1. **Very small parameter drift** – The model starts from a fully pre‑trained checkpoint whose weights already encode a strong language model prior. The 1 000 optimisation steps are performed with a modest learning rate ($2\times10^{-5}$ for LLaMA2, $1\times10^{-6}$ for Mistral) and on batches of size 32–64, so only a tiny fraction of the entire parameter space moves. In practice this amounts to a *fine‑tuning* rather than a *re‑training*: the model retains almost all of its original knowledge and only adjusts enough to accommodate the new positional statistics.

2. **Better initialization from the search** – The evolutionary search that precedes fine‑tuning supplies a RoPE configuration that already preserves most of the information in the original rotary angles (the paper shows up to an 8× extension without any training). Because the interpolation is already close to optimal, the gradient updates needed to adapt to longer contexts are minimal. The optimiser therefore only has to correct a slight mismatch between the interpolated angles and the statistics of the training texts at the new length.

3. **Progressive strategy reduces distribution shift** – Extending directly from 4 k to 2 048 k would introduce a massive shift in position indices (more than 99 % of positions are unseen). By first moving to an intermediate length (128 k → 256 k) and fine‑tuning there, LongRoPE limits each distribution shift to a factor of roughly two. This “curriculum” like progression makes it much easier for gradient descent to converge in very few steps.

4. **Training data are themselves long** – The fine‑tuning data are split into segments of length 128 k or 256 k (the paper uses RedPajama books and Together’s Long‑Data Collections). Even though only one thousand optimisation steps are taken, each step sees many millions of tokens, so the effective number of token‑level updates is still large. Consequently, the model sees enough diverse context patterns at the target length to avoid memorisation of any single document.

5. **Regularisation through implicit “no‑update” of RoPE** – For the first $\hat n$ positions (the paper finds $\hat n$ up to a few hundred), RoPE is left untouched (the original angles are used). This anchors early‑position representations and prevents them from drifting arbitrarily during fine‑tuning, acting as an additional regulariser that stabilises training.

Putting these points together, LongRoPE’s fine‑tuning phase can be viewed as a *lightweight adaptation*: all weights are technically trainable, but because (i) they start from a strong pre‑trained state, (ii) the positional embedding is already near optimal thanks to evolution search, and (iii) the extension is performed gradually with abundant long‑text data, only minute adjustments are required. Hence over‑fitting does not occur even though only about one thousand gradient steps are applied while pushing the context window from a few thousand tokens up to two million.
