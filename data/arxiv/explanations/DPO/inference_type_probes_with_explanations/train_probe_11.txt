According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', if the learned reward model were perfectly accurate but the KL penalty $\\beta$ became extremely large, the optimized policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ would converge toward the reference policy. This follows from the KL-constrained objective in Eq. (1) and its closed-form optimal policy in Eq. (2), where $\\pi_r(y\\mid x) = \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\,\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta} r(x,y)\\right)$; as $\\beta \\to \\infty$, the factor $\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta} r(x,y)\\right) \\to 1$ for all $y$, so $\\pi_r(y\\mid x)$ reduces to $\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)$ after normalization, meaning the KL term dominates and the optimal solution is to stay at the reference policy regardless of reward accuracy. Equivalently from the DPO perspective, the implicit reward $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)}$ enters the preference likelihood through differences scaled by $\\beta$, and when $\\beta$ is very large those differences vanish under the optimality condition, yielding negligible gradients that preserve $\\pi_\\theta \\approx \\pi_\\text{ref}$.
