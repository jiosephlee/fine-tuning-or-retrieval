In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors report that DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO uses ground-truth rewards, which most directly indicates PPO's sample inefficiency. This is sensible because DPO and PPO optimize the same KL-constrained reward maximization objective, namely maximizing Eqs. (1)–(2)’s form of r under the constraint in Eq. (3), but DPO achieves this by an analytical reparameterization that eliminates the need for on-policy sampling and value/baseline estimation: using the closed-form optimal policy for Eq. (3), Eq. (4) gives r in terms of policy ratios, which, after cancellation of the partition function in the Bradley–Terry model, yields the supervised objective in Eq. (7): L_DPO minimizes −log σ(β[log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) − log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x))]), with gradients that upweight misordered pairs and directly push π_θ to increase preferred and decrease dispreferred completions. In contrast, PPO must sample y ∼ π_θ during training, estimate or approximate soft value baselines for the normalization term in Eq. (10), and contend with high-variance policy gradients and additional instability from KL penalties and value learning; the paper’s analysis explicitly ties this to actor-critic instability and variance. Consequently, even with oracle rewards (PPO-GT), PPO’s learning signal is noisier and less data-efficient than DPO’s supervised, closed-form update, which manifests empirically as DPO’s strictly better reward–KL frontier in the controlled sentiment experiment and thus indicates PPO’s sample inefficiency in this setting.
