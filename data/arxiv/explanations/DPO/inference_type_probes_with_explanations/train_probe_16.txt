In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', if human preferences depended on absolute reward levels instead of differences (i.e., on r(x,y) rather than on r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)) the key simplification in DPO's derivation that would fail is the cancellation of the partition function. This is because DPO’s change of variables rewrites rewards as r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x) (Eq. (6)), and the Bradley–Terry preference model only uses reward differences, so when forming r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2) the additive term \beta \log Z(x), which depends only on x, cancels, yielding a preference likelihood that depends solely on the policy ratios and not on Z(x) (Eqs. (7)–(8)), enabling the simple binary cross-entropy objective in Eq. (9); if instead p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) depended on absolute levels r(x,y) (e.g., via a model that includes each r(x,y) separately), the \beta \log Z(x) term would remain inside the likelihood and its gradients, forcing explicit estimation of Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta) or equivalent baselines (cf. Eq. (12)), which is precisely the intractable normalizer that DPO avoids and that makes RLHF with PPO complex and high-variance when treated via reinforcement learning.
