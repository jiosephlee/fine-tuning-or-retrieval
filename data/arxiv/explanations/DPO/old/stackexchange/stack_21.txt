### Is the DPO objective a tight surrogate or a lower bound on the original RLHF objective?
Question:
Since DPO directly maximizes the likelihood of preferences under the reparameterized policy, is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ a tight surrogate to the KL-regularized reward maximization objective, or can it be viewed as a lower bound akin to a variational objective? Under what conditions do their optima coincide?
Answer:
In the DPO paper the authors show that nothing in their derivation relies on a loose relaxation or a variational lower bound—rather, they perform an exact change of variables that turns the usual KL‐regularized RL objective into a pure preference‐likelihood objective. Concretely, recall that RLHF typically seeks

$$
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
$$

and that under very mild conditions (e.g.\ countable action space, bounded reward) its unique optimizer has the closed‐form

$$
\pi^*(y\mid x)
=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).
$$

If one assumes that human comparisons follow the Bradley–Terry model

$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=\sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr),
$$

then substituting the “optimal policy” expression into the BT likelihood makes the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ drop out entirely. What remains is exactly the negative‐log‐likelihood

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}(\pi_\theta)
=-\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\biggl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w\mid x)}
-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\biggr],
$$

with no approximation steps. In other words, DPO is a *tight* surrogate—there is a one‐to‐one correspondence between optimizing the original KL‐regularized reward objective and minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}$.

The only assumptions you need for this equivalence to hold are (1) that the human preferences really do follow a Plackett–Luce (in particular Bradley–Terry) model, (2) that the reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$ in DPO matches the one used in the RLHF objective, and (3) that your model class for $\pi_\theta$ is rich enough to express the exponential tilt $\pi_{\rm ref}\exp(r/\beta)$. Under those conditions Theorem 1 in the paper shows that *every* reward function in the permissible equivalence class (i.e.\ up to an additive function of $x$) can be written as

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)}.
$$

Hence the global minimizer of $\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}$ recovers exactly the same policy that you would have obtained by solving the original RLHF problem.

In practice, if the preference data deviate from the Bradley–Terry assumption or if your policy class cannot perfectly represent the ideal $\pi_{\rm ref}\exp(r/\beta)$, then of course the learned DPO policy may differ from the true constrained optimum. But under the paper’s modeling assumptions and with a sufficiently expressive $\pi_\theta$, there is no gap: optimizing DPO *is* equivalent to directly solving the KL‐regularized reward maximum‐a‐posteriori problem.
