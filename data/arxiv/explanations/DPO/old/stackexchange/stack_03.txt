### How justified is fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximum likelihood on preferred completions when the true SFT policy is unavailable?
Question:
The paper suggests that when the original SFT policy $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ used to sample preferences is unavailable, we can estimate a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions $(x,y_w)$. Is this consistent under the DPO theory? What bias is introduced if the true data-generating policy differed significantly and the dataset contains only pairwise preferences but not a full distribution over $y$?
Answer:
When we introduced DPO (Direct Preference Optimization), a key ingredient was the “reference” policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ against which we measure KL-divergence (see Eq. 11 in the paper). In the usual RLHF pipeline this $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is exactly the supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) model that was used to generate the A/B completions for labeling. But in practice you often only have the static preference dataset—no access to the original SFT model. The paper’s proposal is to re-fit $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by maximum-likelihood on the set of preferred completions

$$
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} \;\leftarrow\; \arg\max_{p}\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w}\bigl[\log p(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
$$

How much does this “plug-in” estimator break the DPO theory? In short:

1.  DPO’s derivation (Thm. 1 and Eq. 11) shows that *for any* fixed reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, optimizing the loss

$$
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
\;=\;
-\,\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l}\Bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(\beta\bigl(\log\pi(y_w)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w)\bigr)
\;-\;\beta\bigl(\log\pi(y_l)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l)\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr]
$$

recovers the exact solution to the KL-regularized RL objective whose baseline is $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. In other words, it *does* solve the “right” RLHF problem for whichever $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ you plug in.

2.  If you knew the *true* SFT policy $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, then DPO with $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} = \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ would be fully equivalent to standard RLHF with that same baseline. But when you only have a preference dataset, you cannot recover the true $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ over all $y$—we only ever see the winning halves $y_w$ of each pair.

3.  By fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ via MLE on the $y_w$’s you get a *consistent* estimator of $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ *on the support of the preferred completions*—i.e.\ for those $(x,y_w)$ you actually observed. In the large-sample limit (preferences truly sampled from $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$), the MLE will converge to the true $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ (up to whatever function of $x$ you pick as normalization), and so the bias vanishes on the support where you have data.

4.  Off that support—i.e.\ for completions you never saw as “winners”—your MLE $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is unconstrained. Since DPO’s implicit reward is

$$
\hat r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$

any errors in $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)$ (the *losers* you did see) or in $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)$ for $y'$ you never saw will distort the scale of that reward and hence the strength of the KL penalty in those regions.

In practical terms this means that **you’re not solving exactly the same RLHF problem** you would if you had the true SFT baseline. Instead you’re solving the RLHF problem whose baseline is your best-fit MLE model. The discrepancy between the two objectives is precisely the model-misspecification error in $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, which in turn depends on how *unrepresentative* your finite preference set is of the full distribution under which it was originally sampled.

– If your preference dataset is large and diverse, and if indeed the human labels *were* collected by querying some $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ that you’ve now re-fitted, then the MLE reference will be very close to the original and the bias will be negligible.  
– If the original SFT was a very rich model over many possible continuations but your dataset captures only a narrow slice of that space, then the MLE $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ will “collapse” onto the winners you saw, mis-calibrating the KL constraint elsewhere. In that regime the DPO solution optimizes a *different* reward/KL trade-off than you would have gotten with the true baseline, and so it can behave sub-optimally.

In summary, **fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by MLE on the preferred completions is a reasonable plug-in under the same consistency guarantees that any maximum-likelihood estimator enjoys—but only on the support where you have data**. Under DPO theory nothing breaks: you still *exactly* optimize the KL-regularized RL objective for the reference you supply. The bias that enters is the standard plug-in bias from using an estimated baseline rather than the true one, and its magnitude tracks how far your fitted $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is from the true SFT policy on the completions and prompts of interest.
