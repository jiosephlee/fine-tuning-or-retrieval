### How would DPO handle ties or indifference in preferences (i.e., $y_w$ equals $y_l$ or tie labels)?
Question:
Some preference datasets include ties or 'no preference' labels. The Bradley–Terry model is binary-preference-based. Are there standard extensions of DPO to handle ties (e.g., Davidson model or Thurstone–Mosteller variants) such that the loss gracefully accounts for indifference? What would the DPO objective look like in that case?
Answer:
In the DPO framework the only place the Bradley–Terry model ever enters is when we write down the likelihood of a human “win/lose” judgment as a sigmoid in the difference of two latent rewards. Ties or “no‐preference” labels simply correspond to a preference model with three outcomes (win, lose, tie) rather than two. Crucially, as long as that model depends only on the differences (or simple symmetric functions) of the latent rewards, the same DPO re-parameterization

$$
r_\theta(x,y)\;=\;\beta\;\ln\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)}
$$

still causes every unwieldy normalizer to cancel, and you end up with a straightforward multinomial‐cross‐entropy in terms of your policy ratios.

Concretely, take the classic Davidson extension of Bradley–Terry, which adds a tie‐parameter $\nu>0$. Let $r_i = r_\theta(x,y_i)$ and define

$$
p(y_1\succ y_2)
=\frac{e^{r_1}}{e^{r_1}+e^{r_2}+2\nu\sqrt{e^{r_1+r_2}}}\,, 
\quad
p(\mathrm{tie})
=\frac{2\nu\sqrt{e^{r_1+r_2}}}{e^{r_1}+e^{r_2}+2\nu\sqrt{e^{r_1+r_2}}}\,,
$$

with $p(y_2\succ y_1)$ given by symmetry. Now substitute

$$
r_i = \beta \ln\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_i\mid x)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{r_i}=\biggl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_i\mid x)}\biggr)^\beta,
$$

so that no unknown partition functions remain. You then fit your model by minimizing the negative log‐likelihood

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}^{\rm tie}
\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_1,y_2,\ell)\sim\mathcal D}\;
\log\bigl[p_\ell\bigl(r_\theta(x,y_1),\,r_\theta(x,y_2)\bigr)\bigr],
$$

where $\ell\in\{\,1\!\succ\!2,\,2\!\succ\!1,\,\mathrm{tie}\}$ indicates which of the three labels the human gave. The gradient still simply raises the likelihood of the human‐selected outcome (win, lose, or tie) and pushes down the others, with per‐example weights that depend on how “surprised” the current model is.

An entirely analogous recipe works for any other pairwise choice model—e.g.\ a Thurstone–Mosteller (Gaussian‐noise) link—by replacing the Davidson probabilities with

$$
p(y_1\succ y_2)
=\Phi\!\Bigl(\frac{r_1-r_2}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\Bigr), 
\quad
p(\mathrm{tie})
=\Phi\!\Bigl(\frac{t-(r_1-r_2)}{\sigma}\Bigr)
-\Phi\!\Bigl(\frac{-t-(r_1-r_2)}{\sigma}\Bigr),
$$

and then plugging in $r_i=\beta\ln\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_i\mid x)}$. In every case the key is that the preference probabilities depend only on differences (or symmetric combinations) of $r_1,r_2$, so the additive indeterminacy (i.e.\ unknown normalizers) drops out, leaving you with a clean three‐way cross‐entropy in $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$. Thus DPO extends “gracefully” to datasets with tie labels simply by swapping the binary logistic link for any tie‐aware choice model.
