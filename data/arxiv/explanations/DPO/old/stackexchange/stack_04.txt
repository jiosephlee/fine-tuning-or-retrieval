### How does DPO handle support mismatch when $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=0$ for some observed $y$?
Question:
Since DPO relies on $\log \tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, what happens if the reference model assigns zero (or numerically tiny) probability mass to an observed completion $y$ present in the preference dataset? Are there standard smoothing or floor-probability tricks to prevent exploding terms, and do these tricks affect the theoretical guarantees?
Answer:
In DPO we take as given a “reference” policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$—typically the SFT model—and then fit our new policy $\pi_\theta$ so as to make the log‐ratio

$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)
= \beta \log\!\Bigl[\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr]
$$

match the human preferences. You’re quite right to worry: if $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=0$ for some $y$ that actually appears in the preference dataset, then that log‐ratio would blow up to $+\infty$. Fortunately, in practice this never happens (or only happens in ways we can easily fix), for two related reasons:

1) **Language models never literally assign zero probability**  
An autoregressive LM with a softmax over a large vocabulary will in principle give every next‐token a strictly positive probability, and hence every full sequence $y$ a positive—but perhaps astronomically small—probability. Therefore when we sample $y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ during the data‐collection phase, by construction $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$. As long as your preference dataset really was produced by sampling from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, you won’t see a hard zero.

2) **Re‐estimating or “floored” $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ if you reuse an external dataset**  
If instead you try to apply DPO to a fixed, off‐the‐shelf human‐preference dataset whose completions were generated by some unknown model, you may not have access to the original $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ or it may not assign support wherever your $y$’s lie. The paper recommends a simple remedy:

  • Fit a small supervised fine‐tuning of your available LM as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on the _preferred_ completions from your dataset:  
    $$
    \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
    = \arg\max_{\pi}\,
      \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
    $$
    This new $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ will have non‐zero support on every $y_w$ in the dataset (and quite often on the corresponding $y_l$ as well, since they share substantial overlap in tokens).

  • If you still worry about very low probabilities, it’s common in RL‐style algorithms to threshold or “floor”  
    $$
    \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
    := \max\bigl(\pi_{\mathrm{ref,raw}}(y\mid x), \epsilon\bigr)
    $$
    for some tiny $\epsilon$ (say $10^{-8}$).

Neither of these steps breaks the DPO theory; they simply redefine your baseline $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to be a closely related distribution with full support on your observed examples. The key theorems in the paper assume only that $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$ wherever you evaluate the log‐ratio, and they go through identically for *any* choice of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. In other words, as long as you view your smoothed or re‐estimated model as *the* reference policy in both your loss

$$
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
= -\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l}\Bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(\beta[\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)]
                         - \beta[\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)]\bigr)\Bigr]
$$

and in the derivation of the closed‐form optimum, the formal guarantees (consistency, recovery of the RLHF‐optimal policy under Bradley–Terry, etc.) remain intact. The only effect of flooring or re‐fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is to shift your entire KL‐constraint “anchor” slightly, exactly as if you had chosen a different SFT model at the start. In practice, these shifts are negligible if $\epsilon$ is small or if your refit model is already close to the original SFT, and they avoid any numerical infinities without undermining the theoretical foundation of DPO.
