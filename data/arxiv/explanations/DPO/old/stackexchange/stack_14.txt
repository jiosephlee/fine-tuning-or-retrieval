### How are temperature at inference and $\beta$ during training related in practice?
Question:
The paper reports that win rate depends on the sampling temperature at inference, while $\beta$ governs the strength of the KL constraint during training. Is there any principled relationship between sampling temperature $\tau$ at test time and the training regularization $\beta$? For instance, does scaling logits by $1/\tau$ partially counteract the effect of the KL regularization imposed by small $\beta$?
Answer:
In DPO (and more generally in KL‐constrained RLHF), the two hyper‐parameters you mention play superficially similar roles—both affect how “peaked” or “flat” the final generation distribution is—but they live in quite different places in the pipeline. The training parameter $\beta$ appears as the Lagrange multiplier in

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[r_\phi(y)\bigr]\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta \,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr],
$$

and governs how strongly the learned policy stays close to the reference model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. At inference time one typically “softens” or “sharpens” the final policy $\pi_\theta$ by sampling from

$$
\pi_\theta^{(\tau)}(y)\;\propto\;\bigl[\pi_\theta(y)\bigr]^{1/\tau}\,,
$$

where $\tau$ is the usual temperature. It is tempting to ask whether choosing $\tau<1$ can “undo” an overly strong KL penalty (small $\beta$), but there is no exact one‐to‐one mapping between them.

To see why, substitute the DPO closed‐form policy

$$
\pi_\theta(y)\;\propto\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\hat r_\theta(y)\Bigr)
$$

into the definition of tempered sampling. You get

$$
\pi_\theta^{(\tau)}(y)\;\propto\;\bigl[\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\bigr]^{1/\tau}
\;\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta\,\tau}\,\hat r_\theta(y)\Bigr).
$$

If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ were uniform, the reference term drops out and you see that dividing the logits by $\tau$ is equivalent to replacing $\beta$ with $\beta\,\tau$ in the reward exponent. In that toy case you could say “temperature $\tau$ at inference behaves like an effective training $\beta'=\beta\,\tau$.”

But once $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is non‐uniform (which it always is in practice, since it’s your SFT or pretrained model), you also end up raising $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to the $1/\tau$ power. Concretely the inference policy becomes

$$
\pi_\theta^{(\tau)}(y)\;\propto\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)^{1/\tau}
\;\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta\,\tau}\,\hat r_\theta(y)\Bigr),
$$

which is not the same functional form as the optimal DPO policy $\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\bigl(\hat r/\beta\bigr)$ for any single $\beta$ (because the SFT prior has been distorted).

Intuitively, $\beta$ and $\tau$ both control entropy—$\beta$ by shaping the learned $\pi_\theta$ during training, and $\tau$ by re-weighing $\pi_\theta$ afterwards—but they operate on different distributions. A smaller $\beta$ makes $\pi_\theta$ stay closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (higher entropy, less reward exploitation), while a smaller $\tau$ concentrates $\pi_\theta$’s mass on its modes (lower entropy, more exploitation of whatever $\pi_\theta$ already learned). You can certainly dial down $\tau$ to get crisper outputs if you feel $\beta$ was too small, but you should think of this as a practical sampling hack rather than a principled inversion of the KL penalty. In short, there’s no clean analytic rule that says “set $\tau = f(\beta)$” except in the degenerate uniform‐prior case. In practice one still tunes $\beta$ during training for the desired reward/KL trade‐off and tunes $\tau$ at inference for the right diversity/exploit balance.
