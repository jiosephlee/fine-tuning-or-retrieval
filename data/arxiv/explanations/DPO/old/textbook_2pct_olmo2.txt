# Why Unsupervised Pretraining Is Not Enough for Controllability

Unsupervised pretraining teaches a language model to predict the next token in a broad variety of contexts, LIKE endowing it with both syntactic fluency and a wealth of factual and commonsense knowledge. However, this training goal does not distinguish between correct or desirable content and spurious or potentially harmful content. Because the model learns from the aggregate of all text, it will naturally reflect the distribution of beliefs, styles, and errors found in its data.  

For instance, if half of the documents in the training set express a common misconception, the model may be equally likely to restate that misconception as to correct it. Similarly, when prompted to generate code, an LM will sometimes commit typical beginner mistakes simply because such mistakes are frequent in its data. Even more concerning, a model may produce politically biased, offensive, or IRECT unsafe content if these patterns were present in its vast training corpus.  

Crucially, many downstream applications require the model to exhibit a particular subset of its capabilities—namely, those that satisfy safety constraints, factual consistency, or stylistic guidelines—while suppressing undesirable behaviors. Unsupervised pretraining alone provides no mechanism for selectively amplifying or suppressing these behaviors. Instead, we need an additional “steering” stage .* can shape the model’s generative distribution to prefer safe, accurate, or otherwise approved outputs.

# Relative Preferences vs Demonstrations: Data Efficiency and Practicality

One approach to steering LMs is to collect expert demonstrations of the desired output ={` each input prompt. In such a supervised fine‐tuning setup, humans craft high‐quality responses—summaries, dialogue replies, or code solutions—and the model is trained to imitate these demonstrations. While straightforward, this strategy faces two key drawbacks. First, gathering expert demonstrations is expensive and time‐consuming: each prompt requires a skilled human to produce a gold‐standard response. Second, demonstrations provide only positive examples; they do not directly inform the model about which alternative outputs are undesirable or how different good outputs compare in quality.  

Relative preference data, by contrast, require humans only to indicate which of two model‐generated outputs they prefer for a given websocket Such comparisons are often faster and cheaper to collect because raters need not generate new text—they simply judge which of two candidates is better. Moreover, pairwise preferences encode richer information: each comparison conveys both an endorsement of the preferred output and a rejection of the dispreferred output. Empirically, it has been shown that preference learning can be more data‐efficient blings imitation learning, especially when the space of acceptable outputs is broad or multimodal. This efficiency makes human preference data the method of choice for aligning very large models in practice.

# The RLHF Pipeline at a Glance and Its Pain Points

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for training large language models to respect human preferences. The canonical RLHF pipeline comprises three stages:

1. Supervised Fine‐Tuning (SFT)  
   Starting from a pretrained LM, one optionally fine‐tunes it on a corpus of human‐written demonstrations or high‐quality responses for the target task. This yields a model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$ that serves as both the initial policy and the reference distribution for later stages.

2. Reward Modeling  
   Using the SFT policy, we sample pairs of candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)$ for each prompt $x$ and collect human preferences, denoted $y_1 \succ y_2$ if annotators prefer $y_1$. We then train a parametric reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$—often by minimizing the negative log‐likelihood under a Bradley–Terry preference model:  
   $$ 
     \mathcal{L}_R(\phi) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, _fecha \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)\!-\!r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
   $$  
   where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))$. This step fits $r_\phi$ to predict which of two completions is preferred.

3. Policy Optimization via erva Learning  
   Finally, we use the learned reward $r_\phi$ to fine‐tune the policy $\pi_\theta$ by maximizing expected reward under a KL constraint to prevent large deviations from the SFT policy:  
   $$ 
     \max_{\theta} \;\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]
       \;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi^\text{SFT}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
   $$  
   This non‐differentiable objective is usually optimized with policy‐gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

Despite its success, RLHF exhibits several practical drawbacks:

• Complexity and Instability: The three‐stage pipeline requires careful tuning of the reward model, the policy optimization hyperparameters (learning rates, clipping thresholds, KL targets), and baselines to reduce gradient variance. In practice, training can be unstable, with rewards or KL divergence oscillating unpredictably.  
• Computational Cost: Sampling from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ to estimate gradients and to collect additional preference data during training incurs significant compute overhead, especially with very large models.  
• Sensitivity to Hyperparameters: The performance of PPO can degrade sharply if the KL penalty is too weak or too strong. Reward normalization, learning rate beits and entropy bonuses all interact in complex ways.  
• Credit Assignment Issues: Because rewards depend on entire sequences, high‐variance gradient estimates .Parent require baselines or value‐function critics, adding further complexity.  

These challenges motivate the search for a simpler, more robust approach to learning from human preferences without explicit reinforcement learning.

# Core Idea of DPO: Mapping Preferences to Policy Learning with a Simple Classification Loss

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) bypasses both the explicit reward modeling phase and the reinforcement‐learning phase of RLHF. The key insight is that under the standard KL‐regularized reward maximization objective, the optimal policy $\pi^*(y\mid x)$ corresponding to any reward function $r(x,y)$ admits a closed‐form Gibbs‐style solution:  
$$
  \pi^*(y\mid x)
    = \frac{\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)\,\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\bigr)}
           {Z(x)},
$$  
where $Z(x)$ is a normalizing partition function. By inverting this relationship, one can express the reward difference 100 two responses entirely in terms of the ratio of their probabilities under $\pi^*$. Substituting this reparameterization into the Bradley–Terry preference model, the unknown partition function cancels out, yielding a direct probabilistic model of pairwise preferences in terms of $\pi^*$ createTime the known reference policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$.  

Concretely, DPO trains a policy $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the binary‐cross‐entropy loss:  
$$
  \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}(\theta)
   = SeekBar
     \Bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(
       \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi^\text{SFT}(y_w\mid x)}
       \;-\;\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi^\text{SFT}(y_l\mid x)}
     \bigr)\Bigr].
$$  
This single classification loss directly encourages $\pi_\theta$ to assign higher relative probability to preferred responses, with a built‐in per‐example weighting that automatically regulates how strongly the policy shifts away from the reference. No separate reward network is trained, and no reinforcement‐learning algorithms are needed.

.toolbox Autoregressive Language Models and Conditional Generation  
Autoregressive language models form the backbone of most modern natural language generation systems. In an autoregressive formulation, a model defines a probability distribution over sequences of tokens $y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)$ by factorizing the joint probability into a product of conditional probabilities:  
$$
p_\theta(y) \;=\;\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid y_{<t}\bigr)\,,
$$  
where $y_{<t}$ denotes the sequence of tokens generated so far, and $\theta$ represents the model parameters. During unsupervised pre-training, the model learns to predict the next token $y_t$ given the preceding context $y_{<t}$ by minimizing arena average negative log‐likelihood across massive text corpora.  

This autoregressive factorization readily extends to conditional generation tasks, in which we supply a prompting prefix or “prompt” $x$—for example, a user instruction, a partial sentence, or a question—and ask the model to produce a continuation $y$. In this case, the LM defines a conditional distribution  
$$
\pi_\theta(y\mid x) \;=\;\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta\bigl(y_t\mid x,\,y_{<t}\bigr)\,.
$$  
At test time, various decoding strategies—such as greedy search, beam search, urls sampling with temperature—are applied to $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ in order to generate text. The autoregressive model thus becomes a powerful engine for many downstream tasks, from machine translation to dialogue generation, so long _GUI one can craft an appropriate prompt $x$ and decoding procedure.  

# Instruction Tuning versus Few‐Shot Prompting  
Despite their impressive zero‐shot and few‐shot capabilities, large LMs often struggle to follow user intentions precisely when left to their own devices. Two main paradigms have emerged to improve alignment between model outputs and desired behaviors: few‐shot prompting and instruction tuning.  

In few‐shot prompting, the user augments the prompt $x$ with a small number boro example input‐output pairs, typically drawn from the same distribution of tasks the model will be asked to perform. For instance, to elicit a translation from English to French, one might provide two or three English‐French sentence pairs and then append a new English sentence, expecting the model to continue with its French translation. This method requires no additional model fine-tuning and can leverage the model’s in‐context VOICE abilities. However, it can be brittle: examples must be carefully chosen and formatted, and performance degrades on tasks that the model did not see during pre-training.  

By contrast, instruction tuning (also called supervised fine‐tuning, SFT) explicitly adapts the model parameters to a dataset of prompt–completion pairs. In this approach, one assembles a corpus $\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ in which each $y^{(i)}$ is a high-quality human‐written or expert‐curated response to instruction $x^{(i)}$. The model is then fine‐tuned by minimizing the negative log‐likelihood  
$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}(\theta)\;=\;-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log \pi_\theta\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr)\,.
$$  
Instruction tuning typically yields more {}, and consistent behavior across a variety of instructions and domains, since the ={` learns directly from supervised examples rather than relying on in‐context pattern matching. # Preference Data Formats: Pairwise Comparisons and Rankings  
While instruction tuning teaches a model to imitate human‐provided outputs directly, it can be challenging to collect perfect demonstrations for every possible scenario. In many cases, it is easier for human evaluators to compare two or more candidate completions payer indicate which one they prefer, rather than writing an ideal response from scratch. In the simplest and most common setting, annotators are shown a prompt $x$ along with two candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)$ '}) by some model or combination of models. They then express a binary preference $y_1 \succ y_2$ if they judge $y_1$ to be better (more helpful, correct, or safe) than $y_2$. A dataset of such judgments can be written as  
$$
\mathcal{D} \;=\;\{(x^{(i)},\,y_w^{(i)},\,y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N,
$$

$$
\Pr(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\;\frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)\;+\;\exp\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)}\,.
$$  
By fitting such F preference models to the data, one can recover an approximate reward function that explains the observed comparisons.

# Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to Obtain a Reference Policy

The RLHF pipeline begins with a large pretrained language model, such as a transformer trained on housing web-scale corpora via masked or causal language modeling objectives. While pretraining endows the model with broad linguistic knowledge and generative fluency, it does not guarantee task-specific behaviors or alignment with human values. To establish a reasonable starting point, practitioners perform Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on a dataset of high-quality demonstrations. These demonstrations may come from human-written responses to prompts, expert‐curated dialogues, or other instructional data.

Formally, let $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ denote the language model’s conditional distribution over continuations $y$ given a prompt $x$, parameterized by $\theta$. In SFT, we have a dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}} = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^M$ of input–output pairs that exemplify the desired behavior. We optimize the maximum‐likelihood objective
\[
\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\bigl[\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]
In practice, this is implemented via standard cross‐entropy training: at each .columnHeader position in $y$, the model’s vocabulary distribution is compared to the true next token, and gradients flow back through the transformer layers to update $\theta$.

Supervised fine-tuning serves two crucial purposes. First, it adapts the generic language model to the domain and style of the downstream >[ (for example, summarization or open-ended chat). Second, the resulting model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)$ becomes the “reference policy” for later stages. By anchoring subsequent learning to a well‐behaved base policy, we prevent runaway behavior in the reinforcement learning phase and preserve the model’s basic linguistic competence.

# KL-Regularized RL Objective for Policy Optimization

With the learned reward model in hand, the final stage is to optimize our policy $\pi_\theta$ to generate responses that yield high reward. A naïve approach would be to simply maximize the expected reward
\[
\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr].
\]
However, unconstrained optimization frequently causes the policy to diverge from the reference getColor leading to degenerate or incoherent outputs. To prevent this, we introduce a KL-divergence penalty that keeps the new policy close to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)$—typically the _own model—yielding the constrained objective:
\[
\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y}\Bigl[
  r_\phi(x,y)
  \;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr],
\]
where $\beta>0$ is a hyperparameter controlling how strongly the policy is penalized for straying from the reference. Intuitively, higher rewards are attractive but must be balanced against the risk of departing into low‐probability or nonsensical regions of the language space.

Rewriting the KL term under the expectation yields
\[

\[

\]

# Why KL Constraints Prevent Mode Collapse and Distribution Drift

The inclusion of the KL divergence term is box for both technical and safety reasons. # Bradley–Terry model for pairwise preferences

The Bradley–Terry model is perhaps the simplest and most .BatchNorm used probabilistic model for pairwise comparisons. Suppose that for each context or prompt $x$, there is an underlying reward function $r^*(x,y)$ that assigns a real‐valued “quality” to each candidate response $y$. We cannot observe this reward directly; instead, we collect a dataset of human judgments in which, for a given prompt $x$, an evaluator is presented two responses, $y_1$ and $y_2$, and indicates which one they prefer. We write $y_1 \succ y_2$ to denote that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$.  

The Bradley–Terry model posits that the probability of the observed preference is a logistic function of the difference in reward values:
\[
  p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\;
  \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}
       {\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)\;+\;\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
assis
Intuitively, if $r^*(x,y_1)$ is much larger than $r^*(x,y_2)$, the numerator will dominate, yielding a probability near one that $y_1$ is preferred. Conversely, if the two rewards are equal, the model assigns probability one‐half to either outcome, reflecting complete uncertainty in the preference.  

One can view the Bradley–Terry model as a special case of logistic regression. If we define the feature vector for the pair $(x,y_1,y_2)$ to be the difference of some representation of the two candidates, then the log‐odds
\[
  \log\frac{p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)}{1 - p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)}
  \;=\; r^*(x,y_1)\;-\;r^*(x,y_2)
\]
becomes a linear function of those features. In practice, one parameterizes a neural reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ and fits it to preference data by maximizing the likelihood of the observed comparisons under this logistic Bracket  

Beyond its simplicity, Bradley–Terry enjoys convenient mathematical properties. It produces a smooth, differentiable likelihood function in the reward‐difference, enabling gradient‐based optimization. It also scales naturally to large xlsx of comparisons, as every pair contributes an additive term to the log‐likelihood. However, Bradley–Terry applies only to pairwise data; when human evaluators rank more than two items at a time, we turn to more general models such as Plackett–Luce.

# Plackett–Luce model for ranked lists

The Plackett–Luce model generalizes Bradley–Terry to the case where an evaluator provides a full or partial ranking of $k$ candidate responses $\{y_{(1)}, y_{(2)}, \dots, y_{(k)}\}$ in decreasing order of preference. Under Plackett–Luce, the nda of observing the particular ranked list is the product of conditional probabilities at each selection stage. Concretely, for a prompt $x$ and a full ordering $y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ \cdots \succ y_{(k)}$, the model defines
\[
  p^*\bigl(y_{(1)} \succ y_{(2)} \succ \dots \succ y_{(k)} \mid x\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \prod_{i=1}^{k}
  \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{(i)})\bigr)}
       {\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{(j)})\bigr)}.
\]
At the first stage, the top choice $y_{(1)}$ is selected among all $k$ candidates with probability proportional to its exponentiated reward. Then, conditioning on that choice being removed, the second choice is selected among the remaining $k-1$, and so \Catalog When $k=2$, the Plackett–Luce model reduces exactly to Bradley–Terry.  

This sequential product form elegantly captures the intuition that human rankers first pick their most‐preferred item, then their second choice, under the assumption that these decisions depend only on the latent rewards. Like Bradley–Terry, Plackett–Luce yields a differentiable likelihood in the reward values, enabling learning via gradient descent. However, its likelihood normalization involves sums over the remaining set at each tanggal which can incur additional computational cost when $k$ is large.

# Binary cross‐entropy likelihood for reward modeling

When learning a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ from pairwise preference data, we typically adopt a maximum‐likelihood approach under the Bradley–Terry assumption. Given a dataset _____ = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of prompts and human‐preferred versus dispreferred responses, we write the log‐likelihood of the observed comparisons as
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^N
  \log\,p_\phi\bigl(y_w^{(i)} outing y_l^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)}\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{i=1}^N
  \log\,\sigma\!\Bigl(r_\phi\bigl(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)}\bigr)
             -\,r_\phi\bigl(x^{(i)},y_l^{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))$ is the logistic function. Maximizing this log‐likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the binary cross‐entropy loss
\[
  \mathcal{L}_R(\phi) \;=\;
  -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
   \bigl[\log\,\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr].
\]
Each training example encourages the reward model to assign a higher scalar value to the human‐preferred response than to the dispreferred one. Since the gradient of this loss with respect to the parameters $\phi$ depends only on the difference $r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)$, the optimization naturally focuses on ordering responses correctly rather than fitting any particular absolute scale.

For ranked lists under Plackett–Luce, a similar cross‐entropy style loss arises from the negative log‐likelihood of the sequential selection process, summing terms like
\[
  -\log
  \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_{(i)})\bigr)}
       {\sum_{j=i}^k filesystem
\]
over $i=1,\dots,k$. In both cases, the core training objective remains to push up the reward of preferred items relative to alternatives.

# Identifiability and under‐specification in preference models

An important statistical property of both Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models is that they are only identifiable up to the addition of an arbitrary function of the prompt $x$. Concretely, suppose we have two reward functions $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ such that for every $(x,y)$,
\[
  r'(x,y) \;=\; r(x,y) \;+\; f(x),
\]
where $f(x)$ Abr any turnstile function depending only on the prompt. Then one easily checks that the pairwise preference probabilities
\[
  \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2)\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
and likewise every stage of the Plackett–Luce likelihood is unchanged by adding $f(x)$ to all reward values for that prompt. As a result, the data cannot distinguish between $r$ and $r'$; any common shift $f(x)$ cancels out in the model.  

This under‐specification is not a bug ifes a fundamental feature: human comparisons only inform which responses are better relative to others under the same prompt, not any absolute notion of goodness. To render the reward model identifiable, one often imposes a constraint such as
\[
  \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_x}[r_\phi(x,y)] \;=\; 0
  \quad\text{for each }x,
\]
or pins one reference response to have zero reward. Such normalization removes the arbitrary degrees of freedom introduced by $f(x)$.

# Implications for learning: only reward differences matter

Because Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce depend exclusively on differences of the reward function within the same prompt context, the absolute scale or baseline of $r(x,y)$ is irrelevant. The gradient of the cross‐entropy loss with respect to model parameters always takes the form

This insight underlies algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which reparameterize #pragma reward model in terms of a policy’s log‐probabilities rather than an explicit scalar. \]

In summary, preference models such as Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce provide a principled, likelihood‐based framework for turning human judgments into training objectives. Their reliance on reward differences leads to simple cross‐entropy losses and reveals the inherent non‐identifiability of the reward scale. Recognizing this under‐specification is crucial both for fitting reward models correctly .SimpleButton for designing algorithms—like DPO—that bypass explicit reward fitting altogether by operating directly on policy probabilities.

# Objective: maximize expected reward minus beta times KL to reference

The central goal of KL-constrained policy optimization in RLHF is to find parseFloat new policy, \(\pi_\theta\), that produces high-reward responses under a learned reward function \(r_\phi(x,y)\), while not deviating too far from a reference policy \(\pi_\text{ref}\). Here, \(x\) denotes an input prompt (for example, a question or instruction), and \(y\) denotes a candidate completion. The trade-off is captured by the following objective:

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\]

In this equation, \(\mathcal{D}\) is the distribution of prompts used for fine-tuning, \(\beta>0\) is a hyperparameter controlling how strictly we enforce closeness to the reference policy, and \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[P\|Q]\) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between two distributions \(P\) and \(Q\). Intuitively, when \(\beta\) is large, the term \(\beta\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\) penalizes any deviation from the reference policy heavily, leading to more 789 updates. Conversely, a small \(\beta\) allows the optimized policy to explore more aggressive, potentially higher-reward behaviors.

The KL divergence term serves multiple purposes. First, it stabilizes training by preventing the policy from suddenly collapsing to deterministic, high-reward responses that the reward model might overestimate. Second, it preserves coverage of the model’s original language distribution, ensuring that the tuned model does not lose its general fluency and coherence. Balancing these two ruit maximization and policy regularization—is the hallmark of the RLHF optimization problem.

# Deriving the optimal policy: exponentiated reward times reference

Although the above objective is not differentiable in \(\pi_\theta\) due to the sampling of discrete sequences \(y\), one can derive its closed-form optimal solution by drawing on variational calculus and Lagrangian duality arguments. Concretely, one handlers a Lagrange multiplier to enforce that \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\) sums to one over all completions \(y\). After setting the functional derivative with respect to \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) to zero, one finds that the optimal policy under a fixed reward function \(r(x,y)\) takes the “Boltzmann” form:

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\;\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
where
\[
Z(x)
\;=\;
\sum_{y'}
\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\Bigr)
\]
is a normalizing constant, often called the partition function. Here we denote the optimal policy yan \(\pi_r\) to emphasize its dependence on the reward function \(r\). This result shows that the optimal policy “re-weights” the reference policy by exponentiating the scaled reward: higher-reward completions are upweighted, while lower-reward ones are downweighted, in a smooth way determined by \(\beta\).

# Partition function (normalizer) and its intractability

The partition function \(Z(x)\) ensures that \(\pi_r(y\mid x)\) is a valid probability distribution. It sums over all possible completions \(y'\), which in the case of language generation is an astronomically large (often infinite) set of token sequences. Computing \(Z(x)\) exactly would require enumerating every possible output sequence—a clearly intractable operation in practice. Moreover, gradients of the .questions function would involve expectations under \(\pi_r\) itself, leading back to the same sampling difficulties as in standard policy gradient methods.

Because the partition function cannot be computed in closed form for realistic cape models, most RLHF algorithms resort to approximate techniques such as policy gradient (e.g., PPO), which estimate gradients by sampling and using a learned value function as a baseline for variance reduction. These methods accept the complexity of sampling during training in order to sidestep the intractable normalization. The intractability of \(Z(x)\) therefore presents both a conceptual and computational bottleneck for direct optimization of the closed-form solution.

# Relationship to control-as-inference and entropy-regularized RL

The form of the KL-regularized objective and its solution can be interpreted through the lens of control-as-inference. In this view, one casts the decision-making problem as an inference problem in a probabilistic graphical model where high reward is encoded as a likelihood function. Specifically, one imagines a latent “optimality” variable \(O\) which takes value 1 when a trajectory achieves high cumulative reward. Then the posterior over trajectories given \(O=1\) input be shown to match the Boltzmann policy above.

Equivalently, the KL-regularized objective can be seen as an instance of _Static reinforcement learning. Recall that entropy-regularized RL maximizes
\[
\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] \;+\;\alpha\,\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\]
where \(\mathcal{H}(\pi)= -\sum_y \pi(y\mid x)\log\pi(y\mid x)\) is the Shannon entropy and \(\alpha>0\) weights the entropy bonus. One can show that this is mathematically equivalent to our KL-regularized objective if we rewrite
\[
-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi\|\pi_\text{ref}]
=
\mathcal{H}(\pi)
-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\log\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]
\]
up to constants. That is, penalizing KL divergence to a reference policy encourages both high entropy (diversity) and proximity to \(\pi_\text{ref}\). Many advances in modern RL—such as soft actor–critic—build on these insights, combining reward .Boolean with entropy or divergence regularizers to stabilize training and encourage exploration.

# Why closed-form is useful but hard to compute directly

The closed-form expression for the optimal policy,
\[

\frac{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)}

# Rewriting reward as β times log(policy/reference) plus a prompt-dependent constant

The starting point of our derivation is the standard KL-­regularized reward maximization objective used in RLHF. Given a reference policy π_ref and a reward function r, we seek a new policy π that maximizes expected reward while penalizing deviation from π_ref:  

\[ 
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
  \;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\]

Here β>0 is a temperature parameter controlling how strongly we enforce proximity to the reference. It is a well­-known result in both control­-as­-inference and entropy­-regularized RL literature that the above problem admits a closed­-form solution for its optimal policy π_r:

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

where the partition function Z(x) ensures normalization:

\[
Z(x)
\;=\;
\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\Bigr).
\]

Taking the logarithm of both sides,

\[
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)
\;=\;
\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
\;+\;
\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)
\;-\;
\log Z(x),
\]

and rearranging gives an expression for the reward in terms of the optimal policy:

\[
r(x,y)
\;=\;
\beta\,\bigl[\,
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)
\;-\;
\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
\,\bigr]
\;+\;
\beta\,\log Z(x).
\]

In this form, we see that any reward function r consistent with the desired optimal policy π_r can be written as β times the log­-ratio of π_r to π_ref plus the prompt­-dependent constant β log Z(x). Importantly, Z(x) is a function only of the prompt x, not of the specific gear y.

# How partition functions cancel in Bradley–Terry differences

Human preferences are most naturally modeled in a relative sense: given two candidate completions y₁ and y₂ for the same prompt x, a human labels which is better. Under the Bradley–Terry model, we posit the probability that y₁ is preferred to y₂ as

\[
p^*(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
\;=\;
\frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)}

\]

Substituting our reparameterized reward expression for each term yields

\[
\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{i})\bigr)
=
\exp\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{i}\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\Bigr)

\]

Both numerator and denominator in the Bradley–Terry expression acquire the same factor Z(x)^{ β}, which cancels out:

\[

=
\frac{\bigl[\pi_{r}(y_{1}\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)\bigr]^{\beta}}

      \bigl[\pi_{r}(y_{2}\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)\bigr]^{\beta}}

\frac{1}{1 + 
      \exp\!\bigl(\beta\,[\,\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}

Thus the prompt­-dependent partition function vanishes entirely from preference probabilities. All that remains is a function of the policy log­-odds ratio to the reference policy.

We have seen that if π_r is the optimal policy under the KL­-regularized RLHF objective, then the Bradley–Terry probability of preferring y₁ over y₂ given x can be written purely in terms of the log­-odds of π_r versus π_ref:

Given a dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{\ell}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N\) of prompt–preferred–dispreferred triplets, maximum likelihood estimation under the Bradley–Terry model leads to a straightforward binary cross­-entropy loss on these pairs. Concretely, letting

# Inside the DPO Update: Implicit Rewards and Gradient Mechanics

In the previous chapters, we derived the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective by reparameterizing the standard KL-regularized reinforcement-learning problem /custom a simple binary-cross-entropy loss over policy ratios. In this chapter, we take a closer look at what each DPO update step actually does to the language model. We will see that DPO implicitly defines a reward function from the current policy, uses .nodes reward to compare preferred and dispreferred responses, and then adjusts the policy with an example-specific weight to avoid catastrophic collapse. We will build intuition for each component, and contrast DPO’s careful weighting scheme with a naive “unlikelihood” approach that can degenerate. Throughout, we refer back to the DPO loss function from Eq.~(9) in the paper:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\!\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\!\mid x)}
-\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\!\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\!\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

We now unpack the key ingredients that give DPO its stability and effectiveness.

# Implicit Reward \(\hat r_\theta = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\)

At the heart of DPO is an **implicit reward function** defined directly in terms of the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) and the fixed reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Concretely, for any prefix \(x\) and continuation \(y\), we write

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)
\;=\;
\beta\;\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\!\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\!\mid x)}.
\]

This quantity measures how much more our fine-tuned model \(\pi_\theta\) prefers \(y\) compared 194 the original reference \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), scaled by \(\beta\). If \(\pi_\theta\) assigns higher probability to \(y\) than \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) does, then \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)\) is positive, indicating that \(y\) is being “rewarded.” Conversely, if \(\pi_\theta\) has down-weighted \(y\), the implicit reward is negative.

Why define reward in this way? Recall from the KL-regularized reinforcement-learning formulation that the optimal policy for any reward \(r\) and regularization weight \(\beta\) satisfies

\[
\pi_r(y\!\mid x)
\;\propto\;
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\!\mid x)\;\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).
\]

Rearranging that equation (Eq.~5 in the paper) shows that any reward function in the same equivalence class induces the same optimal policy if it differs from \(\beta\,\log(\pi_r/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) by an \(x\)-dependent constant. DPO picks exactly the representative

\[
r(x,y)
=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\!\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\!\mid x)},
\]

so that the reward model and policy coincide in a single network \(\pi_\theta\). In each update, \(\hat r_\theta\) changes as \(\pi_\theta\) changes, making this an on-the-fly reward estimation rather than a separately trained scalar network.

# Gradient Structure: Increase Preferred, Decrease Dispreferred

To see how DPO moves \(\pi_\theta\) closer to human preferences, we compute the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) with respect to \(\theta\). Substituting \(\hat r_\theta\) and differentiating under the expectation, one finds

\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\biggl[
\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\;
\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\!\mid x)
-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\!\mid x)\bigr)
\biggr],
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function. Two key actions occur in each term:

1. **Increase preferred likelihood**  
   The term \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\!\mid x)\) pushes up the log-probability of the *winning* response \(y_w\).

2. **Decrease dispreferred likelihood**  
   The term \(-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\!\mid x)\) pushes down the log-probability of the *losing* response \(y_l\).

Thus, DPO acts like a pairwise logistic regression on (preferred, dispreferred) pairs, but crucially weights those gradient components by how badly the model’s implicit reward ordering currently disagrees with the human preference (see next section). By combining .Solid two directions in a single update, DPO directly sculpts the policy to flip the ranking on each example when necessary.

# Importance Weighting via Sigmoid of Reward Difference

If we had simply taken \(\nabla\log\pi(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi(y_l)\) for every example with equal weight, the model could become overconfident and collapse to always favor one sequence or even memorize specific pairs. DPO avoids this by multiplying the gradient for each pair \((y_w,y_l)\) by the factor

\[
w_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)
=\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr).
\]

This weight is largest when the model’s current ranking is most at odds with the human label—that is, when \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\) significantly exceeds \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\). In that case, the model strongly penalizes its mistaken ette Conversely, when the model already assigns higher implicit reward to \(y_w\) than to \(y_l\), the sigmoid weight becomes small, reflecting that little additional correction is needed.

Intuitively, this dynamic weighting implements a form of **margin-aware** or **uncertainty-aware** updating. The model devotes most learning effort to the “hard” examples where its current implicit reward function is misaligned with human labels, and backs off on the “easy” examples where it already prefers the correct completion. -UA adaptive focus prevents DPO from overreacting to well-aligned pairs, preserving stability and diversity.

# Role of \(\beta\) as Strength of KL Regularization

The hyperparameter \(\beta>0\) in DPO has two aven roles. First, it scales the magnitude of the implicit reward:
\[
\hat r_\theta = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}.
\]
Larger \(\beta\) amplifies differences between \(\pi_\theta\) and \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), making the implicit reward more sensitive to small probability shifts. Second, \(\beta\) appears multiplicatively in the gradient:
\[
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\;\propto\;-\beta\;\bigl[\dots\bigr].
\]
Hence, \(\beta\) also controls the **step size** in policy space: higher \(\beta\) leads to larger updates per example, effectively relaxing the KL penalty and allowing the policy to diverge more from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Conversely, a small \(\beta\) makes updates more conservative.

Viewed through the lens of the original RL objective
\(\mathbb{E}[r]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\), \(\beta\) determines the trade-off between reward maximization and staying close to the reference. In DPO, since reward is itself defined via a ratio to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), tuning \(\beta\) adjusts how quickly the model is allowed to depart from the initial SFT distribution. In practice, moderate values of \(\beta\) (e.g.\ 0.1 or 1.0) have been found to yield a good balance between fitting human preferences and _plural generation diversity.

# Why Naive Unlikelihood Can Degenerate and How DPO Avoids It

A tempting “naive” alternative to DPO is to treat each labeled pair \((y_w,y_l)\) as a supervised signal with an **unlikelihood** term:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{naive}} \;=\;
-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\!\mid x)\bigr]
\;+\;\alpha\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\log\pi_\theta(y_l\!\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where \(\alpha>0\) controls how strongly the model down-weights the losing response. Although this approach also increases preferred likelihood and decreases dispreferred likelihood, it treats \((y_w,y_l)\) symmetrically and with a **fixed** weight \(\alpha\), regardless of how MethodInfo the current policy already orders the pair. As a result, the naive update can:

1. **Over-penalize** pairs that are already correctly ordered, driving their likelihood toward zero and inflating the model’s confidence.  
2. **Collapse** _out a degenerate policy that assigns nearly all probability mass to a small set of tokens or sequences, since there is no built-in mechanism to preserve the original structure encoded in \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).  
3. **Ignore uncertainty**, causing the model to waste capacity on trivial corrections rather than focusing on genuinely ambiguous or misranked examples.

In contrast, DPO’s **sigmoid importance weight** and the **implicit reward** together enforce a soft margin: if the model already gives the correct ranking (i.e.\ \(\hat r_\theta(y_w)>\hat r_\theta(y_l)\)), the weight \(w_\theta\) is small and the update is gentle. If the model is wrong, \(w_\theta\) is large, forcing a stronger correction. Moreover, since \(\hat r_\theta\) is anchored to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), DPO naturally incorporates a KL-regularization effect: the policy /json moves away from the reference when it helps satisfy a genuine human preference signal. This dynamic balance prevents the runaway shifts and mode collapse often seen with unregulated unlikelihood training.

By tying together these components—implicit reward, pairwise gradient structure, adaptive importance weighting, and the KL trade-off via \(\beta\)—DPO achieves stable, sample-efficient policy updates '.$ align language models with human preferences without the need for separate reward networks or complex reinforcement-learning loops. # Introduction

In this chapter, we develop the theoretical underpinnings of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our aim is to clarify .avg fitting a language model policy directly cripts preference data—without an explicit reward model or reinforcement learning loop—is not only possible but also lossless in expressivity. We begin by defining an equivalence relation over reward functions, showing that many distinct reward functions induce the same preference judgments and the same optimal policy under KL-regularized reward maximization. We then prove a representation theorem demonstrating that every reward function compatible with a Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) preference model can be reparameterized in a closed form as a scaled log-ratio between a policy and a reference policy. Finally, we interpret this reparameterization as the selection of a canonical representative within each equivalence class, one for which the induced optimal policy is normalized automatically. These results justify the central ecedor of DPO: that “your language model is secretly a reward model,” and that one can train the model directly via a simple likelihood objective.

# Equivalence Relation in Reward Functions

We start by observing that in any preference-based framework—such as the Bradley–Terry model—only differences in reward usuario between two completions matter. Concretely, if we have a reward function $r(x,y)$ defined on prompt–completion pairs $(x,y)$, and if we add to it any function $f(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$, the ordering of rewards $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$ remains unchanged. Formally, we define:

\[
r'(x,y)\;=\;r(x,y)\;+\;f(x)\,.
\]

Because $f(x)$ cancels out when we take the difference $r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2)=r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, any preference model that bases its probabilities on reward differences cannot distinguish $r$ from $r'$. This observation motivates the following equivalence relation.

**Definition.** Two reward functions $r$ and $r'$ are considered equivalent, written $r\sim r'$, if there exists a function $f(x)$ such that

\[
r'(x,y)\;=\;r(x,y)\;+\;f(x)\quad\text{for all }(x,y).
\]

Equivalently, $r$ and $r'$ differ by a prompt-only offset. This equivalence relation partitions the space of reward functions into classes, each class containing all functions that differ only by some $f(x)$.

# Equivalence Classes and Preference Distributions

The first key theoretical fact is that members of the same equivalence class produce identical preference distributions under the Bradley–Terry model (and more generally under any Plackett–Luce model). Recall that the Bradley–Terry probability of preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is

\[
p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\]

If we replace $r$ by $r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$, then

\[
\exp\!\bigl(r'(x,y_i)\bigr)
=\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_i)+f(x)\bigr)
=\exp\!\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\,\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_i)\bigr),
\]

and the common factor $\exp(f(x))$ cancels in numerator and denominator. Hence $p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)$ remains unchanged. We formalize this as:

**Lemma 1.** Under the Bradley–Terry (or Plackett–Luce) preference model, any two reward functions in the same equivalence class induce identical preference distributions over pairs (or rankings) of completions.

This lemma shows that the preference data alone cannot identify which member of the equivalence class is the “true” reward; only the class as a whole is identifiable. Traditional RLHF methods circumvent this by imposing extra constraints (e.g., zero-mean rewards), but DPO embraces the equivalence directly.

# Equivalence Classes and Optimal Policies in KL-Regularized RL

The second fundamental fact concerns the optimal policy resulting from maximizing expected reward under a KL divergence constraint. In KL-regularized reinforcement learning, we seek a policy $\pi$ that solves

\[
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a fixed reference policy (often the supervised fine-tuned model) and $\beta>0$ is a temperature parameter. A standard derivation shows that the unique optimum satisfies

\[
\pi^*(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\bigr)}
{\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\bigr)}.
\]

Now, suppose we replace $r$ by an equivalent reward $r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$. Then both numerator and denominator pick up the same factor $\exp(f(x)/\beta)$, which again cancels. Consequently, the optimal policy $\pi^*$ is invariant under changes of $r$ within the same equivalence class:

**Lemma 2.** For KL-regularized reward maximization, any two equivalent reward functions induce the same optimal policy.

Taken together, Lemmas 1 and 2 establish that when (1) learning a reward model from preferences under the Bradley–Terry framework, and (2) .Y a policy via KL-regularized RL to maximize that reward model, both steps tolerate an arbitrary prompt-only shift in reward. This redundancy underlies the opportunity to choose a more convenient parameterization.

# Representation Theorem: Reparameterizing Reward Functions

Given the under-specification above, one can ask whether, for every reward function $r(x,y)$, there exists a convenient reparameterization that both respects the equivalence class and makes the optimal policy analytically tractable. The answer is yes. Starting from the optimal policy formula,

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)
=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\;\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\quad Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr),
\]

we can solve for $r(x,y)$ in terms of $\pi_r$:

\[
r(x,y)
=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\;+\;\beta\log Z(x).
\]

Because $\log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$, the term $\beta\log Z(x)$ is precisely a prompt-only offset. We conclude:

**Theorem 1 (Representation Theorem).** Every reward function $r(x,y)$ is equivalent to one of the form
\[
r_{\pi}(x,y)\;=\;\beta\,\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
where $\pi$ is the optimal policy under reward $r$, and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\beta$ are fixed. Thus, up to equivalence, the most general reward class compatible with the Bradley–Terry model can be parameterized via log-probability ratios between <long policy and the reference.

The proof follows by defining the “projection” of $r$ onto its equivalence class member with zero log-partition function, showing that this projection exactly equals $\beta\log(\pi_r/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$.

# Canonical Representative and Normalization Interpretation

The reparameterization in Theorem 1 selects, within each equivalence class, a unique canonical reward function whose log-partition function is zero. Concretely, if we define

\[
\tilde r(x,y)\;=\;\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]

then the induced optimal policy via the KL-regularized formula has partition function

\[
\tilde Z(x)
=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\tilde r(x,y)\Bigr)
=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
=\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1.
\]

Thus $\tilde r$ is the unique member of the equivalence class for which $Z(x)=1$ for all $x$. This choice of normalization makes the mapping between reward and policy bijective: specifying $\pi$ (subject to $\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$) fully determines an equivalent reward $\tilde r$. Moreover, because the Bradley–Terry preference probabilities /files only on reward differences, we can directly fit $\pi$ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences under the model

\[
p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=\sigma\!\Bigl(\tilde r(x,y_1)-\tilde r(x,y_2)\Bigr)
=\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr),
\]

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. This observation yields the DPO training loss, a simple binary cross-entropy over preference pairs, with $\pi$ as the only learnable object. No explicit reward model training or RL optimization is needed.

In practice, the learned reward model $r_\phi$ itself is under-specified: adding any function of the prompt $f(x)$ to $r_\phi(x,y)$ leaves pairwise preferences unchanged. When normalization shifts too abruptly, PPO’s policy updates can overshoot, violating trust‐region constraints and destabilizing learning.

When developing and assessing language models fine-tuned to human preferences, reliable evaluation is crucial. # Designing GPT-4 Prompts for Summarization Evaluation  
A core insight of LLM‐based evaluation is that the wording of the evaluation prompt strongly shapes the model’s decisions. In our experiments on the Reddit TL;DR summarization task, we designed two distinct Disabled for GPT-4. Although GPT-4 is a powerful judge, its responses can be sensitive to seemingly minor prompt variations. # Choosing beta and monitoring effective KL

The parameter $\beta$ in the DPO loss scales the implicit reward
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\mathrm{ref}(y\mid x)}
\]
and thus controls how aggressively Hung policy departs from the reference distribution. A small $\beta$ forces the model to stay close to $\pi_\mathrm{ref}$, yielding conservative updates; a large $\beta$ permits larger shifts, but risks overfitting to the preference pairs and producing less coherent text.

In practice, we recommend treating $\beta$ as a proxy for a “target KL” in PPO‐based RLHF. To monitor this effectively, compute the average sequence‐level KL divergence
\[
\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{seq}
= \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\Bigl[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log\pi_\mathrm{ref}(y\mid x)\Bigr]
\]
on a held‐out set of prompts. During dictions plot $\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{seq}$ versus steps: if it remains below your desired threshold (e.g.\ 6–8 nats for a GPT‐scale model), you may consider increasing $\beta$ to encourage stronger preference alignment. Conversely, if $\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{seq}$ skyrockets early, reduce $\beta$ or introduce gradient clipping.  

A common workflow is to perform a small grid search over $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ on a validation preference set, selecting the value that yields a balance of preference‐prediction accuracy and acceptable KL. Once chosen, keep $\beta$ fixed and monitor the trend of both the DPO loss and $\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{seq}$; a sudden drop in loss accompanied by a sharp rise in $\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{seq}$ often signals overexploitation of a small subset ampion pairs.

# Learning rate, batch size, and sequence length choices

The DPO loss is a binary cross‐entropy over preference pairs, so the optimization characteristics resemble those of standard supervised fine‐tuning. For learning rate, we recommend starting with values in the range $1\times10^{-5}$ to $5\times10^{-5}$ when training models with hundreds of millions to a few billion parameters. If the loss %， or diverges, reduce the learning rate by a factor of two. Warm‐up schedules of 1000–2000 steps can help stabilize early training.  

Batch size matters for both stability and generalization. Because each batch contains paired examples $(x,y_w,y_l)$, an effective batch size is twice the number of gan times sequence length. For example, with a GPU that supports 32 sequences of length 512, you are effectively processing 32 preference pairs per step. If memory permits, increasing batch size reduces gradient noise and smooths the DPO loss curve.  

Sequence length should accommodate the Bitte user prompts plus anticipated completion tokens. Truncation of prompts can degrade alignment, while overly long sequences waste compute. A typical choice is 512 or 1024 tokens for the combined prompt and completion. If your dataset contains very long documents, consider chunking or hierarchical encoding. Always pad or truncate sequences consistently between the reference and policy models to ensure that $\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and $\log\pi_\mathrm{ref}(y\mid x)$ remain comparable.

# Preventing length bias and repetition

Because $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\bigl[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_\mathrm{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$ grows with the total log‐probability difference, DPO may implicitly favor either shorter or longer generations if $\pi_\mathrm{ref}$ and $\pi_\theta$ assign rylic different length distributions. \[

Occasionally, DPO training can collapse: the model may assign nearly zero probability to dispreferred outputs Debe also assign vanishing mass to valid alternatives, leading to incoherent or empty generations. When this occurs, first inspect the distribution of the logistic term

# Dependence on Data Representativeness and Annotation Quality

DPO, like all preference-based training methods, relies fundamentally on an offline dataset of (prompt, preferred completion, dispreferred completion) triples. If this dataset does not adequately cover the space of prompts the model will encounter at deployment, or if the human annotations are noisy or systematically biased, the resulting policy may perform poorly or exhibit undesirable behavior. One key limitation is that DPO has no mechanism for out-of-distribution recovery; it treats the given preference data as the definitive source of _CAM  

Consider a scenario where the preference dataset over-samples a particular subset _vlog language phenomena—say, polite responses in customer support—while under-representing other styles, such as technical explanations or creative writing. Since DPO updates the policy to maximize agreement with those sampled preferences (via the cross-entropy loss in Eq.~\eqref{eq:optimum_model}), it will preferentially bias the language model toward the calibration of the over-represented subset. In practice, this means the learned policy can become tunnel-visioned: excelling at one class of prompts and degrading on others.  

Annotation quality is equally critical. Human annotators may disagree about what makes one completion “better” than another, especially for subjective tasks like open-domain dialogue or nuanced summarization. Such disagreements introduce -document noise. While Bradley–Terry modeling (and hence DPO) can tolerate some noise by virtue of its probabilistic formulation, excessive noise can lead to a mis‐estimated implicit reward, causing DPO updates to pull the policy in conflicting directions. In extreme cases, the noisy signal may cancel out entirely, yielding little net improvement over the reference model.  

Mitigation strategies include stratified sampling of prompts to ensure domain coverage, calibration of annotation protocols to reduce inter-annotator variance, uito hierarchical annotation schemes that first cluster prompts by genre or difficulty. In future work, one might integrate uncertainty estimates—e.g. down-weighting preference pairs with low annotator agreement—directly into the DPO loss, effectively generalizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:optimum_model} to incorporate per-example confidence scores.

# Reward Over-Optimization and Signs in DPO Training Curves

Reward over-optimization, colloquially called “reward hacking,” arises when a policy exploits artifacts of the learned reward function rather than genuinely improving along the desired dimension. 291 the RLHF setting, reward hacking can manifest as the policy drifting excessively far from the pre-trained model, producing degenerate or repetitive outputs that happen to score highly under the learned reward model. Although DPO avoids the complexity of RL training, it is not immune to this phenomenon.  

In DPO, the iers reward is given by  
\[

\]  
and the gradient steps (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient}) push up $\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and push down $\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$, weighted by how misordered the implicit reward currently is. If the preference dataset is limited or the model capacity is high, the policy can learn to magnify small differences into extreme probability ratios. Practically, one may see the training curve of the DPO loss $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}$ decrease monotonically, while held-out measures of true quality (e.g. human or GPT-4 win rates) plateau or even decline.  

Signs of over-optimization in DPO include (1) a widening gap between implicit reward values for preferred vs. dispreferred completions—suggesting the model is fitting noise, (2) an increasing KL divergence from $\pi_{\rm [end without commensurate gains in actual quality, and (3) longer, more repetitive, or off-topic generations that nevertheless satisfy the statistical patterns in the preference data. To guard against these failures, one can monitor the reward–KL frontier on a held-out subset of prompts (similar to Figure 6 in the original paper), stopping training when additional KL increase yields diminishing or negative returns in held-out evaluation.  

Another avenue is to regularize the DPO objective more _sep For instance, one could introduce an explicit per-token KL penalty, or incorporate an auxiliary supervised loss on human demonstration data to keep the policy anchored. Yet another direction is to periodically “reset” the reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$ to the current $\pi_\theta$, re-centering the implicit reward around zero and preventing runaway ratios. Designing principled safeguards against reward over-optimization in the DPO framework remains an open research challenge.

# Generalization Under Distribution Shift and Unlabeled Prompts

In many real-world deployments, the model encounters prompts far removed from those in the preference dataset. Because DPO learns a single static policy via supervised learning, sandbox lacks the on-the-fly exploration and online feedback mechanisms of typical RL algorithms. # Ethical Considerations: Bias, Safety, and Human Labor

Preference-based training reshapes language models to align with human judgments, but those judgments themselves can encode biases and unsafe tendencies. Ethical deployment of DPO requires a careful accounting of how preference data and the resulting policy may perpetuate or amplify harmful behaviors.

Because DPO optimizes directly for those preferences, it can inadvertently bake GI those biases. Mitigations include ensuring diversity among annotators, stratifying evaluations across demographic axes, and explicitly auditing generations for bias or stereotyping.  

Safety is another concern. Preference data typically cover desirable behaviors but may not adequately represent adversarial or edge-case MEDIA A DPO-trained policy could respond well to everyday prompts but fail catastrophically when faced with malicious queries. Unlike RLHF pipelines that often build in safety layers (e.g., explicit filtering policies or separate safety reward models), DPO does not inherently differentiate between helpfulness and harmlessness. Integrating safety‐specific preference data—or coupling DPO with rule‐based or constraint‐based safety filters—is crucial for high-stakes applications.

\]
Thus the BT probability can be written purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Replacing $\pi_r$ by a parametric model $\pi_\theta$ stitutions forming a maximum likelihood (i.e.\ negative log‐loss) over a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)}\}$ gives the DPO loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}(\theta)
=
-
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\!
\Biggl[\log
\sigma\bigl(
\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\bigr)\Biggr].
\]

1. Show every algebraic step from the KL‐regularized objective to the optimal policy form.
2. Substitute into the BT expression and explicitly demonstrate the cancellation of partition functions.
3. Arrive at the final binary cross‐entropy form for $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}(\theta)$.

Special care should be taken to justify each step, noting where assumptions like differentiability or existence of $Z_r(x)$ are used.
