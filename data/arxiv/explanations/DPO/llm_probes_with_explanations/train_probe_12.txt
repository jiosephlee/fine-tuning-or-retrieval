According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', when the model already rates $y_w$ much higher than $y_l$, the per-example weight in the DPO gradient, $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$, becomes near zero. This follows directly from the DPO gradient expression, which for a preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$ is $-\beta\,\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w))(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x))]$, where the implicit reward is $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$; if the model already prefers $y_w$ strongly, then $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\gg \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)$, so the argument to the sigmoid, $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)$, is a large negative number, and since $\sigma(z)\to 0$ as $z\to -\infty$, the weight attenuates toward zero, yielding a vanishing update on pairs the model already orders correctly. Intuitively, because the Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on reward differences, and the DPO reparameterization ties these differences to log-probability ratios relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, large positive margins for the preferred response imply a confident, correct preference prediction, so the cross-entropy gradient downweights such easy examples; the scaling by $\beta$ further modulates this margin, reflecting the KL constraint strength and preventing unnecessary or destabilizing updates when the policy is already aligned on that pair.
