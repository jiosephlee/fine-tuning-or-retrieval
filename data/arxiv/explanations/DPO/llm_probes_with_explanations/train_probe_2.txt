According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors' ablation-style reasoning indicates that removing the dynamic, per-example importance weighting from DPO is likely to lead to model degeneration. This follows from the DPO gradient, which scales the push–pull update on each preference pair by the logistic residual term $\sigma\!\left(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\right)$, where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$; this weight is large precisely when the implicit reward (hence the policy) misorders the pair and small when it already orders them correctly, yielding a calibrated, KL-aware correction that mirrors the Bradley–Terry likelihood and the constrained-optimal policy in Eq. (4)–(7). Dropping this factor effectively turns the objective into a naïve margin maximization of $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ (akin to unlikelihood training), which pushes probabilities uniformly regardless of how confident the model already is, breaking the change-of-variables correspondence to the KL-constrained RL objective and removing the self-normalizing effect of $\beta$; as a result, the model can over-inflate preferred responses and over-suppress dispreferred ones, drift far from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, and collapse diversity (i.e., degeneration). Consistent with this mechanism, the authors report in Appendix Table A.2 that a naïve probability-ratio variant without the weighting coefficient leads to degenerate generations, underscoring the stabilizing role of the per-example importance weighting.
