Motivation: From Knowledge to Preferred Behavior

# Capabilities of an unsupervised language model versus controllability needs
Large-scale unsupervised pretraining equips a single language model with a broad repository of factual knowledge, stylistic patterns, and some reasoning tendencies. Through exposure to massive corpora, the parameters of the network encode many ways to continue a prefix, imitate genres, and produce plausible completions for a wide array of prompts. This broad competence is powerful: a single pretrained instance can respond about many topics, perform arithmetic-like steps, and imitate diverse writing voices without task-specific supervision.

However, this general competence is distinct from the narrower objective of producing the particular kinds of responses a developer or end user wants in deployment. The pretrained model’s implicit priorities reflect the varied intents and quality levels present in its training material: some continuations reflect expert-level solutions, others reflect casual opinion or error. When the system is deployed it is often desirable to bias behavior toward a specific subset of the possible continuations—for example, high-quality technical answers, concise summaries, or responses that avoid certain unsafe content. That biasing requirement is what I call controllability: the ability to steer the model’s outputs toward preferred behaviors while retaining the underlying competence and fluency the pretrained instance provides.

Controllability imposes two linked constraints. First, the preferred behavior should improve the outputs on desiderata of interest: factuality, helpfulness, succinctness, safety, or task-specific quality. Second, steering must avoid catastrophic degeneration: the system should not collapse to a narrow set of repetitive replies nor wander into outputs that the preference signal cannot reliably evaluate. Practically, that means any mechanism to push the model in a preferred direction must also include a restraint that keeps the adapted policy close to the original distribution on which the model’s general capability is defined.

# Examples of misalignment: misconceptions, coding quality, safety
Concrete examples illuminate why this steering matters. A pretrained language model may have encoded a widely believed but incorrect claim alongside more accurate statements. If the deployment goal is to provide correct answers, the system must not present the common misconception as the dominant reply; instead it should prefer the higher-quality evidence-based response that may be less frequent in the training text. In other words, the goal is not merely to represent population-level priors, but to select the subset of knowledge one wants the model to express.

In code generation, the same distinction appears. A pretrained instance may have seen both idiomatic, well-tested code and snippets that contain subtle bugs or anti-patterns. When used as a coding assistant, the system should privilege the higher-quality patterns—clear, secure, and correct implementations—even if those appear less often in raw text. Without targeted steering, the model will produce an indiscriminate mixture of both kinds of completions.

Safety concerns provide further examples. The training data may contain toxic or unsafe language produced by some authors. While the model must retain the ability to discuss safety topics when appropriate, the deployment objective commonly requires that the model avoid generating harmful content or must respond with mitigations. Achieving that selective behavior again requires a mechanism to prefer some completions over others, beyond what unsupervised pretraining provides.

Across these cases the underlying challenge is not the absence of capability in the pretrained instance; rather, it is the absence of a reliable, controllable selector that chooses which of the many competent continuations are acceptable for the deployed context.

# Instruction tuning versus preference-based tuning
One widely used way to bias behavior is to adjust the pretrained parameters with examples of desired outputs. A typical procedure is to present many prompts together with human-written or otherwise high-quality completions and update the language model’s parameters to increase the probability of those target continuations. This process, often called supervised fine-tuning, can make the model more responsive to instructions and improve baseline usefulness. It teaches the network to map prompts to the style and content exemplified by the demonstration set.

However, supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations has limitations. High-quality demonstrations can be expensive to collect because they often require expert-level writing or domain expertise. In many settings, rather than crafting exact demonstrations, it is easier for people to make relative judgments: presented two alternative continuations for the same prompt, a rater can reliably indicate which they prefer without producing a new exemplar. This type of comparative signal captures preference at scale while being less demanding per judgment.

Preference-based tuning embraces that kind of feedback. Instead of training on explicit demonstrations, one gathers pairs of continuations for the same prompt and records which of the pair is preferred by a rater. The collected paired judgments give a dataset of comparisons that encode which responses should be assigned higher “score.” This relative format is often more scalable for large corpora of prompts, since producing two completions from a base policy is cheaper than authoring a high-quality completion from scratch for each prompt, and many more prompts can be judged in parallel by non-expert raters.

# Why relative judgments from people are scalable
Relative preference labels have three practical advantages. First, for many tasks people find it easier to compare than to produce: judging between two completions avoids the cognitive load of composing a whole response and lets a rater focus on quality differences. Second, generating candidate continuations can be automated by sampling from an existing reference policy, so the human time is spent only on judgment rather than production. Third, comparative judgments allow broad coverage: the same reference policy can be sampled across many prompts, and each sampled pair elicits information about the underlying scoring function that ranks completions. With a moderate number of comparisons per prompt, one can recover a robust ordering of candidate replies.

From a statistical perspective, pairwise comparisons connect naturally to probabilistic preference models like the Bradley–Terry family. Those models formalize the probability that a rater prefers one completion over another as a function of latent scores assigned to each completion. Fitting such models to batches of pairwise judgments recovers an estimate of the latent scoring function up to certain identifiability constraints. Crucially, the pairwise format transfers well to large-scale collection: many cheap comparisons can produce a dense picture of relative quality across a wide space of candidate outputs.

# Overview of RLHF and its complexity
A common end-to-end pipeline that uses pairwise preferences proceeds in three conceptual stages. First, the pretrained language model is adapted with supervised fine-tuning on a corpus of high-quality demonstrations to obtain a reasonable reference policy; this step helps the later stages by keeping sampled continuations coherent and diverse. Second, the reference policy is sampled to produce candidate continuations for prompts; pairs of such continuations are then presented to people who indicate which they prefer. Third, the collected comparisons are used to fit a scalar function that aims to assign higher numbers to preferred completions; this scalar is then treated as a surrogate reward that guides a subsequent policy optimization step. The policy optimization objective is typically to maximize the expected surrogate reward under the new policy while penalizing deviation from the reference policy, commonly with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence term that preserves coverage and prevents collapse.

Formally, letting \(x\) denote a prompt and \(y\) a completion, the Bradley–Terry preference model states that the probability an evaluator chooses \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) depends on a latent score function \(r^*(x,y)\) through
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) = \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)}.
\]
A parametrized scalar function \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is fitted to the paired comparisons by maximizing the binary log-likelihood. Then the policy \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) is optimized to maximize expected reward under \(\pi_\theta\) while controlling divergence from a reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\), typically solving
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\; y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\, r_\phi(x,y)\,\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big],
\]
for a scalar \(\beta>0\) that trades off reward improvement and proximity to the reference. Because the output \(y\) is discrete, direct differentiation through the sampling process is not available, so practitioners optimize this objective with policy-optimization procedures from the reinforcement literature. These procedures involve sampling trajectories, estimating gradients via Monte Carlo, and managing additional components such as value estimators or baselines. In practice this pipeline is effective but operationally complex: it requires training a separate reward regressor, rolling out a sampling loop during the optimization phase, and tuning many optimization hyperparameters to stabilize training.

# High-level idea of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Direct Preference Optimization offers a distinct route that bypasses fitting a standalone scalar regressor and avoids the explicit reinforcement-style optimization loop. The key insight is algebraic: under the KL-constrained objective above, for any given scalar reward function \(r(x,y)\) the optimal policy has a closed-form relation to the reference policy. Specifically, the optimal policy \(\pi_r\) that maximizes expected reward minus \(\beta\) times KL satisfies
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\big).
\]
Taking logarithms and rearranging yields an exact reparameterization of the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x),
\]
where \(Z(x)\) is the partition function. Crucially, many preference models—including Bradley–Terry—depend only on differences of scores for two completions, and the additive partition-function term cancels when taking those differences. That cancellation means the pairwise preference probability can be expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^*\) and the reference \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), without the intermediate scalar regressor:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big),
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))\) is the logistic function.

This identity suggests a direct fitting objective for a parametrized policy \(\pi_\theta\): take the collection of observed triples \((x,y_{\mathrm{win}},y_{\mathrm{lose}})\) and maximize the likelihood that \(\pi_\theta\) assigns higher implicit score ratios to the preferred completions. Concretely, the loss becomes
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\left[\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\right].
\]
Optimizing this objective updates the policy parameters so that the implicit reward
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;:=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
ranks preferred completions higher than dispreferred ones. The key benefits of this construction are practical and theoretical. Practically, the optimization reduces to a simple binary cross-entropy over pairs: no separate scalar regressor, no inner reinforcement-style sampling loop, and no need to estimate partition functions. Theoretical justification comes from observing that the reparameterization spans the same equivalence classes of reward functions relevant to pairwise preference models: adding a function of the prompt alone does not change pairwise probabilities or the optimal policy, and the DPO form picks a canonical representative of the equivalence class that makes the optimal policy explicit.

Intuitively, a DPO update increases the log-probability of the preferred completion and decreases that of the less preferred completion, but with a per-example weight given by the logistic of the implicit reward difference. This adaptive weighting prevents excessive pushes on examples where the current implicit reward already ranks the pair correctly and concentrates updates on misordered pairs, which helps avoid the collapse phenomena that a naive likelihood-ratio objective can cause.

In summary, Direct Preference Optimization turns the usual two-stage fitting process—first fit a scalar ranking function, then optimize a policy under KL control—into a single-stage procedure that directly adapts a parametrized policy to match observed pairwise preferences while maintaining an explicit proximity penalty to the reference policy. The result is a simpler, more stable method to transform the broad capabilities encoded by an unsupervised language model into the specific, preferred behaviors needed for deployment.
