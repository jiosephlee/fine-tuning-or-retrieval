Foundations: Preference Models and KL-Constrained RL

# Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce preference models

Many practical settings produce judgments not as numeric scores but as pairwise comparisons: given the same prompt or context, two generated continuations are presented and a rater indicates which of the two they prefer. A compact probabilistic model for such pairwise outcomes is the Bradley–Terry formulation. In this view each completion y for a prompt x is associated with a latent scalar score r*(x,y). The probability that completion y1 is preferred to completion y2 is a soft comparison of these scores. In symbols,

\[ p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}. \]

This expression depends only on the difference \(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\); adding any function of x to all scores for that prefix leaves the pairwise probabilities unchanged. That under-specification is a central structural property: only relative scores matter for pairwise comparisons.

The Bradley–Terry form is a special case of the Plackett–Luce family, which handles ranked lists of more than two alternatives by assigning selection probabilities via normalized exponentials of scores. Intuitively, Plackett–Luce views ranking as repeated draws without replacement from a softmax defined by the scores. For our purposes the key takeaways are (a) pairwise preference probabilities are determined by exponentiated scores, and (b) only score differences are identifiable from pairwise judgments.

# Maximum likelihood for reward functions and sigmoid-based negative log-likelihood

Given a collection of triples \((x, y_w, y_\ell)\) in which \(y_w\) denotes the completion preferred over \(y_\ell\) for prefix x, one can fit a parametric scorer \(r_\phi(x,y)\) by maximizing the likelihood implied by the Bradley–Terry model. Writing \(\sigma(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))\) for the sigmoid, the negative log-likelihood objective (to be minimized) takes the compact form

\[ \mathcal{L}_R(\phi) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_\ell)}\Big[ \log \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_\ell)\bigr)\Big]. \]

This objective increases the gap between the fitted score on the preferred completion and the fitted score on the dispreferred completion. Practically, implementations often initialize \(r_\phi\) by taking an existing sequence model and appending a small head that maps a final hidden representation to a scalar reward; the parameters of that head (and sometimes of the underlying model) are then adjusted to reduce \(\mathcal{L}_R\) on the collection of comparisons.

Two important structural points follow from the sigmoid formulation. First, because the preference probability depends on score differences, the objective does not pin down an absolute baseline for scores: a per-prefix constant may be added without changing pairwise likelihoods. Second, the sigmoid-based log-likelihood is convex in the score differences and thus provides a straightforward fit target for scalar-valued parametrizations.

# KL-regularized reward-optimization objective

A common next step is to take a fitted scorer and use it as feedback to modify a conditional sequence generator (a policy) so the generator produces continuations that receive higher scores. Rather than simply pushing the generator to choose high-scoring completions, practitioners typically impose a penalty that keeps the updated generator close to a reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (for example, the sequence model obtained from supervised fine-tuning). This results in an objective that trades off expected reward against a KL-type penalty relative to the reference. In compact mathematical form the per-prefix objective is

\[ \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\, r(x,y)\,\big] \;-\; \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big), \]

and an overall objective averages this expression over the prompts of interest. The scalar \(\beta>0\) controls how strongly deviation from the reference is penalized: small \(\beta\) places more emphasis on matching the reference, while large \(\beta\) permits larger shifts to increase expected reward.

The KL term acts as a regularizer that discourages mode collapse and helps ensure that the updated generator remains within the support where the scorer is reliable. In sequence settings the KL is typically computed as the expected sum over token-level log-ratio terms; this structure will be convenient in what follows.

# Deriving the optimal policy under the KL penalty

The optimization above is a convex variational problem in the space of distributions \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) for each fixed x. One can derive the analytic form of the optimizer by forming the Lagrangian that enforces that \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) sums to one. For a fixed prompt x write the objective to be maximized as

\[ J(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\,r(x,y) \;-\; \beta \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \lambda\Big(1 - \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\Big). \]

Differentiating \(J(\pi)\) with respect to \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and setting the derivative to zero yields

\[ r(x,y) - \beta\bigg( \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + 1\bigg) - \lambda \;=\; 0. \]

Rearranging and exponentiating gives the closed-form optimizer

\[ \pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big), \]

with the normalizing factor (partition function)

\[ Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big). \]

This expression has a clear interpretation: the optimal generator reweights the reference probabilities by an exponential tilt proportional to the scorer \(r\). When \(\beta\) is small the tilt is mild and the generator remains close to the reference; when \(\beta\) is large the generator more strongly prefers high-scoring continuations.

# Partition function and its intractability in sequence generation

The partition function \(Z(x)\) above is a sum over the entire space of possible continuations y for a given prefix. In simple discrete problems this sum can be computed exactly, but in sequence generation the space of possible sequences is astronomically large and direct computation is infeasible. Two common remedies are: Monte Carlo estimation by sampling from some proposal and importance-weighted approximations, or approximating \(Z(x)\) with a learned baseline term. Both techniques incur practical costs: sampling-based estimators incur variance that can destabilize optimization, and learned baselines introduce additional fitted components that must be tuned and can themselves produce bias.

The intractability of \(Z(x)\) therefore constitutes a key computational bottleneck when attempting to use the closed-form optimal policy directly from a fitted reward function. Any procedure that depends on explicit evaluations of \(Z(x)\) for many prefixes will either be very costly or will inherit the instability of high-variance estimates.

# Control-as-inference perspective and soft value functions

An alternative and illuminating way to view the KL-regularized objective is through the lens of probabilistic inference. The optimal form \(\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)\) can be seen as the posterior distribution that results from treating the reference as a prior and the exponential of the reward as a likelihood factor. From this viewpoint the partition function \(Z(x)\) plays the role of a soft value: it is the normalizer that converts the unnormalized quantity \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)\) into a proper probability distribution.

It is useful to rewrite the per-step objective used when updating a parametric policy \(\pi_\theta\). Substituting the expression for the optimal form back into a KL minimization gives an equivalent maximization

\[ \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\, r(x,y) - \beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\big) \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big]. \]

Here the term

\[ V_{\text{soft}}(x) \;:=\; \beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\big) \]

is the soft value associated with the reference prior and reward. It depends only on x (not on the sampled y) and hence acts like a baseline that reduces variance of gradient estimates. In practice, many procedures estimate or approximate \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) with a separate parametric component. That introduces a second fitted object and the need to coordinate its updates with the policy updater; mismatches and variance in that estimation can produce instability in the overall procedure.

Viewed from control-as-inference, the optimization balances three effects: increasing immediate expected reward, accounting for the normalizing soft value that captures the expected exponential reward under the reference, and penalizing departure from the reference through the \(-\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) term. The presence of the soft value baseline is essential for correct gradients but is the source of practical difficulty when it must be approximated or Monte Carlo–estimated in high-dimensional sequence spaces.

The closed-form relationship between reward functions and optimal policies summarized above yields two further observations. First, multiple reward functions that differ by a prefix-dependent additive constant produce the same pairwise preferences and, importantly, the same optimal policy under the KL-penalized objective. Second, if one can reparameterize the scoring function in terms of a policy as

\[ r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \text{(a function of }x\text{)}, \]

then the partition function cancels in pairwise comparisons and the optimization based on comparisons can be expressed directly in terms of policy ratios without needing to compute \(Z(x)\). That reparameterization is the foundation upon which single-stage procedures can be built that fit a policy directly from preference comparisons while avoiding separate partition estimates.

---

This chapter has presented the mathematical primitives that underlie preference-based control for sequence generation: probabilistic pairwise preference models that depend only on score differences, a sigmoid-based maximum likelihood objective for fitting scorers from comparison collections, the KL-penalized optimization that produces an exponential-tilt optimal policy, the intractable partition function that arises in sequence spaces, and the control-as-inference interpretation that identifies the soft value as the normalization term. These elements together explain both why fitting a separate scorer and then optimizing a policy is conceptually straightforward and why it is practically challenging in large discrete output spaces.
