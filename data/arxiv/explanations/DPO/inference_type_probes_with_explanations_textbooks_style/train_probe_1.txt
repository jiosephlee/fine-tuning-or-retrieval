Chapter Title: Preference Modeling and Logistic Interpretation in Direct Preference Optimization

# Background: Preference Learning and the Bradley–Terry Model

Large language models are trained on vast datasets without explicit supervision about desired behavior, which makes it necessary to steer them toward helpful and safe outputs after pre-training. A common pipeline, often referred to as reinforcement learning from human feedback, begins by collecting pairwise human preferences over model outputs for a given prompt and then fitting a parametric model to explain these preferences.

A central tool for modeling pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry model. Given a prompt $x$ and two candidate completions $y_1$ and $y_2$, the model assumes there exists a latent reward function $r(x, y)$ such that the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ is
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x, y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r(x, y_1)\bigr) \;+\; \exp\!\bigl(r(x, y_2)\bigr)}.
\]
This expression depends only on relative rewards. If $r(x,y_1)$ exceeds $r(x,y_2)$ by a large margin, the model assigns high probability to $y_1$ being preferred. If the rewards are equal, the probability is $1/2$.

The Bradley–Terry model can be rewritten to make the dependence on reward differences explicit. Using the identity
\[
\frac{\exp(a)}{\exp(a)+\exp(b)} \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\exp(b-a)} \;=\; \sigma(a-b),
\]
where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+\exp(-z))$ is the logistic function, the preference probability can be expressed as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\left(r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)\right).
\]
This form highlights that preferences are driven by the difference in latent rewards between two responses for the same prompt.

# Reward Modeling as Binary Classification

The Bradley–Terry model enables maximum likelihood estimation of the reward function $r(x, y)$ using a dataset of labeled preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, where $y_w$ denotes the preferred completion and $y_l$ the dispreferred completion. Parameterizing the reward as $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and adopting the Bradley–Terry likelihood, reward modeling becomes a binary classification problem over pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ given $x$:
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
This objective is the negative log-likelihood of the observed pairwise preferences under the Bradley–Terry model. The logistic function $\sigma$ transforms the reward difference $r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)$ into a probability that the preferred completion is indeed preferred.

The intuition behind this loss mirrors standard binary classification: the model should assign higher reward to $y_w$ than to $y_l$ when $y_w$ is labeled as preferred, and the logistic function penalizes violations of this ordering. When the difference $r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)$ is large and positive, the loss contribution is small because the model is confident and correct; when the difference is small or negative, the loss contribution is large, encouraging the reward function to separate the preferred and dispreferred completions more decisively.

Thus, reward modeling from preferences reduces to optimizing a classification loss over pairwise differences in scores. Because the loss depends only on differences $r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)$, adding any function of $x$ to all rewards leaves the likelihood unchanged. This under-specification is intrinsic to pairwise models and motivates how downstream policy optimization can be made tractable.

# From Rewards to Policies: The DPO Change of Variables

While traditional pipelines fit $r_{\phi}$ via the classification loss and then optimize a policy with reinforcement learning, Direct Preference Optimization leverages an analytical mapping between rewards and optimal policies to eliminate the RL step. The KL-constrained reward maximization objective,
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
has a solution of the form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right),
\]
with $Z(x)$ a partition function ensuring normalization. Rearranging yields an expression for the reward in terms of the optimal policy:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Crucially, the Bradley–Terry model depends only on reward differences. Substituting this reparameterization into the preference probability and taking differences cancels the partition function, yielding a preference model directly in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\]
Replacing $\pi^*$ with a parametric $\pi_{\theta}$ yields a maximum likelihood objective over policies:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]
This is structurally the same logistic form as in reward modeling, but with the “scores” now given by the log-probability ratios $\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ scaled by $\beta$. DPO thus turns the RL objective into a simple classification-style optimization over the policy itself.

# Intuition for the Logistic Form in DPO

Understanding why DPO retains a logistic form sheds light on its stability and simplicity. The Bradley–Terry model posits that preferences depend on differences in latent scores. When we reparameterize those scores as
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
the preference probability becomes $\sigma\!\left(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l)\right)$. Optimizing the likelihood of observed preferences now means adjusting $\pi_{\theta}$ so that, relative to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, preferred outputs receive higher implicit rewards than dispreferred outputs.

The gradient of the DPO loss explicitly reflects this mechanism:
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)\,\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) \;-\; \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
Here, the logistic weight $\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)$ emphasizes examples where the current implicit reward misorders the pair, increasing the learning signal when the model is more wrong. This weighting emerges naturally from the logistic likelihood and helps prevent degenerate behavior by focusing updates on corrections rather than indiscriminate probability shifts.

The presence of the logistic function in both reward modeling and DPO reflects a common statistical structure: pairwise preferences modeled via sigmoid-transformed differences of scores, with those scores expressed either as explicit rewards or as policy-relative log-probabilities. In both cases, the learning objective is a cross-entropy over binary outcomes, and the optimization pushes the model to widen the margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs.

# Equivalence Classes of Rewards and Identifiability

Pairwise preference models such as Bradley–Terry determine probabilities from differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, not absolute values. If two reward functions differ by a function of the prompt alone, i.e.,
\[
r(x,y) - r'(x,y) \;=\; f(x),
\]
they induce the same preference distribution. This under-specification is compatible with policy optimization: all reward functions in an equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained reward maximization objective. Within each class, DPO selects a representative that makes the induced optimal policy analytically tractable by ensuring that the reparameterized distribution is valid:
\[
\sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right) \;=\; 1.
\]
This condition pins down a specific representative reward whose associated optimal policy can be written in closed form and whose preference likelihood has the same logistic structure in terms of policy ratios. As a result, DPO can optimize policies directly from preferences without fitting an explicit standalone reward model or estimating partition functions.

# Conclusion: The Logistic Perspective on Preferences and Policies

Preference learning with pairwise comparisons is governed by a simple and powerful mathematical structure: probabilities expressed as logistic functions of differences in latent scores. In the Bradley–Terry framework, this manifests as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\left(r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)\right),
\]
and yields a negative log-likelihood over preference pairs that is identical in form to a binary classification loss. Direct Preference Optimization preserves this structure under a change of variables that replaces explicit rewards with policy-relative log-probabilities, leading to the objective
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]
By recognizing and exploiting the logistic form intrinsic to pairwise preference data, DPO reduces the complexity of the traditional pipeline, replacing reinforcement learning with a classification-style objective over the policy. This perspective ties together the theoretical underpinnings of preference modeling and the practical stability and simplicity of DPO.
