Chapter Title: The Role of the KL Coefficient β in Direct Preference Optimization

# Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a method for training language models directly from preference data without an explicit reinforcement learning (RL) loop. It replaces the conventional two-step RLHF pipeline—reward modeling followed by RL—with a maximum likelihood objective defined on preferences, under a theoretically grounded change of variables. Central to DPO, as in KL-regularized RLHF objectives, is a scalar parameter, denoted by β, that governs the trade-off between reward maximization and the divergence of the fine-tuned policy from a reference policy. Understanding how β controls the policy’s deviation from the reference is crucial for both theoretical clarity and practical training.

This chapter develops a precise and intuitive understanding of the β parameter in DPO, its connection to the KL-regularized objective used in RLHF, and how it shapes the policy’s divergence from the reference model. We will build from the original RLHF formulation, derive the optimal policy, connect it to the DPO parameterization, and interpret the resulting learning dynamics. Throughout, we will make clear how β mediates the degree of deviation from the reference policy, which is measured by the KL divergence.

# KL-Regularized Reward Maximization and the Optimal Policy

The starting point is the standard KL-regularized RLHF objective used to fine-tune a language model policy. Given a reference policy π_ref and a learned reward function r, the policy π_θ is optimized by

max_πθ E_{x∼𝔻, y∼πθ(·|x)}[r(x,y)] − β D_KL[πθ(·|x) ∥ π_ref(·|x)],

where β ≥ 0 sets the strength of the KL regularization. This objective pushes π_θ to increase the expected reward while discouraging large deviations from π_ref, as measured by the KL divergence term. The scalar β weighs the KL penalty relative to reward maximization.

A key analytical fact about this objective is that its optimal policy admits a closed-form solution:

π_r(y|x) = (1/Z(x)) π_ref(y|x) exp((1/β) r(x,y)),

where Z(x) is the partition function Z(x) = ∑_y π_ref(y|x) exp((1/β) r(x,y)). This expression reveals how β modulates the influence of the reward on the policy relative to the reference. The factor exp((1/β) r(x,y)) “tilts” π_ref toward higher-reward completions; the larger the exponent’s scale, the more aggressively the policy reshapes the reference distribution.

The role of β in this expression is therefore transparent: it appears in the denominator in the exponent. When β is smaller, the exponent 1/β is larger, magnifying differences in r(x,y) and inducing a more dramatic shift from the reference. Conversely, when β is larger, the exponent shrinks, and the factor exp((1/β) r(x,y)) contributes less to reweighting π_ref. Intuitively, this means the learned policy will stay closer to the reference when β is large and will deviate more strongly when β is small. In terms of KL divergence, this optimal-policy form makes it clear that β governs how far the new policy tilts away from π_ref.

# Reparameterizing Reward in Terms of the Policy

DPO builds on the same KL-regularized objective, but instead of explicitly fitting a reward and then solving the RL problem, it reparameterizes the reward in terms of the optimal policy. Starting from the optimal policy expression above and rearranging, we obtain the change of variables

r(x,y) = β log(π_r(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β log Z(x).

This identity expresses reward differences in terms of differences of log-probabilities between the optimal policy and the reference policy, up to an additive term depending only on the prompt x. This is crucial because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on reward differences between completions for the same x, and the partition term β log Z(x) cancels out in those differences. Substituting this reparameterization into the preference model yields a likelihood in terms of the policy directly, allowing DPO to train π_θ purely from preference data.

Under the Bradley–Terry model, human preference probabilities take the form

p*(y_1 ≻ y_2 | x) = 1 / (1 + exp(β log(π*(y_2|x)/π_ref(y_2|x)) − β log(π*(y_1|x)/π_ref(y_1|x)))),

and the DPO loss for a parametrized policy π_θ becomes a binary cross-entropy over preference pairs:

ℒ_DPO(π_θ; π_ref) = − E_{(x,y_w,y_l)∼𝔻}[ log σ( β log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) − β log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)) ) ].

Here, β plays the same conceptual role as in the KL-regularized RL objective: it sets the strength with which relative policy–reference log-probability gaps are used to explain and fit the observed preferences. However, because DPO fits the policy directly via a preference-likelihood, β also scales the learning signal in the loss, which influences the magnitude and smoothness of the updates.

# How β Controls Deviation from the Reference Policy

With both the optimal policy expression and the DPO loss in hand, we can interpret the effect of β on the policy’s divergence from the reference. There are two complementary perspectives:

1) The optimal-policy perspective. For a given reward r, the closed-form solution expresses π_r as a reweighted version of π_ref with weights exp((1/β) r). As β increases, the reweighting is softened, reducing the deviation from π_ref. As β decreases, the reweighting is sharpened, increasing the deviation from π_ref. In other words, the KL divergence D_KL[π_r ∥ π_ref] is controlled by how strongly exp((1/β) r) distorts π_ref; smaller β yields larger distortions and thus typically larger KL, while larger β yields smaller distortions and typically smaller KL.

2) The DPO loss perspective. The DPO objective uses the term β log(π_θ/π_ref) inside a logistic classifier. The gradient of the loss with respect to θ reveals how β scales the update:

∇_θ ℒ_DPO = − β E_{(x,y_w,y_l)∼𝔻}[ σ( r̂_θ(x,y_l) − r̂_θ(x,y_w) ) ( ∇_θ log π_θ(y_w|x) − ∇_θ log π_θ(y_l|x) ) ],

where r̂_θ(x,y) = β log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) is the implicit reward induced by the current policy and the reference. Two effects of β are evident: it directly multiplies the gradient magnitude and it scales the implicit reward r̂_θ. Larger β amplifies the argument of the logistic and the overall gradient scale, which can increase the strength of updates that push π_θ to increase log-probability ratios in favor of preferred completions. Because these updates grow the relative log-probabilities with respect to π_ref, the training dynamics can lead to larger KL from the reference as β increases, unless counterbalanced by convergence dynamics or early stopping. Thus, from the perspective of the training dynamics under this loss, larger β typically produces stronger pushes away from the reference in service of fitting the preference likelihood.

These two perspectives fit together in a useful way. The KL-regularized RL optimum ties β to the target closeness of π to π_ref via the closed-form tilt exp((1/β) r). Meanwhile, the DPO loss uses β to scale how aggressively the model uses policy–reference log-probability gaps to fit preferences. In practice, as we optimize the DPO loss, increasing β strengthens the per-example signal to enlarge those gaps. This typically allows the learned policy to deviate more from π_ref, and the measured KL between π_θ and π_ref increases during training as β grows. At the same time, in the underlying KL-regularized formulation, β remains the coefficient on the KL penalty.

# Intuition for β as a Trade-Off Parameter

The unifying intuition is that β is a knob that trades off fidelity to human preferences against conservativeness relative to a known good baseline model. The reference policy π_ref encapsulates desirable prior behavior, and the preference data encourage targeted adjustments toward better completions. β governs how far those adjustments go:

- In the KL-regularized objective, β controls the penalty on divergence from π_ref. A larger β increases the relative weight on the KL term, pushing the optimal policy to remain closer to π_ref for a given reward r. A smaller β relaxes the penalty, allowing larger shifts.

- In the DPO training objective, β scales the log-probability ratio inside the logistic and the overall gradient updates. A larger β strengthens the updates that widen policy–reference gaps on preference pairs, typically resulting in a policy that drifts further from π_ref during training and exhibits higher KL.

These two viewpoints are consistent once we recognize that the DPO loss is an estimation procedure defined entirely in terms of the policy, while the KL-regularized solution describes the optimizer’s target given a fixed reward and β. In DPO, β simultaneously identifies the equivalence class of rewards represented by β log(π/π_ref) and sets the strength of the preference-fitting updates, so increasing β typically increases the realized KL from π_ref over the course of training.

# Practical Consequences for Model Training

In practice, the choice of β directly shapes the reward–KL frontier achieved by preference-trained policies. DPO, PPO, and other KL-regularized methods share the same constrained objective structure and therefore compare naturally by examining both achieved reward and divergence from the reference. Because DPO’s β amplifies the policy–reference log-probability gaps in the loss, increasing β typically increases the aggressiveness with which the model separates preferred from dispreferred responses, and hence increases the policy’s deviation from π_ref, i.e., increases the measured KL. Conversely, decreasing β typically dampens this effect, maintaining a policy closer to π_ref with lower KL.

From a modeling standpoint, this means that β can be used to tune how conservative the resulting policy is. A smaller β tends to keep the policy closer to the reference, while a larger β tends to move it further away as it better fits the preference annotations. In evaluation, this appears as a shift along the reward–KL frontier: different β values trace out different trade-offs between quality under the learned or proxy reward and similarity to the base model.

# Summary

DPO leverages a principled change of variables that makes the optimal KL-regularized policy analytically tractable and directly estimable via a preference likelihood. The scalar β is the key trade-off parameter throughout this construction. In the KL-regularized formulation, it specifies how strongly the policy is constrained to remain near the reference. In the DPO loss, it scales the strength of the preference-fitting signal through policy–reference log-probability ratios. Because larger β strengthens those updates, it typically allows the policy to deviate more from the reference during training, which is reflected in increased KL between π_θ and π_ref. Understanding and tuning β is therefore essential to achieving the desired balance between alignment to preferences and stability with respect to a trusted reference model.
