Chapter Title: Direct Preference Optimization as Binary Classification

# Introduction
Large language models are initially trained in an unsupervised fashion and then steered toward desired behavior using human preferences. The standard reinforcement learning from human feedback pipeline decomposes preference learning into two stages: first fit a reward model that explains the collected comparisons, then optimize a policy to maximize that reward subject to a Kullback-Leibler divergence constraint. Direct Preference Optimization reframes this process by eliminating explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. It derives an objective over the policy that is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences under a Bradley-Terry model. At its core, this objective applies a logistic function to paired comparisons and minimizes a binary cross-entropy loss. In this chapter, we explain why training a policy with the DPO loss is most analogous to training a binary classifier, connect that analogy to the underlying preference models used in the paper, and show how the change of variables transforms reward learning into direct policy learning without loss of generality.

# Human Preference Modeling and Binary Classification
The preference learning setting begins with a dataset of pairwise comparisons. For each prompt $x$, a supervised fine-tuned model $\\pi^{\\mathrm{SFT}}$ generates two candidate completions $(y_1, y_2)$, and a human annotator labels one as preferred, denoted $y_w \\succ y_l \\mid x$. A central modeling assumption is that an unobserved reward function $r^*(x,y)$ generates these preferences according to the Bradley-Terry model. Under Bradley-Terry, the probability of preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given $x$ is
\\[
p^*(y_1 \\succ y_2 \\mid x) \\,=\\, \\frac{\\exp\\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\\bigr)}{\\exp\\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\\bigr) + \\exp\\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\\bigr)} \\,=\\, \\sigma\\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\\bigr),
\\]
where $\\sigma$ is the logistic function. With a parametric reward $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$, maximum likelihood estimation reduces to minimizing the negative log-likelihood
\\[
\\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\\phi},\\mathcal{D}) \\,=\\, -\\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\\sim\\mathcal{D}}\\bigl[\\log\\sigma\\bigl(r_{\\phi}(x,y_w) - r_{\\phi}(x,y_l)\\bigr)\\bigr].
\\]
This is the textbook binary cross-entropy loss, where the “logit” for the correct label is the reward difference $r_{\\phi}(x,y_w) - r_{\\phi}(x,y_l)$. The inputs are pairs $(x,(y_w,y_l))$ and the target label says “$y_w$ should be preferred over $y_l$.” Thus, fitting $r_{\\phi}$ via Eq. $(2)$ is directly analogous to training a binary classifier that predicts which of two responses is preferred, with the classifier’s score given by the difference of rewards.

This framing highlights two key properties. First, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on reward differences, not absolute reward levels. Second, the optimization is a standard supervised learning problem with a logistic link, which admits stable training dynamics and well-understood generalization properties under appropriate data assumptions.

# From Reward Models to Policies: Change of Variables
The reinforcement learning fine-tuning step traditionally maximizes expected reward while constraining deviation from a reference policy $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$:
\\[
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}} \\, \\mathbb{E}_{x\\sim\\mathcal{D},\\, y\\sim\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)}\\bigl[r_{\\phi}(x,y)\\bigr] \\, - \\, \\beta\\,\\mathbb{D}_{\\mathrm{KL}}\\bigl[\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)\\,\\Vert\\,\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\bigr].
\\]
The optimal policy $\\pi_r$ under this objective has the closed form
\\[
\\pi_r(y\\mid x) \\,=\\, \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\,\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)\\,\\exp\\Bigl(\\frac{1}{\\beta}\\,r(x,y)\\Bigr),
\\]
with $Z(x)$ a partition function. Rearranging, we can express the reward in terms of the policy:
\\[
r(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_r(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)} \\, + \\, \\beta\\,\\log Z(x).
\\]
This change of variables is the key insight: under Bradley-Terry, the partition function cancels in reward differences, so the probability of a preference can be written directly in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. Concretely,
\\[
p^*(y_1\\succ y_2\\mid x) \\,=\\, \\sigma\\Bigl(\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi^*(y_1\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\\mid x)} \\, - \\, \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi^*(y_2\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\\mid x)}\\Bigr).
\\]
This derivation implies we can perform maximum likelihood over preferences by parameterizing the policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ directly and optimizing a loss that depends on $\\pi_{\\theta}$ only through the same logistic form applied to paired comparisons.

# The DPO Loss as Binary Cross-Entropy
Leveraging the mapping from rewards to policies, Direct Preference Optimization defines its objective over the policy:
\\[
\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}(\\pi_{\\theta};\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}) \\,=\\, -\\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\\sim\\mathcal{D}}\\Bigl[\\log\\sigma\\Bigl(\\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\\mid x)} \\, - \\, \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\\mid x)}\\Bigr)\\Bigr].
\\]
This is structurally identical to the reward-modeling loss, with a different “score” function. Instead of using the raw reward difference $r_{\\phi}(x,y_w) - r_{\\phi}(x,y_l)$, DPO uses the difference of implicit rewards
\\[
\\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y) \\,=\\, \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)},
\\]
so the logit becomes $\\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_w) - \\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_l)$. In other words, the policy itself induces a reward via its log-probability ratio to the reference, and training $\\pi_{\\theta}$ with $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ optimizes a binary cross-entropy objective that predicts which of two responses is preferred. The model’s “classifier score” for a pair is the difference of these implicit rewards. Because the logistic function $\\sigma$ is applied to the paired score and the loss takes the negative log-likelihood, $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ matches the canonical form of binary classification.

This analogy extends naturally to intuition. Each training example is a pair $(y_w,y_l)$ for a given prompt $x$ together with a binary label indicating that $y_w$ should be ranked above $y_l$. The DPO objective pushes the policy to assign higher relative probability to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, scaled by $\\beta$ and measured against the same reference $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$ used to collect preferences. The logistic link converts these relative log-probability differences into probabilities of the preferred label, and the cross-entropy loss penalizes misclassifications.

# Gradient Interpretation and Practical Implications
The gradient of $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ reveals exactly how the “classifier” updates the policy:
\\[
\\nabla_{\\theta}\\,\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}(\\pi_{\\theta};\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}) \\,=\\, -\\beta\\,\\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\\sim\\mathcal{D}}\\Bigl[\\sigma\\bigl(\\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_l) - \\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_w)\\bigr)\\,\\bigl(\\nabla_{\\theta}\\log\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x) \\, - \\, \\nabla_{\\theta}\\log\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)\\bigr)\\Bigr].
\\]
This expression admits a classifier-like interpretation:
- The inner difference $\\nabla_{\\theta}\\log\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x) - \\nabla_{\\theta}\\log\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)$ increases the likelihood of the preferred completion and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion, mirroring the gradient direction of a binary classifier that raises the score of the positive example and lowers the score of the negative example.
- The coefficient $\\sigma\\bigl(\\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_l) - \\hat{r}_{\\theta}(x,y_w)\\bigr)$ acts as an importance weight. It is larger when the implicit reward model currently ranks the dispreferred completion above the preferred completion, i.e., when the classifier is wrong. This gives harder, misordered examples more influence, a characteristic also seen in well-behaved classification losses.
- The scale $\\beta$ governs the strength of updates, consistent with its role in the original KL-constrained optimization.

Importantly, using this weighting avoids degenerate behavior observed with naive probability ratio objectives. From the classification perspective, the logistic weighting modulates step sizes to correct misclassifications without overconfident shifts, preserving stability.

# Relationship to RLHF and Advantages of the Classification View
Direct Preference Optimization is derived from the RLHF objective, but its training loop is the same as optimizing a binary cross-entropy classifier over paired examples. This has several consequences that follow directly from the paper’s analysis:

First, the DPO reparameterization preserves the class of reward functions that are identifiable under the Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce frameworks. Because preferences depend only on reward differences, rewards that differ by a per-prompt offset $f(x)$ lie in the same equivalence class and induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy. The DPO form selects a representative in this class by enforcing that the induced policy is a valid distribution, yielding $r(x,y) = \\beta\\,\\log\\frac{\\pi(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)}$ up to the irrelevant per-prompt constant. Thus, optimizing $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ is equivalent to maximum likelihood under Bradley-Terry, but with the policy as the parameter, not a standalone reward network.

Second, because the objective is a simple binary cross-entropy over static preference data, it eliminates the need to sample from the policy in the training loop or to optimize a separate value function. This avoids instabilities associated with actor-critic algorithms that estimate soft value functions or baselines to control variance. In the classification view, the “baseline” is implicit: differences against $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$ and the logistic transform absorb normalization terms that would otherwise appear in policy gradients.

Third, the classification analogy illuminates why DPO achieves stable and performant trade-offs between reward and KL divergence. The policy learns to separate preferred from dispreferred responses with a margin controlled by $\\beta$ and measured against a fixed reference, producing an efficient frontier without the complexities of reinforcement learning.

# Limits and Scope of the Analogy
It is important to recognize the scope of this analogy. Training with $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ is most analogous to training a binary classifier in the sense that the loss is a binary cross-entropy over pairwise comparisons with a logistic link and a score function that is a difference of “logits.” However, the “logits” come from an implicit reward defined by the policy’s log-probability ratio to a reference model, not from an independent scoring network. The parameter $\\beta$ connects this classifier-like loss back to the original KL-constrained reward maximization objective. Moreover, while the optimization problem is formulated as classification, the learned object is a generative policy $\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)$, and the classifier’s scores are tied to that policy’s probabilities. The analogy is thus precise at the level of the loss and training dynamics, but the endpoint is a calibrated policy that both encodes an implicit reward and generates text, rather than a standalone discriminative classifier.

Within this scope, the binary classification perspective provides clear intuition: each training step increases the margin between preferred and dispreferred completions; misordered examples receive higher corrective weight; and the reference model and $\\beta$ regularize updates to prevent over-deviation. This perspective, grounded in the Bradley-Terry model and the change-of-variables derivation, captures the essence of DPO’s simplicity and stability.
