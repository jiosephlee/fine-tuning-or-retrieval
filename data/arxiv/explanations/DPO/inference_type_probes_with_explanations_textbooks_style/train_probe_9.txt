Chapter: Why Direct Preference Optimization Yields Lower-Variance Gradients

# From RLHF to DPO: How the Training Objective Shapes Gradient Behavior

Large language models are commonly fine-tuned to align with human preferences through a pipeline known as reinforcement learning from human feedback. This pipeline typically fits a reward model and then optimizes a policy to maximize reward under a Kullback–Leibler (KL) constraint with respect to a reference policy. In the widely used PPO-based approach, the training loop requires sampling from the policy during optimization and maintaining stability through actor-critic structure, baselines, and careful tuning.

Direct Preference Optimization takes a different route. Instead of first fitting an explicit reward model and then running reinforcement learning, DPO leverages a reparameterization of the reward and an analytical mapping from reward to the optimal policy to construct a single-stage maximum likelihood objective over the policy itself. This objective, unlike PPO-based RLHF, does not require sampling from the policy during fine-tuning. As a result, the training dynamics resemble supervised learning with a binary cross-entropy loss over pairwise preferences, making optimization simpler, more stable, and computationally lightweight.

This shift in training paradigm matters for gradient behavior. In PPO-based RLHF, gradients arise from on-policy samples and depend on stochastic returns, estimated baselines, and learned value functions. In contrast, DPO’s gradients are computed from fixed, offline preference pairs through a deterministic loss that directly adjusts the relative log probabilities of preferred and dispreferred completions. Because DPO avoids the reinforcement learning loop and sampling from the policy, one would expect its gradient variance during fine-tuning to typically be lower.

# The KL-Constrained RLHF Objective and Its Sources of Instability

The canonical RLHF optimization problem used in prior work is a KL-regularized reward maximization:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, \, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where \(r_{\phi}\) is a learned reward function, \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a reference policy (often the supervised fine-tuned model), and \(\beta\) controls conservativeness with respect to the reference. Optimizing this objective with PPO requires sampling \(y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\) inside the training loop and estimating gradients that depend on stochastic rewards and KL terms. 

A deeper look at this formulation reveals a normalized “soft” value structure embedded in the optimal policy. The exact optimizer of the KL-constrained objective satisfies
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x,y)\right),
\]
with the partition function \(Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y))\). When one casts the PPO fine-tuning step as minimizing \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi^*]\) where \(\pi^*\) is induced by \(r_{\phi}\), algebra yields the objective
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\biggl[ \underbrace{r_{\phi}(x,y) \;-\; \beta \log \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \beta)} \;-\; \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL term}} \biggr].
\]
The normalization term \(f(r_{\phi}, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \beta)\) acts like a soft value function of the reference policy. While it does not change the optimal solution, omitting it leads to policy gradients with high variance. Prior practice uses learned value functions or baselines to control this variance, but these techniques themselves can be difficult to optimize, and even with baselines, the reliance on on-policy sampling and stochastic rewards introduces instability. In short, the actor-critic structure, policy sampling, and baseline estimation are all potential sources of gradient variance in PPO-based RLHF.

# DPO’s Reparameterization: From Rewards to Policies Without Sampling

DPO replaces the two-stage “fit reward, then optimize with RL” pipeline by reparameterizing the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. Starting from the optimal policy form above, taking logarithms and rearranging yields
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Under the Bradley–Terry preference model
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
the partition function cancels when considering reward differences, giving a preference probability expressed solely in terms of \(\pi^*\) and \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\):
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \right)}.
\]
This insight leads directly to the DPO maximum likelihood objective over a parametric policy \(\pi_{\theta}\):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
Here, \(\mathcal{D}\) is a fixed, offline dataset of pairwise preferences between completions sampled from the reference model. Crucially, this objective does not require sampling from \(\pi_{\theta}\) during optimization. It is a supervised binary cross-entropy loss evaluated on the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), making fine-tuning a straightforward and stable classification problem.

# The DPO Gradient: Deterministic Weighting Without Baselines

To understand DPO’s training dynamics, inspect its gradient. Writing the implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) is
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; -\beta \, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}} \Bigl[ \sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr) \,\bigl( \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) \;-\; \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x) \bigr) \Bigr].
\]
This update increases the likelihood of preferred completions and decreases the likelihood of dispreferred completions, with a deterministic importance weight \(\sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w))\) that is larger when the model’s implicit reward ranks the pair incorrectly. The weighting is computed from the current policy and the reference policy without any need for sampling-based baselines or learned value functions. 

In the actor-critic formulation of PPO-based RLHF, baseline terms are introduced to reduce the variance of policy gradients, but these baselines must be estimated, and their optimization can be unstable. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization yields a reward function that does not require any baselines: the necessary normalization is implicitly handled by the preference model’s dependence on reward differences and the fixed reference policy. This design eliminates a prominent source of gradient variance.

# Why Avoiding the RL Loop Reduces Gradient Variance

Putting these pieces together, the expectation that DPO exhibits lower gradient variance during fine-tuning follows from three characteristics grounded in its formulation:

First, DPO’s optimization does not sample actions from the current policy inside the training loop. The expectation in \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) is taken over a fixed dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), so gradients depend on observed preference pairs rather than stochastic on-policy rollouts. This removes the variability introduced by sampling \(y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\) at every update, a core source of variance in PPO-based RLHF.

Second, DPO’s gradient does not rely on learned baselines or value functions to control variance. In the actor-critic view of PPO-based RLHF, normalization terms like \(f(r_{\phi}, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \beta)\) act as soft values and are critical for variance reduction; omitting or poorly estimating them leads to high-variance gradients. DPO sidesteps this entirely: its reparameterization and binary cross-entropy formulation inherently normalize through reward differences relative to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), yielding a deterministic importance weight within the gradient.

Third, DPO’s loss is a standard supervised objective. It directly adjusts relative log probabilities \(\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) - \log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\) under a logistic link, exactly as in maximum likelihood estimation for Bradley–Terry-style preference models. Supervised objectives of this form are well known to be stable to optimize compared to reinforcement learning objectives that involve sampling, credit assignment over sampled trajectories, and high-variance returns.

These properties align with the paper’s broader observations: PPO’s actor-critic structure can be unstable due to high-variance gradients when normalization is imperfect, while DPO offers a stable, performant, and computationally lightweight alternative by eliminating the reinforcement learning loop and sampling during fine-tuning.

# Practical Implications for Training Aligned Language Models

DPO transforms preference learning from an RL problem into a supervised classification problem over pairwise comparisons. Practically, this means:

It uses a single maximum likelihood objective over \((x, y_w, y_l)\) tuples and requires only forward and backward passes through the policy network evaluated on these fixed pairs. No on-policy rollouts or sampling from \(\pi_{\theta}\) are needed during fine-tuning.

The implicit reward \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) is defined directly by the policy and reference, avoiding separate reward-model fitting and value-function estimation in the training loop.

The KL constraint is preserved through the dependence on the reference policy inside the loss, balancing preference adherence with conservativeness in a way that is analytically connected to the original RLHF objective.

Because these ingredients avoid the stochastic elements of PPO-based RLHF that commonly inflate gradient variance, one would expect DPO’s gradients to be typically lower-variance during fine-tuning. The paper’s empirical findings—stable optimization, efficient reward–KL tradeoffs, and strong performance on tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue—are consistent with this expectation and underscore the benefits of DPO’s reparameterization and training design.
