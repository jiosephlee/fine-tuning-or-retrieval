Chapter Title: Importance Weighting in Direct Preference Optimization and Its Role in Preventing Model Degeneration

# Background: From RLHF to Direct Preference Optimization
Large-scale language models acquire broad capabilities through unsupervised pre-training, but aligning their behavior to human preferences typically requires an additional fine-tuning stage. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been the dominant paradigm: fit a reward model to human preferences and then optimize a policy to maximize that reward subject to a Kullback–Leibler (KL) constraint that keeps the policy close to a reference model. This KL constraint is crucial, as it prevents the model from drifting far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate, and it helps prevent mode collapse.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) simplifies this pipeline by avoiding explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning altogether. The key idea is to reparameterize the KL-constrained RL objective so that the optimal policy under that objective can be expressed in closed form. Under standard preference models like Bradley–Terry, this change of variables eliminates the need to estimate the normalization constant (partition function) that typically complicates policy extraction. As a result, DPO optimizes a simple binary cross-entropy loss on paired preference data, directly in terms of the policy probabilities and a fixed reference model.

Formally, the classical KL-constrained RL objective is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\ y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr] \ - \ \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where the parameter \(\beta\) controls the strength of the KL regularization relative to reward maximization. The optimal policy for any reward \(r\) takes the form
\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \ = \ \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
\]
with partition function \(Z(x)\). Rearranging yields
\[
r(x,y) \ = \ \beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \ + \ \beta \log Z(x).
\]
In the Bradley–Terry preference model, preferences depend only on differences of rewards between two completions, so the unknown \(\log Z(x)\) cancels. This leads to an objective directly over the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \ = \ -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
\]
where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function, and \((x,y_w,y_l)\) are labeled preference pairs with \(y_w \succ y_l\).

This reparameterization has a deeper implication: the policy itself acts as a reward model. Precisely, we can define an implicit reward
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \ = \ \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
and the DPO loss becomes a maximum likelihood objective for a Bradley–Terry model over differences \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l)\). In this sense, “your language model is secretly a reward model.”

# The DPO Gradient and Dynamic Per-Example Importance Weighting
To understand how DPO updates the policy, it is instructive to examine the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\). Differentiating the loss yields
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \ = \ -\,\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\underbrace{\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr)}_{\text{dynamic, per-example importance weight}}\Bigl(\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}_{\text{increase likelihood of } y_w} - \underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}_{\text{decrease likelihood of } y_l}\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]
The term \(\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr)\) plays the role of a dynamic, per-example importance weight. It is larger when the implicit reward \(\hat{r}_{\theta}\) orders the pair incorrectly, i.e., when \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) > \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\), and it is smaller when the pair is already ordered correctly by the model. Intuitively, this weighting adapts the strength of the update to how “wrong” the model is on each example. The update always pushes probability mass toward \(y_w\) and away from \(y_l\), but it does so more aggressively when the model currently prefers \(y_l\) over \(y_w\), and more gently when the model already prefers \(y_w\).

This per-example weighting interacts with the KL constraint through \(\hat{r}_{\theta}\). Because \(\hat{r}_{\theta}\) is defined as a log ratio against the reference model, the updates operate in a space where changes are measured relative to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The parameter \(\beta\) uniformly scales the update magnitudes, reflecting the intended trade-off between adherence to preferences and proximity to the reference. Together, the importance weighting and the KL grounding help prevent overly aggressive updates on examples that are already well-ordered, while ensuring focused corrections on examples where the model disagrees with the preference labels.

# Why Importance Weighting Prevents Model Degeneration
The dynamic, per-example importance weighting is not just a convenience—it is crucial for stable training. The authors explicitly note that a naive probability ratio objective that omits this weighting can cause the language model to degenerate. Understanding why requires tracing how the gradient behaves when the weighting is removed.

Suppose we drop the \(\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr)\) term and update solely with \(\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\), possibly scaled by \(\beta\). This unweighted update treats every preference pair identically, regardless of whether the model already strongly prefers \(y_w\) or is uncertain. As a result, the optimizer keeps pushing probability mass toward already-preferred completions with equal force, rather than tapering updates when the ordering is correct. Over many iterations, these indiscriminate updates can amplify confidence in already high-probability responses, while aggressively suppressing dispreferred responses across the board.

This behavior invites several pathologies the paper cautions against. First, it counteracts the stabilizing influence of the KL constraint, since unweighted updates do not reflect how far the model has already moved in reward space relative to the reference; they can overly concentrate probability mass on a few completions. Second, by failing to focus on genuinely misordered pairs, the optimizer can waste update budget reinforcing the obvious, leading to rapid over-optimization of particular responses and a reduction in diversity. The broader RLHF discussion emphasizes that the KL constraint is intended to prevent “mode-collapse to single high-reward answers,” and the authors’ ablation-style reasoning shows that removing the weighting term undermines this goal.

In contrast, with the importance weighting included, updates are largest where the model’s implicit reward assigns a higher score to the dispreferred completion than to the preferred one. This focuses learning on genuine errors and slows updates when the ordering is already correct, helping maintain a balance between improving adherence to preferences and preserving a healthy spread of probabilities. The sigmoid-shaped weight naturally saturates as the model’s ordering becomes more confident, thereby inhibiting runaway reinforcement of already-correct pairs.

# The Naive Probability Ratio Objective and Its Failure Modes
The paper highlights that a naive probability ratio objective—one that increases the relative log probability of preferred to dispreferred responses without the dynamic per-example weight—can cause model degeneration. This observation aligns with the gradient analysis: if the update does not modulate its strength based on the current reward ordering, it can become uniformly forceful across all examples. Such uniformity ignores whether a given preference is informative for the current model state.

When every example is pushed with similar intensity, two related failures arise. The first is over-sharpening. Because preferred responses often start out more probable under the reference model (especially after supervised fine-tuning), repeatedly applying strong updates to already clear-cut pairs can make the policy increasingly peaky. The second is suppression without discrimination. Dispreferred completions are consistently pushed down regardless of whether they were marginally worse or already extremely unlikely. Together, these forces accelerate a collapse of the policy’s support, concentrating mass on a small subset of responses and reducing diversity.

The authors link this phenomenon to “model degeneration,” and further point to empirical evidence, noting that a naive version without the weighting coefficient can cause degeneration. This finding is consistent with their broader motivation for a KL-constrained objective and with DPO’s formulation, which couples KL regularization in the implicit reward with a per-example weight that reflects the current ordering error.

# Connections to KL Regularization and Actor–Critic Instability
The DPO reparameterization also clarifies why omitting stabilizing terms leads to poor behavior in RLHF. In actor–critic formulations, the objective includes normalization terms that act like soft value functions. Without these, policy gradients can have high variance, making learning unstable. Prior works have used baselines and reward normalization to manage this instability, but these are themselves difficult to optimize or approximate.

By defining the implicit reward as \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), DPO produces a reward model that does not require additional baselines. The per-example importance weight then ensures that the gradient is largest where the model’s ordering is wrong, and small where the ordering is correct, acting as a variance-reducing mechanism tailored to the preference-learning setting. Removing this weight is akin to discarding a key control that keeps updates well-calibrated to the current policy state, and it invites the same instability actor–critic methods experience without good baselines: high-variance, overly aggressive updates that degrade the policy.

Moreover, the KL grounding of \(\hat{r}_{\theta}\) makes the updates directly sensitive to deviations from the reference model. The dynamic weight complements this by making updates sensitive to deviations from the correct ordering in the preference data. When both sensitivities are present, DPO can improve adherence to preferences while maintaining stability and diversity. When the dynamic weight is absent, the KL sensitivity alone may not be sufficient to prevent the peaky distributions that characterize degeneration, especially if \(\beta\) is set to allow meaningful deviation from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

# Practical Implications and Evidence within the DPO Framework
The DPO pipeline is simple: sample paired completions from a reference model and collect human preferences, then optimize \(\pi_{\theta}\) with the DPO loss. In settings where a standard supervised fine-tuned model \(\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}\) exists, it serves as \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\); otherwise, a reference can be constructed by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions. Throughout, the dynamic per-example importance weighting is a central element of the update.

The authors emphasize, both in their derivation and in experiments, that this weighting is important. They specifically caution that a naive probability ratio objective without the weighting coefficient leads to degeneration, and they provide empirical comparisons to methods that similarly push up preferred and push down dispreferred responses without the DPO-specific weighting. In broader experiments, DPO consistently produces favorable reward–KL frontiers and robust win rates compared to PPO and other baselines, supporting the view that its stabilizing features—rooted in the reparameterized reward and the dynamic weighting—are not optional.

Taken together, the theoretical construction and empirical observations point to the same conclusion: the dynamic, per-example importance weighting in DPO is integral to stable learning. Removing it discards the mechanism that focuses updates where they are needed and tempers them where they are not, increasing the likelihood of model degeneration through over-sharpening and loss of diversity.
