Direct Preference Optimization and the Role of Per-Example Importance Weighting

# Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a simple, reinforcement learning-free approach for training language models to align with human preferences. It starts from the same constrained objective that underpins reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and arrives at a closed-form optimal policy that can be optimized via a maximum likelihood objective over preferences. A central practical and conceptual insight in DPO is how it updates the model to increase the probability of preferred responses relative to dispreferred responses, while avoiding pathological behaviors that can arise from naively pushing probability ratios too aggressively. This chapter explains why DPO’s per-example importance weighting is critical: it selectively scales updates based on how incorrect the model’s current implicit reward ordering is, and thereby prevents model degeneration.

We begin by reviewing the preference learning setup and the RLHF objective. We then derive DPO’s objective and its gradient, and interpret the per-example importance weighting term in the gradient. Finally, we explain how this weighting helps avoid degeneration and connect these insights to empirical and theoretical observations from the paper.

# From Preferences to Policies: The RLHF Objective

The RLHF pipeline couples a reward model trained from human preferences with reinforcement learning to optimize a language model policy under a Kullback–Leibler (KL) constraint relative to a reference policy. Concretely, one begins with a supervised fine-tuned model $\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$, collects preference data $\\mathcal{D} = \\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\\}_{i=1}^N$ where $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$ under a Bradley–Terry (BT) model, and fits a reward model $r_{\\phi}(x,y)$ by maximizing likelihood:

$$
\\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\\phi}, \\mathcal{D}) = -\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\\sim \\mathcal{D}}\\bigl[\\log \\sigma(r_{\\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\\phi}(x, y_l))\\bigr],
$$

where preferences satisfy $p^*(y_1 \\succ y_2 \\mid x)=\\sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$ under the BT model. RLHF then optimizes a policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$ to maximize reward with a KL constraint to a reference policy $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$ (often $\\pi^{\\text{SFT}}$):

$$
\\max_{\\pi_{\\theta}}  \\mathbb{E}_{x\\sim \\mathcal{D}, y\\sim \\pi_{\\theta}(y \\mid x)}\\bigl[r_{\\phi}(x, y)\\bigr] - \\beta\\mathbb{D}_{\\textrm{KL}}\\bigl[\\pi_{\\theta}(y\\mid x)\\mid \\mid \\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\bigr].
$$

This objective has an optimal policy of the form

$$
\\pi_r(y\\mid x) = \\frac{1}{Z(x)}\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)\\exp\\left(\\frac{1}{\\beta}r(x, y)\\right),
$$

where $Z(x)$ is a partition function. Although theoretically straightforward, estimating $Z(x)$ can be expensive, and RL optimization introduces instability and complexity.

# DPO: Reparameterizing the Reward into the Policy

DPO avoids explicit reward modeling and RL by reparameterizing the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. Taking logs and rearranging the optimal policy yields

$$
r(x,y) =\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_r(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y\\mid x)} + \\beta \\log Z(x).
$$

Crucially, under the BT preference model, only differences of rewards matter, so the partition function $\\beta \\log Z(x)$ cancels when computing preference probabilities. Substituting the reparameterization into the BT model gives the preference likelihood directly in terms of the optimal policy $\\pi^*$ and $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$:

$$
p^*(y_1\\succ y_2 \\mid x)=\\frac{1}{1 + \\exp\\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_2\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_2\\mid x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi^*(y_1\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_1\\mid x)}\\right)}.
$$

This change of variables allows learning the optimal policy via maximum likelihood over preferences, using a parameterized policy $\\pi_{\\theta}$:

$$
\\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}(\\pi_{\\theta}; \\pi_\\text{ref}) = -\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\\sim \\mathcal{D}}\\left[\\log \\sigma \\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_w\\mid x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_{\\theta}(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y_l\\mid x)}\\right)\\right].
$$

In effect, the policy network simultaneously represents the language model and an implicit reward model. The implicit reward is defined as

$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y \\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y \\mid x)}.
$$

This construction maintains the full expressivity of rewards under the BT (and more general Plackett–Luce) preference families: any reward equivalence class consistent with these models can be represented by $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y)$ for some $\\pi_\\theta$, guaranteeing that the reparameterization does not restrict the class of learnable rewards.

# The DPO Gradient and Per-Example Importance Weighting

To understand how DPO increases the relative probability of preferred responses while avoiding degeneration, examine the gradient of $\\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}$ with respect to $\\theta$:

$$
\\nabla_\\theta \\mathcal{L}_\\text{DPO}(\\pi_\\theta;\\pi_\\text{ref}) = -\\beta\\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \\sim \\mathcal{D}} \\bigg[\\sigma(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w))\\Big[\\nabla_\\theta\\log \\pi(y_w \\mid x) - \\nabla_\\theta\\log\\pi(y_l \\mid x)\\Big]\\bigg].
$$

There are two key components:

1. The directional update increases $\\log \\pi(y_w \\mid x)$ and decreases $\\log \\pi(y_l \\mid x)$, directly raising the preferred response’s probability and lowering the dispreferred response’s probability.

2. The magnitude of the update is scaled by a per-example importance weight, $\\sigma(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w))$, which is larger when the implicit reward currently ranks the dispreferred completion above the preferred one, i.e., when $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) > \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w)$. Conversely, when the model already correctly ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$, this weight becomes smaller.

The importance weight therefore focuses learning on errors in the implicit reward ordering. If the model is already consistent with the preference label, the update is small; if the model is inconsistent, the update is large. The coefficient $\\beta$ further controls the overall strength of updates, reflecting the KL constraint that limits deviation from $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$.

# Why Importance Weighting Prevents Degeneration

Naively, one might attempt to optimize preferences by always pushing the preferred probability up and the dispreferred probability down with equal weight across examples, or by maximizing a simple probability ratio objective without a weighting term that depends on the model’s current ordering of completions. The paper reports that such naive variants can cause the language model to degenerate, as shown in their experiments (Appendix Table referenced in the text), meaning the model loses desirable properties such as diversity or stability and collapses into undesirable modes.

DPO’s per-example importance weighting mitigates this risk in several ways grounded in its formulation:

First, it adapts the update magnitude to the model’s current level of inconsistency with the preference label. If the model already assigns higher implicit reward to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, the term $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w)$ is negative, so $\\sigma(\\cdot)$ is small, and the update shrinks. This prevents over-correction on examples where the model is already aligned, which could otherwise push probabilities to extremes and reduce diversity.

Second, when the model misorders the pair (i.e., it places the dispreferred completion above the preferred completion), the weight $\\sigma(\\cdot)$ increases, focusing learning on fixing genuine errors. This selective pressure avoids uniformly aggressive updates that might amplify noise or overfit to idiosyncratic pairs, both of which are routes to degeneration.

Third, the weighting operates in tandem with the KL constraint implemented via the reference policy in the DPO objective, since $\\hat{r}_\\theta$ is defined relative to $\\pi_{\\text{ref}}$. This anchoring discourages large shifts away from the distribution on which the implicit reward is valid, further stabilizing updates. In the reparameterized view, the KL constraint is inseparable from the implicit reward definition, so the per-example weighting and the KL anchoring act coherently.

The paper emphasizes the importance of this weighting by noting that a naive probability ratio objective without it can cause degeneration, whereas the DPO update, with its logistic weighting on the reward difference, avoids such outcomes by modulating updates according to how “wrong” the model’s current implicit reward ordering is. Empirically, the authors report that this weighting is important: the naive method without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate.

# Intuition Through the Implicit Reward

The implicit reward $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y)$ provides a useful lens. Because

$$
\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y) = \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi_\\theta(y \\mid x)}{\\pi_\\text{ref}(y \\mid x)},
$$

it measures how much the current policy deviates from the reference policy on a given completion. The per-example weight $\\sigma(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w))$ is therefore a function of deviations that increase the likelihood of dispreferred responses relative to preferred ones. If the model has drifted in a way that elevates $y_l$ above $y_w$, the weight grows, and the update both pushes $y_w$ up and $y_l$ down, but only as much as needed to correct the misordering. If the model has not drifted, the update is minimal.

This dynamic enforces a conservative, error-corrective regime: the model is nudged toward preference-consistent rankings while remaining close to the reference distribution. The degeneracy observed with naive objectives likely arises when updates are indiscriminate, pushing probabilities without regard to whether the current ordering is already correct, and without grounding in the KL-based implicit reward. In contrast, DPO’s weighting injects a notion of “how wrong” the model currently is, and scales corrections accordingly.

# Practical Consequences and Empirical Evidence

The paper presents experimental evidence that DPO’s formulation yields stable training and avoids degeneration associated with naive objectives. Specifically:

1. The gradient formulation highlights that examples are weighted “by how much higher the implicit reward model $\\hat{r}_\\theta$ rates the dispreferred completions,” directly tying update magnitude to the severity of error.

2. The authors report that a naive version of the method without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate, whereas DPO remains stable.

3. Across tasks—controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue—DPO produced efficient reward–KL frontiers, matched or exceeded RLHF methods based on PPO, and did so with minimal tuning and without sampling in the training loop, suggesting that the weighting contributes to both stability and performance.

In sum, DPO’s per-example importance weighting is not a mere numerical convenience; it is a principled mechanism derived from the BT preference likelihood under the implicit reward reparameterization that modulates learning pressure in proportion to the model’s error on each pair. This prevents over-aggressive updates that can lead to mode collapse or other degenerate behaviors, while persistently correcting misordered preferences.

# Conclusion

Direct Preference Optimization transforms the RLHF objective into a simple maximum likelihood problem over preferences by reparameterizing rewards into policy log ratios relative to a reference. This yields an implicit reward $\\hat{r}_\\theta$ and a loss whose gradient increases the probability of preferred completions and decreases that of dispreferred ones. Crucially, the per-example importance weighting $\\sigma(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x, y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta (x, y_w))$ scales updates according to the current error in the implicit reward ordering. This mechanism focuses learning on genuine misorderings, avoids excessive corrections when the model is already aligned, and works hand-in-hand with the KL anchoring to the reference policy. The result is a stable, performant algorithm that increases the relative probability of preferred responses while avoiding the degeneration observed with naive, unweighted objectives.
