Chapter Title: Preventing Mode Collapse in Preference-Based Fine-Tuning: Why Direct Preference Optimization Works

# The Problem of Mode Collapse in Preference Learning

Large language models are fine-tuned to align with human preferences by leveraging datasets of pairwise comparisons between model-generated responses. A central challenge in this setting is to adjust the model so that it reliably prefers high-quality responses while maintaining diverse, coherent behavior. A concise failure mode in this process is mode collapse: the model concentrates its probability mass on single high-reward responses, sacrificing diversity and drifting away from the behaviors it was originally trained to exhibit. In the context of existing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) pipelines, the authors explicitly warn that a naive probability-ratio objective can cause this collapse, in which the model learns to prioritize preferred examples so aggressively that it degenerates into producing few or even single responses per prompt.

The RLHF objective is formulated to prevent such degeneration by incorporating a divergence penalty against a reference policy. The typical constrained objective maximizes expected reward while penalizing deviations from a reference policy via a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence term. This combination is not merely a technicality; the KL penalty is a crucial stabilizer that prevents the model from over-optimizing the reward function at the cost of diversity. Without carefully structured control, the tendency to allocate probability mass heavily to high-reward responses leads to undesirable behavior, including narrow, repetitive outputs that fail to generalize.

# Why Naive Probability-Ratio Objectives Lead to Degeneration

To understand why naive probability-ratio updates collapse, consider the intuitive approach of simply increasing the log-probability of preferred completions and decreasing the log-probability of dispreferred completions. Such an objective can be phrased as making the ratio $\pi_{\theta}(y_w \mid x)/\pi_{\theta}(y_l \mid x)$ large for each preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$ attached to prompt $x$. If applied uniformly across the dataset, this update can be overly aggressive: for prompts where the model already prefers $y_w$ over $y_l$, the objective continues to push the ratio higher; for prompts where the model is incorrect, the update is similarly strong but may push towards extreme concentration on the preferred example. This ubiquitous pressure encourages sharpening of the distribution, often beyond what is warranted or stable, leading to concentration of probability on single completions.

Crucially, this naive treatment ignores two stabilizing considerations. First, it neglects the relationship to the reference policy, ${\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$, which provides a baseline for how probabilities should be distributed given the model’s original training. Second, it treats all preference pairs equally, even when the model already ranks them correctly or incorrectly by large margins. Without moderating the strength of updates based on how wrong the model is and how far it is allowed to deviate from the reference policy, the consequence is runaway sharpening of probability mass. The authors note empirical evidence of this phenomenon: a naive probability-ratio objective without proper weighting causes the language model to degenerate, exhibiting mode collapse.

# The KL-Constrained RLHF Objective as a Stabilizer

The standard RLHF objective formalizes this stabilization by maximizing expected reward while constraining deviation from a reference policy through a KL penalty:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \, y \sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[ r_{\phi}(x, y) \bigr] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[ \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \bigr].
\]
Here, the parameter $\beta$ controls the strength of the constraint; larger $\beta$ enforces closeness to the reference, which counteracts mode collapse by discouraging excessive concentration. The optimal solution to this constrained objective admits a closed form:
\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} \, r(x, y)\right),
\]
where $Z(x)$ is a partition function. This form makes the stabilizing role of the KL term explicit: the optimal policy is proportional to the reference policy scaled by the exponentiated reward, and the partition function normalizes the distribution. Thus, even when the reward favors certain completions strongly, the policy remains tethered to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which reduces the risk of collapsing onto single responses.

The constrained optimization is therefore not an incidental design choice; it embodies a principled balance between reward maximization and diversity preservation. It recognizes that over-optimization of reward in isolation can produce brittle, degenerate policies and enforces a trust region around behaviors known to be sound from pre-training or supervised fine-tuning.

# Direct Preference Optimization: A Safe Alternative to Naive Ratio Updates

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a way to optimize the same KL-constrained objective without a separate reinforcement learning loop. It reparameterizes the reward in terms of the policy and the reference model, leveraging a change of variables that cancels the partition function when preferences depend only on reward differences, as in the Bradley–Terry model. Specifically, the human preference probability can be expressed in terms of the policy and reference policy:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)} \right)}.
\]
This leads directly to a maximum-likelihood training objective over policies:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[ \log \sigma \!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
Several properties of this objective guard against mode collapse. First, the policy is trained relative to the reference policy via ratios, not in isolation. Second, the scaling by $\beta$ ties update strength to the KL constraint, explicitly aligning optimization pressure with the permitted deviation. Third, and most importantly, the loss induces a specific gradient structure with dynamic weighting that moderates updates based on how mistaken the model currently is:
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}})
= -\beta \, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \Bigl[ \sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr) \bigl( \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi(y_l \mid x) \bigr) \Bigr],
\]
where the implicit reward is
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
\]
The key element in this gradient is the logistic weighting $\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr)$, which assigns higher weight when the model’s implicit reward ranks the dispreferred completion higher than the preferred one. This means that when the model is already correct, the update is smaller; when the model is wrong, the update is larger. Such per-example adaptive weighting preserves learning efficiency while avoiding indiscriminate sharpening. The authors report that a naive version of probability ratio updates that omits this weighting leads to degeneration, underscoring the importance of this mechanism in preventing mode collapse.

# Implicit Rewards and the Role of the Reference Policy

A central insight of DPO is that the language model can be viewed as implicitly parameterizing a reward function through its ratio to a reference policy:
\[
r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Under preference models like Bradley–Terry, only reward differences matter, so the partition function $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels. DPO thereby trains the policy directly to match the preference distribution induced by the optimal KL-constrained policy, without ever sampling from the policy in the training loop or explicitly estimating the partition function. This reparameterization belongs to an equivalence class of reward functions that induce the same preferences and the same optimal policy. As shown, for any reward function consistent with the Plackett–Luce family (including Bradley–Terry), there exists a representative in its equivalence class of the form $\beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$.

This equivalence clarifies why DPO is not narrowing the space of learned rewards, and why it can exactly recover the optimal policy that would be produced by RLHF under the same preference model. It also explains why the KL constraint is embedded in the DPO training: the implicit reward is defined relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and the optimization adjusts the policy’s log-probabilities in a manner that respects the constraint governed by $\beta$. By working in this reparameterized space, DPO keeps the optimization aligned with the stabilizing influence of the reference policy, which is precisely what prevents mode collapse.

# Empirical Signals of Degeneration and DPO’s Robustness

The paper’s experiments analyze the trade-off between reward and KL divergence, demonstrating that DPO achieves a more efficient frontier than PPO, including when PPO has access to ground-truth rewards. In addition to aggregate metrics, the authors explicitly caution that naive probability-ratio objectives, which ignore the adaptive weighting in the DPO gradient, can cause degeneracy. This empirical warning is consistent with the theoretical considerations above: if all preference pairs are pushed with equal strength to increase the ratio of preferred to dispreferred completions, the result is rapidly growing certainty and collapsing diversity.

DPO’s robustness, by contrast, arises from both its principled derivation and its practical training behavior. The per-example weighting moderates updates based on how incorrectly the model is ordering completions, and the dependence on the reference policy ensures that the model does not drift arbitrarily far from the behaviors it has been trained on. Notably, the authors find DPO to be stable and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the language model during fine-tuning and avoiding significant hyperparameter tuning. While DPO and PPO optimize the same underlying objective in theory, DPO’s training dynamics avoid the instabilities attributed to actor–critic methods and do so without introducing degenerate sharpening.

# Practical Implications for Preference-Based Fine-Tuning

From a practical standpoint, the lesson is clear: avoid naive probability-ratio objectives that indiscriminately magnify the log-probability of preferred examples and suppress the log-probability of dispreferred ones. Instead, employ an objective that incorporates a KL constraint to a reference policy and an adaptive per-example weighting tied to how misordered the implicit reward is. The DPO loss embodies both of these properties by construction, yielding updates that increase the likelihood of preferred completions and decrease the likelihood of dispreferred completions, but only in proportion to how necessary the correction is and within the bounds enforced by $\beta$.

This approach resolves a major source of instability in RLHF pipelines, where reward over-optimization and high-variance policy gradients can lead to brittle behavior. By reparameterizing the reward and training directly on preferences, DPO translates what would be a complex reinforcement learning procedure into a simple maximum-likelihood optimization that remains faithful to the theoretical guarantees of preference models. The result is a training method that aligns language models with human preferences while preserving diversity and avoiding the collapse onto single high-reward responses.
