In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', training $\\pi_\\theta$ with the DPO loss $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ (Eq. 8), which applies the logistic function $\\sigma$ to paired comparisons, is most analogous to training a binary classifier. This is because Eq. (8) takes the exact binary cross-entropy form used in logistic regression over pairwise preference labels under the Bradley–Terry model. Concretely, the human preference probability is modeled as $p^*(y_w \\succ y_l \\mid x) = \\sigma\\big(r^*(x,y_w)-r^*(x,y_l)\\big)$, and the corresponding maximum-likelihood objective for a reward model is the binary log-loss in Eq. (2): $\\mathcal{L}_R = -\\mathbb{E}[\\log \\sigma(r_\\phi(x,y_w)-r_\\phi(x,y_l))]$. DPO replaces the explicit reward difference with the reparameterized margin $\\beta\\log\\tfrac{\\pi_\\theta(y_w\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\\mid x)} - \\beta\\log\\tfrac{\\pi_\\theta(y_l\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\\mid x)}$, yielding $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}(\\pi_{\\theta};\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}) = -\\mathbb{E}[\\log \\sigma(\\text{margin})]$. This is precisely the negative log-likelihood of a binary classifier that predicts the preferred item in each pair, with the Bradley–Terry likelihood mapped into policy space via the change of variables in Eq. (7). Thus, DPO is maximum-likelihood estimation on paired labels in the same way that standard binary classification is, but with the “logit” supplied by the policy–reference log-odds rather than a standalone reward.

Moreover, the DPO gradient further cements the analogy to binary classification. The paper shows
$\\nabla_\\theta \\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}} = -\\beta\\,\\mathbb{E}\\Big[\\sigma\\big(\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y_l) - \\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y_w)\\big)\\big(\\nabla_\\theta\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_w\\mid x) - \\nabla_\\theta\\log \\pi_\\theta(y_l\\mid x)\\big)\\Big]$,
where $\\hat{r}_\\theta(x,y) = \\beta\\log\\tfrac{\\pi_\\theta(y\\mid x)}{\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}(y\\mid x)}$. This mirrors the logistic loss gradient: the update increases the score of the “positive” example ($y_w$) and decreases that of the “negative” example ($y_l$), with an importance weight $\\sigma(\\text{negative margin})$ that is larger when the model currently “misclassifies” the pair (i.e., assigns higher implicit reward to $y_l$ than $y_w$). Such weighting is characteristic of logistic regression’s emphasis on hard or misclassified examples. Consequently, DPO optimizes a binary cross-entropy over pairwise labels under the Bradley–Terry likelihood, making training $\\pi_\\theta$ with $\\mathcal{L}_{\\mathrm{DPO}}$ most naturally understood as training a binary classifier over comparisons, albeit with the classifier’s logit induced by the policy’s log-probability ratios relative to $\\pi_{\\mathrm{ref}}$.
