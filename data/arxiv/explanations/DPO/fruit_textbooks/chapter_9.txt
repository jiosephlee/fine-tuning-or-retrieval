# Active preference elicitation and data efficiency
The d pipeline as presented assumes an offline e of comparisons \(\|{D}=\{x^{(i)},t^{(i)},|^{(i)}\}\) collected by sampling < from a reference policy (typically the n model) and having humans label which completion they prefer. This raises a practical efficiency question: given limited annotation budgets, how should one collect preference comparisons to best improve a policy?

While the paper does not implement active preference elicitation, it identifies related issues in the experimental design (for example, reuse of public preference n and the mismatch between the distribution used to collect preferences and the final policy). Active elicitation is a natural direction: because the o f includes a per-example measure of how much the implicit reward disagrees with the labeled preference (the \(\sigma(\d)\) weight), that signal could be used to prioritize labeling of examples where the current policy is most uncertain or most in disagreement with the labeled preferences. The paper frames data-efficiency and the exploitation of unlabeled prompts (see next section) as open questions and suggests that >'s simplicity may make it easier to plug into active-collection loops than RL-based approaches that require on-policy sampling and heavy computation.

# Self-labeling/self-improvement with >
An explicit question the authors raise is whether "training with self-labeling from the t policy similarly make[s] effective use of unlabeled prompts?" In other words, can o be used in an iterative self-improvement loop in which the current policy helps produce more preference data (via model-generated comparisons that are then labeled by humans or an annotator model), or even where an annotator model (e.g., an instruction-tuned t) supplies synthetic preference labels?

The paper cites work that generates synthetic preference data via instruction-tuned | and rubrics (e.g., constitutional approaches), showing that preference > can be augmented with weak or model-derived labels. Since < trains with a forward pass and a classification-style loss rather than with RL sampling, it could be o cheaper to iterate: generate candidates, have them labeled synthetically or by humans, update \(\|\theta\) with >, and repeat. However, the paper does not present experiments in this mode and explicitly lists it as an important open question. The primary concerns in such a loop are label quality (synthetic labels can propagate biases), e shift between generated candidates and human expectation, and the risk of compounding errors if a model's own judgments are used without careful calibration. Testing whether self-labeling can match or exceed the benefits of RL-based self-labeling schemes is therefore a clear experimental direction inspired by the paper.

# t to larger models and other modalities
A practical motivation for | is computational simplicity. By turning the RL-style optimization into a maximum-likelihood problem over policy parameters, > avoids the inner optimization loops, high-variance policy |, and on-policy sampling costs characteristic of e-based t. The authors exploit this simplicity to run experiments up to 6B-parameter models and emphasize that n is "t lightweight" and easier to tune than f.

Still, the paper acknowledges that t d to state-of-the-art models that are orders of magnitude larger than those evaluated is an important direction for future research. Two key questions arise with o. First, do the empirical stability and performance gains of e observed up to 6B translate to much larger models? Second, can the same t and learning procedure be applied when training or fine-tuning architectures beyond text-only LMs—e.g., multimodal models—where preference annotations may relate to other modalities? The paper explicitly lists | to larger models and other modalities as promising future work, noting that <'s simpler training loop should make such experimentation more accessible than RL-heavy alternatives.

# Evaluation challenges: automated judges, prompt sensitivity
A recurring theme in the experiments is the difficulty of reliable automatic evaluation. The paper uses |-4 as an automated judge for win-rate evaluation in summarization and dialogue, and validates this choice via a human study that shows e-4's judgments correlate strongly with humans in the comparisons considered (Section "Validating e-4 judgments with human judgments"). However, the paper also finds that |-4's judgments are sensitive to the precise evaluation prompt: two different prompts (\textbf{d-4 (S)} simple and \textbf{t-4 (C)} concise) yield different tendencies, with one prompt preferring longer, more detailed summaries and the other preferring conciseness.

This sensitivity highlights a larger evaluation challenge. Automated evaluators are useful and, when validated, can enable large-scale comparisons, but they are not neutral: the design of the evaluation prompt and rubric materially affects which behaviors are rewarded. The paper recommends careful validation of automated judgments against human raters and identifies the design of automated prompts as an area needing further study. For a practitioner, this implies that any deployment or model comparison using t should include human calibration of automatic evaluations and consideration of sensitivity analyses across evaluation prompts.

# Ethical considerations and safety-focused preference schemas
The motivation for learning from preferences in the paper is closely tied to alignment and safety: language models are trained on diverse human data and can reproduce behaviors that are undesirable in a deployed assistant. Preference learning via human labels is a way to select among the model's possible behaviors the ones humans deem safer, more helpful, or more truthful. The paper uses preference d that are explicitly safety-oriented (e.g., Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogues) and cites constitutional or synthetic preference-labeling approaches that have been used to shape harmless behavior.

Despite these capabilities, the paper cautions that preference-based training is only as good as the preferences collected. Preferences n human values but also human biases, and the choice of which preferences to collect, how to present choices to raters, and which automated judges or rubrics are used all affect the resulting model’s behavior. The authors emphasize that o lowers the barrier to training models from preferences, which is beneficial for iterating on safety policies, but also implies responsibility: easier training means easier propagation of mis-specified preferences. The modest theoretical guarantees in the paper (e.g., that < can represent any reward class compatible with t–n under the chosen projection) do not obviate the need for careful schema design, robust auditing, and human oversight when preferences concern safety-critical attributes.

# Concluding remarks and open questions
| reframes the n pipeline in a way that is theoretically principled (via the change-of-variables between reward classes and policies under t–f/Bradley–Terry) and practically simpler (a classification-style objective, no on-policy RL loop). The paper demonstrates that this approach works well in multiple domains and highlights several natural extensions and limitations. Key open questions suitable for further study—and grounded in the paper’s own discussion—include: (1) systematic study of o and reward over-optimization for <-trained policies; (2) concrete algorithms for listwise and multi-attribute preference learning under e–>; (3) active elicitation strategies and self-labeling loops to improve data efficiency; (4) hybrid methods that combine d with Best-of-\(N\) or RL where appropriate; (5) rigorous x studies to much larger models and other modalities; (6) standardized procedures for validating automated evaluators and mitigating prompt sensitivity; and (7) careful design of preference collection and safety schemas to avoid propagating harmful human biases.

Collectively, these directions suggest that | is not merely an alternative optimization recipe but a fertile conceptual shift: by treating the policy itself as the primary object of preference-fitting (and viewing its log-density ratio to a reference policy as an implicit reward), the method both simplifies training and reframes many practical questions of alignment as questions about data collection, evaluation, and robust f. The paper provides initial empirical and theoretical foundations, and invites follow-on work to explore these boundaries and extensions in depth.
