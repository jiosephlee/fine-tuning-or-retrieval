# <|endoftext|> fine-tuning (SFT) for <|endoftext|> tasks
The standard RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) workflow typically begins with a <|endoftext|> fine-tuning (SFT) stage. In SFT, a pre-trained, unsupervised language model that has learned broad textual patterns and world knowledge is further trained on a curated dataset of high-quality, task-specific examples (for instance, dialogue transcripts, summaries, or demonstrations of instruction-following). The purpose of SFT is to produce a reasonable starting policy, commonly denoted $\pi^\text{SFT}$, that already generates plausible and relevant outputs for the <|endoftext|> task before any preference-based learning is applied.

There are two key reasons for this SFT stage. First, a model initialized from unsupervised pretraining will often be too unconstrained to give consistently task-appropriate outputs; SFT focuses the model on the target distribution. Second, preference datasets used later are usually collected by sampling from an SFT-style model, so training an explicit SFT model produces a reference policy that matches how the preference data were generated. In practice, $\pi^\text{SFT}$ becomes the working reference model for later stages of the pipeline, both as a source to generate candidate completions for human labeling and as the initial policy for RL optimization.

# Preference data collection: prompts, pairwise comparisons
After SFT, the next step in RLHF is to collect preference data. The typical procedure is to sample candidate completions for prompts using the SFT model, present pairs (or small sets) of these candidate responses to human labelers, and record which candidate the human prefers. A labeled datum therefore takes the form $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $x$ is the prompt (prefix or instruction), $y_w$ is the human-preferred completion, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred completion. Collecting relative judgments (pairwise comparisons) is often easier and more reliable than collecting absolute <|endoftext|> scores or perfect demonstrations, which makes pairwise preference data an attractive supervision signal.

This preference dataset, denoted $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, is treated as an empirical sample from an unknown human preference distribution. The labels are assumed to reflect some latent scoring or reward function on completions; learning to predict or satisfy those preferences is the central aim of RLHF. The fact that the data are generated by sampling from an SFT-like model motivates using that same model as the reference policy during later optimization steps to control <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|>

# Reward modeling via <|endoftext|> (BT) and Plackett–Luce (PL) models
To turn pairwise comparisons into a usable training signal, RLHF pipelines typically introduce a parametric reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ and fit it to the preference dataset under a probabilistic preference model. A common choice is the <|endoftext|> (BT) model (a special case of the more general Plackett–Luce (PL) family). Under the BT model, the probability that humans prefer $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is modeled as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)},
\]
where $r^*$ is the unknown true latent reward function assumed to generate the labels. In practice, a parametric reward model $r_\phi$ is trained by maximum likelihood on the collected comparisons, which reduces to minimizing a binary <|endoftext|> loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the <|endoftext|> function. This is simply the negative log-likelihood of the observed pairwise choices under the BT probability.

The PL family (which includes BT) models only relative differences of the latent scores, so it under-specifies the absolute scale or additive offset of $r$. # Practical challenges: instability, compute, and implementation complexity
Taken together, the pipeline—SFT, reward modeling, and RL fine-tuning—creates practical headaches that motivate alternative approaches. The first source of overhead is computational cost. RLHF commonly requires training multiple networks: the SFT model, the reward model, and then the policy/actor and often a separate <|endoftext|> network. The RL stage requires generating samples from the policy in the inner loop of training, which can be expensive especially for large language models. Some deployment strategies <|endoftext|> Best-of-$N$) even require many samples at <|endoftext|> time. Estimating or approximating partition functions and normalizing <|endoftext|> for Boltzmann-style policies is also computationally burdensome.

Instability is another prominent issue. Because the reward model is learned from finite preference data and is only defined up to additive functions of $x$ under BT/PL models, its predictions can be unreliable away from the sampled region. Training a policy aggressively to maximize such a learned reward can cause policy drift into regions where the reward model is inaccurate, leading to degenerate or unsafe generations. Actor-critic algorithms used in the RL stage can suffer high variance in <|endoftext|> estimates; the partition-function-like <|endoftext|> term acts like a soft value that ideally should be accounted for with <|endoftext|> but learning such <|endoftext|> or estimating them via Monte Carlo introduces more variance and tuning complexity. These factors make PPO-based RLHF sensitive to many hyperparameters and prone to instability unless carefully engineered.

Finally, the implementation burden is significant. RLHF is not a single loss or <|endoftext|> training run: it is a multi-component pipeline that requires coordinating data collection (sampling with the reference model), human labeling, reward-model fitting, and RL optimization (with sampling and <|endoftext|> machinery). Each step has its own hyperparameters and failure modes. The paper frames these practical challenges as the central motivation for seeking simpler alternatives that preserve the theoretical objectives of RLHF while avoiding many of the computational and stability problems described above.

In summary, the canonical RLHF pipeline—SFT, preference collection, reward fitting under BT/PL, and KL-constrained RL fine-tuning with PPO—has a clear conceptual structure but brings substantial practical complexity: multiple models to train, expensive on-policy sampling, partition-function estimation, high-variance <|endoftext|> requiring <|endoftext|> or critics, and sensitive hyperparameter tuning.
