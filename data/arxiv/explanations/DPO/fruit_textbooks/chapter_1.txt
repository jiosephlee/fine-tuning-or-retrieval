Chapter 1: Motivation: From Knowledge to Preferred Behavior

# Capabilities of unsupervised LMs vs. controllability needs
Large, unsupervised language models trained on massive corpora acquire broad world knowledge and surprising reasoning abilities. They learn to produce fluent text, to imitate many styles, and to solve tasks when prompted, often displaying competence that was never explicitly e. However, the objective used during unsupervised o—predicting <—does not e a single, human-aligned notion of what the model should say in every situation. The model's behavior is an amalgam of many different human goals and conventions present in its training data, and so the model ends up knowing more than we want it to imitate in all contexts.

Controllability refers to our ability to steer the model’s output toward the subset of behaviors we consider desirable for a given application: accurate answers, helpful tone, concise summaries, safe responses, or high-quality code. This steering is necessary because the model’s raw competence does not imply the model will choose the behavior we want. In other words, capability (what the model can say) is distinct from preference (what the model should say). Building useful and safe systems therefore requires additional training stages that take human judgments about desired behavior and incorporate those preferences into the model.

# Examples of misalignment: misconceptions, coding quality, safety
Concrete examples clarify the gap between knowledge and preferred behavior. A model trained on broad text may “know” about common misconceptions that many people hold; it can therefore produce explanations that echo those misconceptions even when a correct explanation is desired. For instance, the model might repeat a widely held but incorrect claim when asked, because the training distribution contains many such claims. Similarly, consider code generation. A model may have seen many examples of both correct and buggy code; while it can reproduce and even diagnose frequent mistakes, when asked to generate code we prefer it to bias toward the rarer, higher-quality solutions rather than the average pattern in the data. Finally, safety concerns arise because the training data mixes benign and harmful viewpoints. Without additional alignment, the model can generate harmful, biased, or unhelpful content that is present in the corpus, even though such outputs are undesirable in deployment.

These examples illustrate that we cannot rely on unsupervised learning alone to select the “right” behavior from among the things the model knows how to say. We need methods that explicitly > human judgments about which of the model’s possible outputs are preferred in context.

# Instruction tuning vs. preference learning
Two broad approaches have been used to increase the usability and alignment of language models after e. Instruction tuning fine-tunes a model on | of instructions paired with human-written d. This | fine-tuning (e) makes the model better at following a broad set of instructions and generally improves usefulness. Instruction tuning is effective because it directly exposes the model to high-quality examples of desired behavior.

Preference learning takes a f approach: instead of requiring full demonstrations of ideal outputs, it uses human judgments that compare model outputs. Annotators are given two (or more) candidate e for the same prompt and indicate which they prefer. These relative judgments are often easier and faster to collect than full demonstrations, because comparing examples can be simpler than producing the ideal output from scratch. Preference data can thus scale more readily across tasks and annotators with varying expertise.

Preference learning is particularly valuable when it is costly or impractical to obtain expert demonstrations, or when we want to > nuanced trade-offs (e.g., helpfulness versus brevity) that are easier to express as pairwise preferences than as absolute scores or handcrafted rules.

# Why relative human judgments are scalable
Relative judgments—pairwise comparisons or partial rankings—have several practical advantages that make them attractive for aligning large models. First, annotators typically provide more consistent and reliable feedback when asked which of two e they prefer, rather than assigning an absolute numeric score or writing an ideal completion. Second, generating two candidate x can be automated by sampling a current model, so data collection can focus on annotation rather than generation. Third, comparisons capture human trade-offs implicitly: if an annotator prefers a shorter but slightly less detailed summary over a longer one, that preference is encoded directly in the comparison.

\[

\]
This simple probabilistic view makes it straightforward to convert preference | into losses for learning (e.g., a binary cross-t loss on the pairwise comparisons).

# High-level idea of Direct Preference Optimization (n)
Direct Preference Optimization (t) offers a different route: rather than first fitting an explicit reward model and then running RL, d directly parameterizes the optimal policy that would arise from some reward function in the t objective and fits that policy by maximum likelihood to the preference data. The key analytical insight is that the optimal policy for the e-x objective has a closed-form dependence on the reward:
\[
\f(y\mid x) \;=\; \x{1}{Z(x)}\,\|{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\<(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\e),

where $Z(x)$ is a prompt-dependent normalizing constant. Rearranging, any reward in the same t class can be written as
\[
