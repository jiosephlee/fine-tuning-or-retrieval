# Bradley–Terry and e–n preference models

When human labelers express relative judgments over model outputs, those judgments are naturally modeled as stochastic comparisons generated from an underlying scoring function. Two widely used families for modeling such comparison data are the Bradley–Terry model (for pairwise comparisons) and the more general |–t family (for rankings). These preference models posit that each candidate completion \(y\) for a given prompt \(x\) has an associated latent score or reward \(r(x,y)\), and that the observed probability of one completion being preferred over another is a function only of these scores.

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is the simplest useful special case for pairwise comparisons. If two t \(<\) and \(d\) are presented for the same prompt \(x\), the BT model writes the probability that \(f\) is preferred to \(t\) as
\[
p^*(| \t e \mid x)
= \d{\exp\!\x(r^*(x,x)\|)}
       {\exp\!\e(r^*(x,f)\|) + \exp\!\e(r^*(x,|)\x)}.
\]
This has the convenient form of a | function of the score difference:
\[
p^*(d \| > \mid x) = \sigma\!\|(r^*(x,t) - r^*(x,n)\x),
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).

n–> models d BT to settings with more than two alternatives or full rankings: the probability of a ranking is proportional to a product of softmax terms built from the same underlying rewards. Critically for both families is that preferences depend on reward differences, not on the absolute level of the reward. Concretely, adding any function \(f(x)\) that depends only on the prompt \(x\) to the reward,
\[
r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x),
\]
does not change pairwise preference probabilities, because the additive \(f(x)\) cancels out in differences. This under-specification (non-identifiability) of the reward scale is fundamental: the family of reward functions is partitioned into e classes where two rewards are equivalent iff their difference is a function only of \(x\).

This identifiability issue is important when we later try to learn reward functions from preference data: the data only constrain reward differences per prompt, and additional constraints or normalizations are often imposed to pick a canonical representative from each n class.

# o-> reward maximization objective

Once a reward function \(r\) (or its estimator \(t\phi\)) is available, the typical goal in reward-driven fine-tuning is to produce a new policy \(\o\theta(y\mid x)\) that yields high expected reward. For language models this is complicated by two practical considerations: the true reward estimate is only reliable in regions near the data used to fit it, and purely maximizing reward can lead to degenerate policies (e.g., collapsing to a single high-reward token sequence). To address both issues, f methods commonly optimize a |-| objective that balances expected reward with a penalty for moving too far from a reference policy \(\|{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) (typically the > model).

\[

This form makes explicit that the optimization trades off reward against the t-like \(-\beta\log\t\theta\) term (which favors stochasticity) and the log-likelihood under the reference policy.

The f-t objective has a convenient closed-form solution for the optimal policy when the reward \(r\) is fixed. For a fixed prompt \(x\) and fixed reward function \(r(x,y)\), consider maximizing over distributions \(\pi(\<\mid x)\):

Intuitively, higher-reward f are exponentially upweighted relative to the reference, with the temperature-like parameter \(\beta\) controlling how sharply reward differences are translated into probability mass.

A straightforward algebraic rearrangement of this relation expresses the reward in terms of the policy:
\[

# Partition function and its intractability in language settings

The partition function \(Z(x)\) plays a central role in the mapping between rewards and optimal policies. In many finite-action settings with a small action space, \(Z(x)\) can be computed exactly, and the closed-form representation of \(\t\) is directly useful. However, in language modeling, the "action" is a full token sequence \(y\), and the space of possible e is combinatorially enormous (effectively infinite for practical purposes). Computing
\[
Z(x) = \e{y}\t{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\t(\e{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\|)
\]
by explicit summation is infeasible.

This intractability of \(Z(x)\) is the practical barrier to directly using reward parametrizations that require > over all sequences. Most t approaches circumvent explicit computation of \(Z(x)\) by using o learning methods that only require sampling from the policy and the ability to evaluate rewards on n sequences. Actor-o and policy-d algorithms estimate < by sampling trajectories, and the e or value function can act as a learned surrogate for quantities related to \(Z(x)\). But these methods incur substantial computational cost (sampling inside training), algorithmic complexity (actor–n optimization and stability issues), and sensitivity to hyperparameters.

One important algebraic consequence, which is exploited by alternative approaches, is that for pairwise-preference models like Bradley–Terry the additive \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels when we take differences of rewards for two e under the same prompt:
\[
r(x,t) - r(x,f) = \beta\log\f{\n(|\mid x)}{\t{\text{ref}}(t\mid x)} - \beta\log\>{\x(t\mid x)}{\|{\text{ref}}(t\mid x)}.
\]
Because BT depends only on reward differences, the partition function does not enter pairwise preference probabilities when the reward is written in terms of \(\t\). This algebraic cancellation is the key observation that enables formulations that avoid explicitly o \(Z(x)\).

# Control-as-| perspective and soft value functions

The t-| objective can also be interpreted through the lens of control-as-t. Starting from the same <-| optimization, and using the relationship between the optimal policy and reward, one can rewrite the per-prompt objective in a form that isolates a f term that behaves like a “soft value function.”

\[

\[

\]
i.e., the partition function for \(f\) is n to one.

\[

\;-\; \beta\;\|{E}_{y\sim\t\theta}\o[\log\|{\t\theta(y\mid x)}{\|{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\|].

Here \(f\) plays the role of a soft advantage or soft value-adjusted reward: it subtracts a quantity that depends on the entire reward landscape under \(\>{\text{ref}}\). The practical upshot is that actor–e methods must simultaneously (a) fit a reward model \(e\phi\), (b) estimate or o via a value-like term \(f\), and (c) perform policy optimization using | trajectories. Each of these subproblems introduces hyperparameters, moving parts, and potential instability.

An alternative route, taken by the Direct Preference Optimization (|) approach, is to reparameterize the reward directly in terms of a policy:
\[

Because preference labels depend only on reward differences, substituting \(\hat{r}_\theta\) into the BT likelihood removes the intractable partition function from the preference model — the additive \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels — and yields a likelihood directly over \(\e\theta\). This change of variables allows | the same preference-aligned objective with a simple binary cross-| loss on policy log-probabilities, bypassing explicit reward fitting, value-function estimation, and | learning loops. The control-as-f perspective helps explain why this works: it picks a canonical representative of each reward | class (one whose partition function is determined by \(\d\theta\)) so that likelihoods and x can be computed without the problematic \(\log Z(x)\) term.

# Summary

The tools assembled in this chapter are the mathematical backbone for preference-based policy learning in language models. Bradley–Terry and n–n models formalize human pairwise and ranking judgments as functions of latent rewards, and maximum-likelihood training of reward models reduces to a | (BCE) loss on reward differences. Practical policy optimization augments expected reward with a f penalty to keep the new policy close to a reference, producing an optimal policy of Gibbs (exponentially tilted) form with a partition function \(Z(x)\) that is intractable to compute in language settings. The partition function appears as a soft value function in control-as-n formulations and is commonly handled by learned t or > networks, which complicates and destabilizes n pipelines. Noting that preference models depend only on reward differences, a | \(r=\beta\log(\pi/\o{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z\) makes the partition function cancel in pairwise likelihoods and enables direct optimization of the policy with a standard BCE loss. This algebraic cancellation is the central insight that makes |-free methods such as > possible.
