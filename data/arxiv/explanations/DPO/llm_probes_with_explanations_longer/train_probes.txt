In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', to optimize a policy directly from preference data instead of doing reward estimation followed by RL, DPO trains the policy using a simple supervised loss, namely binary cross-entropy. The key derivation starts from the KL-constrained RLHF objective and its optimal policy form, which implies that for any reward function the optimal policy satisfies
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right).
$$
Rearranging gives an equivalent parameterization of rewards in terms of policy likelihood ratios,
$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$
Under the Bradley–Terry preference model, human comparisons depend only on reward differences, so the unknown partition term \( \beta\log Z(x)\) cancels when forming \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\). Substituting the reparameterization into the Bradley–Terry likelihood yields a preference probability expressed entirely in terms of the policy and the reference policy,
$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)=\sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} \right),
$$
which leads directly to a maximum-likelihood objective over preferences that is just binary cross-entropy:
$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi;\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} \right)\right].
$$

This supervised loss is equivalent to fitting a Bradley–Terry model but with a change of variables that makes the policy itself the parameterization of the implicit reward; in particular, DPO optimizes the same KL-regularized reward objective as RLHF without explicitly estimating a separate reward model or running RL. The gradient of the loss clarifies its supervised nature: defining the implicit reward \( \hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), the update increases \( \log \pi(y_w\mid x)\) and decreases \( \log \pi(y_l\mid x)\), weighted by how incorrectly the current implicit reward orders the pair:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sigma\!\big(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\,\big( \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l\mid x) \big) \right].
$$
Thus, DPO turns preference learning into standard supervised training with binary cross-entropy on pairwise comparisons, while preserving the optimality conditions of the original RLHF objective through the policy–reward reparameterization and the Bradley–Terry likelihood.

According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors' ablation-style reasoning indicates that removing the dynamic, per-example importance weighting from DPO is likely to lead to model degeneration. This follows directly from the DPO gradient analysis: the update for a preference pair is multiplied by the factor \( \sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big) \), where \( \hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \). When the model already ranks the preferred response higher (i.e., \( \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) \gg \hat r_\theta(x,y_l) \)), the weighting shrinks toward 0, dampening further pushes and preserving diversity; when the ranking is wrong, it grows toward 1, prioritizing large corrective updates. Removing this per-example weight amounts to applying the same push to increase \( \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) \) and decrease \( \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) \) for every pair, regardless of confidence. That uniform pressure over-optimizes already-correct pairs, drives probabilities toward the extremes, and—because the implicit reward is the log-ratio relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)—accelerates divergence from the reference distribution beyond what the KL constraint governed by \(\beta\) can counteract, producing low-entropy, collapsed behaviors.

The paper’s empirical observations support this mechanism: the authors note that a naïve probability ratio objective “without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate” (cf. the gradient interpretation and Appendix results). Intuitively, the weighting implements a self-normalizing step-size that ties the update magnitude to the current margin in the Bradley–Terry likelihood \( \sigma\big(r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l)\big) \) after reparameterizing rewards as \( r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \). Conversely, when the model incorrectly assigns higher implicit reward to $y_l$, the weight becomes large, focusing learning on hard examples and correcting misorderings efficiently. This weighting arises from the curvature of the logistic (binary cross-entropy) objective on the margin of the reward differences and, together with the $\beta$ scaling that enforces the KL constraint via $\hat r_\theta$, prevents the aggressive, unbounded probability pushes that the naive probability-ratio or unlikelihood objectives can cause—degeneration that the paper reports empirically in Appendix Table (unlikelihood_generations).

According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', when the model already rates $y_w$ much higher than $y_l$, the per-example weight in the DPO gradient, $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$, becomes near zero. This follows directly from the form of the DPO gradient and the properties of the logistic function. In DPO, the implicit reward is $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, and the gradient for a preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$ includes the factor $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$. If the model already assigns a much larger implicit reward to $y_w$ than $y_l$, then $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) \ll 0$, so the logistic $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ evaluated at a large negative argument is close to $0$. Consequently, the multiplicative weight on the update $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ is near zero, and the gradient contribution from that example vanishes, as illustrated in the paper’s annotated gradient where this term is “higher weight when reward estimate is wrong.”

This behavior is sensible and desirable in the DPO framework because it focuses learning on the examples where the model’s implicit reward ordering is incorrect or only weakly correct. When $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) \gtrsim \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$, the difference becomes non-negative, making $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ close to $1$, which intensifies the update to increase $\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decrease $\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$. Conversely, when the margin is already large in the correct direction, further pushing probabilities would be unnecessary and potentially destabilizing. The per-example weighting thus acts as an adaptive importance weight tied to the Bradley-Terry likelihood, preventing over-optimization and the degeneration the authors observe with a naive probability ratio objective. It also harmonizes with the KL constraint through $\hat{r}_\theta$, ensuring updates are strongest where they reduce preference errors while remaining conservative where the policy already aligns with preferences.

This follows directly from the DPO gradient expression derived in the paper: $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w))\bigl(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\bigr]$, where $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. The scalar weight $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function, which is monotone increasing; it is close to $1$ when its argument is large and positive (meaning $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)\gg \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)$, a severe misranking) and close to $0$ when its argument is large and negative (meaning the model correctly ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$ with margin). Therefore, the contribution of each comparison to the gradient is largest precisely when the implicit reward misorders the pair, and smallest when the model already agrees with the preference.

This weighting is a standard property of cross-entropy over pairwise preferences under the Bradley–Terry parameterization the paper uses. Because the DPO objective is $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\bigr]$, its gradient naturally focuses learning where the current policy violates the preference model, amplifying corrections on “hard” or misranked pairs. Intuitively, the update increases $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decreases $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$, but the step size is modulated by how wrong the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ is on that example. The paper notes this dynamic, per-example importance weighting is crucial for stability; a naive unweighted probability-ratio objective can lead to degeneration, whereas the sigmoid weighting concentrates learning on errors, akin to well-behaved logistic regression, while the $\beta$ factor properly calibrates the strength of updates under the KL constraint.

In the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', the authors show that to align a language model with preferences without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning, DPO applies a specific transformation to make the RLHF objective optimizable with a simple classification loss: change of variables. Concretely, they start from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective and its optimal policy form, which under a general reward \(r\) is
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]
Rearranging yields an expression for the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference,
\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
\]
where \(\log Z(x)\) is a normalization that depends only on \(x\). Because the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on reward differences, \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\), the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel, and the preference probability can be written directly in terms of the optimal policy’s log-odds relative to the reference:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\right).
\]
Maximizing likelihood of the observed preference dataset then reduces to a binary cross-entropy loss over these logits, yielding the DPO objective
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
\]
which is exactly a simple classification loss on preference pairs.

This change of variables is theoretically sound and does not reduce expressivity. The paper formalizes an equivalence relation over reward functions, \(r\sim r'\) iff \(r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\), under which both the Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce preferences and the KL-constrained optimal policy are invariant. They prove that for any reward class compatible with these preference models, there exists a representative of the form
\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
so optimizing \(\pi_\theta\) under \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) is equivalent to fitting a (reparameterized) reward that induces the same optimal policy as in RLHF. The resulting gradient
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\right],
\]
with \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), directly increases the relative likelihood of preferred responses while adaptively weighting examples by how incorrect the current implicit reward ordering is. Thus, the change-of-variables both eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling and RL sampling, and makes the RLHF objective optimizable via a stable, simple classification loss.

According to the paper 'Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model', when increasing the relative probability of preferred responses, DPO's per-example importance weighting helps avoid model degeneration. The DPO loss arises from fitting the Bradley–Terry preference model directly in terms of the policy, yielding a logistic cross-entropy over the difference of reparameterized rewards, where the implicit reward is defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. Differentiating the DPO objective gives the update

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. This multiplicative term, $\sigma\!\left(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\right)$, acts as a per-example importance weight: it is close to $1$ when the model misorders the pair (the dispreferred $y_l$ has higher implicit reward than the preferred $y_w$), and close to $0$ when the ordering is already correct and confident. Consequently, DPO applies large corrective updates only where needed and attenuates updates where the model already agrees with preferences, preventing runaway increases in probability ratios that can drive the policy toward pathological extremes.

This damping effect addresses the degeneration observed with naïve objectives that push up $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and push down $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ uniformly (e.g., simple unlikelihood or an unweighted probability-ratio objective), which the paper reports can cause language models to degenerate (see Appendix Table referenced as showing unlikelihood-generations). Because the weight comes from the Bradley–Terry maximum-likelihood fit, it embodies calibrated confidence: examples near the decision boundary receive moderate updates, confidently correct examples receive tiny updates, and confidently wrong examples receive strong corrections. Together with the KL control via $\beta$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which constrains $\hat{r}_\theta$ through $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, this per-example importance weighting keeps optimization well-behaved—raising the relative probability of preferred responses without collapsing diversity or amplifying errors into degenerate policies.
