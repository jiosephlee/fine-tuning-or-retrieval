This actor-critic or policy-gradient approach requires on-policy sampling: the model must generate trajectories during training to estimate gradients, and stabilizing the optimization typically requires training a value function (critic) or using baselines to reduce variance. In practice, a parametric reward model $r_\phi$ is trained by maximum likelihood on the collected comparisons, which reduces to minimizing a binary logistic loss: \[ \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr], \] where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Chapter 2: The RLHF Pipeline and Its Pain Points  The standard RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) workflow typically begins with a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage. The typical procedure is to sample candidate completions for prompts using the SFT model, present pairs (or small sets) of these candidate responses to human labelers, and record which candidate the human prefers. Therefore, RLHF implementations typically turn to policy-gradient style algorithms, with proximal policy optimization (PPO) and REINFORCE being common choices. Instability is another prominent issue. There are several practical complications that arise. The PL family (which includes BT) models only relative differences of the latent scores, so it under-specifies the absolute scale or additive offset of $r$. First, the KL-related normalization that appears in exact expressions (the partition function in the Boltzmann form) is expensive to compute, so practitioners approximate or use sample-based baselines. That is, adding any function of the prompt $x$ to $r(x,y)$ does not change the predicted pairwise probabilities. Each step has its own hyperparameters and failure modes. The RL stage requires generating samples from the policy in the inner loop of training, which can be expensive especially for large language models. These pain points motivate rethinking the parameterization and optimization approach used in RLHF so that preference learning can be performed more directly and stably. Estimating or approximating partition functions and normalizing constants for Boltzmann-style policies is also computationally burdensome. The purpose of SFT is to produce a reasonable starting policy, commonly denoted $\pi^\text{SFT}$, that already generates plausible and relevant outputs for the downstream task before any preference-based learning is applied. Second, actor-critic methods are sensitive to hyperparameters: learning rates, clipping thresholds, target KL schedules, and so on. Because the language generation action space is discrete and the constrained objective above involves expectations over samples from the policy, it is not differentiable end-to-end in $\theta$ in the straightforward way. The fact that the data are generated by sampling from an SFT-like model motivates using that same model as the reference policy during later optimization steps to control distributional shifts. There are two key reasons for this SFT stage. First, a model initialized from unsupervised pretraining will often be too unconstrained to give consistently task-appropriate outputs; SFT focuses the model on the target distribution. Third, PPO-style updates themselves are not guaranteed to be stable when the reward signal is noisy or misspecified, which is often the case with learned reward models. Under the BT model, the probability that humans prefer $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is modeled as \[ p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}, \] where $r^*$ is the unknown true latent reward function assumed to generate the labels. Actor-critic algorithms used in the RL stage can suffer high variance in gradient estimates; the partition-function-like normalization term acts like a soft value that ideally should be accounted for with baselines, but learning such baselines or estimating them via Monte Carlo introduces more variance and tuning complexity. Practically, reward models are often normalized (e.g., mean zero over some data) to reduce variance, but this under-specification is an inherent property of BT/PL formulations. RLHF commonly requires training multiple networks: the SFT model, the reward model, and then the policy/actor and often a separate critic/value network. In summary, the canonical RLHF pipeline—SFT, preference collection, reward fitting under BT/PL, and KL-constrained RL fine-tuning with PPO—has a clear conceptual structure but brings substantial practical complexity: multiple models to train, expensive on-policy sampling, partition-function estimation, high-variance gradients requiring baselines or critics, and sensitive hyperparameter tuning. Operationally, the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ is typically the SFT model that generated the preference dataset, so the KL regularization enforces that the optimized policy stays near the data-generating distribution. Conceptually, it constrains the learning problem to remain close to the distribution on which the reward model is accurate; this is critical because reward models trained on limited comparison data can be misleading far from the sampled region. In SFT, a pre-trained, unsupervised language model that has learned broad textual patterns and world knowledge is further trained on a curated dataset of high-quality, task-specific examples (for instance, dialogue transcripts, summaries, or demonstrations of instruction-following). Because the reward model is learned from finite preference data and is only defined up to additive functions of $x$ under BT/PL models, its predictions can be unreliable away from the sampled region. Constraining the policy also helps retain generation diversity and avoid degenerate solutions (e.g., collapsing to a single high-reward output). Second, preference datasets used later are usually collected by sampling from an SFT-style model, so training an explicit SFT model produces a reference policy that matches how the preference data were generated. Some deployment strategies (e.g., Best-of-$N$) even require many samples at inference time. From this constrained objective, one can show (using standard arguments from control-as-inference and maximum-entropy RL) that the optimal policy corresponding to a particular reward function $r$ takes the Boltzmann form \[ \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right), \] with partition function $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. These factors make PPO-based RLHF sensitive to many hyperparameters and prone to instability unless carefully engineered. Training a policy aggressively to maximize such a learned reward can cause policy drift into regions where the reward model is inaccurate, leading to degenerate or unsafe generations. The usual practical trick is to form a scalar per-completion reward that incorporates both the learned reward and a KL-correction term: \[ \tilde r(x,y) = r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta\bigl(\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr), \] and then apply PPO to maximize expected $\tilde r$ under $\pi_\theta$. Collecting relative judgments (pairwise comparisons) is often easier and more reliable than collecting absolute scalar scores or perfect demonstrations, which makes pairwise preference data an attractive supervision signal. This is simply the negative log-likelihood of the observed pairwise choices under the BT probability. The standard formulation is a KL-regularized reward maximization: \[ \max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr], \] where $\beta>0$ is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the penalty for deviating from the reference policy. This expression makes explicit how the reward and the KL constraint combine: the policy reweights the reference probabilities by exponentiated, scaled reward. Once a reward model $r_\phi$ has been learned, the next stage is to fine-tune a language model policy $\pi_\theta$ to produce completions that receive high reward, while preventing the policy from drifting too far away from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually set to the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$). To turn pairwise comparisons into a usable training signal, RLHF pipelines typically introduce a parametric reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ and fit it to the preference dataset under a probabilistic preference model. In practice, $\pi^\text{SFT}$ becomes the working reference model for later stages of the pipeline, both as a source to generate candidate completions for human labeling and as the initial policy for RL optimization. The labels are assumed to reflect some latent scoring or reward function on completions; learning to predict or satisfy those preferences is the central aim of RLHF. This preference dataset, denoted $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, is treated as an empirical sample from an unknown human preference distribution. RLHF is not a single loss or supervised training run: it is a multi-component pipeline that requires coordinating data collection (sampling with the reference model), human labeling, reward-model fitting, and RL optimization (with sampling and variance-reduction machinery). A labeled datum therefore takes the form $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $x$ is the prompt (prefix or instruction), $y_w$ is the human-preferred completion, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred completion. Taken together, the pipeline—SFT, reward modeling, and RL fine-tuning—creates practical headaches that motivate alternative approaches. The KL term plays several important roles. After SFT, the next step in RLHF is to collect preference data. Finally, the implementation burden is significant. The paper frames these practical challenges as the central motivation for seeking simpler alternatives that preserve the theoretical objectives of RLHF while avoiding many of the computational and stability problems described above. The first source of overhead is computational cost.
