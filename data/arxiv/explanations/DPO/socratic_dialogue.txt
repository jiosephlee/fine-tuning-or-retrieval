Tutor: Before we dive into DPO, can you summarize what problem RLHF is trying to solve with large language models?

Student: Sure. Big LMs learn lots from unsupervised text, but they aren’t aligned to what we want. RLHF uses human preferences to steer them. Typically, you first do supervised fine-tuning (SFT), then train a reward model from human comparisons, and finally use RL (like PPO) to optimize the LM to get high reward while staying close to the original model.

Tutor: Good. Why is that third step—RL with PPO—considered painful?

Student: Because it’s unstable and complex. You need on-policy sampling during training, a learned value baseline, a bunch of hyperparameters, and it can be compute-heavy and finicky.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, DPO claims we don’t need that RL step. Any guess how that might be possible?

Student: Maybe they avoid explicit reward modeling? Or they find a way to turn the RL objective into a supervised objective?

Tutor: You’re on the right track. Let’s recall two ingredients in standard RLHF. First, the Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model: it says the probability a human prefers $y_1$ over $y_2$ depends on $\exp\big(r(x,y_1)\big)$ vs $\exp\big(r(x,y_2)\big)$, i.e., only on $r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)$. Second, the RL objective: maximize expected reward subject to a KL constraint from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, typically the SFT model. Are you familiar with the optimal policy form under that KL constraint?

Student: I think so. The optimal policy is proportional to $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$, where $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. There’s also a partition function $Z(x)$ to make it a proper distribution.

Tutor: Great. That’s key. Now, here’s the trick: take logs and rearrange. You can write the reward as
$r(x,y) = \beta\big(\log \pi^{*}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big) + \beta \log Z(x).$
What happens if you plug this expression for $r$ into the BT model for $p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)$?

Student: Since BT uses a difference of rewards, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms cancel! So the preference probability can be written entirely in terms of $\log \pi^{*}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ for $y_w$ and $y_l$.

Tutor: Exactly. So if we parametrize a policy $\pi_\theta$ and assume preferences follow BT, we can fit $\pi_\theta$ by maximum likelihood on human comparisons, with a loss that’s just a binary cross-entropy over a logistic of $\beta$ times $\big((\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)) - (\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x))\big)$. No RL loop needed. Does that make sense?

Student: So instead of learning $r$ and then doing RL to find $\pi$, we directly learn $\pi$ by pretending $\pi$ defines the reward via $\beta \log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$? That’s clever. But why is that legitimate?

Tutor: Two reasons. First, BT only identifies reward up to an $x$-dependent constant. Many different rewards induce the same pairwise preferences. Second, the KL-constrained optimal policy depends only on the equivalence class of the reward (shifts by functions of $x$ don’t change the optimal policy). The DPO parameterization $\hat r(x,y) = \beta \log\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right]$ picks a canonical representative in each equivalence class. You don’t lose expressivity: any BT-consistent reward class can be written this way for some $\pi_\theta$.

Student: So “your LM is secretly a reward model” means the model’s log-prob ratio to the reference actually acts like a reward?

Tutor: Precisely. The implicit reward is $\hat r(x,y) = \beta \log\!\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right)$. Now, what would the gradient of this DPO loss push the model to do on each comparison $(x, y_w, y_l)$?

Student: Intuitively, it should increase $\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decrease $\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$. But I guess there’s also a weight based on how wrong the current model is?

Tutor: Right. The gradient looks like a weighted difference:
$\text{weight} \times \left[\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\right],$
where the weight is $\sigma(\hat r(x,y_l) - \hat r(x,y_w))$—large when the model rates the loser above the winner, small when it already gets the order right. That per-example weighting stabilizes training; naïve “unlikelihood” (always push down $y_l$) can degenerate.

Student: Got it. So $\beta$ scales the strength of this push and relates back to the KL constraint.

Tutor: Yes. Lower $\beta$ means stronger pressure to separate winner from loser (looser KL), higher $\beta$ keeps the policy closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Speaking of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, where does it come from in practice?

Student: If there’s an SFT model, use that. If not, the paper suggests training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood of the preferred responses $y_w$ to match the distribution of the data pairs.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, compare DPO with PPO from a “control as inference” lens. The PPO actor tries to match the optimal posterior policy proportional to $\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp\!\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\right)$. To stabilize gradients, PPO needs a baseline or value function to approximate the log-partition term; otherwise variance is high. With DPO’s reparameterization, that normalizing term cancels in the BT objective, so you don’t need a learned value baseline or on-policy rollouts.

Student: So DPO removes the main sources of instability: no on-policy sampling, no value function, no return normalization hacks.

Tutor: That’s the idea. Now, what evidence did the paper provide that this simpler procedure still optimizes the RLHF objective well?

Student: They did a controlled sentiment experiment where ground-truth reward is a sentiment classifier. They plotted reward vs KL relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. DPO dominated PPO, even PPO with ground-truth rewards, achieving better reward at the same KL.

Tutor: Right. And on real preference datasets?

Student: On Reddit TL;DR summarization, DPO had higher GPT-4 win rates than PPO and was more robust across sampling temperatures. On Anthropic’s single-turn dialogue, DPO improved over a Preferred-FT baseline and matched or beat a Best-of-128 sampling baseline, which is strong but expensive. They also showed the DPO model generalized better than PPO to CNN/DailyMail, again via GPT-4 win rates.

Tutor: Good. Were GPT-4 judgments validated?

Student: Yes. They ran a human study and found GPT-4’s preferences (with a careful prompt emphasizing conciseness) correlated well with human raters—about as well as humans agree with each other.

Tutor: Excellent. Any caveats or limitations you noticed?

Student: A few:
- It assumes the BT (or Plackett–Luce) preference model; if preferences violate that, the fit could be off.
- Choice of $\beta$ matters for the reward–KL trade-off.
- It relies on the quality and distribution of the preference dataset; mismatch with $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ can hurt, though they mitigate it.
- Potential over-optimization issues weren’t fully explored.
- Results were up to 6B parameters; scaling to much larger models remains to be shown.

Tutor: Well summarized. One last conceptual check: Why is it okay that DPO never computes the partition function $Z(x)$?

Student: Because BT uses reward differences across two responses for the same $x$. In $r(x,y) = \beta \log\!\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right) + \beta \log Z(x)$, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ term is identical for both responses and cancels. So the likelihood of preferences depends only on the log-probability ratios, which we can compute exactly from $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

Tutor: Perfect. If you had to explain DPO in one sentence to a colleague, what would you say?

Student: DPO replaces the RL step of RLHF with a simple logistic loss over pairs of responses, by reparameterizing the reward as the log-probability ratio between the learned policy and a reference model—so the LM itself acts as the reward model and we get a stable, efficient training procedure that matches or beats PPO.
