### Why does DPO sometimes outperform PPO even with ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT)?
Question:
In the sentiment control experiment, the reward–KL frontier for DPO dominates PPO and even PPO-GT (which uses the true reward). Conceptually, if both are optimizing the same KL-regularized objective, why can DPO exceed PPO with ground-truth rewards? Is the gap due to variance/baseline issues in PPO, insufficient exploration, or a mismatch in how the sequence-level KL is targeted? Any intuition on where PPO-GT loses optimality compared to maximizing the closed-form optimum implied by the DPO reparameterization?
Answer:
Short answer: even when both methods are trying to maximize the same KL‑regularized reward objective, DPO can beat PPO (even PPO supplied with the ground‑truth reward) because DPO optimizes an analytically derived reparameterization of the optimal policy directly, while PPO optimizes the same objective only approximately via high‑variance, sampling‑based actor‑critic updates that require estimating a normalization/value term and use per‑trajectory Monte‑Carlo gradients. Those practical approximations create bias, variance, and optimization/representation mismatches that make PPO-GT fail to reach the closed‑form optimum that DPO targets.

Why that happens (intuitively and technically), grounded in the paper
1) DPO solves for the optimal policy class analytically. The paper shows that the optimizer of
   $\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$
has the closed form $\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)$. Rewriting $r$ in terms of $\pi$ (Eq. 5 in the paper)
   $r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$
and plugging this into the Bradley–Terry preference model cancels the unknown partition function $Z(x)$. DPO therefore converts the preference likelihood into a simple binary‑cross‑entropy objective directly over $\pi_\theta$ (Eq. 8). In short: DPO parameterizes the reward→optimal policy mapping and optimizes the policy that, by construction, sits in the set of exact optimizers of the KL‑constrained problem (modulo the usual under‑specification of adding an $x$‑dependent constant to rewards).

2) PPO optimizes the same objective only indirectly and approximately. The RL formulation requires a normalization (soft value / partition function) term:
   $f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta) = r - \beta\log\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big).$
That term does not change the optimum but does appear in policy gradients (Eq. 11). To handle it PPO uses sampling, a value function baseline, reward normalizations and many heuristics (clipping, advantage normalization, etc.). Those estimators have nontrivial variance and bias in sequence generation problems: sequence‑level Monte‑Carlo estimates of returns are noisy, the learned value baseline may be poorly estimated, and single‑sample baselines are high‑variance approximations of the partition function. The paper explicitly points out this source of instability and variance as a reason actor‑critic methods are fragile in RLHF-style setups (Section 4.2).

3) Practical mismatch: sequence‑level KL vs per‑token approximations and optimization dynamics. PPO implementations often work with per‑step/actor‑critic decompositions, clipping/penalties that operate tokenwise or with approximate trajectory returns. Even if PPO-GT has the true reward, the optimization dynamics and the way the KL penalty is applied/estimated (and how the value function is used to form advantages) can steer learning away from the sequence‑level optimum that DPO is effectively targeting in closed form. DPO’s loss is a pairwise logistic objective that directly nudges $\pi_\theta$ to satisfy the Bradley–Terry ordering with a built‑in, per‑example importance weight (the $\sigma(\cdot)$ factor in the DPO gradient) that downweights easy examples and focuses updates where the policy’s implicit reward ordering is wrong; this weighting both stabilizes training and prevents model collapse that naive ratio objectives suffer from (paper’s gradient discussion).

4) Exploration / sample inefficiency and Best‑of‑N effects. PPO updates rely on samples from the policy (or from replay); sampling limitations or conservativeness heuristics can limit the diversity of trajectories seen during optimization. The paper’s experiments show PPO can be outperformed even when it gets ground‑truth reward because DPO uses the offline preference pairs and directly solves the reparameterized MLE—there is no need to re‑sample trajectories to estimate the normalizer or to fit a separate value network. Best‑of‑N baselines (which decouple reward estimation from policy optimization) are computationally expensive but sometimes competitive; they illustrate that shortcomings of PPO are in part an optimization/sampling issue rather than a pure representational one.

Net effect. Because DPO removes the need to estimate the partition function/value baseline and trains the policy with a low‑variance, well‑specified classification objective derived from the exact optimality condition, it achieves a policy closer to the analytical optimum of the KL‑regularized objective. PPO, even with true rewards, must approximate the same conditions via noisy policy gradients and value estimation; those approximations produce variance, bias, and instability that make PPO‑GT converge to worse reward/KL tradeoffs in practice.

Takeaway. The gap is not a mysterious mismatch in the mathematical objective (both are optimizing the same KL‑constrained criterion) but a mismatch in parameterization and optimization. DPO targets the optimum in closed form (after a well‑motivated reparameterization), which yields a simpler, lower‑variance learning problem; PPO optimizes the same objective via sampling and learned baselines, which introduces practical error sources (variance, bias, instability, suboptimal exploration) that explain why PPO‑GT can underperform DPO.
