\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does DPO handle length bias or verbosity in preferences?
Question:
In summarization/dialogue, longer outputs often get higher log-prob mass simply due to more tokens, and preference models can be sensitive to verbosity. Since the DPO score is the sequence-level log-ratio, does this inherently favor longer answers? The paper mentions that GPT-4 prompts were adjusted to penalize verbosity during evaluation, but what about training? Are there techniques (e.g., length normalization, per-token averaging, penalties) compatible with Eq. (8) to reduce verbosity bias without breaking the derivation?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — because DPO’s implicit reward is the sequence-level log-ratio
$\hat{r}(x,y) = \beta \log\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right]$, it can inherit any length/verbosity biases present in the sequence probabilities. That does not mean DPO always favors longer outputs, but if longer outputs systematically get higher log-prob mass under the learned ordering (or if human labels reward verbosity), DPO will push towards that. You can mitigate this, but most simple “per-token averaging” or per-example re-scaling schemes are not algebraically neutral to the DPO derivation and will change the theoretical object you are fitting.

Why the concern arises (intuitively and technically)
DPO turns a Bradley–Terry preference model into a loss on policies via the identity $r(x,y) \simeq \beta \log\left[\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right]$ (up to an x-only constant that cancels when taking differences). Because $\pi(y|x)$ is a sequence probability (product of token probs), its log scales roughly with sequence length. If the reference model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the current policy $\pi_\theta$ assign probabilities in a way that correlates with length (for example, the policy assigns relatively higher probability mass to longer sequences than the ref does, or human labels tend to prefer longer answers), then the log-ratio will tend to be larger for longer sequences and DPO’s gradients will push the policy in that direction. The DPO loss in Eq. (8) is sequence-level (it uses the whole-sequence log-probabilities), so length effects are not automatically normalized away.

What you can do without “breaking” DPO’s derivation
There are techniques that preserve the intended modeling assumptions of DPO (i.e., staying within the representation $r = \beta \log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)$ up to x-only constants) and therefore do not invalidate the derivation or its guarantees.

1) Encode desired length bias in the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Because the implicit reward is a contrast between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, changing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a legitimate and derivation-compatible way to change what counts as “reward.” If you want the learned policy not to prefer verbose outputs, make $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ include a length prior that down-weights long completions (e.g., train $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ on preferred-length data, or multiply $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)$ by an explicit length penalty term and renormalize). This is equivalent to changing the reference baseline in the KL-constrained objective and preserves the DPO reparameterization.

2) Collect preferences that penalize verbosity / use labeling instructions. The Bradley–Terry model links preferences to reward differences; if human labels explicitly penalize verbosity (or you instruct raters to prefer concise answers when quality ties), the preference dataset will reflect that and DPO will learn the corresponding implicit reward. The paper mentions adjusting GPT‑4 evaluation prompts to penalize verbosity — do the analogous thing at data collection time.

3) Use length-matched (or length-balanced) preference pairs. If your training comparisons strongly confound quality and length (e.g., the preferred example is almost always longer), the model will learn that length is a proxy for quality. Sampling or filtering pairs so that comparisons do not systematically differ in length reduces that confound while leaving the DPO objective intact.

4) Add explicit input features to the policy that encode length preferences. You can condition $\pi_\theta$ or $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ on an auxiliary “desired length” signal or include length as a context token. That changes the model class but still keeps the form $r \propto \log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)$.

What will break the derivation (and why)
Any modification that replaces the sequence-level log-ratio in Eq. (8) with a per-example operation that cannot be written as $\beta \log\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right]$ plus an x-only term will break the neat change-of-variables used to derive DPO and therefore loses the theoretical equivalence to fitting a reward in the Bradley–Terry family.

Examples of such “breaking” changes:

- Per-token averaging: replacing the sequence log-ratio by $\frac{1}{|y|}\log\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right]$ amounts to using a per-example scaling factor $1/|y|$ that depends on $y$. That cannot be absorbed into a constant $f(x)$ (which must depend only on $x$), so the reparameterization theorems no longer apply exactly. You’ll be optimizing a different objective and lose the guarantees that DPO gives.

- A per-example temperature $\beta(y)$ or other $y$-dependent reweighting similarly changes the family of representable rewards to something else. The DPO derivation assumes a fixed $\beta$ and the specific exponential family relation in Eq. (3).

That said, in practice people sometimes apply such hacks and they may help empirically — but you should treat them as heuristics, not as preserving the theoretical mapping DPO relies on.

Practical recommendations
If verbosity is a problem in your setting, I suggest the following, in order:

1. First prefer data-level and reference-policy fixes: instruct labelers (or the automatic annotator) to penalize verbosity and set $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to reflect a desired length prior (e.g., by fine-tuning $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ on length-constrained preferred completions or adding an explicit length penalty into $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$). These keep you inside the DPO framework and preserve its theoretical properties.

2. If you cannot recollect labels, filter or reweight training pairs so that length is not systematically correlated with the preferred sample (length-matched pairs or importance weights).

3. If necessary, experiment with heuristic normalizations (per-token averaging or dividing the log-ratio by $|y|$) but treat them as empirical modifications: they can reduce verbosity but they invalidate the strict DPO derivation and its guarantees. If you go this route, validate carefully (e.g., via held-out human judgments) and be aware you’re optimizing a different objective.

4. Finally, also address evaluation: as the paper shows, evaluation prompts can strongly affect measured win rates for verbosity. Make sure your automatic or human evaluation explicitly penalizes verbosity if that is a desired property.

Short conclusion
DPO does not magically remove length bias: because it uses sequence-level log-probability ratios as the implicit reward, verbosity effects in $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, $\pi_\theta$, or the preference labels will drive behavior. The principled ways to reduce verbosity without “breaking” the DPO theory are to change $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to encode a length preference, collect preferences that penalize verbosity, or deconfound length in the training pairs. Per-token averaging or per-example rescaling will alter the theoretical setup and should be used only as empirical heuristics with careful validation.
