\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does DPO truly enforce a KL constraint, or only encourage it implicitly?
Question:
The RL objective in Eq. (3) includes a KL penalty term with coefficient \beta, and the closed-form optimal policy has a log-density ratio proportional to the reward. In DPO, we optimize a logistic preference loss over log-density ratios without explicitly regularizing KL during training. Does minimizing Eq. (8) guarantee that the learned \pi_\theta will have a controlled KL to \pi_\text{ref}, or do we just hope that the learned ratios that explain preferences stay within a reasonable range? If we want a specific KL target (say, expected sequence-level KL \approx 6), how do we ensure DPO hits it?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO does not impose a hard, explicit KL constraint during parameter updates, but it does implicitly optimize the same KL-regularized objective as RLHF. The scalar $\beta$ in the DPO loss plays the role of the KL-coefficient from the RL objective, so changing $\beta$ moves the learned policy along the reward–KL frontier — but there is no closed‑form guarantee that a single choice of $\beta$ will produce an exact expected-sequence KL (e.g. $\approx 6$). In practice you must tune $\beta$ (or use a dual / search procedure) and measure KL on validation prompts to hit a specific target.

Why this is the case (intuitive + technical): the paper shows that the optimal policy for the RLHF objective
$\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$
has the closed form $\pi_{r}(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\big(r(y,x)/\beta\big)$. Re‑parameterizing rewards by the policy (Eq. (6)/(7) in the paper) gives the DPO likelihood
$L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}) = -\mathbb{E}_{\text{data}} \log \sigma\!\big(\beta\,(\log(\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{ref}}))_w - \beta\,(\log(\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{ref}}))_l\big)$,
so DPO directly fits a policy that is consistent with some reward in the equivalence class of RLHF rewards. Thus DPO is optimizing under the same model class and $\beta$ has the same interpretation as the KL regularizer in the original RL objective.

Why this is not the same as a hard constraint: although the reparameterization ties the implicit reward to the log density ratio $\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{ref}})$, the training loss is a classification (cross‑entropy) objective on pairs. There is no per‑update constraint enforcing $ \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})] \le K$. Equivalently, DPO makes the policy parametrize the reward class that would be optimal if you had solved the KL‑regularized RL problem, but DPO training itself does not enforce that the resulting expected KL equals any pre‑specified value. In other words, $\beta$ controls the strength of the implicit KL regularization, but the mapping $\beta \mapsto$ actual achieved expected KL depends on model capacity, optimization dynamics, initialization ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$), dataset of preferences, early stopping, and sampling-temperature at eval.

How to get an expected sequence-level KL $\approx 6$ in practice: treat $\beta$ as a controllable hyperparameter and tune it to hit your target KL on a held‑out validation set (the paper does exactly this when plotting reward–KL frontiers). A practical recipe:

1. Pick a validation set of prompts representative of deployment.
2. Run short DPO fine‑tuning jobs at a few $\beta$ values spanning the plausible range (e.g. log‑spaced). For each run measure the sequence-level KL $\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\text{val}}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big]$.
3. Fit a monotonic mapping $\beta \mapsto \mathrm{KL}$ (often smooth) and either interpolate or run a binary search / line search on $\beta$ to reach KL $\approx 6$.
4. Use early stopping and seed averaging to reduce variance in the measured KL.
If you need more precise control, use an outer loop that adjusts $\beta$ during training (a Lagrangian / dual approach): treat $\beta$ as the Lagrange multiplier and adapt $\beta$ up/down depending on whether the current expected KL is below/above the target. This mirrors how constrained optimization is often solved in practice. The paper does not present that variant, but the theoretical equivalence justifies using $\beta$ as the multiplier.

Other knobs and cautions: the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ matters — if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is mismatched to how the preference data were collected you can bias KL measurements (the paper recommends initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ or fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on preferred completions). Also KL is sequence‑level (sum of per‑token KLs) and depends on sampling temperature used at evaluation; compute the KL under the same decoding you will use in deployment. Finally, because DPO is a likelihood objective rather than an explicit constrained optimizer, there will be some variance and no strict guarantee of hitting the exact numeric KL target without search or an adaptive $\beta$ schedule.

Bottom line: $\beta$ in DPO plays the same role as the KL penalty in Eq. (3) and controls how close $\pi_{\theta}$ stays to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, but DPO does not mathematically enforce a precise KL value during training. To reach a target expected KL (e.g. $\approx 6$), tune $\beta$ (or adapt it with a Lagrangian outer loop) and monitor KL on validation prompts; this is the practical approach used in the paper to produce the reward–KL frontiers.
