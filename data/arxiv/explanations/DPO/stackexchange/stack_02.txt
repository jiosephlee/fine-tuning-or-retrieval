### What assumptions are needed for Theorem 1 about representing any reward class as r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that, under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with Plackett–Luce/Bradley–Terry can be represented via r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} for some policy \pi. What exactly are the "mild assumptions"? Do we need \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x) > 0 for all y (full support), or any regularity on the function class of \pi? Also, how does this depend on whether y is a full sequence vs a token, i.e., does the result implicitly require absolute continuity between \pi and \pi_\text{ref} at the sequence level?
Answer:
Short answer: the “mild assumptions” in Theorem 1 are exactly what you need to make the partition function and the reparameterization well-defined and to avoid zero-probability degeneracies. Concretely, the proof requires (1) $\beta > 0$ finite and the partition sum $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\big( r(x,y)/\beta \big)$ to be finite and positive for every $x$, and (2) $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to assign positive mass to every $y$ you care to represent (i.e.\ $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) > 0$ for all $y$ in the support of the reward class). There is no intrinsic parametric regularity requirement on the candidate $\pi$ in the theorem (the result is existence/representation), but in practice your model class must be expressive enough to approximate the $\pi$ that the theorem guarantees.

More intuition and implications. The constructive proof in the paper starts from an arbitrary reward $r(x,y)$ and forms the optimal policy
$\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \exp\!\big( r(x,y)/\beta \big)$.
Taking logs and rearranging gives the projection
$\tilde{r}(x,y) = \beta \log\!\big( \pi_r(y\mid x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \big)$,
which lies in the same Plackett–Luce / Bradley–Terry equivalence class as $r$ (they differ only by an $x$-dependent constant, which cancels in pairwise comparisons). This argument only works when the normalization/partition function $Z(x)$ exists and is finite, and when multiplying by $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ doesn’t force impossible zeros. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) = 0$ for some $y$ but $\exp\!\big( r(x,y)/\beta \big)$ is finite, then $\pi_r(y\mid x)$ will be zero and you cannot recover a positive-probability $\pi$ that would represent that reward using the stated formula. In other words, you need absolute continuity of $\pi_r$ with respect to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ($\pi_r \ll \pi_{\text{ref}}$) at the level you’re representing $y$; otherwise the log-ratio is undefined (or $-\infty$) and you’ve lost representational power.

Sequence vs token-level $y$. The theorem is stated at the distribution-over-completions level, so the requirement is absolute continuity at the sequence (full-completion) level: for each prompt $x$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ must give positive probability to every full sequence $y$ that the reward class or optimal policy assigns positive probability to. If your models are autoregressive token models, a sufficient and common way to ensure this is to require token-level support: $\pi_{\text{ref}}(\text{token}_t \mid \text{prefix}) > 0$ for every token that can appear. Token-level full support implies full-sequence full support (product of positive numbers is positive). Conversely, if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ has zero probability for some token at some prefix, that removes entire branches of sequences from representability.

No hidden function-class assumption in the theorem. Theorem 1 is an existence/representation statement: for every reward class (under the mild assumptions above) there exists a $\pi$ such that $r = \beta \log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ up to the usual $x$-dependent additive constant. The paper does not assume a particular parametric family for $\pi$. In practice, however, to realize this result you must be able to parametrize and learn a $\pi$ that approximates the constructive $\pi_r$; so practical success depends on your model class and optimization.

Practical takeaways. (i) Use a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with (approximate) full support over completions you might want to promote — SFT models initialized from data usually satisfy this, or you can add small smoothing. (ii) Ensure the partition sum $Z(x)$ is finite (this is automatic for finite action sets and for reasonable real-valued rewards; for unbounded rewards one must check growth conditions). (iii) Remember the equivalence ambiguity: reward functions are only identified up to adding functions of $x$; the DPO reparameterization picks a canonical representative by enforcing the normalization in Eq. (6) of the paper.

If you need a tighter statement for your setting: require $\beta \in (0,\infty)$, for each prompt $x$ the set of $y$ is countable (or a measure-space where the integral defining $Z(x)$ converges), and $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) > 0$ for every $y$ with finite $\exp\!\big( r(x,y)/\beta \big)$. Under those conditions the theorem’s construction is valid and no further regularity assumptions on $\pi$ are needed for the theoretical representation result.
