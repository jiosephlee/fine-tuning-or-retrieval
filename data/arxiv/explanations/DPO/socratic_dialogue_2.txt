\title{Socratic Dialogue #2 for the paper: DPO}

Tutor: Before we dive into Direct Preference Optimization, can you summarize what you already know about RLHF and what confuses you about DPO?

Student: I know the standard pipeline: you start with a language model trained on general text, do supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations, then collect human preferences on model outputs, train a reward model on those preferences, and finally use reinforcement learning (often PPO) to fine-tune the model to maximize the reward with a KL penalty to stay close to the SFT model. What confuses me is how DPO claims to avoid both reward modeling and RL while still optimizing the same objective. And the title—“Your language model is secretly a reward model”—I don’t fully get that.

Tutor: Great starting point. Let’s build up from the RLHF pipeline and see where DPO fits. Can you recall the role of the KL penalty in PPO-based RLHF?

Student: Yes, during RL optimization, they maximize expected learned reward while penalizing deviation from the reference policy (usually the SFT model) with a KL term, controlled by a parameter $\beta$. It prevents the policy from drifting too far, which helps stability and keeps generations reasonable, and also keeps the reward model in-distribution.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, what’s the standard preference modeling assumption used when fitting the reward model?

Student: The Bradley–Terry model, I think. It says the probability that $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ given $x$ is $\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. So preferences depend only on reward differences.

Tutor: Perfect. That “only differences matter” point is crucial. It implies that if you add any function of $x$ alone to $r(x,y)$, the preference probabilities stay the same. Now, the RL objective being optimized in PPO-based RLHF is expected reward minus $\beta$ times KL from the reference. Do you know the form of the optimal policy for that KL-constrained reward maximization?

Student: I’ve seen the formula that the optimal policy is proportional to the reference policy times $\exp(r/\beta)$: $\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$. There’s a normalization constant over $y$ for each $x$.

Tutor: Right. That normalization is the partition function $Z(x)$. Now, here’s a key mathematical trick that DPO exploits: if $\pi^*(y\mid x)$ is proportional to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ times $\exp(r/\beta)$, then we can rewrite $r$ in terms of $\pi^*$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Can you see how?

Student: If $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$, then $\log\pi^*(y\mid x)=\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)+\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}-\log Z(x)$. Rearranging, $r(x,y)=\beta\big(\log\pi^*(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\big)+\beta\log Z(x)$. So reward equals $\beta$ times the log-probability ratio plus a constant that depends only on $x$.

Tutor: Excellent. Now, recall that the Bradley–Terry preference probability depends only on the difference $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. If you plug the expression for $r$ above into that difference, what happens to the partition function term?

Student: It cancels out because it’s $\beta\log Z(x)$ subtracted by itself.

Tutor: Exactly. So the preference model can be written directly in terms of $\pi^*$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, without any explicit reward function or partition function. Concretely, the probability that $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$ given $x$ becomes
$\sigma\big(\beta[\log\pi^*(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)]-\beta[\log\pi^*(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)]\big)$. Do you see why that’s powerful?

Student: Because it means we can fit the optimal policy $\pi^*$ directly by maximizing the likelihood of the human preferences, using a simple binary cross-entropy loss over those log-prob ratios. No need to fit a separate reward model and then run RL.

Tutor: That’s the essence of DPO. It re-parameterizes the reward-learning objective as a policy-learning objective. What is the loss function then?

Student: It’s the expectation over preference triplets $(x,y_w,y_l)$ of
$-\log\sigma\big(\beta\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)\right]-\beta\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)\right]\big)$.
So we take the policy we’re training, compute log-probs for the preferred and dispreferred outputs, subtract the reference log-probs, scale by $\beta$, and apply a logistic loss.

Tutor: Well stated. Notice how the reference model appears explicitly in the loss. Why do you think the reference matters?

Student: The reference defines the anchor in the KL-regularized RL objective. Without it, the mapping between rewards and policies isn’t properly anchored; plus, the KL constraint is what kept PPO stable. Here, the reference plays the same role but in a simple loss, and it ties the policy updates to staying close to the SFT distribution.

Tutor: Right. Now, let’s examine the gradient of that loss to get intuition. If we differentiate with respect to $\theta$, the parameters of $\pi_\theta$, we get a term that increases log-prob of $y_w$ and decreases log-prob of $y_l$, but there’s a weight. Do you remember what that weight looks like?

Student: The weight is $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))$, where the implicit reward is $\hat r(y)=\beta\left[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\right]$. So when the model currently ranks $y_l$ higher than $y_w$ (which is wrong), that sigmoid weight is large and the update is strong; when the model already ranks $y_w$ higher, the weight is small, and the update is gentle.

Tutor: Great grasp. That dynamic weighting is essential: it prevents the model from over-optimizing the likelihood ratio when it already agrees with the preference, which can help avoid degeneration you might see in simple unlikelihood training. Why might naïve unlikelihood cause degeneration?

Student: Because unlikelihood just pushes down on the dispreferred $y_l$ regardless of context or how well the model already distinguishes the pair. It doesn’t calibrate by an implicit reward difference or account for the KL constraint, so it can push too hard, reduce diversity, or cause other undesirable shifts.

Tutor: Exactly. DPO’s weighting implicitly encodes the KL strength via $\beta$ and the current gap in implicit rewards. Now, you asked about “Your language model is secretly a reward model.” Based on what we’ve discussed, can you guess what they mean?

Student: I think they mean that the trained policy $\pi_\theta$, relative to the reference policy, defines an implicit reward function $\hat r(x,y)=\beta\left[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\right]$. And because the Bradley–Terry model only cares about differences in rewards, this $\hat r$ is functionally equivalent, for preferences, to any reward function in the same equivalence class.

Tutor: Precisely. Two reward functions that differ by a function of $x$ alone induce the same preference probabilities. That’s an equivalence relation on reward functions. Moreover, any such equivalent rewards also induce the same optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective. So DPO effectively picks a canonical representative of each equivalence class: the one whose partition function normalization is absorbed by re-expressing rewards as a log-probability ratio with respect to the reference.

Student: So the theorem is basically that for any reward function consistent with the Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) preference data, there exists a policy $\pi$ such that $\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ is in the same equivalence class as that reward. Therefore, learning $\pi$ via DPO is not losing expressivity relative to learning an explicit $r$ first.

Tutor: Exactly. The paper formalizes this with a projection operator that subtracts the log partition term so that the reparameterized reward becomes $\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. This selection fixes one representative in the equivalence class—the one for which the induced policy is already normalized. Because preferences only identify a reward up to that class, DPO is as expressive for preferences as explicit reward models, but with a direct route to the optimal policy.

Student: That clarifies the title! The policy embodies a reward model, via its log-prob ratios against the reference.

Tutor: Great. Now let’s contrast this with PPO-based RLHF. Can you describe one major source of instability in PPO training on language models?

Student: It’s often variance from sampling on-policy and from value estimation. You’re using a learned reward model and an actor-critic method; training must estimate baselines or values to reduce variance, and the reward scale can drift, leading to tricky credit assignment and unstable updates. Also, PPO can be sensitive to hyperparameters like KL target and clipping.

Tutor: Correct. The paper reframes the PPO objective via the control-as-inference perspective. The normalization term we talked about—the log partition function—acts like a soft value function; failing to account for it raises gradient variance. PPO uses explicit variance reduction: learned value functions, reward normalization, human baseline completions, etc. DPO bypasses all of this by switching variables: it uses a classification objective that implicitly handles the normalization through the policy-reference log-prob ratios and requires no on-policy sampling or value learning.

Student: So DPO avoids sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during training, needs no critic or value function, and still optimizes the same underlying KL-regularized objective thanks to the reparameterization. That’s a compelling simplification.

Tutor: Exactly. Now let’s get practical. Suppose you have a dataset of preferences, i.e., for each prompt $x$, two completions $y_w$ (winner) and $y_l$ (loser), and you have a reference model—ideally the SFT model used to generate those $y$’s originally. How would you set up DPO training?

Student: I’d freeze the reference model. For each preference triple $(x,y_w,y_l)$, I’d compute log-probabilities under $\pi_\theta$ and under $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for $y_w$ and $y_l$. Then, I’d compute the log-probability ratio difference:
$\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)\right]-\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)\right]$.
Multiply by $\beta$, pass through the logistic loss, and average over the batch. Then backprop into $\pi_\theta$ only. Iterate over the dataset.

Tutor: Yes. Two implementation nuances often come up:

- Sequence vs token scoring: Typically, you use the sum of token log-probs for the completion (optionally normalized by length). Consistency with how KL is computed matters if you compare to PPO metrics.

- Reference mismatch: If the dataset was generated by some SFT you no longer have, you can train a reference by supervised fine-tuning on just the preferred completions, to approximate the original distribution. That reduces distribution shift in the denominator.

Student: And $\beta$ controls how strongly we penalize moving away from the reference since it scales the log-ratio. High $\beta$ means more conservative updates (stronger KL penalty), while low $\beta$ lets the policy deviate more aggressively. Does DPO choose $\beta$ to target a particular KL?

Tutor: In principle, yes—$\beta$ sets the trade-off. In practice, you often sweep a small set of $\beta$ values and pick based on validation metrics or reward-KL frontier, just like you’d sweep PPO KL targets. A nice property reported in the paper is that DPO is less sensitive to hyperparameters than PPO, so modest tuning can suffice.

Student: What about degenerate behaviors? The paper mentions that a naive probability ratio objective without the dynamic weighting can lead to degeneration. How exactly does the DPO weighting prevent that?

Tutor: The logistic weight $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))$ gives high weight only when the model is mistaken (assigns higher implicit reward to $y_l$). If the model already ranks correctly with a large margin, the weight is low; it won’t keep pushing the ratio indefinitely. This is akin to large-margin classification where confident correct predictions contribute less. In contrast, unlikelihood training keeps pushing down $y_l$ no matter what, potentially collapsing diversity or overfitting to the specific negative example.

Student: That makes sense. Does DPO need a balanced dataset of pairs? What if the preferences are noisy?

Tutor: The Bradley–Terry model is a probabilistic model of noisy choices, so DPO naturally accommodates noise. If the data is contradictory, it will settle on a policy that best fits the overall pairwise likelihood. As always, data quality matters, but the logistic regression-like nature is robust to reasonable levels of noise.

Student: How does DPO compare to PPO in experiments?

Tutor: The paper reports several settings:

1) Controlled sentiment generation on IMDb prefixes. Here the ground-truth reward is a sentiment classifier. They compare algorithms by the reward–KL frontier: average reward vs average sequence-level KL from the reference. DPO produces the most efficient frontier, outperforming PPO and even a PPO variant using the true reward (PPO-GT). That’s striking: although they optimize the same KL-constrained objective, DPO reaches better reward for the same KL. It highlights DPO’s stability and efficiency versus PPO’s variance and sensitivity.

2) Summarization on Reddit TL;DR using a GPT-J SFT. They evaluate with GPT-4 as a judge and validate GPT-4 against humans in a separate study. DPO outperforms PPO across sampling temperatures, achieving about 61% win rate at temperature 0, versus ~57% for PPO. DPO is more robust to temperature, while PPO’s performance degrades quickly at higher temperatures. DPO also matches or exceeds Best-of-N using a learned reward model, without the test-time sampling cost.

3) Single-turn dialogue on Anthropic Helpful-Harmless (HH) with Pythia-2.8B. There’s no SFT, so they trained a reference via supervised fine-tuning on preferred completions (Preferred-FT). DPO improves over Preferred-FT and reaches performance comparable to Best-of-128 sampling (which is computationally expensive), and better than a PPO baseline they tried from a public recipe.

Student: How did they justify using GPT-4 as an evaluator?

Tutor: They ran a human study comparing GPT-4 judgments under two prompts (one simple, one that also emphasized conciseness) with human preferences. Human agreement with GPT-4 was similar to human-human agreement. They settled on the “concise” GPT-4 prompt because it correlated better with humans.

Student: Interesting. What about generalization? If the policy learned on Reddit summaries is applied to news articles, what happens?

Tutor: They tested out-of-distribution generalization on CNN/DailyMail. DPO continued to outperform PPO significantly when evaluated by GPT-4 against ground-truth news summaries. This suggests the DPO policy generalizes at least as well as, possibly better than, PPO-trained policies, despite DPO not using extra unlabeled RL prompts.

Student: I see. So what are the theoretical guarantees? Besides the equivalence of rewards up to an $x$-only function, what else do they claim?

Tutor: They formalize:

- Under Bradley–Terry or more general Plackett–Luce models, rewards are identifiable only up to adding $f(x)$. All rewards in an equivalence class induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective.

- The reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$ implies that the class of reward functions consistent with preferences can be represented by $r'(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, i.e., by choosing the canonical representative where the partition function is absorbed into the policy normalization. In other words, you can represent any class via some policy $\pi$; you don’t lose generality.

- Consequently, fitting the Bradley–Terry model directly in terms of $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (the DPO loss) is maximum likelihood under this reparameterization.

Student: And the control-as-inference interpretation shows that PPO is trying to minimize KL between $\pi_\theta$ and the optimal $\pi^*$, but it needs a baseline/value to handle the normalization term, whereas DPO bakes it in via the reparameterization. That explains the stability benefit.

Tutor: Nicely summarized. Shall we talk about practical tips and pitfalls if you were to implement DPO?

Student: Yes, please.

Tutor: Key recommendations:

- Reference model: Use the exact SFT model that generated the preference pairs if possible. If not, fit a reference by supervised training on preferred completions to mimic the original distribution. Freezing the reference is standard.

- $\beta$ selection: $\beta$ controls how conservative the policy is. Larger $\beta$ increases the KL penalty and keeps the policy closer to the reference (more conservative); smaller $\beta$ reduces the penalty and allows larger deviations (more aggressive movement). Sweep a small grid; DPO is typically not too sensitive.

- Length handling: Compute sequence log-prob sums consistently for both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. If you normalize by length, do it consistently. Beware that normalizing can change the KL frontier; pick and stick with one.

- Batching and stability: Use mixed-precision carefully for stable log-prob ratios. Because you’re subtracting log-probs, numerical stability matters.

- Tokenization and prompts: Make sure prompts $x$ are identical when computing $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ log-probs. Minor prompt mismatches can cause large log-ratio noise.

- Dataset reuse: DPO uses fixed offline preference data—no need for on-policy rollouts. That makes it cheaper and easier to reproduce.

- Monitoring: Track average KL to reference (sequence-level) on a held-out set, as well as reward proxies (if any) and validation win rates. You want to see a reasonable increase in KL and improvement in win rate.

Student: Are there cases where DPO might struggle?

Tutor: A few limitations and open questions:

- Out-of-distribution shifts: While early results are promising, more systematic study is needed. If your available preferences cover a narrow region, both PPO and DPO can misgeneralize.

- Overoptimization and reward hacking: Even though DPO doesn’t explicitly optimize a learned reward model, it still fits to noisy human preferences. If preferences contain systematic bias, the model will learn it.

- Multi-objective or richer structure: Bradley–Terry handles pairwise choices. If you have listwise rankings or more complex preference signals, DPO can be extended to Plackett–Luce, but the details matter.

- $\beta$ tuning and calibration: Although DPO is more robust than PPO, $\beta$ still matters for the reward–KL trade-off.

- Reference mismatch: If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ poorly matches the original sampling distribution for the pairs, the log-ratio may be biased. The “Preferred-FT as reference” trick helps but is not perfect.

- Large-scale scaling: They showed up to around 6B parameters. Scaling to frontier models likely works but may bring engineering considerations.

Student: In practice, I’ve seen people use Best-of-N sampling with a reward model at inference time to boost quality. How does that compare to DPO?

Tutor: Best-of-N can be strong but is computationally expensive, especially for large $N$, because you sample many completions and pick the best according to a reward model. In experiments, DPO achieves similar or better win rates than Best-of-N at moderate $N$ without test-time cost. Plus, Best-of-N relies on the quality of the reward model at inference; DPO avoids that by folding preferences directly into the policy.

Student: Got it. How does DPO ensure diversity, like not collapsing to a single mode? Is that mostly the KL effect?

Tutor: Yes, the KL constraint implicit in the log-ratio keeps the policy close to the reference distribution, which is generally diverse. Also, the weighting diminishes pressure on examples the model already gets right, reducing the tendency to push too hard on specific exemplars. In practice, DPO models tend to retain or even improve diversity relative to unlikelihood baselines.

Student: Does DPO need any special tricks like reward normalization, as PPO does?

Tutor: Not usually. PPO often needs reward normalization or a baseline to stabilize gradients. DPO’s loss is a straightforward binary cross-entropy on a log-ratio margin; there’s no learned critic, no baseline, and no on-policy sampling. That’s part of why it’s simpler and more stable.

Student: The paper mentions that DPO optimizes the same objective as PPO—reward maximization with a KL constraint—but does so exactly via classification. Is that precise? Aren’t there assumptions?

Tutor: Good question. The precision is: under the Bradley–Terry (or Plackett–Luce) preference model, and the KL-regularized setup where the optimal policy has the exponential tilt form $\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$, rewriting the preference likelihood in terms of $\pi^*$ yields the DPO objective. So maximizing the DPO likelihood is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation of the latent reward up to an $x$-only function, and simultaneously recovering the corresponding optimal policy. In that sense, it is “optimizing the RLHF objective exactly” under those modeling assumptions, and it avoids estimating the partition function.

Student: That feels satisfying. Could we walk through a concrete mini-example to see how the update behaves?

Tutor: Sure. Suppose for a given prompt $x$, the reference gives higher probability to $y_l$ than to $y_w$: say $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x) = -5$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x) = -3$. Your current policy assigns $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) = -4$ and $\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) = -2$, so it also prefers $y_l$. The log-ratio difference is
$$
\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)\big]-\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)\big] = (-4-(-5))-(-2-(-3)) = 0.
$$
With $\beta=1$, the logistic loss sees $\sigma(0)=0.5$. The gradient pushes to increase $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and decrease $\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ symmetrically. Now, after a few updates, suppose $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) = -2.5$ and $\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) = -3.5$, so the model now prefers $y_w$ by a margin. The log-ratio difference becomes
$$
\big[-2.5-(-5)\big]-\big[-3.5-(-3)\big]=(2.5)-(-0.5)=3.
$$
Now $\sigma(-3)$ is small (about $0.047$), so the update weight is small; the model stops pushing hard. This shows how DPO quickly corrects violations and then stabilizes.

Student: Nice. How do they measure KL in experiments?

Tutor: They report sequence-level KL between the trained policy and the reference, computed as the sum of per-token KL terms along generated sequences. This aligns with the KL penalty in RLHF. On the sentiment task, they plot achieved reward vs that KL to produce a frontier.

Student: About temperatures during sampling: DPO was more robust than PPO to higher temperatures?

Tutor: Yes. In summarization experiments, DPO maintained higher win rates across a range of temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance dropped as temperature increased. That suggests DPO retained better calibration and quality across sampling settings.

Student: Given this, when would you still choose PPO over DPO?

Tutor: A few scenarios:

- If you need to incorporate environment dynamics or long-horizon credit assignment beyond single-turn completions, PPO or other RL methods may be necessary. DPO is naturally suited to bandit-style, single-response preferences.

- If you must exploit a learned reward in contexts where online exploration matters (e.g., active data collection), PPO provides a direct route to learning from that reward. Though note that DPO can also be used in active settings by regenerating preference pairs.

- If your preference signal cannot be well-modeled by pairwise comparisons or the Plackett–Luce family, you may need a different objective. DPO can be extended, but PPO with a custom reward might be more straightforward there.

Student: Are there extensions of DPO to listwise rankings?

Tutor: Yes, the same change-of-variables idea applies to Plackett–Luce models for listwise rankings. The loss becomes a cross-entropy over permutations expressed in terms of $\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The algebra is a bit more involved, but the core insight—cancelling the partition function and optimizing the policy directly—remains.

Student: Are there any preconditions for DPO to work well?

Tutor: The main ones:

- A reasonably good reference model so the KL-relative objective makes sense and the dataset pairs are in-distribution.

- A preference dataset representative of your target behavior. If preferences are inconsistent or biased, the learned policy inherits that.

- Care with numerical stability in log-prob computations and sequence formatting.

Beyond that, it’s remarkably straightforward compared to PPO.

Student: How big are the computational savings?

Tutor: Significant. You don’t need to sample from the model in the training loop, don’t train a separate reward model, and don’t run RL rollouts or train a critic. It becomes a single-stage supervised optimization over static data. The paper also reports less hyperparameter tuning compared to PPO, contributing to efficiency.

Student: In the sentiment experiment, how did DPO beat PPO even with ground-truth reward?

Tutor: Likely due to optimization stability. PPO must balance policy updates with value estimation, reward normalization, and precise control of KL divergence via clipping or KL penalties. Even with perfect reward, optimization can be suboptimal. DPO directly optimizes a convex-like classification loss (in terms of the margin), which is easier to optimize reliably. So practically, DPO can reach better points on the reward–KL frontier.

Student: This is convincing. If I wanted to explain DPO in one sentence to a colleague, what would you suggest?

Tutor: Try: DPO replaces the two-stage “learn a reward model, then do RL” pipeline with a single-stage, RL-free objective that directly fits the policy to human preferences by maximizing a Bradley–Terry likelihood written in terms of policy-to-reference log-probability ratios—so your policy implicitly is the reward model.

Student: That’s crisp. Last question: could you recap the core mathematical steps that make DPO possible?

Tutor: Sure, step by step:

1) Start from the KL-regularized RL objective: maximize expected $r(x,y)$ under $\pi_\theta$ minus $\beta$ times $\operatorname{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$.

2) The optimal policy under this objective has the exponential tilt form: $\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$.

3) Invert that relation to express $r(x,y)$ as $\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$.

4) Plug this into the Bradley–Terry preference model, which only depends on differences $r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$. The partition function $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels.

5) The resulting preference likelihood depends only on the policy $\pi$ (relative to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$), not on an explicit reward. Maximize that likelihood with respect to $\pi_\theta$ to obtain the optimal policy directly.

6) Interpret $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$ as an implicit reward. Because BT only fixes rewards up to adding $f(x)$, any reward consistent with preferences is equivalent to one represented by that log-ratio; hence, no loss of expressivity.

Student: Thank you. This dialogue really clarified both the mechanics and the intuition. I feel comfortable explaining and implementing DPO now.

Tutor: Wonderful. As you implement, remember to mind the reference model, $\beta$ sweep, and numerical stability. And if you extend to listwise preferences or multi-turn settings, the same change-of-variables intuition remains a useful guide.
