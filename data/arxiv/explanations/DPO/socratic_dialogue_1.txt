\title{Socratic Dialogue #1 for the paper: DPO}

Tutor: Before we jump in, can you tell me what you already know about RLHF and what confuses you about Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)?

Student: I know RLHF usually means you first train a reward model from human preferences and then fine-tune the language model with PPO to maximize that reward while keeping it close to a reference model via a KL penalty. What confuses me is how DPO claims you can skip explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning, yet still optimize essentially the same objective. Also the tagline “Your language model is secretly a reward model” sounds clever, but I’m not sure what it really means.

Tutor: Great starting point. Let’s unpack the standard RLHF pipeline first, then we’ll see how DPO re-parameterizes it. Could you summarize the three phases of the classic pipeline?

Student: Sure:
- SFT: Supervised fine-tuning of a base LM on high-quality demonstrations to get an SFT model.
- Reward modeling: Sample pairs of responses from the SFT model for prompts, get human preferences over the pairs, and fit a reward model so that it predicts the preferred response under a Bradley-Terry-style likelihood.
- RL optimization: Use PPO (or a similar RL method) to optimize the policy for high reward while penalizing KL from the reference model, usually the SFT model.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, let’s zoom in on the Bradley-Terry (BT) part. What is the BT model assuming about human preferences?

Student: I think BT says the probability that response y1 is preferred to y2 given a prompt x equals exp(r*(x,y1)) divided by exp(r*(x,y1)) + exp(r*(x,y2)), where r* is the true (unknown) reward function humans use implicitly.

Tutor: Correct. So we fit a parametric model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to maximize the BT log-likelihood over preference pairs, essentially a binary cross-entropy classification between winners and losers. Then, traditionally, we do RL with a KL constraint:
- Maximize $\mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta\,\operatorname{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$,
- Where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is typically the SFT policy.

Why is that KL term important?

Student: As I understand it, it prevents the policy from drifting too far from where the reward model is accurate, avoids mode collapse, and helps maintain diversity by not collapsing to a few high-reward outputs.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, there’s a known form for the optimal policy of this KL-constrained RL objective for any fixed reward $r$. Do you recall it?

Student: Vaguely. The optimal $\pi_r$ is proportional to the reference policy times $\exp(r/\beta)$, normalized by a partition function $Z(x)$. So $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \dfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)}{Z(x)}$.

Tutor: Perfect. That tiny equation is the key to DPO. If that’s the optimal policy for a reward $r$, then you can solve for $r$ in terms of the policy as:
$r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$.

The issue with using this relation directly in RL is that $Z(x)$ is intractable to compute. But watch what happens when we plug this into the BT likelihood, which only depends on reward differences between two responses $y_1$ and $y_2$.

Student: The $Z(x)$ terms cancel when you take $r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)$, right?

Tutor: Exactly. So the preference probability under BT can be rewritten purely in terms of the policy-to-reference ratios:
$P^*(y_1 \text{ preferred to } y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\right]\right)$.

This says: if we had the optimal policy $\pi^*$ for the RLHF objective, then it would make the BT preference probability above match human preferences.

Student: So DPO flips the usual sequence. Instead of fitting r and then doing RL to get pi, it directly fits pi so that these BT probabilities match the data. Is that right?

Tutor: Precisely. DPO defines a policy loss analogous to the BT reward-model loss:
$L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ \log \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$.

So we treat training $\pi_\theta$ as a simple binary classification over pairs—no PPO, no on-policy rollouts. Just compute log-probabilities of $y_w$ and $y_l$ under $\pi_\theta$ relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and push the ratio in the direction indicated by the preference.

Student: That’s clean. But how does that connect to the “language model is secretly a reward model” phrase?

Tutor: Notice the implied reward: define $\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. If you plug $\hat r_\theta$ into the BT likelihood, that’s exactly the inner expression inside the sigmoid. So by training the policy with DPO, you’re implicitly training a reward function—just encoded as the policy’s log-prob ratios against the reference. In that sense, the LM policy “is” the reward model, up to an $x$-dependent constant that doesn’t affect preferences.

Student: Ah, because the BT model only cares about differences, and adding any function f(x) to rewards doesn’t change the comparisons. That’s the identifiability issue in BT, right?

Tutor: Correct. Reward functions are only identified up to an additive function of $x$. Two rewards $r$ and $r'$ that differ by only $f(x)$ induce the same BT preference distribution and, importantly, the same optimal KL-constrained policy. DPO selects a canonical representative of each equivalence class: the one whose induced partition function makes the normalized policy exactly $\pi_\theta$. The paper formalizes this by showing any reward class consistent with BT can be represented as $r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ for some policy $\pi$. So the policy parameterization is as expressive (modulo that $f(x)$) as standard reward models, but gives you the policy directly.

Student: That’s elegant. So we’re not losing modeling power by skipping an explicit reward network? We just pick a canonical representative in each equivalent class that ties directly to the policy ratios.

Tutor: Exactly. Now, let’s build intuition for the DPO gradient. If you differentiate $L_{\text{DPO}}$ with respect to $\theta$, you get something like:
- Increase log-prob of the winner $y_w$ and decrease log-prob of the loser $y_l$,
- Weighted by a factor $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$, which is large when the model gets the preference wrong or is uncertain, and small when it already strongly prefers $y_w$.

Student: So it’s like a dynamic importance weight that focuses the update on the mistakes, scaled by beta. Why is that important?

Tutor: If you naïvely did “likely winners, unlikely losers” without this weight, you can push too aggressively and degenerate the model—over-penalizing nuanced cases, causing instability or collapse. The weighting tempers the update and bakes in the KL constraint strength via $\beta$. Empirically, the paper shows naïve unlikelihood methods can degrade generation quality, while DPO remains stable.

Student: I see. It’s like margin-aware reweighting: when the margin is small or negative, push hard; when it’s already large and correct, push lightly.

Tutor: Nicely put. Now, remember the RL objective required PPO or other actor-critic methods. Those methods can be unstable because you need to estimate a baseline or a value function to control variance of the policy gradient, especially due to that partition function-like normalization term. DPO sidesteps all of that by reparameterizing into a log-prob ratio classification problem in which the intractable normalizer cancels out in pairwise comparisons.

Student: Makes sense. Can we talk about the role of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ more? Is it always the SFT model?

Tutor: Ideally, yes. The preference data were generated by sampling from the SFT model, so $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ equals that SFT policy. Using the correct reference keeps the log-ratio meaningful and aligns with the region where the preference pairs came from. But sometimes you don’t have the exact SFT policy—say, the data are from a third party. In that case, the paper suggests approximating the reference by training a model on the winners only (a quick supervised fine-tune), to make $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ closer to the true distribution that produced the data. That mitigates distribution shift when you compute the ratios.

Student: And beta controls how conservative the policy should be relative to the reference, right?

Tutor: Yes—$\beta$ plays the same conceptual role as the KL penalty strength in PPO. Larger $\beta$ enforces more conservativeness (the optimal policy stays closer to the reference by downweighting $r/\beta$), while smaller $\beta$ allows more aggressive shifts away from the reference. In practice, DPO tends to require much less hyperparameter tuning than PPO: you pick a reasonable $\beta$ and train with standard supervised-learning infrastructure.

Student: Before we get to experiments, can we compare DPO to just training on winners (Preferred-FT) or to unlikelihood training?

Tutor: Sure:
- Preferred-FT only increases the likelihood of $y_w$. It ignores $y_l$, which means it may not separate close calls effectively; it also lacks a mechanism to control drift from the reference beyond standard regularizers.
- Unlikelihood adds a penalty to reduce probability of $y_l$. Without the DPO weighting, this can over-penalize and hurt fluency or diversity, as the model might learn to uniformly avoid certain patterns instead of learning fine-grained preferences.

DPO increases winner likelihood and decreases loser likelihood, but crucially with the calibrated, pairwise, ratio-based weighting grounded in the KL-constrained optimality conditions. That’s why it tends to be more stable and robust.

Student: Alright, experiments then. What did they test?

Tutor: Three settings:
1) Controlled sentiment generation: Input is a movie review prefix; outputs should be positive. Crucially, they can measure true reward using a sentiment classifier. This lets them plot reward versus KL divergence frontiers for different algorithms.
2) Summarization (Reddit TL;DR): Evaluate with GPT-4 as a judge to measure win rate against references, and validate GPT-4 against human raters.
3) Single-turn dialogue (Anthropic Helpful/Harmless): Again evaluate with GPT-4 win rates.

Student: And how did DPO do in the sentiment control?

Tutor: DPO produced the best reward-KL frontier—achieving higher reward at a given KL than PPO. Interestingly, it even outperformed PPO that was given the ground-truth reward (PPO-GT). This highlights both the stability and efficiency of DPO’s objective and the challenges PPO faces even with perfect reward. The likely reasons include variance in PPO updates, reliance on on-policy sampling, difficulty in tuning, and the importance of DPO’s built-in weighting.

Student: That’s striking—beating PPO with ground-truth reward. How about summarization?

Tutor: On TL;DR summarization, they fine-tuned GPT-J with DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT and evaluated at different sampling temperatures. DPO achieved about 61% win rate with GPT-4 judging at greedy decoding (temperature 0), exceeding PPO’s roughly 57% at its best temperature. DPO was also more robust across temperatures; PPO degraded sharply at higher temperatures. Best-of-N sampling with a learned reward was strong but computationally expensive at inference; DPO matched or exceeded it without that cost.

Student: Did they check that GPT-4 judging was reasonable?

Tutor: Yes. They ran a human study on a subset, with two GPT-4 prompts: a “simple” one and a “concise” one that also rewarded conciseness. Human–GPT-4 agreement was comparable to inter-human agreement, especially with the concise prompt, so they used that for the main evaluations. It’s an important step because evaluator prompts matter.

Student: And in dialogue?

Tutor: They used the Anthropic HH data: for the subset with a single human prompt and two model responses labeled by preference. Without an off-the-shelf SFT for that exact setup, they trained a Preferred-FT reference model and then DPO on top. DPO outperformed Preferred-FT and matched or beat a Best-of-128 baseline, which is expensive at inference. A public PPO-based model for that dataset underperformed substantially (at least under the prompts and settings they tried), suggesting DPO offers a more reliable path with less complexity. DPO also converged quickly.

Student: Did they test generalization out of distribution?

Tutor: Yes, they evaluated the TL;DR-trained policies on CNN/DailyMail news summarization, comparing win rates against the ground-truth summaries judged by GPT-4. DPO outperformed the PPO policy there as well. This suggests the DPO policy generalizes at least as well as PPO in this setting, despite not leveraging extra unlabeled prompts during training like PPO often does.

Student: So summarizing the empirical picture: DPO is simpler, avoids on-policy sampling, reaches better reward-KL trade-offs, performs as well or better than PPO on preference tasks, and generalizes comparably or better. What about training stability?

Tutor: Stability is a big part of the story. PPO involves several moving parts: advantage estimation, clipping, value function learning, subtle reward normalization, delicate rollout lengths and batch sizes. DPO turns the entire process into a single supervised classification loss over preference pairs. No sampling from the current policy in the training loop, no training a separate value network, no PPO clip hyperparameters. The per-example weighting in DPO substitutes for a learned baseline in a way, by focusing the gradient where the model’s implicit reward ranking is wrong.

Student: You mentioned an equivalence-class view of rewards and a theorem in the paper. Could you restate the core idea?

Tutor: Two rewards are equivalent if they differ by an $x$-only term $f(x)$. Under BT or Plackett-Luce models:
- They induce the same preference distribution.
- They yield the same optimal KL-constrained policy.

The paper proves that for any reward class consistent with BT, there exists a representative of the form $r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ for some policy $\pi$. In other words, the reparameterization covers all relevant reward classes. Moreover, the representative it picks is the one whose partition function integrates to 1 when exponentiated against the reference policy—i.e., it makes the corresponding optimal policy exactly equal to $\pi$.

Student: This ties back to “secretly a reward model”: the policy encodes r up to that f(x).

Tutor: Exactly. DPO exploits this to switch the optimization variable from $r$ to $\pi$, preserving the optimality structure but getting rid of expensive RL machinery.

Student: You also hinted at a connection between actor-critic instability and normalization terms. How does DPO sidestep that?

Tutor: In control-as-inference interpretations of RL with a KL penalty, the objective contains a normalization term—like a soft value function or log partition function. In PPO or actor-critic, you approximate or subtract something akin to this term to stabilize updates, often via a learned baseline. DPO’s change-of-variables and pairwise comparison cause that normalizer to cancel in the BT likelihood, so you never have to estimate it. That’s a major source of stability.

Student: Let’s talk practicalities. How do you compute those log-prob ratios in practice? Over tokens?

Tutor: Typically, sequence probability is the product over tokens, so log sequence prob is the sum of token log-probs. You compute $\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ at the sequence level (possibly normalized for length), take their difference to get the log-ratio, multiply by $\beta$, then form the pairwise difference (winner ratio minus loser ratio) and apply a sigmoid + cross-entropy loss. You can also apply truncation, EOS handling, or per-token masking as in standard LM training pipelines.

Student: And what if the preference dataset wasn’t collected from the same reference we’re using?

Tutor: That’s when it’s helpful to train a new $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by supervised fine-tuning on winners, to approximate the original sampling distribution. The closer $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is to the data generator, the more meaningful the log-ratio is. If you skip this adjustment, you can introduce bias or instability because you’re comparing probabilities under a mismatched reference.

Student: What about choosing beta?

Tutor: $\beta$ controls policy shift via the KL trade-off: larger $\beta$ makes updates more conservative (keeping the policy closer to the reference), smaller $\beta$ permits larger shifts. In experiments, DPO tends to be robust across a reasonable range, and it’s easier to tune than PPO’s multiple interacting hyperparameters. But it still matters: too large a $\beta$ can underfit and not improve much over the reference; too small a $\beta$ can overfit preferences and drift too far.

Student: What are the main caveats or limitations the paper mentions?

Tutor: A few:
- Preference models: DPO assumes BT or Plackett-Luce is a reasonable model for how preferences arise. If human judgments violate these assumptions systematically (e.g., context-dependent or intransitive preferences), that can limit fit.
- Reference mismatch: If you don’t have the true reference that generated the pairs, the log-ratio may be off. Fine-tuning a new reference on winners helps but isn’t perfect.
- Over-optimization: Even without explicit rewards, you can still over-optimize for the training preferences and degrade other qualities. The paper notes a slight late-stage performance drop in one setting that might be related.
- Scaling and settings: They tested up to 6B parameters and across several tasks, but not at the absolute frontier of model size or in complex multi-turn task setups; more study is needed.
- Evaluations: GPT-4 judging is strong but prompt-sensitive; they did human validation but there’s room to improve evaluator prompts and coverage.

Student: Does DPO extend to more than two responses, like listwise preferences?

Tutor: Yes. BT is a special case of Plackett-Luce (PL) for pairwise comparisons. The same reparameterization carries over to PL, where the preference likelihood is over rankings rather than pairs. The paper notes this extension and provides derivations in the appendix. In practice, many datasets provide pairwise preferences, but PL can be applied when you have full rankings.

Student: How does DPO compare to Best-of-N methods? Those can be strong, but expensive at inference.

Tutor: Best-of-N samples many completions and picks the one with highest reward under a learned reward model. It often yields strong performance but is impractical for large $N$ because your inference cost scales with $N$. DPO shifts the policy itself so that a single sample is already likely to be the best. In experiments, DPO matches or exceeds Best-of-N in several cases—without that inference-time sampling cost.

Student: I’m curious about why PPO with ground-truth reward didn’t beat DPO in the sentiment experiment. Isn’t that surprising?

Tutor: It is surprising at first glance. But remember, PPO still has to solve a tricky credit assignment and variance reduction problem with on-policy sampling, advantage estimation, clipping, and tuning. DPO’s objective is exactly the maximum-likelihood fit implied by the optimal policy structure and the BT model, and it avoids the variance issues. This reduces the gap between the “ideal” objective and what you actually optimize. So in practice, DPO can realize more of the theoretical optimum even when PPO has a perfect reward signal.

Student: If DPO is so straightforward, why do we need PPO at all anymore?

Tutor: There are still scenarios where reinforcement learning is advantageous:
- When you need to optimize non-preference signals like sparse environment rewards.
- When you want to incorporate long-horizon credit assignment beyond single-turn sequence-level feedback.
- When you want to fine-tune via on-policy exploration to discover novel behaviors not present in the preference dataset.

DPO shines for offline preference datasets where you want to align a model with human judgments using a simple, stable training loop.

Student: Does DPO have any analog to reward shaping or value baselines?

Tutor: The per-example weighting in DPO acts somewhat like a learned baseline because it reduces gradient magnitude when the model already orders $y_w$ above $y_l$ with high confidence. There’s no explicit value function to learn; the normalization that would be estimated by a baseline in actor-critic simply cancels out in the BT formulation. You can think of the reference model and $\beta$ as the main “shaping” knobs.

Student: Are there any implementation gotchas?

Tutor: A few practical tips:
- Use the exact same tokenizer and vocabulary for $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
- Ensure your log-prob computation matches the sampling scheme that produced $y$’s (e.g., teacher forcing over the full sequence).
- Consider sequence length normalization if you see length biases (e.g., divide log-prob by length).
- Start from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for $\pi_\theta$ to ensure smooth optimization.
- Monitor sequence-level KL $\operatorname{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ to ensure you don’t drift too far; adjust $\beta$ if needed.
- Be cautious with batch construction; randomize pairs to avoid correlated gradients.

Student: You mentioned monitoring KL—how does DPO control KL if it’s not explicitly in the loss?

Tutor: It’s implicit through the ratio structure and $\beta$. Larger $\beta$ constrains updates and typically yields smaller KL shifts; smaller $\beta$ permits larger shifts. Some practitioners also add an auxiliary penalty on KL if they want even stricter control, but in the paper’s experiments, the implicit control was sufficient.

Student: What about temperature at inference time? The experiments showed differences there.

Tutor: Yes, DPO often remains robust across a range of temperatures, whereas PPO performance sometimes collapses at higher temperatures. This likely reflects DPO’s smoother training signal and better-calibrated distribution over outputs. For deployment, choose a temperature that balances determinism and diversity, and validate with task-specific metrics or preference evaluations.

Student: Can DPO help with attributes like safety and harmlessness, where data can come from AI judgment (RLAIF)?

Tutor: Absolutely. The DPO pipeline is agnostic to whether preference labels come from humans or a rubric-guided LLM. It’s natural to apply DPO to RLAIF data to steer safety attributes. Because DPO training is cheap and stable, iterating over preference datasets becomes easier.

Tutor: Let me test your understanding with a small thought experiment. Suppose for a fixed prompt $x$, the reference policy thinks two responses $y_A$ and $y_B$ are equally likely. In the preference data, humans prefer $y_A$ to $y_B$ 90% of the time. How will DPO adjust the policy?

Student: Since $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_A\mid x) = \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_B\mid x)$, the log-ratio term reduces to $\log \pi_\theta(y_A\mid x) - \log \pi_\theta(y_B\mid x)$, scaled by $\beta$. The model will be pushed to increase that difference, i.e., increase $\pi_\theta(y_A\mid x)$ and decrease $\pi_\theta(y_B\mid x)$, until the implied BT probability $\sigma\!\left(\beta(\log \pi_\theta(y_A\mid x) - \log \pi_\theta(y_B\mid x))\right)$ matches around $0.9$. The per-example weight is higher when the model’s current difference is smaller than needed, so training emphasizes this pair until it’s calibrated.

Tutor: Exactly. And what if the reference heavily prefers $y_B$, but humans still prefer $y_A$?

Student: Then the model has to move further from the reference to reverse the ordering. Because the log-ratio subtracts $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}$, if $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_B\mid x)$ is large, $\pi_\theta$ has to work harder to beat it for $y_A$. $\beta$ modulates how much it’s willing to move—larger $\beta$ keeps the policy closer to the reference (more conservative), while smaller $\beta$ allows more aggressive shifts to match the preferences.

Tutor: Very good. Now, a final synthesis question: In one or two sentences, what is the core insight of DPO?

Student: DPO reparameterizes the KL-constrained RLHF objective by expressing the reward in terms of the policy-to-reference log-probability ratio, and then fits the policy directly to match human preferences under the Bradley-Terry model. This turns preference optimization into a simple supervised classification loss that implicitly learns the reward without RL, yielding a stable, efficient, and performant alternative to PPO-based RLHF.

Tutor: Excellent summary. Before we wrap up, what open questions or future directions seem most interesting to you based on this paper?

Student: A few:
- Understanding DPO’s generalization and robustness on truly out-of-distribution prompts, especially with larger models.
- Exploring how to best choose or adapt $\beta$ during training or across tasks.
- Studying reward over-optimization phenomena in the DPO setting and how to detect or prevent them.
- Extending DPO to multi-turn interactions and combining it with small amounts of on-policy data when needed.
- Improving automated evaluation prompts to better emulate human judgments across domains.

Tutor: Those align well with the paper’s discussion. If you were to implement DPO next week, what would your minimal checklist look like?

Student: Something like:
- Load an SFT model as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (or train one on preference winners if none is available).
- Prepare a preference dataset of $(x, y_w, y_l)$ pairs.
- Initialize $\pi_\theta$ from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
- Choose a $\beta$, say $0.1$ or $0.5$ as a starting point.
- For each batch: compute log-probs of $y_w$ and $y_l$ under both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; form the log-ratio difference; apply the binary cross-entropy loss via sigmoid; backprop; update.
- Periodically compute sequence-level KL to ensure we don’t drift too far; adjust $\beta$ if needed.
- Validate with either a simple classifier metric (if available) or LLM-based judging; optionally run a small human validation.

Tutor: Perfect. You’ve got the gist and the details. Any last questions?

Student: One last curiosity: if I had access to both a strong reward model and a strong preference dataset, is there any benefit to combining PPO and DPO?

Tutor: Interesting idea. You could imagine a hybrid: use DPO to pre-align the policy quickly and stably, then do a small amount of PPO fine-tuning with an explicit reward to further refine behaviors where preferences are sparse or where you want on-policy exploration. Or use DPO as the main method and rely on Best-of-N for a few critical contexts offline. The paper shows DPO alone is strong, but hybrids could be valuable in special settings.

Student: Thanks, this really clarified the big picture and the math. I feel like I finally understand why they say the language model is secretly a reward model.

Tutor: You’re welcome. The beauty of DPO is that it takes a complex RL pipeline and distills it into a principled supervised learning problem by leveraging the structure of preferences and KL-regularized optimal policies. It’s a great example of how a clever reparameterization can simplify both theory and practice.
