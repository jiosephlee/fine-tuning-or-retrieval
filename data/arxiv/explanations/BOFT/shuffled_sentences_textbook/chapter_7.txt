# Practical checklist and closing remarks

The Cayley transform involves small-matrix inverses for each block; very large gradient steps could, in theory, push $Q$ toward regions where $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is ill-conditioned, so sticking to stable schedules mitigates numerical issues. Monitor conditioning of block transforms and reduce step size or increase regularization if instabilities appear. A few practical notes about the loop that follow from this parameterization: initialize $Q=0$ to get exact identity start; implement each block Cayley transform efficiently (batched small-matrix operations are natural because blocks are identical-sized tiles); and keep the skew-symmetry constraint by parameterizing only the independent entries (this avoids numerical drift that would otherwise need explicit symmetrization). BOFT is applied by learning a left-multiplicative orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0$. BOFT also starts from the identity (all $Q=0$), which is a conservative initialization that helps prevent large abrupt changes early in finetuning. This spectral-norm preservation is one of the reasons OFT and BOFT show stable training behavior: the dominant singular value (and therefore the scale of activations produced by $\bm{W}^0$) is preserved, avoiding sudden amplification of activations that can destabilize optimization. Each forward pass forms the orthogonal blocks via the Cayley formula and composes the BOFT factor as the product of these sparse orthogonal components. - Initialize all skew-symmetric block parameters $Q$ to zero so each BOFT factor starts as identity. Identity initialization corresponds to initializing all $Q$ blocks to zero so every factor starts as the identity. Because the sparsity pattern is fixed by $(m,b)$ and $d$, these permutations do not change during training and can be reused to gather/scatter the inputs into batched small-block operations. - Use conservative finetuning schedules consistent with orthogonal finetuning practice: identity initialization plus modest learning rates and standard warmup/decay strategies. Following these practical guidelines—all grounded in the BOFT construction and properties described in the paper—enables reliable, parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning across large language models, vision transformers, and diffusion U‑Nets, while keeping training stable and inference trivial to deploy. Two qualitative trade-offs follow directly from the paper’s analysis and experiments:  - Increasing $m$ (more stages) improves the ability of the factorization to transmit information across indices and makes the composite orthogonal matrix denser/more expressive. If you must use lower precision for speed, validate numerically that $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ stays well-conditioned for the steps taken. In practice the nonzero pattern of each BOFT component is a permutation of a block-diagonal tiling of small blocks, so it is natural to parameterize each small block by the corresponding small skew-symmetric submatrix $Q_{\text{block}}$ and apply the Cayley transform blockwise. Store $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ in the same format you would store any finetuned weights; at inference time the model runs with the fused dense linear layer and there is no need to apply the factorized orthogonal components on the fly. When optimizing, the trainable variables are the independent entries of the skew-symmetric blocks (only the upper triangle or an equivalent parameter vector needs to be stored). This eliminates any extra latency at inference and simplifies serialization and deployment: you can export the standard weight tensors (no custom factor format required). BOFT provides two core knobs that determine parameter count and expressivity for a layer of neuron dimension $d$: the number of butterfly stages $m$ and the block-size parameter $b$. - After finetuning, fuse $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain dense inference weights with no extra runtime cost. - The paper does not prescribe specific mixed-precision rules, but standard care for numerics applies: because the Cayley transform involves small-matrix inverses on blocks, keep those transforms in a precision that preserves stability (for example, compute small-block inverses in fp32 if using fp16 training globally). - Choose an $(m,b)$ pair per layer guided by the effective parameter formula $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ and the trade-offs discussed: smaller $b$ with larger $m$ is often more parameter-efficient but increases training-time computation. A common workable approach (consistent with the paper’s use cases) is to prioritize large-$d$ linear layers (where the potential representational effect of an orthogonal transform is larger) and to focus on later layers if you expect task-specific adjustments mainly in higher-level features. Concretely, once BOFT has converged you can compute \[ \bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}=\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0, \] where $\bm{R}$ is the full composed orthogonal matrix (product of the learned butterfly components). The intended effects are analogous to dropout in additive adapters: it regularizes the adaptation so the model does not overfit to small downstream datasets, encourages robustness to missing components, and prevents co-adaptation among butterfly stages or blocks. Use these observations to allocate budget across layers: for a fixed total parameter budget, you can increase $m$ for layers where you need dense, long-range mixing (favoring smaller $b$), or you can increase $b$ when you prefer larger local block transformations with fewer stages. Because BOFT composes multiple sparse factors, the forward and backward passes perform multiple small-matrix computations per finetuning step; this is the intended trade of added computation for reduced parameter count. The paper proposes a multiplicative Dropout specifically for BOFT: at each training step randomly select a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and, independently, a fraction $p_2$ of the $2b\times 2b$ diagonal blocks within each selected component, and replace those selected components/blocks with identity matrices for that step. However, for checkpointing or for fast conversion to inference weights it is appropriate to compute the fused dense $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ once and cache that fused matrix. - Increasing $b$ increases the number of parameters per stage by expanding each atomic block and can also increase local expressivity, but for the same parameter budget the paper finds that smaller $b$ combined with larger $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient in approximating dense orthogonal matrices. The paper does not mandate a single universal selection strategy, so treat this as a flexible design choice guided by available budget and observed finetuning behavior. Gradients flow through the Cayley transform back into the $Q$ parameters. A practical advantage of BOFT emphasized in the paper is that the learnt orthogonal factors can be fused into the pretrained model after finetuning, producing a single set of modified weights with no inference-time overhead. - Cache the composed orthogonal matrix (or the product of factors) only when needed. Because of these properties, BOFT typically behaves at least as stably as OFT and is more stable than purely additive low‑rank updates that can change singular values (for example, LoRA can alter the spectrum of $\bm{W}^0$). To guarantee orthogonality during training BOFT follows the same high-level strategy used in OFT: parameterize each orthogonal factor via a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ and the Cayley transform. Implementational best practices consistent with the paper’s description:  - Precompute and store the block layout and permutation indices for each factor. If you want to preserve the factorization for modularity (for example, to transport only the relatively small BOFT parameters rather than the entire fused dense matrices), you can also serialize the small skew-symmetric block parameters for each stage and recompose on a target machine. The same block-size repeats many times; using batched small-matrix kernels is more efficient than many individually sized calls. - Implement the butterfly sparsity by precomputing permutation maps and performing batched small-block Cayley transforms and multiplies for computational efficiency. The butterfly extreme ($m\approx \log d$ with small $b$) achieves dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. Also remember runtime: larger $m$ increases per-step computation because more sparse factors are composed each forward/backward pass, so if training-time compute is the binding constraint you may choose larger $b$ and smaller $m$. Chapter 7: Training BOFT in Practice  BOFT represents each orthogonal factor as a product of small orthogonal blocks. For the common choice of $2\times 2$ blocks a $2\times 2$ skew-symmetric block has the form \[ Q_{\text{2x2}}=\begin{bmatrix}0 & -q\\ q & 0\end{bmatrix}, \] and applying the Cayley transform on that block yields an orthogonal $2\times 2$ rotation-like matrix. For larger block sizes (the $2b\times 2b$ blocks that appear in block-butterfly components) we similarly constrain the parameter block to be skew-symmetric and compute the Cayley transform on that block to obtain a $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block. The paper gives the effective number of trainable parameters per linear layer as \[ \text{effective params} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m, \] where each stage contributes blocks of size $2b\times 2b$ composed from skew-symmetric parameters. By construction $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, and left-multiplying an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$. The paper does not prescribe exact numeric schedules; in practice pick the same finetuning learning-rate family you would use for OFT and monitor convergence, increasing regularization (smaller LR or higher multiplicative dropout) if instability appears. Because BOFT preserves pairwise neuron angles and spectral norm, it is particularly attractive for layers that encode stable pretrained geometric relationships (for example, high-capacity feed-forward blocks and embedding/projector layers). That said, standard finetuning best practices still apply: conservative optimization (smaller initial learning rates, warmup, and gradual decay schedules commonly used in finetuning) are compatible with BOFT. - Use multiplicative Dropout (selecting proportions $p_1$ of components and $p_2$ of blocks to replace with identity each step) when downstream data are limited to reduce overfitting. BOFT inherits several stability-friendly properties from orthogonal finetuning. Because BOFT already enforces orthogonality and starts from identity, moderate multiplicative dropout can help when the downstream dataset is small; when data is plentiful the dropout rates can be reduced or turned off. Tuning $p_1$ and $p_2$ trades regularization against expressivity: larger values give stronger regularization but reduce the effective transformation capacity each step. - Because BOFT composes multiple sparse orthogonal components, organize computations as sequences of block-sparse multiplies following the butterfly schedule. The paper demonstrates BOFT across language models, vision transformers, and diffusion models, implying a broad applicability: if a layer is realized as a dense linear map acting on a neuron-dimensional space of size $d$, BOFT can be applied to that layer by selecting an appropriate $(m,b)$ configuration for that $d$. This allows reusing memory buffers and minimizing temporary allocations. This scheme is multiplicative because it temporarily neutralizes selected factors (making them identity) rather than zeroing an additive update. Practically, when constrained by compute or parameter budget one often needs to select a subset of layers to adapt. The butterfly components are structurally sparse: each component is a block-diagonal arrangement of small blocks, and the nonzero pattern of higher-order components is a permutation of the smallest $2\times 2$ tiling pattern. This reproduces the pretrained model exactly at step zero and avoids initial perturbation. Because BOFT updates weights multiplicatively (multiplying a learned orthogonal $\bm{R}$ on the left of pretrained $\bm{W}^0$), conventional additive dropout schemes for adapters do not directly apply. Concretely, for a full factor $\bm{R}$ BOFT uses factors of the form \[ \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}, \] where $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. - Implement the Cayley transform and its inverse-related calculations in a batched way over the blocks. In architectures such as transformers and U‑Nets these occur everywhere: projection matrices in multi-head attention, the feed-forward (MLP) layers, embedding projection layers, and linear layers in U‑Net encoder/decoder blocks. That characterization naturally suggests the layers where BOFT will have the most direct effect: layers implemented as linear maps (matrix multiplications) where transforming neuron geometry preserves pretrained structure. The paper points out that BOFT’s final orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model and thus are compatible with standard inference pipelines. During training you typically recompute factors each step because they change; caching the full dense product for every step defeats the purpose of a factorized parameterization. That structural regularity is advantageous for implementation.
