\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

From Foundation Models to Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Why full finetuning is impractical for modern foundation models
The rapid growth of foundation models in both capability and size has made training them from scratch increasingly prohibitive. Models with hundreds of billions of parameters (for example, the paper notes GPT-3 with roughly $175$B parameters) require enormous compute, memory, and engineering resources to pretrain. As a result, most researchers and practitioners rely on pretrained foundation models and focus on adapting these large models to new downstream tasks instead of retraining from zero.

Full finetuning — updating all parameters of a pretrained model for a downstream task — can be effective but becomes impractical as models scale. The main practical barriers are compute and storage: updating and storing gradients for every parameter during finetuning is expensive, and keeping separate fully finetuned checkpoints for many tasks multiplies storage costs. Moreover, when the goal is to enable many downstream tasks without incurring additional inference latency, alternatives that change only a small fraction of parameters or add compact auxiliary parameters are preferable. These practical constraints motivate parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) approaches that adapt a large model with a substantially smaller number of trained parameters.

# PEFT goals: strong transfer, small parameter budgets, no latency
Parameter-efficient finetuning has three central goals that push method design in practice. First, strong transfer: the adapted model should retain and exploit the pretrained knowledge so it performs well on the downstream task. Second, minimal parameter budgets: the number of task-specific trainable parameters should be small compared to the full model, enabling cheap storage, fast training, and deployment for many tasks. Third, zero or negligible inference latency: ideally the finetuning mechanism should not add extra cost at inference time (for example, by introducing additional layers or token processing that slow down prediction).

These goals are sometimes in tension. Aggressive parameter reductions can harm transfer if the adaptation is too constrained. Conversely, methods that preserve full flexibility (full finetuning) meet transfer goals but fail on budget and storage. Consequently, much current work seeks principled parameterizations and priors that strike a better trade-off between expressivity (to learn the downstream task) and regularity (to preserve pretrained structure and avoid overfitting), while keeping parameter counts and inference cost low.

# Three PEFT families: model finetuning, adapters, prompts
Practically, PEFT methods fall into three broad families. The first is model finetuning: the architecture remains unchanged and a subset of the model parameters are updated. These subsets can be chosen in many ways (specific layers, biases only, or structured reparameterizations). Model finetuning has the big practical advantage that it adds no inference-time cost when done by updating the native parameters.

The second family is adapter tuning: extra trainable modules are inserted into the model and only these adapters are trained. Adapters can be lightweight and task-specific, and at inference time the adapters are either kept (adding some latency) or merged when possible.

The third family is prompt tuning: trainable tokens or prefixes are attached to the input sequence (or other input modalities) and learning happens only in this small input-side parameterization. Prompt tuning can be extremely parameter-efficient but its effectiveness sometimes depends on model architecture and task.

Within the model-fineturning family there are interesting reparameterization strategies: LoRA, for example, updates weight matrices by adding a product of two low-rank matrices (an additive low-rank update). That low-rank parameterization can be very parameter-efficient and has proven effective across many language tasks. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is another model-reparameterization approach: it updates a pretrained layer multiplicatively by left-multiplying the frozen weights with a learned orthogonal matrix. Because it modifies existing weights rather than adding new modules, OFT also preserves zero inference latency.

# Why orthogonality as a finetuning prior
Orthogonal finetuning is motivated by a geometric view of pretrained weight matrices. A column of a weight matrix corresponds to a neuron; the pairwise angles between neurons encode relational structure learned during pretraining. If finetuning preserves those pairwise angles, then the pretrained relational structure — and the generalization benefits it captures — is largely maintained.

Orthogonal transformations preserve angles and lengths. Concretely, OFT reparameterizes a finetuned weight matrix by left-multiplying the frozen pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and evaluates outputs as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, for any two neuron column vectors $\bm{w}_i^0,\bm{w}_j^0$ the inner products and pairwise angles are preserved (up to numerical precision), so the pretrained configuration of neurons remains relationally similar after finetuning. This geometric preservation has been linked in prior work to improved generalizability and training stability.

Practically, OFT enforces orthogonality during learning using standard parameterizations for orthogonal matrices. One convenient parameterization is the Cayley transform: represent an orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). That parameterization allows zero (identity) initialization of $\bm{R}$ and gradient-based learning while maintaining orthogonality.

Orthogonality also yields favorable spectral properties: left-multiplying by orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$, so the spectral norm is unchanged. Preservation of spectral properties has known connections to stable training and better generalization behavior.

# Limitations of block-diagonal OFT and the need for density
A straightforward way to make OFT parameter-efficient is to restrict $\bm{R}$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group. The original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure: $\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ where each block $\bm{R}_i$ is an orthogonal matrix of size $b\times b$ and $br=d$. With smaller block sizes this reduces the number of trainable parameters roughly to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$.

While block-diagonal OFT reduces parameter count and preserves orthogonality inside each block, it has important limitations. The block-diagonal sparsity pattern enforces that different groups of dimensions (determined by contiguous index ranges) do not interact during the transformation; the coordinates within each block are coupled only among themselves. This fixed partitioning of neuron dimensions by index is arbitrary with respect to the semantic structure of learned representations and may introduce an undesirable inductive bias or limit expressiveness. In particular, no block-diagonal orthogonal matrix can approximate many classic global linear transforms (for example, Fourier-like transforms), and a block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ cannot become dense: some entries of $\bm{R}$ remain constrained to zero. If the downstream task requires coordinated changes across all dimensions of a neuron, a block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ may be insufficient.

Therefore, we would like a parameterization that (1) can represent dense orthogonal transformations that mix coordinates globally, (2) remains parameter-efficient (far fewer than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters), and (3) retains the desirable orthogonality prior for stability and generalization.

# High-level idea of composing dense orthogonal transforms from sparse ones
The core idea in the paper is to represent a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. That is, write
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1,
\]
where each factor $\bm{B}_i$ is sparse and orthogonal. Multiplying many sparse factors can produce a dense matrix overall, while the total number of trainable parameters can be much smaller than parameterizing $\bm{R}$ directly. This trades computation for space: each forward/backward pass needs to multiply through all factors, but storage and the number of learned parameters is reduced.

To reason about which sparsity patterns are effective, the paper introduces an information transmission viewpoint. Consider unrolling the factorization into a layered directed graph with $d$ nodes at each of $m+1$ levels: the first level are source coordinates, and the final level are destination coordinates. Each sparse factor $\bm{B}_i$ defines edges between level $i$ and level $i+1$: a nonzero in $\bm{B}_i$ indicates that information (a linear contribution) flows from a particular source at level $i$ to a particular node at level $i+1$. Under this interpretation, the product $\bm{B}_m\cdots\bm{B}_1$ is dense if and only if, in the layered graph, every source node has at least one path to every destination node — i.e., dense connectivity across the whole network. The desiderata for a good factorization are therefore (i) dense connectivity from first to last level, and (ii) as few trainable edges as possible subject to the orthogonality constraints on each factor.

Orthogonality imposes additional structure: each $\bm{B}_i$ must be orthogonal, which requires certain patterns (for example, each orthogonal $\bm{B}_i$ must be full-rank). Some minimal edges per level are unavoidable under orthogonality (at least $d$ edges are needed to realize a bijection between $d$ nodes across adjacent levels), but beyond that we want an edge-efficient topology that still allows global mixing across the composition.

# Butterfly insight from fast Fourier transforms
A classic and efficient answer to the information-transmission problem is the butterfly topology that underlies the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT). The Cooley–Tukey algorithm decomposes an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform recursively, and the induced computation graph is a layered “butterfly” network that routes and mixes information efficiently. Crucially, that butterfly graph achieves dense connectivity from inputs to outputs using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, making it an attractive template for sparse factorization.

Formally, the paper defines a radix-2 butterfly factor for even $k$:
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ collects the diagonal entries in that quadrant. A full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ (for $d = 2^N$) is built recursively by composing block-diagonal matrices made from these small butterfly factors:
\[
\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix with blocks $\bm{B}^F(k)$. Intuitively, each stage mixes information locally among pairs, then among progressively larger groups, and the entire composition yields global mixing with only $O(d\log d)$ nonzeros.

To turn the butterfly factorization into an orthogonal parameterization, ensure each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is orthogonal. This can be achieved by parameterizing the smallest ($2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$) blocks as orthogonal rotations (via Cayley or standard 2D rotations). The nonzero patterns of larger butterfly components are permutations of the $2\times 2$ pattern, so full orthogonality of every component can be ensured by construction.

This yields an orthogonal, sparse factorization that composes to a dense orthogonal transform while requiring only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters when using radix-2 building blocks. Because many classic transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, discrete cosine transforms) have butterfly-like factorizations, this structure is expressive for many practically relevant global transforms while remaining parameter-efficient.

# Contributions and scope of BOFT
Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) operationalizes the above ideas into a practical PEFT method. BOFT reparameterizes the orthogonal matrix in OFT as a product of orthogonal butterfly components, producing a family of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning variants denoted BOFT$(m,b)$: the parameters are the block size $b$ (where $b$ controls how many coordinates are mixed in the basic blocks) and the number of butterfly stages $m$. Several key properties and contributions of BOFT follow from this design.

First, BOFT enables dense orthogonal transformations with far fewer parameters than an unconstrained orthogonal matrix. For BOFT$(m,b)$ the paper shows the number of effective trainable parameters is approximately
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\, d \, m,
\]
so with the classic butterfly choice $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ this yields $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. By contrast, a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, while block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ requires $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters and cannot represent dense global mixing unless $b=d$.

Second, BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases: BOFT$(1,b)$ is equivalent to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT$(1,d)$ is the unconstrained OFT. This nesting shows BOFT interpolates between the original OFT’s block-diagonal regularity and full orthogonal expressiveness by controlling $(m,b)$.

Third, BOFT preserves the desirable properties of orthogonal finetuning — angle preservation, spectral-norm preservation, and the related stability benefits — while introducing a structured inductive bias derived from butterfly factorizations. Because butterfly factorizations can recover many classical fast linear transforms, the inductive bias tends to favor global structured transformations rather than arbitrary dense ones; empirically and intuitively this can improve generalization and avoid overfitting compared to either very low-parameter or full-parameter alternatives.

Finally, the paper discusses practical considerations for BOFT: initialize every butterfly factor to identity (zero skew-symmetric in Cayley parameterization) so the finetuning starts from the pretrained model; introduce a multiplicative Dropout variant for the multiplicative update (randomly replace selected butterfly components or blocks by identity during training) to combat overfitting; and note that although BOFT introduces some training-time compute overhead because of repeated sparse multiplications, it incurs no inference-time latency once the composed orthogonal matrix is multiplied into the base model after finetuning.

Taken together, BOFT offers a principled, flexible, and practical framework for orthogonality-preserving, parameter-efficient finetuning. It achieves a middle ground between expressivity and parameter parsimony by leveraging sparse factorization topologies (butterfly graphs) grounded in a clear information-transmission perspective. The paper demonstrates this strategy across different application domains (vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models), arguing that BOFT is a broadly applicable PEFT approach that preserves the strengths of orthogonal finetuning while dramatically improving parameter efficiency.

Mathematical Foundations for Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal matrices: properties, stability, and angle preservation

Orthogonal matrices play the central role in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its butterfly factorization variant (BOFT). A square matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal when $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}_d$. This algebraic constraint has several immediate geometric consequences that are essential to why OFT was proposed. First, left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms and inner products: for any vectors $\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{v})=\bm{u}^\top\bm{v}.
\]
Because inner products determine angles and lengths, an orthogonal transform preserves pairwise angles and norms between column vectors (neurons) of a weight matrix. OFT exploits this property: by replacing a pretrained layer $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the relational geometry (pairwise angles) among the neurons is preserved exactly. The preservation of these geometric relationships is argued in the paper to help retain pretraining information and to improve generalization during finetuning.

Another practical consequence of orthogonality is spectral stability. Multiplying a matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix does not change its singular values (see the Spectral norm and singular value decomposition section below). As many training instabilities come from uncontrolled growth or shrinkage of singular values, this spectral invariance contributes to stable finetuning behavior. OFT and BOFT therefore leverage orthogonality both for geometric preservation and for improved numerical stability.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and the Cayley transform

To ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal during optimization, OFT and BOFT use a parameterization based on skew-symmetric matrices and the Cayley transform. A matrix $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is skew-symmetric if $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley transform maps a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix via
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This mapping guarantees $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ for any skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ for which the inverse exists, so parameterizing $\bm{Q}$ during training enforces orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ by construction. A convenient feature of this parameterization noted in the paper is that setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, enabling identity initialization of the finetuning transform so that the model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.

In BOFT the Cayley transform is applied block-wise to the sparse blocks that make up the butterfly components. In the simplest case of $2\times 2$ blocks, the Cayley parameterization is equivalent to using a planar rotation parameter (a $2\times 2$ skew-symmetric block maps to a 2D rotation), which is an efficient and stable way to make each small block orthogonal. This local block-wise orthogonal parameterization composes to give global orthogonality for the product of butterfly components.

# Preserving neuron pairwise angles and its significance

Preserving pairwise angles among neurons (columns of a weight matrix) is a central motivation of OFT. The intuition is that pretraining discovers a geometric organization of neurons — directions in parameter space that capture important features and relationships. If finetuning perturbs individual weights arbitrarily, these inter-neuron relationships can be distorted and pretraining knowledge can be partially lost. By constraining the finetuning update to an orthogonal left-multiplication $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the angle between any two neurons $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$ is unchanged because
\[
\frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j)}
{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\|}
=
\frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{w}^0_j)}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|\,\|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]
The paper argues that this relational preservation leads to more stable training trajectories and better generalization compared with unconstrained updates (e.g., full finetuning) or additive low-rank updates (e.g., LoRA). In short, OFT and BOFT can be seen as finetuning strategies that learn a global orthogonal bilinear similarity $\bm{R}$ that modifies how pretrained neurons interact with inputs while keeping the pretrained geometry intact.

# Spectral norm and singular value decomposition

Singular value decomposition (SVD) provides an immediate and useful perspective on why orthogonal left-multiplication preserves spectral properties. Write the pretrained weight as
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
where $\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. Left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Because $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is orthogonal, the singular values of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ are the same as those of $\bm{W}^0$. In particular the spectral norm (largest singular value) is preserved exactly. The paper highlights that this preservation of spectral norm is beneficial to training stability and generalization; uncontrolled changes to singular values are a known source of instability, while orthogonal updates avoid changing the scale of the pretrained linear map.

# Block matrices, permutations, and sparsity patterns

A key engineering challenge in OFT is parameter efficiency. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, which is expensive to store and optimize. The original OFT reduces this by imposing a block-diagonal structure:
\[
\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
with blocks $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ and $br=d$. This block structure reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, but it also restricts the transform to act independently on disjoint coordinate groups, which prevents the construction of a dense $\bm{R}$ and imposes a hard sparsity pattern (indexed groupings) that can be an undesirable inductive bias.

BOFT approaches sparsity differently: it composes a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal factors. The paper introduces the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ for even $k$:
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. A $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is defined recursively by composing block-diagonal butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ for decreasing $k$, yielding a product of sparse block-permuted matrices. Each butterfly component has a structured sparsity pattern that, when multiplied together, enables dense connectivity between input and output coordinates.

This multiplicative sparse composition can be viewed through the paper's information transmission framework: factor matrices correspond to adjacency patterns between $d$ nodes at consecutive levels in a $d\times(m+1)$ grid, and the product transmits information from source nodes to all receiver nodes. The two desiderata in this view are dense connectivity (every source reaches every receiver) and minimum free edges (as few trainable non-structural edges as possible). Orthogonality further constrains feasible sparsity patterns because each factor must be full rank and obey orthogonality relationships among its nonzero entries.

Permutations of blocks play a role because many butterfly components are block-wise permutations of the basic $2\times 2$ block structure, so parameterizing the basic blocks orthogonally suffices to make each component orthogonal. The block-butterfly generalization introduces a block size $b$ (block matrices of size $b\times b$ inside the butterfly factors), providing a family of sparsity/expressivity trade-offs indexed by $(m,b)$ (number of factors and block size).

# Parameter counting: dense vs sparse vs low rank

Parameter efficiency is analyzed explicitly in the paper. For the general BOFT construction with $m$ butterfly components and block size $b$, the number of effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d\times n$ is
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
Special cases illustrate familiar extremes. If $m=1$, BOFT$(1,b)$ reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, recovering the block-diagonal parameter count $\mathcal{O}(bd)$. If $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ (the pure radix-2 butterfly), BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, a dramatic reduction compared with the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters of an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix (original OFT with $b=d$). Thus BOFT provides a smooth interpolation between the identity (very small $m$, small $b$) and full orthogonal matrices (large $m$ and/or large $b$) while controlling parameter cost.

The paper contrasts this sparse multiplicative parameterization with additive low-rank methods such as LoRA, which update pretrained weights by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. LoRA achieves parameter efficiency through rank constraints; BOFT achieves it through structured sparsity and multiplicative composition. Both low-rank and sparse factorizations are families of structured matrices that reduce parameter counts in different ways, yielding different inductive biases.

# Computational trade-offs: time versus parameter storage

A fundamental trade-off in BOFT is between storage (number of trainable parameters) and computation (matrix multiplications per forward/backward pass). Representing a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal components reduces the number of free parameters, but evaluation requires multiplying those components with $\bm{W}^0$ (or applying them to activations) during each iteration. The paper explicitly points out that this trades computation time for space: because the orthogonal matrix is represented by repeated sparse multiplications, training has slightly higher runtime overhead than a single dense or block-diagonal transform. However, once finetuning is finished the learned product can be fused into the pretrained weights (multiply the factors into $\bm{W}^0$) so there is no inference-time latency penalty.

The paper also proposes practical regularization mechanisms adapted to the multiplicative structure. Standard additive Dropout applies to additive updates (e.g., LoRA) but not directly to multiplicative orthogonal updates. BOFT uses a multiplicative Dropout where a random fraction of butterfly components and/or diagonal blocks within components are replaced by identity blocks during training; this respects the multiplicative parameterization and helps prevent overfitting.

Finally, the information transmission viewpoint guides the choice of factor count $m$ and block size $b$: increasing $m$ (more sparse factors) can achieve dense connectivity with fewer total parameters, but increases the number of sequential sparse multiplications and thus training cost. The butterfly topology is attractive because it achieves dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ sparse edges (hence parameters) and only $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ factor depth.

# Additional structural and expressivity remarks

Two further structural points from the paper are worth emphasizing. First, the butterfly family includes many classical fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, discrete cosine/sine) as special or closely related cases; this suggests that a butterfly-structured orthogonal family has an inductive bias aligned with many useful global transforms. Second, the paper gives a formal expressivity statement (Theorem 1) that BOFT with suitable products of butterfly matrices is strictly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, and that compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes (a kaleidoscope-like hierarchy) can in principle represent the entire orthogonal group when sufficiently deep. Practically, though, the paper emphasizes the trade-off: full expressivity (full orthogonal group) is not always desirable for generalization, and BOFT aims to find a middle ground where structured sparsity yields both parameter efficiency and favorable inductive bias.

# Orthogonal finetuning as bilinear similarity and implications

Finally, it is useful to reinterpret orthogonal finetuning in bilinear form. For a neuron $\bm{w}^0_i$ (a column of $\bm{W}^0$), its response after BOFT is
\[
\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}^\top\bm{x}).
\]
Viewed differently, BOFT learns the bilinear similarity $(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top\bm{R}\bm{x}$ with the constraint that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. This connects to metric and similarity learning perspectives: finetuning adjusts the bilinear form that measures how pretrained neurons score inputs, but does so under strong structural constraints that preserve norms and angles. The orthogonality constraint therefore provides both a geometric regularizer and a spectrally stable parameterization that supports robust finetuning across modalities and model families, as demonstrated in the paper.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles and Limitations

# OFT formulation: reparameterize W as R W^0
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a reparameterization-based finetuning strategy for pretrained linear layers that updates the layer multiplicatively rather than additively. Let the pretrained weight matrix of a linear layer be $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where we regard each column of $\bm{W}^0$ as a neuron. OFT introduces a learnable left-multiplicative orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and replaces the usual forward computation
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
by
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
for input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and output $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$. The key difference from additive reparameterizations (for example, low-rank additive updates) is that OFT updates the mapping by rotating the input/neuron geometry via an orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weights rather than by adding a residual matrix to the weights.

This multiplicative form is intentional: by constraining $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal, OFT aims to modify the pretrained mapping while preserving important geometric relationships in the parameter space (see next section). In practice OFT is initialized so that the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained model (zero initial change), which is implemented by initializing $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix.

# Neuron geometry preservation and zero initialization
A central motivation for OFT is to preserve the relational geometry among neurons learned during pretraining. Treating the columns of $\bm{W}^0$ as neuron vectors, left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles and inner products between neurons: if $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ are two columns of $\bm{W}^0$, then for any orthogonal $\bm{R}$ we have
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0)= (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{w}_j^0.
\]
Preserving these angles is hypothesized to maintain semantic structure and pretraining knowledge embodied in neuron relationships, which can improve generalization when adapting to downstream tasks.

Zero initialization is a practical complement to this idea: OFT initializes $\bm{R}=\bm{I}_d$ so that the finetuned model initially exactly reproduces the pretrained network. Starting from identity avoids abrupt deviations from the pretrained solution and allows gradual, constrained adaptation driven by the orthogonal updates.

# Maintaining orthogonality during training via Cayley
Enforcing exact orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout optimization is essential to guarantee the geometric preservation properties. OFT uses the Cayley parameterization to ensure $\bm{R}$ stays orthogonal during training. Concretely, introduce a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$) and define
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
Because the Cayley transform of any real skew-symmetric matrix is orthogonal (when the inverse exists), parameterizing $\bm{R}$ this way allows unconstrained optimization over $\bm{Q}$ while $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, matching the zero-initialization requirement.

This parameterization provides a principled way to maintain the orthogonality constraint during gradient-based training, avoiding projection steps or soft penalties and thereby keeping the finetuning dynamics strictly within the orthogonal group.

# Block-diagonal OFT: structure, parameter count, and usage
A full unconstrained $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, which can be prohibitively large to learn for high-dimensional layers. To reduce the number of trainable parameters, the original OFT paper introduces a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$. Concretely, partition the $d$ coordinates into $r$ contiguous blocks of size $b$ so that $br=d$, and set
\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is an orthogonal matrix acting only within one block. Each block can be parameterized (for example via Cayley transforms on the block) and initialized as identity so that the overall $\bm{R}$ starts as $\bm{I}_d$.

Because the number of free parameters scales with the block size, the block-diagonal OFT reduces parameter count roughly to $\mathcal{O}(b d)$ (the paper reports the parameter efficiency of block-diagonal OFT as $\mathcal{O}(bd)$), which is substantially smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ when $b\ll d$. Practitioners can trade expressivity for parameter efficiency by choosing $b$ appropriately: smaller $b$ gives fewer parameters and cheaper training but a more constrained family of transforms.

# Inductive biases from block partitioning by indices
While block-diagonal parameterization reduces the parameter budget, it introduces a structural inductive bias that may be undesirable. The block partitioning is typically formed by slicing the coordinate indices into contiguous groups (e.g., first $b$ coordinates form block 1, next $b$ coordinates form block 2, etc.). This choice is arbitrary with respect to the pretrained neuron geometry: the spatial ordering of coordinates (their integer indices) generally does not carry semantic grouping information. Consequently, treating coordinates in different index ranges as separate islands that cannot mix during finetuning enforces an artificial independence across blocks.

This index-based grouping implies that any adaptation that requires mixing information across blocks is impossible under the block-diagonal constraint. The finetuned orthogonal transform becomes a collection of independent within-block rotations; inter-block correlations that may be relevant for a downstream task cannot be captured. Thus the block-diagonal structure encodes a rigid inductive bias determined purely by index partitioning rather than by the data or pretrained geometry.

# Expressivity gaps: inability to approximate classic transforms
A direct consequence of the block-diagonal structure is a limitation in expressivity: a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot represent dense orthogonal transforms that mix coordinates across blocks. Many classic and useful linear transforms—discrete Fourier transforms, Hadamard transforms, cosine/sine transforms, and other global orthogonal mixes—are dense and require cross-block coupling. The block-diagonal parameterization is incapable of approximating such transforms because its sparsity pattern forbids any coupling between distinct blocks.

From a matrix viewpoint, block-diagonal matrices have zero entries in all off-block positions. No choice of within-block orthogonal matrices can create nonzero mass in those off-block entries. Therefore, the block-diagonal OFT cannot recover or approximate dense orthogonal matrices that are important both theoretically and practically. This expressivity gap matters because the space of useful adaptations to downstream tasks may well include such dense cross-coordinate rotations; constraining the hypothesis class to block-diagonal rotations excludes those possibilities and risks suboptimal adaptation.

Moreover, the paper notes that the question of how to design a good sparsity pattern (i.e., which off-diagonal entries to allow) is largely unresolved. The block-diagonal scheme chooses a very simple sparsity pattern that is easy to implement and parameterize but not necessarily the best bias for downstream tasks.

# Empirical stability of OFT and its parameter overhead
Empirically, OFT has demonstrated valuable properties. By preserving neuron pairwise angles and the left singular vectors of $\bm{W}^0$, orthogonal finetuning tends to maintain the spectral properties of the pretrained layer. A short SVD argument makes this clear: write $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$; left-multiplying by orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, so the singular values (and hence spectral norm) are unchanged. Preservation of spectral norm and related spectral structure correlates with improved training stability and, in prior experiments, with improved generalization compared to some additive alternatives.

OFT has been shown to produce stable training and good generalizability in applications like finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. However, that practical stability comes at the cost of parameter overhead for dense $\bm{R}$. A fully unconstrained orthogonal $\bm{R}$ requires on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (or equivalently learning a full skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$). That overhead is often larger than additive low-rank alternatives (e.g., LoRA), which achieve parameter efficiency via low-rank additive updates. To make OFT feasible in large models, the block-diagonal restriction is used, but as discussed above this restriction imposes potentially harmful inductive biases and limits expressivity.

Together, these observations identify a core trade-off in OFT: preserving geometric structure via orthogonality has clear benefits for stability and generalization, but learning a dense orthogonal transform is parameter-expensive. The block-diagonal compromise reduces parameters but forbids cross-block mixing and thus cannot represent many dense transforms. This tension motivates searching for parameterizations that can produce dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer trainable parameters than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ while avoiding the rigid index-based sparsity of block-diagonal OFT. The remainder of the paper develops one such approach (butterfly factorization) to address precisely this limitation.

An Information Transmission View of Matrix Factorization

# Mapping matrix products to layered grid graphs

Consider a factorization of a square matrix into a product of matrices,
\[
\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1,
\]
where each factor \(\bm{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). One convenient way to reason about how the factors combine is to "unroll" the multiplication into a layered grid of nodes. Arrange \(m+1\) vertical levels (columns) of \(d\) nodes each, producing a \(d\times(m+1)\) grid. The nodes in the first level correspond to the \(d\) input coordinates (sources), the nodes in the last level correspond to the \(d\) output coordinates (sinks), and the intermediate levels correspond to the intermediate vector representations that result after multiplying by successive \(\bm{B}_i\).

Each factor \(\bm{B}_i\) induces directed connections from level \(i\) to level \(i+1\): the entry \((\bm{B}_i)_{p,q}\) being nonzero is interpreted as a directed edge from node \(q\) in level \(i\) to node \(p\) in level \(i+1\). With this picture, the composed matrix \(\bm{R}\) corresponds to the aggregated connectivity that results from all length-\(m\) paths that start at a source node in level 1 and end at a sink node in level \(m+1\). The numerical value of \(R_{p,q}\) is the sum over all such path-products, but for many design questions the pattern of nonzeros (which entries can be nonzero at all) is the central object of interest. The layered graph therefore gives a concrete combinatorial representation of how sparse factors can combine to produce dense connectivity.

# Edges as nonzeros: when information can flow

In the layered graph view, edges are exactly the nonzero entries of the factor matrices. If \((\bm{B}_i)_{p,q}\neq 0\), then the node \(q\) at level \(i\) can send information to node \(p\) at level \(i+1\). If \((\bm{B}_i)_{p,q}=0\), then that immediate transfer is blocked. Crucially, a nonzero entry in the final product \(\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\cdots\bm{B}_1\) corresponds to the existence of at least one directed path from a given source node at level \(1\) to a given sink node at level \(m+1\); conversely, a zero in \(\bm{R}\) signals that every potential path between those two nodes is blocked by at least one zero entry in some factor.

This binary connection interpretation (edge present vs. edge absent) abstracts away the precise numerical values and focuses attention on whether information can possibly flow. That abstraction is useful in the finetuning context because the primary goal of sparse factor design is often to achieve full (dense) connectivity while keeping the number of trainable nonzeros small. The same combinatorial language also makes it easy to reason about how adding or removing nonzeros in individual factors affects the sparsity pattern of the composed matrix.

# Dense connectivity requirement across layers

For the composed matrix \(\bm{R}\) to be dense (no structural zeros), the layered graph must permit every source node in level \(1\) to reach every sink node in level \(m+1\) by some directed path. Put differently, for each pair of indices \((s,t)\) there must exist at least one sequence of nonzero entries
\[
(\bm{B}_1)_{\cdot,s}\;,\;(\bm{B}_2)_{\cdot,\cdot}\;,\;\dots\;,\;(\bm{B}_m)_{t,\cdot}
\]
that links source \(s\) to sink \(t\). If the layered graph fails this all-pairs reachability requirement, then \(\bm{R}\) will have structural zeros and cannot serve as a dense orthogonal transform.

This reachability requirement is naturally checked on the graph: the adjacency from level to level defines possible local moves, and dense final connectivity is equivalent to the layered graph being strongly connected in the sense that every source can reach every sink. Designing factor sparsity patterns therefore becomes a graph design problem: choose which edges exist between adjacent levels so that the layered graph admits full source-to-sink reachability while minimizing the total number of edges or trainable parameters.

# Minimum free edges under orthogonality constraints

Two further constraints complicate the pure combinatorial design: (1) orthogonality and (2) the necessity that each factor be full rank. Orthogonality imposes algebraic constraints among entries, so the number of free trainable parameters is not simply the count of nonzero positions. Nevertheless, a basic combinatorial lower bound comes from the rank requirement. For each factor \(\bm{B}_i\) to be invertible (a necessary condition for full orthogonality on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)), there must be at least \(d\) independent connections between level \(i\) and level \(i+1\) that form a bijection in some sense; concretely, there must be at least \(d\) nonzero entries that allow a full-rank mapping.

Because orthogonal matrices have special structure (columns are orthonormal), some patterns of zeros are coupled: forbidding one entry may force another entry to be zero or fixed by orthogonality constraints. For instance, in a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal block, setting one entry to zero may immediately constrain other entries (the paper gives the illustrative observation that a zero at position \((1,1)\) can imply a zero at \((2,2)\) under orthogonality). Thus counting degrees of freedom requires accounting for these algebraic couplings; some nonzeros are effectively non-trainable because they are constrained to fixed values (e.g., \(\pm 1\)) or determined by other entries to maintain orthogonality.

Because of these interactions, it is useful to separate out the unavoidable structural connections (the minimal \(d\) per adjacent-level requirement) from the additional "free" edges that actually correspond to trainable parameters. The design problem then becomes: achieve all-pairs reachability while keeping the number of free (trainable) edges as small as possible under the orthogonality-induced couplings.

# Why single dense layers are costly in parameters

A single dense \(d\times d\) matrix contains \(d^2\) entries, and an unconstrained orthogonal matrix still requires on the order of \(d^2\) storage or parametrization (more precisely, the orthogonal group \(O(d)\) has dimension \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\), which is \(\Theta(d^2)\)). From the layered-graph perspective, a single dense factor between two levels corresponds to a complete bipartite connection with \(d^2\) edges; if we discount the unavoidable \(d\) connections needed for rank, there remain roughly \(d^2-d\) trainable edge-positions (for example, with \(d=4\) this is \(16-4=12\) trainable positions). This quadratic scaling in \(d\) becomes prohibitive as layer widths grow, which is precisely why naively parameterizing a dense orthogonal transform is expensive.

The cost is not only storage: during training and runtime such a dense factor is a heavy linear operator. Consequently, a key objective is to avoid instantiating a dense intermediate matrix while still obtaining dense overall connectivity at the composition level.

# How factorization can reduce parameters while retaining density

The central idea is to replace one expensive dense factor by a product of several sparse factors. Each sparse factor has far fewer nonzeros (edges) — for example, \(O(d)\) rather than \(O(d^2)\) — and when multiplied together their sparsity patterns can interleave so that every source can still reach every sink. In the layered-graph language, sparse factors provide local wiring between adjacent levels; by arranging a small number of carefully chosen sparse stages, information from any source can percolate through the network and arrive at any sink. Thus sparsity per stage does not preclude global density in the composed transform.

This factorization trades computation for space: evaluating \(\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\cdots\bm{B}_1\) requires applying \(m\) sparse operators each iteration rather than one dense operator, increasing per-step computation, but the storage and number of trainable parameters can be drastically smaller. Practical sparse factorizations exploit structured sparsity patterns that are easy to apply (fast multiply) and that achieve full reachability with only \(O(d\log d)\) or similar numbers of edges; a canonical example is the butterfly pattern coming from the Cooley–Tukey FFT, which uses \(O(d\log d)\) edges to link \(d\) sources to \(d\) sinks.

To make the accounting concrete, the paper gives small illustrative numbers: a naive dense connection between two levels for \(d=4\) implies \(16\) edges of which \(4\) are unavoidable, leaving \(12\) free/trainable positions. A particular five-stage sparse factorization shown in the paper uses only \(10\) edges yet still achieves dense final connectivity. The butterfly topology can do even better for the same \(d\), using only \(8\) edges while maintaining reachability. These examples demonstrate how factorization can reduce the total number of edges (trainable positions) while still composing to a dense \(\bm{R}\).

# Design desiderata from the transmission viewpoint

The layered-graph, information-transmission perspective points to a small set of clear desiderata when crafting sparse factorizations for dense transforms:

1. Dense connectivity: the layered graph must provide a path from each source node in level \(1\) to each sink node in level \(m+1\). Without this, some entries of \(\bm{R}\) are structurally zero and the transform cannot be dense.

2. Minimum free edges: subject to orthogonality and full-rank constraints, the total number of trainable nonzeros (free edges) should be as small as possible. Reducing this count improves parameter efficiency and reduces overfitting risk.

3. Orthogonality-compatible patterns: the sparsity pattern should admit orthogonal parametrizations of the factors or otherwise be compatible with the algebraic constraints orthogonality imposes. Some naive sparse patterns cannot be realized by orthogonal matrices, or do so only with many constrained (non-trainable) entries; good designs make it straightforward to enforce orthogonality locally (for example, by grouping into small orthogonal blocks such as \(2\times 2\) rotations).

4. Computational practicality: since factorization increases the number of operator applications per forward/backward pass, the chosen pattern should admit efficient multiplication (fast kernels, structured permutations, blocked operations) to keep training time reasonable.

Viewed through these desiderata, the problem of designing parameter-efficient dense orthogonal transforms becomes a network-topology problem: pick the per-stage adjacency so that the graph attains global reachability while keeping the extra degrees of freedom (trainable edges) low, and do so with patterns that can be realized as orthogonal factors and multiplied efficiently. The butterfly topology is one concrete solution that satisfies these desiderata well: it achieves dense reachability with \(O(d\log d)\) edges, its components can be implemented using small orthogonal blocks (e.g., \(2\times 2\) rotations), and it corresponds to efficient staged mixes that are computationally friendly.

The information-transmission view therefore turns an algebraic matrix-factorization problem into an intuitive combinatorial and architectural design problem, providing clear principles for searching sparse factorizations that achieve dense composed behavior while being parameter-efficient.

Butterfly Structures and Fast Transforms

# Cooley-Tukey FFT and recursive decomposition

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm is a canonical example showing how a seemingly dense linear transform can be computed with far fewer operations by exploiting recursive structure. At a high level (as used in the paper), the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey algorithm reduces an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform (DFT) into two $\tfrac{N}{2}$-point DFTs and then combines their outputs. Repeating this divide-and-conquer decomposition recursively produces a computation that is much cheaper than performing the full dense matrix multiplication directly.

This recursive decomposition is important because it induces a specific, regular sparsity pattern in the intermediate linear maps used to implement the transform. Rather than thinking of the DFT as a completely dense $N\times N$ matrix, Cooley–Tukey expresses it as a product of a small number of sparse matrices. Each sparse factor only mixes limited pairs or blocks of coordinates, and the composition of these few sparse steps produces the global, dense mixing of the full transform.

In the paper’s perspective, this recursive structure is the origin of the butterfly topology: the repeated splitting and recombining at different scales gives rise to layers of pairwise (or small-block) interactions whose arrangement across stages is precisely the butterfly pattern. The practical consequence is that transforms that appear globally mixing (like the Fourier transform) can be implemented as the product of $O(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $O(d)$ nonzeros, instead of a single dense $O(d^2)$ matrix.

# Butterfly graphs: topology and mixing behavior

A butterfly graph is the natural network-topology representation of the factorization produced by Cooley–Tukey–style decompositions. Imagine $d$ source nodes on the left and $d$ sink nodes on the right, and between them a sequence of intermediate levels (one level per sparse factor). A butterfly topology arranges these levels so that, although each node only connects to a small number of nodes in the next level, after passing through all levels every source can reach every sink. In other words, locally sparse connections composed across stages produce global reachability.

Viewing matrix factorization as information transmission (the paper’s framing), each sparse factor corresponds to the adjacency between two adjacent levels in a layered network. Dense connectivity from all sources to all sinks means information from any source node has at least one path to any sink node. The butterfly topology achieves this with very few edges: instead of naively connecting every pair across adjacent levels (which would cost $O(d^2)$ edges per level), the butterfly arranges $\log d$ levels where each level has only $O(d)$ edges, and the sequence of levels together enables every-to-every communication.

This mixing behavior is what makes butterfly graphs powerful: the global mixing (dense effective matrix) is obtained by composing a small number of local mixing steps. From a parameter-efficiency point of view, this is precisely the desideratum when trying to represent dense transforms with few trainable parameters.

# Butterfly factors, components, and recursive construction

The paper formalizes the basic local mixing building block as a butterfly factor. For even $k$ (in particular when $k$ is a power of two), a butterfly factor of size $k$ is written as

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]

where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ controls the diagonal entries within the $2\times 2$ block structure. These $k\times k$ blocks are the elementary pairwise mixing units: they mix pairs of coordinates in a structured interleaving across stages.

Given these local blocks, the full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ (for $d=2^N$) is constructed recursively. At each recursion level one forms block-diagonal components made of many copies of the small butterfly factors and composes them across scales:

\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1\!\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2\!\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)
\end{bmatrix}
= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}\!\left(d,\tfrac{d}{2}\right)\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]

where each butterfly component is a block-diagonal matrix of many copies of $\bm{B}^F(k)$. Concretely, the component at block size $k$ is denoted

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\ldots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big),
\]

so each component mixes within disjoint length-$k$ blocks but the permutation and recursive composition ensure that, collectively, all coordinates are mixed across the entire vector.

This recursive product viewpoint makes the factorization explicit: the full transform is the product of $\log d$ (or more generally $m$) sparse block-diagonal components, each consisting of $d/k$ repeated $k\times k$ butterfly factors. Each component has only $O(d)$ nonzeros, and composing $m$ such components yields a dense matrix whose effective sparsity pattern corresponds to the layered butterfly graph.

# Relationship to permutations and block structure

One convenient way to describe butterfly components is as permutations of very simple local mixing blocks. The $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ component is a block-diagonal matrix of $2\times 2$ blocks; intuitively, it mixes fixed adjacent pairs of coordinates. Higher-level components (larger $k$) are simply permutations of these $2\times 2$ mixing patterns at coarser scales: their nonzero patterns are permutations of the base pattern. That is, every component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ can be obtained from $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ by permuting rows and columns blockwise.

This permutation viewpoint is important because it explains both why the repeated local pairwise operations eventually mix distant coordinates and why every component can be parameterized in the same simple manner. It also supports the block generalization used in BOFT: replacing scalar diagonal entries by $b\times b$ blocks yields block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each nonzero entry is itself a small matrix. By parameterizing the smallest blocks (for instance each $2b\times 2b$ constituent block) to be orthogonal, the entire blockwise-permuted components remain orthogonal matrices. Permutation structure thus lets one reuse a single small parameterization pattern across levels while accomplishing global reordering and mixing.

# Why butterfly achieves global mixing efficiently

From the information-transmission perspective introduced in the paper, two desiderata guide the design of sparse factorizations that yield effective dense transforms: (i) dense connectivity — every source must be able to reach every sink after the sequence of sparse stages, and (ii) minimum free edges — the factorization should use as few trainable connections as possible subject to orthogonality constraints.

A single dense orthogonal matrix trivially satisfies (i) but pays $O(d^2)$ in free (trainable) parameters. A block-diagonal factorization reduces parameters but breaks global connectivity: coordinates in different blocks never interact, so the composed transform cannot be dense. The butterfly achieves both desiderata simultaneously: it provides dense connectivity across stages but uses only $O(d\log d)$ edges in total. Concretely, a butterfly factorization with $m=\log d$ components each containing $O(d)$ nonzeros yields a total of $O(d\log d)$ nonzeros, yet when composed those sparse stages produce a dense mapping from inputs to outputs.

There is also a structural reason tied to orthogonality. Orthogonal factors must each be full-rank and thus have at least $d$ nonzeros (in a certain combinatorial sense), but the butterfly achieves dense overall behavior while keeping each factor close to this minimum per-level complexity. In practice, the butterfly uses a carefully arranged set of pairwise (or small-block) orthogonal rotations so that local rank requirements are met while global mixing accumulates across levels. This trade-off — many light-weight mixing stages rather than one heavy-weight dense stage — is why butterfly structures are so efficient at producing global mixing.

# Recovering classic transforms: Fourier, cosine, Hadamard

A striking feature of the butterfly architecture is that many classical fast linear transforms are special cases of butterfly factorizations. The Cooley–Tukey radix-2 realization of the discrete Fourier transform is the canonical example: it explicitly expresses the DFT as the product of sparse butterfly components (plus diagonal twiddle factors and permutations). Similarly, other fast linear transforms such as discrete cosine transforms and the Hadamard transform admit factorizations whose nonzero patterns align with butterfly-like stagewise pairwise mixing.

Because these classical transforms decompose into repeated local mixing and permutations, a properly configured butterfly factorization can exactly represent them. The paper emphasizes that the butterfly structure, together with suitable choices of the small block parameters and permutations, can perfectly recover many fast transforms. This property is what grounds the argument that a butterfly-parameterized orthogonal family contains useful and widely employed structured transforms, and why such parameterizations are likely to provide beneficial inductive bias when used for finetuning: the parameter space includes transforms with known good global mixing properties.

# Implications for parameter-efficient dense transforms

Translating the butterfly idea into parameter-efficient dense transforms has direct practical consequences in model adaptation. If a dense orthogonal transform on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is represented directly, it requires $O(d^2)$ parameters. In contrast, composing $m$ orthogonal butterfly components of block size $b$ leads to a parameter count that scales linearly in $d$ and linearly in the number of components: for BOFT (the orthogonal butterfly finetuning parameterization in the paper) the number of effective trainable parameters is

\[
\text{parameters} = \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

Taking the pure butterfly case with $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ gives $O(d\log d)$ parameters, a huge reduction compared to $O(d^2)$. Compared to the block-diagonal OFT (which has $O(bd)$ parameters for block size $b$ but cannot achieve dense transforms unless $b=d$), the butterfly offers a way to realize dense orthogonal transforms with parameter cost closer to the block-diagonal case while retaining global expressivity.

This efficiency enables practical trade-offs between expressivity and regularity. The butterfly parameterization is not the full orthogonal group, but it contains many structured and useful transforms and can be made arbitrarily more expressive by increasing $m$ and/or $b$. In particular, BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as a special case (when $m=1$) and the full dense orthogonal parameterization as another special case (when $m=1$ and $b=d$). Thus the butterfly construction gives a smooth family of parameterizations from minimal, highly regular transforms toward full expressivity, all while maintaining favorable parameter scaling.

In finetuning contexts, this means one can obtain dense, globally mixing orthogonal updates to pretrained weights without paying the $O(d^2)$ parameter cost. The butterfly structure provides a compact, structured hypothesis class that both recovers many useful transforms (which encourages good inductive bias) and admits efficient optimization and initialization strategies (for example, identity initialization by setting each orthogonal component to identity so the initial transform equals the pretrained model).

In summary, butterfly graphs and their associated sparse factorization turn the problem of representing dense global linear maps into a staged information-transmission design problem. By exploiting recursive decomposition, permutations, and small orthogonal local blocks, butterfly factorizations achieve global mixing with only $O(d\log d)$ nonzeros, and thereby provide a principled route to parameter-efficient dense transforms in practical finetuning applications.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Method and Parameterization

# BOFT definition: product of orthogonal butterfly components

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly) parameterizes the orthogonal finetuning matrix as a product of multiple structured, sparse, orthogonal factors. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, BOFT replaces the usual finetuned forward pass
with
\[
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where the learnable orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is written as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
Each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a sparse block-structured matrix whose nonzero pattern is a permuted block-diagonal arrangement of small dense blocks; the superscript $b$ indicates a block-size parameter that we will explain below. Every component is constrained to be orthogonal:
\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d
\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,m.
\]

This multiplicative factorization does two things at once. First, by composing many sparse orthogonal factors we can represent a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ while keeping the number of trainable parameters much smaller than a fully unconstrained $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. Second, because every factor is orthogonal, the overall transform preserves the spectral properties of the pretrained weight matrix (multiplying on the left by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ does not change singular values of $\bm{W}^0$), an attribute that benefits stability and generalization.

# Ensuring orthogonality via 2x2 rotations or Cayley on blocks

To keep each factor orthogonal during optimization we build each component from small orthogonal blocks that are easy to parameterize. The simplest building block is a $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotation (or reflection), which can be parameterized directly by a single angle. Another widely used way to parameterize orthogonal matrices is the Cayley transform: given a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, the Cayley map
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}
\]
produces an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ (assuming the inverse exists). BOFT uses these facts at the small-block level: each small dense block inside a butterfly component is parameterized so that the block stays orthogonal throughout training. In practice this is implemented by using $2\times 2$ rotations (or their block generalizations) or by Cayley-transform parameterizations for the small blocks.

The reason this works well is that the nonzero pattern of every butterfly component is a permutation of the same set of small blocks: if we can ensure orthogonality on the smallest unit (e.g., each $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ block), then a suitable permutation and block-diagonal assembly produces a full component that is orthogonal. This reduces the orthogonality maintenance to many small, numerically stable orthogonal parameterizations rather than trying to enforce orthogonality on a huge dense matrix directly.

# Block butterfly extension with block size b

BOFT generalizes the radix-2 butterfly by grouping contiguous scalar entries into blocks of size $b$. Formally, a block-butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors but with each scalar replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix. The scalar butterfly factor for an even size $k$ is written as
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
and in the block extension each entry of the diagonal vectors $\bm{d}_i$ becomes a $b\times b$ block. The block parameter $b$ therefore controls the granularity: small $b$ yields many sparse scalar-level connections (more factors needed to achieve density), while larger $b$ increases the per-factor parameter budget (fewer factors needed). Each $2b\times 2b$ block can be parameterized orthogonally (for example, via Cayley on a $2b\times 2b$ skew-symmetric matrix), which guarantees the block-butterfly component is orthogonal. Because butterfly components at different scales are permutations or reshaping of the $2b\times 2b$ block pattern, the same block-level orthogonal parameterization can be used for all components.

The block extension enables a smooth trade-off between parameter count and computational cost: choosing larger $b$ reduces the number of blocks and hence the number of components required for a given expressivity; choosing smaller $b$ (down to $b=2$) reduces parameters per component and yields the classic sparse butterfly connectivity that is most parameter-efficient asymptotically.

# Choosing the number of factors m to achieve density

The number of multiplicative factors $m$ controls how much information can be mixed across coordinates and therefore whether the composed matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is dense (i.e., every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate). There is a direct relationship between $m$, $b$, and whether the composition yields dense connectivity: the deeper the product (larger $m$), the more opportunities for information to flow across the $d$ coordinates via the sparse edges of each component.

A convenient guideline from the butterfly construction is that a block-butterfly built from components at scales down to blocks of size $b$ requires about
\[
m \approx \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)
\]
components to yield a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. In the common scalar-butterfly case $b=2$ (the radix-2 case), this reduces to $m\approx \log d$, which gives the familiar $O(d\log d)$ connectivity of classical FFT-style butterflies. Thus, to make $\bm{R}(m,b)$ dense you increase $m$ until the composition spans all coordinates; the exact minimal $m$ depends on $d$ and the chosen block granularity $b$, but the logarithmic dependence in $d$ is the key property that gives BOFT its parameter efficiency.

There is an important practical trade-off encoded in the choice of $m$ and $b$. Increasing $m$ (more factors) lowers the number of parameters needed to realize dense connectivity, because each factor can be made very sparse; but more factors increase the per-step compute cost (more matrix multiplications during training). Increasing $b$ (larger blocks) reduces the number of blocks and can reduce $m$ needed to reach density, but each block then contains more parameters. BOFT exposes this trade-off so a practitioner can tune $(m,b)$ for the desired balance of parameter budget and training-time cost.

# Parameter count: about \frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m per d-by-n layer

A convenient closed-form expression for the effective number of trainable parameters BOFT introduces per finetuned linear layer of size $d\times n$ is
\[
\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
This formula captures the dependence on block size $b$, hidden dimension $d$, and number of factors $m$. Intuitively, each butterfly component contains $d$ degrees of freedom distributed into many small orthogonal blocks; across $m$ components these contribute linearly in $m$. The factor $(b-1)/2$ encodes the per-block degrees of freedom after enforcing orthogonality at the block level (orthogonality removes some scalar degrees of freedom within each block). The paper reports this expression as the effective number of trainable parameters for BOFT($m,b$).

Two helpful concrete specializations illustrate the scaling:

- If we take the canonical butterfly case $b=2$ and choose $m\approx \log d$ to obtain a dense $\bm{R}$, then
  \[
  \text{params}\approx \tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)\,d\,\log d = \tfrac{1}{2} d\log d = O(d\log d),
  \]
  giving the compact asymptotic parameter count that is much smaller than the $O(d^2)$ of a full orthogonal matrix.

- If we set $m=1$ (only a single component) and choose block size $b$, the formula becomes
  \[
  \text{params}\approx \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d,
  \]
  which matches the cost of a block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (see the next section).

Because this parameter count measures the effective trainable degrees of freedom BOFT adds to a layer, it is the right quantity to compare to other parameter-efficient schemes when budgeting adaptation parameters.

# Special cases: BOFT(1,b) equals block-diagonal OFT; BOFT(1,d) equals full OFT

BOFT subsumes OFT and its block-diagonal variant as particular choices of $(m,b)$:

- BOFT$(1,b)$ (a single orthogonal component with block granularity $b$) is exactly the block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning used in the original OFT work when that work partitioned the $d$ coordinates into blocks of size $b$ and applied an independent orthogonal on each block. Thus BOFT$(1,b)$ recovers block-diagonal OFT.

- BOFT$(1,d)$ (single factor with block size $d$) corresponds to a single dense orthogonal factor of full dimension and therefore recovers the unconstrained full-orthogonal OFT (the original OFT formulation where the learnable $\bm{R}$ is a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix).

These identifications make BOFT a genuine generalization: by varying $m$ and $b$ we can smoothly move between extreme regularity (identity-like transforms when $m$ is small and blocks are small) and full expressivity (full orthogonal when $m$ and $b$ are chosen to permit it).

# Dense orthogonal with O(d \log d) parameters using butterfly (m \approx \log d, b = 2)

The most parameter-efficient dense option arises when we choose the scalar butterfly ($b=2$) and take roughly $m\approx \log d$ components. In that setting BOFT constructs a classical radix-2 butterfly factorization: each component is highly sparse but the product of $m$ such components mixes information across all $d$ coordinates. Using the parameter-count formula,
\[
\text{params}\approx \tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)\,d\,\log d=\tfrac{1}{2}\,d\log d,
\]
so BOFT realizes a dense orthogonal matrix with only $O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters rather than $O(d^2)$. This is the key efficiency gain of BOFT: the dense connectivity is achieved by composing few sparse orthogonal factors that are inexpensive to store. The logs here are base-2 in the radix-2 construction, but the asymptotic logarithmic dependence is the central message.

Because the butterfly factorization can exactly realize many classic fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard-like), BOFT’s sparse factors have an inductive structure that is often a good compromise between expressive power and regularizing structure. Empirically, BOFT with $b=2$ and $m\approx \log d$ achieves much better parameter efficiency than block-diagonal OFT with the same parameter budget.

# Merging BOFT into the base model after training for zero inference overhead

One practical advantage of BOFT is that the learned orthogonal factors can be merged into the pretrained weight matrix at the end of finetuning, producing a single static weight matrix for inference. Specifically, once BOFT has learned $\bm{R}(m,b)$, we can form the merged weight
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{final}} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
and store $\bm{W}^{\text{final}}$ as the new frozen weights of the layer. This multiplication is done once after finetuning; at inference time no extra factorized multiplication is needed and there is no additional latency compared to standard finetuning. In other words, BOFT’s training-time multiplicative structure does not impose inference-time penalties: the model used for inference is identical to the familiar linear layer with a single dense weight matrix.

This property follows from BOFT’s multiplicative form and the fact that every factor is independent of the input at inference time. It lets BOFT enjoy parameter-efficiency during finetuning (fewer learned degrees of freedom to store and potentially less overfitting), while preserving the practical deployment simplicity of standard finetuning: after the one-time merge step the runtime is identical to a model that had been trained normally.

# Summary and practical remarks

BOFT provides a principled, parameter-efficient generalization of orthogonal finetuning by composing many small orthogonal butterfly components. Ensuring orthogonality via $2\times 2$ rotations or Cayley transforms at the block level makes the approach numerically stable and simple to implement. The block size $b$ and the number of factors $m$ give a concrete knob to trade parameter count against computational cost and expressivity: BOFT$(1,b)$ recovers block-diagonal OFT, BOFT$(1,d)$ recovers full OFT, and the scalar butterfly $b=2$ with $m\approx\log d$ yields a dense orthogonal transform with $O(d\log d)$ parameters. Finally, BOFT-trained orthogonal factors can be merged into the pretrained weights after finetuning so that inference incurs no additional overhead. These characteristics make BOFT a flexible, efficient, and practical method for orthogonal finetuning of large foundation models.

Training BOFT in Practice

# Identity initialization for all butterfly components
A central practical design choice in BOFT is to start finetuning from the exact pretrained model so that the finetuned model does not deviate abruptly from the pretrained solution. The paper enforces this by initializing every butterfly component to represent an identity mapping. Concretely, BOFT parameterizes each orthogonal butterfly component via a Cayley parameterization (see next section); identity initialization is achieved by setting the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter to zero so that each component equals the identity. With this initialization the composed orthogonal modifier
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}
\]
starts as the identity matrix, and the finetuned forward pass
\[
\mathbf{z}=\big(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\big)^\top \mathbf{x}
\]
is exactly the pretrained forward pass at step zero. This zero (identity) start is analogous to zero-initializing additive updates (e.g., LoRA) and is important both for gentle adaptation and for stable optimization in early steps.

In implementation, ensure the layers that parameterize the small orthogonal blocks are initialized to yield identity blocks. When using the Cayley transform the skew-symmetric parameter tensors should be initialized to all zeros; when using explicit 2×2 rotations, initialize their angles to zero.

# Maintaining orthogonality with stable Cayley updates
BOFT enforces orthogonality of every butterfly component throughout training. The paper follows prior OFT work and uses the Cayley parameterization for this purpose. Using a skew-symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (so \(\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}\)), the Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\]
When each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is constructed from (block-)Cayley-parameterized 2×2 or 2b×2b orthogonal blocks, orthogonality of each component — and thus of the composed \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) — is guaranteed at every update. The Cayley parameterization also admits the identity initialization by setting \(\mathbf{Q}=0\).

From an implementation standpoint, maintain the skew-symmetric constraint on the learned parameter \(\mathbf{Q}\). The simplest approach (as used in the paper) is to parameterize an unconstrained tensor \(\mathbf{A}\) and set \(\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^\top\) before applying the Cayley formula. This keeps the optimization parameter space unconstrained while guaranteeing orthogonality for the effective factors. Because the Cayley transform requires computing \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) for each block, implement these inverses at the block level (2×2 or 2b×2b) rather than on the full \(d\times d\) matrix; blocks are small, so inversions are cheap and numerically stable.

Note that by construction BOFT preserves important spectral properties: multiplying \(\mathbf{W}^0\) on the left by an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) does not change the singular values of \(\mathbf{W}^0\). The paper highlights this preservation as a contributor to training stability.

# Multiplicative dropout on butterfly blocks for regularization
Because BOFT updates weights multiplicatively (left-multiplying the frozen pretrained \(\mathbf{W}^0\) by \(\mathbf{R}\)), standard additive Dropout does not directly apply. The paper introduces a multiplicative Dropout mechanism tailored to BOFT that respects the factorized, block-wise structure of butterfly components.

Concretely, multiplicative Dropout randomly selects a percentage \(p_1\) of the butterfly components and, independently within each retained component, a percentage \(p_2\) of its block-diagonal subblocks. For every selected component or subblock, the method temporarily replaces that component/subblock by the identity block for that training step. Because each block is orthogonal and identity blocks are orthogonal, this substitution preserves the orthogonality constraint while stochastically reducing the effective capacity of \(\mathbf{R}\) during training. At the extremes this resembles turning off entire factor layers or turning off subblocks inside factors.

Implementation notes from the paper: perform multiplicative Dropout at training time only; keep the identity initialization and Cayley parameterization intact so dropped blocks are simply bypassed by identity. The multiplicative Dropout provides a structured regularization that mirrors how LoRA uses Dropout on additive updates but is compatible with the multiplicative, orthogonal update regime of BOFT.

# Integration points in Transformers: attention, MLP, embeddings
BOFT is a layer-wise reparameterization: it replaces or augments a pretrained linear layer \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) by left-multiplying with an orthogonal modifier \(\mathbf{R}\) so the effective weight becomes \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). This design makes the integration points straightforward in standard Transformer-style architectures, because Transformers are composed of many linear projections.

The natural integration points are any linear projection whose left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix corresponds to a neuron-space transform. Typical candidates include the Q/K/V/output projections in attention, the dense layers inside MLP blocks, and (where applicable) projection layers used by embeddings or token output heads. The BOFT paper applies this same left-multiplication idea across layers in its empirical study on vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models; in all cases each finetuned linear layer shares its layer-specific orthogonal modifier acting on the neuron's dimension.

Because BOFT modifies the neuron representation by an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) shared across neurons in the same layer, it preserves the geometric relations among neurons (pairwise angles) while allowing task-specific rotation/refinement. The paper also emphasizes that BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as BOFT(1,b) and the full orthogonal OFT as BOFT(1,d), so integration is consistent with prior OFT placements.

# Handling non power-of-two dimensions: padding and permutations (limitations noted)
The BOFT construction in the paper is defined assuming \(d=2^N\) and uses radix-2 butterfly components. The formal definition of a butterfly factor \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) and the recursive construction rely on \(k\) being a power of two. The paper therefore treats the power-of-two assumption as part of the canonical BOFT design and builds a block-butterfly generalization where each scalar entry in the base butterfly becomes a \(b\times b\) matrix, enabling BOFT(m,b) for various block sizes.

The paper does not present a detailed, prescriptive algorithm for all non-power-of-two \(d\). In practice, when \(d\) is not a power of two the options discussed or implied by the BOFT formulation are: (1) use a block size \(b\) such that the block partitioning aligns with \(d\), i.e., work with block-butterfly components where the block tiled structure matches the layer dimension; or (2) fall back to BOFT with fewer factor layers (for example BOFT(1,b), which is block-diagonal OFT) if an exact radix-2 decomposition is not feasible. The paper states the power-of-two assumption explicitly and reports BOFT as a family parameterized by \(m\) and \(b\), but does not formalize additional mechanisms like padding or dynamic permutation for arbitrary \(d\). Treat this as a practical limitation highlighted in the paper.

# Compute and memory costs: factor count versus runtime
BOFT trades parameter count for additional (but modest) runtime computation during training. The paper gives an explicit formula for the number of effective trainable parameters for a linear layer of size \(d\times n\) under BOFT:
\[
\text{effective trainable parameters} = \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
\]
where \(m\) is the number of butterfly components used and \(b\) is the block width in the block-butterfly components. In the canonical butterfly setting (\(m=\log d\) and \(b=2\)) this becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, a substantial reduction from full orthogonal OFT's \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters and comparable or favorable relative to other PEFT methods.

The trade-off is runtime during training: composing \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) requires applying \(m\) block-sparse orthogonal factors to the pretrained weight (or to activations, depending on implementation). Because each factor is sparse and highly structured (block-diagonal with small dense blocks), the per-factor cost is modest; however, more factors \(m\) increases the number of such small multiplies performed per forward/backward pass. The paper notes the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT but emphasizes that after finetuning the learned orthogonal matrices can be merged into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) so that there is no extra inference latency.

Memory-wise, BOFT stores the small Cayley parameters per small block rather than a full \(d\times d\) matrix. This yields the parameter savings described above; intermediate memory for applying factors can be kept low by applying factors in-place at the block level and avoiding materializing full \(d\times d\) dense intermediates.

# Practical optimizers, learning rates, and training schedules
The paper frames BOFT within the general practice of gentle finetuning: start from identity initialization and use conservative optimization so the pretrained knowledge is preserved. While the paper does not prescribe a single optimizer or a fixed learning rate schedule, it highlights two general principles that inform practical choices:

1. Start from identity (zero Cayley parameters) so the model begins at the pretrained solution. This makes relatively small initial updates desirable.

2. Orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), which empirically improves training stability. This property relaxes some instability modes but does not replace the general need for careful optimizer and learning-rate selection typical of finetuning large models.

Accordingly, the paper follows standard finetuning practice: modest learning rates relative to training-from-scratch, typical finetuning schedules (fewer total steps than pretraining), and use of regularization such as multiplicative Dropout described above. The Cayley parameterization and identity init remove the need for ad hoc weight decay on the orthogonal parameters in many runs, but practitioners should still tune optimization hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay applied to any non-orthogonal parameters, and dropout rates \(p_1,p_2\)) per task and model scale. The paper demonstrates BOFT's robustness across vision, language, and diffusion finetuning tasks under these general finetuning practices.

# Merging weights for deployment and compatibility with quantization
One clear operational advantage of BOFT is that after finetuning you can merge the learned orthogonal modifier into the original pretrained weight matrix: compute the product \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) and store that dense matrix back into the model. The paper stresses this point: although BOFT incurs modest extra computation during training (due to applying the sequence of butterfly components), the merged model has no inference-time overhead compared to the original pretrained model.

Merging yields a standard dense weight matrix and therefore removes any need to ship BOFT factor objects to production. The paper explicitly highlights that there is no additional inference latency after merging. The paper does not include a dedicated study of post-merge quantization, but because the merged weights are ordinary dense parameters, they can be evaluated with the same deployment/quantization pipelines used for the underlying model; practitioners should validate quantization behavior for their target precisions and hardware as usual.

--- 

Summary (what to implement from the paper)
- Initialize every butterfly Cayley parameter to zero so each factor starts as identity and \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\mathbf{I}\).
- Maintain orthogonality by Cayley parameterization at the block level (2×2 or 2b×2b), computing \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^\top\) and \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\).
- Use multiplicative Dropout by randomly replacing a fraction \(p_1\) of factors and \(p_2\) of subblocks with identity during training.
- Apply \(\mathbf{R}\) as a left-multiplicative modifier to layer weight matrices (attention projections, MLP dense layers, embeddings/projections), i.e. change \(\mathbf{W}^0\) to \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\).
- Choose \(m\) and \(b\) to trade off parameter count and runtime: canonical butterfly yields \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters when \(m=\log d,b=2\); effective parameters scale as \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m\).
- After finetuning, merge \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) to eliminate inference overhead.

The paper documents empirical success of this recipe across vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models and highlights the practical benefits (parameter efficiency, spectral-property preservation, and post-training mergeability) while noting the modest additional training-time cost and the power-of-two formulation caveat.

Expressivity, Theory, and Properties of BOFT

# Empirical approximation of random orthogonal matrices

One way to evaluate how expressive a parameterization of orthogonal matrices is, is to try to approximate a randomly sampled dense orthogonal matrix and measure how well the parameterized family can match it as the number of trainable parameters grows. The paper carries out exactly this experiment for matrices of size $512\times 512$. Concretely, a target orthogonal matrix is sampled (using a standard random orthogonal generator) and various BOFT and OFT parameterizations are optimized to minimize some matrix approximation loss; results are averaged over 10 random seeds. In the reported plot the x-axis is the number of effective trainable parameters and the y-axis is the approximation error.

The reported outcome is that BOFT achieves lower approximation error for the same parameter budget than the block-diagonal OFT. Several concrete patterns from the experiment are highlighted in the paper. First, BOFT variants with small block size $b$ but many factors $m$ (for example BOFT($9,2$)) outperform block-diagonal OFT with much larger block size (for example BOFT($1,16$) which equals block-diagonal OFT with $b=16$), while using far fewer parameters. Second, configurations that trade block size for depth — e.g. BOFT($6,4$) vs BOFT($2,16$) — show that additional factor layers can compensate for smaller blocks, achieving similar approximation error with reduced parameter count.

Intuitively, this empirical test demonstrates that the structured factorization used by BOFT can express rich transformations more parameter-efficiently than a single block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. The experiment does not assert that BOFT always matches the expressivity of a full dense orthogonal matrix with arbitrarily few parameters; rather, it shows that BOFT explores a more powerful subspace than block-diagonal OFT at the same per-layer block size, yielding better approximation-per-parameter in practice.

# BOFT versus OFT expressivity at fixed b

To compare expressivity at a fixed block size $b$, it is important to understand what each parameterization permits. Block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ parameterizes orthogonal matrices of the form
\[
\mathrm{diag}(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_r),\qquad R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\quad br=d,
\]
so the $d$-dimensional space is partitioned into $r$ independent subspaces and there is no mixing across these blocks. Any orthogonal transformation representable by block-diagonal OFT cannot exchange information between different blocks; this is its fundamental limitation.

BOFT with the same block size $b$ instead composes multiple block-structured orthogonal components in series. Each component is a block-butterfly component (a blockwise permutation of $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotations at the smallest granularity) and the overall transform is their product:
\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
with each $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ orthogonal. Even when each component individually looks block-structured, their composition permits interactions across the original block partition: a given input coordinate may propagate through successive components to affect coordinates that lie in different blocks. This repeated mixing is what gives BOFT strictly greater expressive power than a single block-diagonal OFT with the same $b$.

The paper formalizes this comparison in Theorem 1 by stating that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Practically, this means for a fixed budget of per-block parameterization, adding more factors (increasing $m$) allows BOFT to approximate transformations that block-diagonal OFT cannot represent, because BOFT introduces routings and permutations between the blocks across layers. The empirical approximation experiment described above illustrates this: BOFT configurations with small $b$ but larger $m$ achieve better approximations than block-diagonal OFT with larger $b$ but $m=1$.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchy: multiplying butterfly matrices to span O(d)

Theorem 1 in the paper also points toward a pathway to full expressivity: by composing many appropriately chosen butterfly matrices (and their transposes), one can span the entire orthogonal group $O(d)$. The paper phrases a constructive idea: multiply butterfly matrices in a layered pattern such as
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d),
\]
where each $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ is itself a butterfly matrix. More compactly, the paper defines a generalized composition
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
    = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
and notes that when $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the resulting family can represent all orthogonal matrices. In the literature this multilayer composition is called a kaleidoscope hierarchy: repeated patterns of structured components (butterfly factors and their transposes) can emulate arbitrary orthogonal transforms.

This statement has two implications. First, the butterfly parameterization is not inherently constrained to be a tiny subset of $O(d)$ — with enough layers and appropriate choices it can be made universal. Second, universality requires growth in the number of factors and therefore in the number of trainable parameters; practical BOFT configurations deliberately stay below this universal regime to retain parameter-efficiency and regularization. Thus the kaleidoscope hierarchy establishes an upper bound (full representational capacity) but also motivates why intermediate BOFT configurations are attractive: they sit between trivial (identity) and universal (full $O(d)$) extremes.

# Trade-off between expressivity and regularity for generalization

A recurring theme in finetuning large pretrained models is that more expressivity is not always better. Full finetuning (free parameters everywhere) indeed offers universal function approximation for the model’s parametrized family, but in practice it often leads to overfitting, instability, or poorer generalization on limited downstream data. The paper emphasizes that a good finetuning parameterization should balance expressivity (the ability to represent useful task-specific changes) and regularity (constraints that preserve pretraining structure and prevent runaway adaptation).

BOFT embodies such a trade-off: by constraining fine adjustments to be orthogonal and further structuring those orthogonal matrices with butterfly factorization, BOFT restricts the hypothesis class to a structured submanifold of $O(d)$. This restriction preserves important pretraining geometry (see the spectral discussion below) and limits degrees of freedom compared to unrestricted orthogonal or dense finetuning, but is more flexible than block-diagonal OFT because successive butterfly layers allow controlled cross-block mixing. Empirically and intuitively, this constrained but expressive family provides a sweet spot: it admits meaningful transformations that adapt the model while keeping the finetuned weights close (in a geometric and spectral sense) to the pretrained ones, which helps generalization.

In short, BOFT deliberately enlarges OFT’s hypothesis class relative to block-diagonal OFT but retains inductive structure relative to full orthogonal matrices; this intermediate capacity aligns well with the regularization needs of finetuning.

# Spectral properties: preserving spectral norm and stability

A central advantage of orthogonal finetuning (OFT and BOFT) is the preservation of singular values of the pretrained weight matrix when the learned transform is left-multiplied. Let the pretrained weight matrix be decomposed by singular value decomposition (SVD) as
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
where $\bm{U},\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values. Multiplying on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ yields the finetuned weight
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Because $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is orthogonal, the singular values of $\bm{W}$ are exactly the diagonal entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$ — that is, the singular value spectrum is preserved. In particular, the spectral norm (largest singular value) is unchanged.

This spectral preservation has two important consequences. First, it promotes training stability: the Lipschitz properties of linear layers are controlled because multiplying by $\bm{R}$ does not amplify or shrink the singular values. Second, it tends to preserve pretraining geometry and prevents extreme conditioning changes that can harm gradient propagation or encourage overfitting. The paper notes that such preservation is a known contributor to improved generalization and stability in practice.

Because BOFT enforces orthogonality at each component and in their product, it inherits all of these spectral benefits while also providing a parameter-efficient factorization.

# Bilinear similarity view: learning \(w^{0\top}_i R x\)

BOFT can be interpreted from a geometric similarity perspective. Consider a linear layer with pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ whose columns are the neurons $\bm{w}^0_i$. The finetuned forward pass for the $i$-th output neuron is
\[
z_i = \big(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\big)^\top \bm{x} = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Equivalently, one can write the scoring form as a bilinear similarity between the pretrained neuron and the input, parameterized by $\bm{R}$:
\[
z_i = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{R}\,\bm{x},
\]
(we may absorb transposes depending on the convention for row/column vectors). From this viewpoint, BOFT is learning a bilinear form $\bm{R}$ that modifies how pretrained neurons compare to inputs, but does so under the strong constraint that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal (and structured by butterfly factorization).

This connects BOFT to two classical concepts. First, distance/metric learning: $\bm{R}$ acts like a learned linear transform in which distances or inner products are measured; constraining $\bm{R}$ shapes how similarities are computed without changing length scales (orthogonality preserves norms). Second, bilinear forms in linear algebra: the parameterization is directly a bilinear operator that maps $(\bm{w}^0_i,\bm{x})$ to a scalar. The orthogonality constraint and structured factorization together make this bilinear learning problem highly regularized, which again helps stability and generalization.

# Structured inductive bias and links to classic transforms

The butterfly structure is not an arbitrary sparsity pattern — it is the same structural topology that underlies many classical fast linear transforms such as the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform and the Hadamard transform. These transforms achieve global mixing of coordinates through a small number of local mixing stages arranged in a butterfly graph. By building BOFT using blockwise butterfly components, the parameterization implicitly favors transforms that can be composed from these local stages and permutations.

This structural similarity is an inductive bias: BOFT more readily represents transforms that resemble or can be composed into classical fast transforms. That inductive bias is desirable for several reasons discussed in the paper. First, many natural signal transforms (frequency decompositions, orthogonal basis changes) have compact representations in butterfly-like factorizations; thus BOFT can more easily capture useful reparameterizations that align with typical structure in data. Second, because BOFT can recover many classic transforms exactly, it is not imposing an overly restrictive prior — rather, it's imposing a prior that matches known useful bases. This contrasts with block-diagonal OFT which cannot recover such global transforms because it never mixes across blocks.

In practice, such a structured inductive bias guides optimization toward solutions that generalize better: by constraining the class of allowable adjustments to those that are locally composable and globally expressive, BOFT reduces the risk of overfitting while retaining the ability to implement semantically meaningful changes.

# Implicit bias from sparse factorization

Finally, beyond explicit structural priors, sparse matrix factorization itself introduces implicit biases in learning dynamics and solutions. When a dense orthogonal matrix is represented as the product of sparse orthogonal factors, the optimization landscape and gradient trajectories change: parameter updates are distributed across the factors and must cooperate to create global effects. This cooperative, factorized representation often induces an implicit regularization effect — certain dense matrices are easier to reach through coordinated updates of sparse factors, while others are effectively discouraged.

The paper points to this phenomenon as another source of BOFT’s good generalization. Sparse factorization constrains the set of feasible transformations in a way that is not merely a count of parameters but also a constraint on how representations can be composed during optimization. This kind of implicit bias has been studied in other settings (and is referenced in the paper), and in the context of BOFT it helps explain why BOFT can achieve strong finetuning performance with fewer effective parameters than unconstrained alternatives.

In summary, BOFT combines explicit structural biases (butterfly factorization, orthogonality) with implicit optimization biases (factorized sparse parameter updates) to produce a parameter-efficient, spectrally stable, and expressive finetuning family. It sits between the extremes of block-diagonal OFT (high regularity, limited cross-block mixing) and full orthogonal or dense finetuning (maximal expressivity but weak regularization), often delivering a better practical trade-off for downstream adaptation.

Positioning BOFT Among PEFT Methods

# LoRA: additive low rank versus BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal update
LoRA and BOFT represent two fundamentally different reparameterization philosophies for parameter-efficient finetuning. LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix with an additive low-rank correction: if the pretrained weight is $\bm{W}^0$, LoRA replaces it at finetuning time with $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where $\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ are trainable and $r\ll d,n$ is a chosen rank. Because the trainable degrees of freedom scale with $r$, LoRA’s parameter cost grows roughly like $\mathcal{O}(dr)$ (plus $\mathcal{O}(rn)$), and practitioners control the budget by choosing $r$ small. The additive nature means the pretrained weight and the update coexist linearly; initialization of the added terms at zero yields zero deviation at the start of finetuning.

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) follows a multiplicative strategy: the finetuned weight is obtained by left-multiplying the frozen pretrained matrix by an orthogonal matrix, i.e. $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal. This multiplicative, purely orthogonal transform preserves geometric structure of the pretrained weights: it preserves pairwise angles among neurons and preserves singular values of $\bm{W}^0$ (spectral norm). In the original OFT, $\bm{R}$ was trained as a full or block-diagonal orthogonal matrix; BOFT makes this multiplicative orthogonal idea parameter-efficient by factorizing $\bm{R}$ into a product of sparse orthogonal (butterfly) components so that a dense orthogonal transform can be represented with far fewer parameters (for the butterfly instantiation BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters).

The practical consequences of these differences are clear. LoRA’s additive low-rank update directly expands the weight space along a low-dimensional affine subspace; it is extremely flexible for capturing low-complexity adjustments and is simple to implement. BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal update instead restricts updates to orthogonal transformations of the input neurons, enforcing a strong geometric regularity while enabling dense global changes via structured sparse factorization. Thus LoRA favors compact, low-rank residual changes, while BOFT favors globally structured, norm- and angle-preserving transformations implemented in a parameter-efficient way.

# Adapters and prompt tuning: parameter location and latency trade-offs
Adapter tuning and prompt tuning take distinct approaches to where and how the additional parameters are introduced. Adapter tuning inserts small trainable modules into the network’s architecture (typically between layers or inside attention/MLP blocks). Those modules change the computational graph: they are executed at inference time and therefore can introduce extra compute and latency, depending on their size and placement. Prompt tuning appends trainable prefix tokens to inputs (or trains continuous prefixes); typically it does not change the model architecture nor add new layers, but it changes the inputs the model receives. Prompt tuning therefore often incurs negligible architectural overhead at inference (the model remains unchanged) but modifies the token stream the model processes.

Model finetuning methods that keep the architecture unchanged—such as OFT and BOFT—have an important practical advantage: no inference-time architectural change is required. As the paper emphasizes, model finetuning “introduces no inference latency.” In particular, because BOFT and OFT are implemented by multiplying the pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix, after training the learned orthogonal factor can be merged into the frozen weights (matrix multiply once) and the deployed model runs with the same architecture and same computational pattern as the original pretrained model. By contrast, adapters that add modules typically cannot be merged away and thus can increase inference cost; prompt tuning keeps inference cost nearly identical but has fundamentally different representational trade-offs (it manipulates inputs rather than weights).

Therefore, when choosing among adapters, prompt tuning, and architecture-preserving finetuning (OFT/BOFT), a practitioner must trade off where they want capacity (inside the model vs. input space), whether adding inference-time computation is acceptable, and whether the downstream task demands transformations that are more naturally expressed as added modules or as global transformations of the existing weights.

# Stability and generalization: orthogonality versus low-rank updates
Orthogonality injects two related stability- and generalization-promoting constraints. First, an orthogonal left-multiplication $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ preserves the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. Because many optimization pathologies are linked to spectral properties of weight matrices, preserving the spectral norm helps maintain stable forward and backward signal magnitudes; the paper explicitly notes that orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm and that this property has been tied to improved stability and generalization in prior work. Second, orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neuron vectors, which aligns with geometric priors (hyperspherical energy) that have been associated with generalization in pretrained representations. Empirically, OFT showed strong generalizability and more stable training on text-to-image diffusion finetuning, which motivates using orthogonal constraints more broadly.

Low-rank additive updates like LoRA do not generally preserve spectral norms or pairwise angles of pretrained weight matrices. They expand the function class in a different direction—by allowing direct additive modifications in a low-dimensional subspace—which can be highly effective when the needed adaptation is well captured by a low-rank change. But because they are additive and unconstrained in norm, they can move the model’s spectral properties away from their pretrained regime, potentially harming stability if not handled carefully (for example, via small learning rates or explicit regularization).

BOFT aims to combine the best of both worlds within the orthogonal finetuning paradigm: it inherits orthogonality’s spectral and angular preservation (hence favorable stability and generalization traits) while dramatically reducing parameter cost through structured factorization. The paper’s experiments and theoretical discussion show BOFT can represent dense orthogonal transforms with far fewer trainable parameters than full OFT, and that the structured inductive bias (butterfly factorization) often helps generalization further because it aligns with classical fast transforms.

# Latency and merging: why model finetuning has no inference overhead
A practical appeal of the model finetuning family (which includes OFT and BOFT) is the ability to merge finetuning parameters into the original model weights for deployment. Because OFT/BOFT produce a modified weight of the form $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight, one can compute the product $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ once at the end of training and replace the original layer weight with $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$. After this merge, the model’s runtime computational graph and per-inference FLOPs are identical to the pretrained baseline—hence “no inference latency.”

This contrasts with adapter modules, which are inserted into the forward pass and must be executed at inference time, so they typically add runtime cost that cannot be removed unless a separate merge strategy is engineered. Prompt tuning generally has no extra internal modules to execute, so inference latency is also essentially unchanged, but the two approaches express changes in different parts of the model (inputs vs. weights).

One caveat the paper notes is training-time overhead. BOFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal factors; during training each forward and backward pass must apply these factors, so BOFT’s training runtime is slightly larger than OFT’s if OFT uses a single block-diagonal factor. However, this training overhead is temporary: at deployment the factors are folded into $\bm{W}^0$ and there is no inference cost penalty.

# Butterfly-based sparse training from scratch versus BOFT for finetuning
Butterfly parameterizations are not novel in isolation: prior work has used butterfly matrices to parameterize linear transforms for training networks from scratch, to accelerate matrix-vector multiplication, and to learn fast transforms. Those prior approaches typically reparameterize the network’s own weight matrices directly and train all parameters from initialization (often to realize a compact, structured model that is cheap at inference). Examples cited in the paper include works that learn fast linear transforms or use butterfly factorizations to stabilize RNNs and approximate kernels.

BOFT departs from that “training-from-scratch” usage in an important way. Instead of making the network’s primary weight matrices themselves be butterfly-structured and training from zero, BOFT keeps the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ frozen and applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ on top of it. The butterfly structure is applied to parameterize $\bm{R}$, not to replace $\bm{W}^0$. This distinction matters for both goals and constraints: BOFT leverages pretraining (and the pretrained spectral/representational structure), and the butterfly factors are intended to be small, trainable modifications that preserve geometry while increasing expressivity with a tiny parameter budget. In contrast, butterfly-based sparse training from scratch attempts to learn a performant model entirely within the structured parameter family.

Thus the two lines of work are complementary: prior butterfly parameterizations aim to produce small, fast models ab initio, while BOFT applies butterfly structure as a way to parameterize a compact orthogonal finetuning transformation on top of a large pretrained model. BOFT’s strategy is tailored to the modern practical setting where pretraining is expensive and finetuning must be parameter- and compute-efficient while preserving pretrained knowledge.

# When to choose BOFT, OFT, or LoRA under different constraints
Choosing among BOFT, OFT (block-diagonal or full), and LoRA involves trading off expressivity, parameter budget, training/runtime overhead, and the desired inductive bias:

- If strict budget on finetunable parameters is the primary constraint and the adaptation can plausibly be captured by a low-rank residual, LoRA (with a very small rank $r$) is an excellent default: it is simple, has very small parameter count scaling like $\mathcal{O}(dr)$, and is widely used. LoRA is particularly attractive when you want minimal increase in training-time computation and extremely compact adapters.

- If preserving spectral properties and neuron-angle relationships is critical for stability or generalization (the paper cites success of OFT in text-to-image diffusion finetuning), and parameter budget is large enough to allow a full orthogonal transform, then OFT (full orthogonal) is the natural choice: it enforces the orthogonality constraint directly and is maximally expressive among orthogonal transforms. However, full OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often infeasible.

- If you want the benefits of orthogonality (spectral/angle preservation) but cannot afford $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, BOFT offers a middle ground: by factorizing $\bm{R}$ into sparse orthogonal butterfly components BOFT can represent dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters (BOFT($m,b$) uses roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters; the canonical butterfly instantiation yields $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ when $m=\log d$ and $b=2$). BOFT therefore is a recommended choice when you need dense, globally expressive orthogonal transformations but have only modest parameter budgets.

- If you can only afford very small block-diagonal orthogonal transforms (for example BOFT with $m=1$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT), block-diagonal OFT provides a simple orthogonal alternative with parameter cost $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, where $b$ is block size. This is an intermediate choice that trades expressiveness for parameter savings.

Finally, temporal/compute constraints during training matter: BOFT has slightly higher training-time cost because it composes multiple sparse factors each step, whereas low-rank additive updates are typically cheaper per step. So when tight training-time budgets are binding and inference deployment constraints are loose, LoRA may be preferable; when inference matching the pretrained model exactly (no extra components) combined with orthogonality-preserving finetuning is important, BOFT is especially compelling.

# Composability and potential hybrid methods
The BOFT framework is naturally composable and admits generalizations that blur clear boundaries between orthogonal factorization and other parameter-efficient ideas. The paper shows that BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as a special case (BOFT($1,b$) = block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$) and that more general compositions of butterfly matrices (a kaleidoscope hierarchy) can approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices when sufficiently many factors are composed. Concretely, composing alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes can span the full orthogonal group in the limit described in the paper.

This structural modularity suggests several axes for hybrid designs. One can imagine combining a BOFT multiplicative orthogonal factor with a small additive low-rank LoRA-style term, creating a hybrid of multiplicative geometric regularity plus additive fine corrections. Alternatively, one can vary block size $b$ and depth $m$ per layer to allocate parameter budget where it matters most, or mix BOFT transforms on some layers with LoRA on others. Because BOFT factors are orthogonal and can be merged into the pretrained weights at the end, any such hybrid that produces a final weight matrix in closed form can likewise be merged before deployment.

The paper itself notes that sparse factorizations (like the butterfly family) and low-rank factorizations are both major families of structured matrices; exploiting both families together is a natural future direction. Any concrete hybrid should be designed with the desiderata the paper emphasizes—dense connectivity (to allow global information flow) and minimum free edges (parameter efficiency under orthogonality)—in mind.

In short, BOFT’s factorized orthogonal parameterization is not a single opaque gadget but a flexible building block. It can be tuned (via $m$, $b$) or combined with other PEFT primitives to match particular constraints: aggressive parameter compression, strict spectral preservation, minimal inference overhead, or minimal per-step training cost.

# Summary
BOFT sits at a distinctive point in the PEFT design space: like OFT it leverages multiplicative orthogonal updates that preserve angles and spectra and thus offer stability and generalization benefits, but it uses butterfly factorization to achieve parameter efficiency comparable to, and in many regimes better than, low-rank additive schemes. Compared to adapters and prompt tuning, BOFT (as a model-fineturning method) preserves the model architecture at inference time and therefore can be merged with zero inference overhead. Its training-time cost is modestly higher because of the composed sparse factors, and its inductive bias (butterfly sparsity) both reduces the hypothesis class and aligns with classical fast transforms—an alignment the paper argues explains part of BOFT’s empirical success. Practitioners should select between LoRA, OFT, and BOFT by weighing parameter budget, need for spectral/angle preservation, acceptable training overhead, and whether inference latency must remain identical to the pretrained baseline; the composable nature of BOFT also leaves room for hybrid strategies that combine the strengths of multiple PEFT families.

Empirical Studies Across Vision, Language, and Generative Modeling

# Evaluation setup and datasets across modalities

The empirical evaluation in the paper is organized to test BOFT across three broad classes of foundation models: large vision transformers (ViTs), large language models (LLMs), and text-to-image diffusion models. The central experimental goal is to measure how well BOFT adapts pretrained models to downstream tasks while using far fewer trainable parameters than a full orthogonal reparameterization, and to compare BOFT to other parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) approaches such as the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and additive low-rank methods like LoRA. Across modalities the same high-level experimental design is used: start from a frozen pretrained model, insert a layerwise multiplicative orthogonal transform parameterized by BOFT, finetune only the BOFT parameters (with identity initialization), and measure downstream task performance, generalization behavior and training stability.

Although the paper covers multiple concrete downstream tasks in vision and language (classification/transfer for ViTs, instruction tuning and classification for LLMs, and controllable generation for diffusion models), the emphasis is on cross-modal trends rather than a single benchmark. For each modality the evaluation measures include: (i) task accuracy or generation quality on held-out validation/test splits, (ii) the number of additional trainable parameters introduced by the PEFT method, (iii) training stability indicators (e.g., whether training converges reliably and whether learning produces large spectral excursions of weights), and (iv) qualitative assessments when applicable (e.g., generated images for diffusion models). Comparisons are performed against OFT (both full and block-diagonal variants), LoRA-style low-rank additive updates, and other common PEFT baselines to place BOFT’s parameter–performance trade-offs in context.

Two methodological points are common across experiments. First, BOFT always uses identity initialization for its orthogonal components (the Cayley-parametrized skew-symmetric blocks are initialized to zero), ensuring the pretrained model behavior is preserved at the start of finetuning. Second, BOFT experiments include a multiplicative Dropout variant (see the description below) to assess regularization benefits and robustness against overfitting. Results reported in the paper center on how BOFT’s structural parameterization (the choices of the number of butterfly stages $m$ and block size $b$) affects accuracy, parameter cost and stability.

# Language tasks: instruction tuning and classification

In the language experiments the objective is to adapt large pretrained LLMs to new tasks such as instruction-following and text classification while minimizing the number of tuned parameters. The paper positions BOFT as an orthogonal, multiplicative alternative to additive low-rank updates (LoRA) and to the prior OFT approach. BOFT operates by inserting a layer-wise orthogonal transform $\bm{R}(m,b)$ so that a pretrained layer with weight $\bm{W}^0$ performs $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ during finetuning; only the parameters inside the factorized orthogonal $\bm{R}(m,b)$ are updated.

Two properties of BOFT are relevant to language tasks. First, since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved under the multiplicative update, which the paper argues aids training stability for large models. Second, BOFT’s parameter count can be tuned to a desired budget: a BOFT configuration is denoted BOFT$(m,b)$ and introduces roughly
\[
\text{effective trainable parameters} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
per $d\times n$ layer (the derivation follows from parameterizing the $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks used in each butterfly component). When $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT attains the classical butterfly parameter scaling $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$.

Empirically, the paper reports that BOFT achieves strong performance on instruction tuning and classification tasks while using far fewer parameters than a full OFT reparameterization. Qualitatively, BOFT retains the pretrained model’s behavior at initialization and adapts smoothly, avoiding the instability sometimes observed when tuning many dense parameters. When comparing BOFT to LoRA-style updates, the multiplicative orthogonal formulation preserves spectral properties and yields different inductive biases that can be advantageous for certain language adaptation scenarios (e.g., where preserving pairwise neuron angles and overall geometry of representations helps generalization).

# Vision tasks: transfer with ViTs and related benchmarks

For vision, the experiments adapt large pretrained Vision Transformers (ViTs) to downstream transfer tasks. The experimental pattern mirrors that for language: freeze the backbone weights, apply BOFT layerwise to transform neuron coordinates multiplicatively, and finetune only the BOFT parameters. The paper emphasizes two experimental themes for vision models. First is parameter efficiency: by selecting BOFT configurations with small $b$ and larger $m$ (more butterfly stages), one can reach dense effective orthogonal transforms while using dramatically fewer training parameters than full OFT. Second is generalization and stability: BOFT’s structured parameterization forms a constrained hypothesis class that often improves transfer performance compared to either highly reduced block-diagonal OFT or purely additive low-rank adaptations.

The conceptual reasons behind these empirical patterns are explained in the paper. Butterfly factorizations reuse sparse, structured mixing (the butterfly components) to enable global mixing across neuron dimensions with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzero parameters, so a BOFT-configured ViT can realize complex rotations of neuron axes without the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost of an unconstrained orthogonal matrix. In practice the paper finds that BOFT configurations that more closely approximate dense orthogonal matrices (larger total number of stages $m$) tend to close the performance gap with full OFT while still remaining parameter-efficient; conversely, block-diagonal OFT (BOFT with $m=1$) is weaker at transfer unless block sizes are large. This trade-off is borne out consistently across the vision experiments reported.

# Diffusion models: controllable generation and adaptation

One of the motivating application areas for OFT was controllable finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models, and the paper extends that line of work by applying BOFT to diffusion model adaptation. The BOFT formulation is applied to the diffusion U-Net (or other linear layers within the diffusion backbone) so that the pretrained generative model can be fine‑adapted to new concepts or controllable attributes by learning orthogonal transforms that rotate internal neuron coordinates while leaving pretrained weights otherwise frozen.

Qualitatively, the paper emphasizes two benefits of BOFT in diffusion adaptation. First, the orthogonal multiplicative update preserves spectral properties of each finetuned linear layer, which contributes to the stability of the iterative sampling dynamics in diffusion generation and helps avoid mode collapse or training instabilities that might arise if large unconstrained updates were applied directly. Second, because BOFT can produce a dense orthogonal matrix from a compact factorization, it can realize subtle, global changes to the model’s internal geometry that are useful for controllable generation tasks (for instance, aligning representations to encode a new attribute) while still fitting within tight parameter budgets.

The paper reports that BOFT matches or improves upon OFT and other PEFT methods for controllable generation problems while using far fewer trainable parameters in many configurations. It also includes qualitative examples (generated images) to illustrate that BOFT preserves generation fidelity while enabling control.

# Ablations on m, b, and sparsity patterns

A central ablation axis in the experiments is the BOFT structural hyperparameters: the number of butterfly components (stages) $m$ and the internal block size $b$ used in the block-butterfly components. These two integers trade off expressivity, sparsity, and parameter count. The paper makes several consistent observations:

- For fixed total parameter budget, BOFT configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (more stages composed of small $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks) tend to be more expressive than those with larger $b$ and fewer stages. This aligns with the information transmission view: more stages yield denser effective connectivity from inputs to outputs while each stage retains sparsity.

- BOFT($1,b$) equals block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. BOFT($1,d$) recovers the full dense OFT. On the other hand, BOFT($\log \tfrac{2d}{b}, b$) implements the full block-butterfly factorization producing a dense orthogonal matrix but with parameter complexity governed by $m$ and $b$.

- In the random-matrix approximation simulation discussed in the paper (approximating a random $512\times 512$ orthogonal), BOFT showed clear parameter-efficiency advantages compared to block-diagonal OFT: for example, BOFT$(9,2)$ approximated the target matrix better than BOFT$(1,16)$ while using far fewer parameters. This empirical ablation demonstrates that increasing $m$ (more butterfly factors) is an effective way to increase expressivity without quadratic parameter growth.

- The paper also considers sparsity-pattern comparisons conceptually: block-diagonal sparsity (the OFT default) inhibits any mixing across blocks and hence cannot approximate many classic linear transforms, while the butterfly sparsity pattern is specifically chosen to maximize dense connectivity across log-many stages with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzeros. The information transmission perspective formalizes these trade-offs.

These ablations guide practical choices: if the objective is to approach the expressivity of full OFT with limited parameters, prefer more stages $m$ with small block size $b$; if one wants extreme parameter parsimony and is willing to limit mixing, a larger $b$ with fewer stages can be chosen.

# Parameter–performance and stability trade-offs

Across modalities the empirical picture in the paper highlights a multi-faceted trade-off between parameter count, task performance (accuracy or generation quality), and training stability. BOFT sits in an intermediate location in this space: it is more parameter-efficient than full OFT, more expressive than block-diagonal OFT at comparable parameter counts, and imposes a structured prior that often improves generalization relative to unconstrained dense updates.

Two mechanisms underlie BOFT’s favorable stability behavior. First, orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ preserves singular values of the pretrained layer $\bm{W}^0$, preventing sudden inflation of spectral norms that can destabilize optimization (this is a property shared with OFT but lost in most additive low-rank schemes). Second, the structured factorization restricts the hypothesis class, reducing the tendency to overfit when downstream data are limited. To combat overfitting further, the paper introduces a multiplicative Dropout variant for BOFT: instead of dropping individual additive weights, the multiplicative Dropout randomly selects a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and a fraction $p_2$ of diagonal blocks inside each chosen component and replaces them with identity matrices during training. This is a natural analogue of dropout for multiplicative, orthogonality-preserving updates.

Empirically the authors report that BOFT achieves a more stable finetuning trajectory than many high-capacity alternatives, and that the multiplicative Dropout option provides an effective regularizer in low-data regimes. Because BOFT can be tuned to any desired parameter budget via $(m,b)$, practitioners can directly explore the parameter–performance frontier to meet application constraints.

# Orthogonal interpolation behavior and qualitative analyses

An intriguing behavior that arises from BOFT’s matrix-factorization view is a weight interpolation phenomenon. Because the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is the product of several orthogonal butterfly components and each component is initialized to identity, one can inspect intermediate reconstructions or perform smooth interpolations by scaling or partially applying stages. The paper calls attention to this feature (and refers to a figure illustrating it), and emphasizes two practical uses.

First, the interpolation gives a diagnostic for how much each stage contributes to the final transformation. Early stages that remain near identity indicate layers where the pretrained geometry is already suitable for the downstream task; stages that undergo larger rotations indicate where significant adaptation occurs. Second, interpolation can be used to produce ensembles or gradual transitions between pretrained and adapted behavior: because all factors are orthogonal, linear interpolation in the factor parameters can lead to smooth changes in model outputs without abrupt spectral shocks.

Qualitative analyses in the diffusion experiments demonstrate that BOFT preserves generation fidelity while enabling controlled changes; in vision and language tasks, analyses of learned rotations and representation changes indicate that BOFT learns compact, distributed adjustments rather than concentrated changes confined to small subspaces. These qualitative findings support the argument that BOFT’s structured multiplicative updates yield semantically meaningful and stable adaptations.

# Comparisons with OFT, LoRA, and other PEFT baselines

The paper situates BOFT relative to two dominant PEFT paradigms. The first is OFT: multiplicative reparameterization by an orthogonal matrix learned per layer. OFT preserves geometric relations among neurons and spectral norms but suffers from high parameter cost when the orthogonal matrix is dense. OFT’s earlier proposal to use block-diagonal orthogonal matrices reduces parameters but loses global expressivity. BOFT generalizes OFT in two ways: BOFT($1,b$) recovers block-diagonal OFT, and BOFT($1,d$) recovers full OFT. More generally, BOFT($m,b$) spans a family of structured orthogonal matrices that interpolate between identity and full orthogonality while controlling parameter cost. Theorem 1 in the paper formalizes that suitably composed butterfly matrices can approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices, and that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size.

The second paradigm is LoRA-style additive low-rank reparameterizations. LoRA updates the pretrained weight as $\bm{W}^0+\Delta$ with $\Delta$ low-rank. This additive approach has low parameter count and is widely used in language finetuning. The paper contrasts LoRA and BOFT conceptually: LoRA’s inductive bias is low-rank additive corrections, whereas BOFT’s bias is orthogonal multiplicative transformations that preserve spectral properties and neuron-angle geometry. These different priors lead to different practical behaviors: LoRA is flexible and parameter-slim for many language tasks; BOFT tends to provide better-specified geometric preservation that can aid generalization and stability in settings where preserving pretrained geometry matters.

Empirically, the paper states that BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art PEFT methods by a considerable margin across the studied tasks while using competitive parameter budgets. It also emphasizes that BOFT’s runtime overhead during training is slightly larger than OFT due to repeated sparse matrix multiplications, but that there is zero inference latency after merging the learned orthogonal matrices into the pretrained weights.

# Concluding remarks about empirical findings

Taken together, the empirical study in the paper demonstrates that the butterfly parameterization (BOFT) provides an effective and practical way to bring orthogonal finetuning to resource‑constrained settings. The information transmission perspective motivates the butterfly sparsity pattern as an efficient means to achieve dense connectivity with few trainable entries, and experiments across vision, language and generative models corroborate the intuition: BOFT attains a favorable parameter–performance–stability trade-off, subsumes prior OFT variants, and offers useful diagnostic and interpolation behaviors thanks to its factorized structure.

Practical takeaways for a practitioner: (i) choose BOFT$(m,b)$ to match a target parameter budget—smaller $b$ and larger $m$ tend to be more expressive per parameter; (ii) keep identity initialization so the pretrained model is preserved at start of finetuning; (iii) consider multiplicative Dropout $(p_1,p_2)$ to regularize in low-data settings; and (iv) exploit the orthogonal interpolation diagnostics to inspect where adaptation is occurring. These guidelines summarize the empirical lessons distilled in the paper’s cross‑modal evaluation.

A Practitioner’s Playbook for BOFT

# Choosing m and b for a target parameter budget

BOFT parameterizes the finetuning orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal block-butterfly components with block size $b$. A compact expression for the number of effective trainable parameters per finetuned linear layer of input dimension $d$ follows directly from the paper:

- Effective trainable parameters per layer
  \[
  P(m,b;d)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m.
  \]

This formula is the single most useful tool for matching BOFT to a parameter budget. To plan allocation, proceed as follows.

First determine the total parameter budget you want to spend on BOFT across the model, call it $P_{\text{total}}$. If you want to finetune $L$ layers with identical $d$, a simple equal-allocation starting point is to assign $P_{\text{per-layer}} = P_{\text{total}}/L$ and solve for an integer pair $(m,b)$ satisfying
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d\,m \approx P_{\text{per-layer}}.
\]
Because $b\ge 2$ and $m$ is usually an integer (number of butterfly components), there is a small discrete search space: choose candidate $b$ values (powers of two are natural since butterfly structures are defined for power-of-two widths) and compute the required $m$ as
\[
m \approx \frac{2P_{\text{per-layer}}}{(b-1)d}.
\]
Round $m$ up to an integer and re-check the resulting parameter cost. Conversely, if you fix $m$ (for example to target a certain expressivity depth), compute $b$ from the same equation.

Interpretation and trade-offs. Two knobs control the trade-off between expressivity and parameter-efficiency:

- Increasing $m$ (more butterfly components in series) generally increases expressivity: more factors allow richer dense orthogonal matrices to be realized.
- Increasing $b$ (larger blocks) increases per-component parameter cost but also increases local block expressiveness; BOFT with $m=1$ and block size $b$ reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, while BOFT with $m=\log(2d/b)$ and a small $b$ can produce a dense orthogonal matrix.

Put simply, if you have a very tight parameter budget, prefer smaller $b$ and compensate with a larger $m$ until your budget allows a satisfactory approximation. If you can afford many parameters, increasing $b$ (or choosing $m$ large enough to produce a dense orthogonal) will give a larger hypothesis class. Use the above equation to enumerate feasible (m,b) pairs and pick points along the trade-off curve that match your policy on expressivity vs. compactness.

# Layer selection strategies in Transformers

BOFT is formulated as a layer-wise transform: for a frozen pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ it replaces the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is shared across neurons in that layer. This makes BOFT naturally applicable to the many linear projections inside Transformer blocks (attention projection matrices and MLP dense layers).

Because BOFT parameter cost scales linearly with the layer width $d$, a straightforward budgeting heuristic rooted in the parameter formula is to prioritize layers where $d$ is largest or where model behavior is most sensitive. Concretely:

- If you must limit the number of BOFT-equipped layers, prioritize the largest linear layers (largest $d$) when you want the highest absolute expressive impact per layer, because each such layer can alter a larger subspace of the representation.
- If you must minimize parameter overhead while preserving broad coverage, consider applying BOFT with smaller $(m,b)$ to many layers rather than heavy BOFT on a few layers; because costs scale with $d$, per-layer choices can be made proportional to $d$ to equalize parameter spend across the model.
- Remember BOFT($1,b$) is equivalent to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT($1,d$) is the full OFT. Use BOFT($1,b$) when you want a cheap, minimally invasive transform; increase $m$ when block-diagonal expressivity becomes a bottleneck.

The paper demonstrates BOFT across vision and language Transformer architectures; use those experiments as confirmation that BOFT works applied to standard linear projections, but allocate parameters using the exact per-layer formula above to meet budgets.

# Regularization: multiplicative dropout rates and schedules

BOFT uses a multiplicative dropout that is conceptually adapted to its multiplicative orthogonal update (additive dropout would not directly apply). The mechanism described in the paper is:

- In each training step (or as a regularization action), randomly pick $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks inside each selected component, and replace those blocks with identity matrices for that step.

This multiplicative dropout is analogous in intent to LoRA’s dropout (it reduces overfitting by stochastically removing parts of the learned transform), but it operates multiplicatively across the product of orthogonal components. The paper presents the mechanism but does not prescribe fixed values for $p_1$ or $p_2$; therefore, treat $(p_1,p_2)$ as regularization hyperparameters to tune per-task.

Practical considerations grounded in the paper:

- Identity initialization plus multiplicative dropout interact well: because BOFT components are initialized to identity (via Cayley parameterization with zero skew-symmetric matrices), multiplicative dropout starting from identity preserves the small-initial-change behavior desirable for finetuning.
- Use multiplicative dropout when you suspect overfitting (small dataset, large BOFT capacity). It plays the same role as dropout in low-rank updates mentioned in the literature and in the paper.

# Optimizer, learning rate, and warmup choices

The paper emphasizes that finetuning typically benefits from small deviations from pretrained weights; practitioners commonly use smaller learning rates for finetuning. From BOFT’s design and the orthogonality constraint:

- Identity initialization: BOFT initializes each Cayley parameter skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ to zero so that $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}=\bm{I}$ at start. This encourages small initial changes and argues for modest learning rates during early training.
- Orthogonality preserves spectral norm: multiplying the pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix preserves its singular values and thus the spectral norm, which the paper notes tends to help training stability. This property reduces one source of sensitivity to learning rates compared to unconstrained multiplicative changes.

Although the paper does not mandate a specific optimizer or numeric hyperparameter values, align your optimizer and LR schedule with standard finetuning practice: prefer conservative initial learning rates, consider short warmup phases to stabilize early steps, and rely on the preserved spectral properties as a safety net when choosing step sizes. Use multiplicative dropout and smaller learning rates together if you observe instability.

# Numerical tips for stable Cayley updates

BOFT enforces orthogonality of each component using the Cayley parameterization:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). The paper gives two immediate operational points that should guide implementation and debugging:

- Identity initialization is achieved by initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. This is the recommended start for finetuning.
- The Cayley form guarantees orthogonality for any skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ for which $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible; the paper uses Cayley because it preserves orthogonality during updates.

Implementation checklist grounded in the paper’s description:

- Ensure explicit skew-symmetry: parametrize only the upper (or lower) triangular entries of $\bm{Q}$ and set the other triangular entries as negatives to enforce $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$ exactly in floating-point arithmetic.
- Compute $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ carefully and monitor the condition number of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ during training; identity initialization keeps $\bm{Q}$ small early on, which reduces risk, but it's prudent to add checks in debug runs to catch numerical instability arising from unexpectedly large $\bm{Q}$ updates.
- Since BOFT factorizes $\bm{R}$ into many small orthogonal blocks (often $2\times 2$ blocks), use exact closed-form formulas where available for small block inverses (e.g., analytic 2x2 inverses) to reduce numerical error, consistent with the paper’s emphasis that butterfly components can be parameterized via small orthogonal blocks.

The paper does not prescribe specific numerical safeguards or precision modes; the above items are the direct numerical implications of using Cayley parameterization and identity initialization.

# Working with quantization and memory-limited settings

Two points in the paper directly impact deployment in constrained environments:

- Training-time cost: BOFT introduces extra matrix multiplications because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a product of $m$ components. The paper notes the runtime overhead during training is slightly larger than OFT (which may matter in memory- or time-limited training environments).
- Inference-time merging: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal factors can be multiplied into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ and the product stored as a single weight matrix. The paper explicitly highlights that there is no additional inference latency after merging; the multiplicative BOFT transform is folded into the model parameters.

Practical workflow consistent with the paper:

- For memory-limited training, be prepared for modest extra compute cost; if training memory is the bottleneck, you can choose smaller $(m,b)$ values or apply BOFT to fewer layers.
- For inference and quantization, first fold the learned $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$:
  \[
  \bm{W}^{\text{final}} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0,
  \]
  and then apply your usual quantization and compression pipeline to $\bm{W}^{\text{final}}$. Because BOFT preserves spectral norms and is orthogonal during training, folding does not introduce new numerical pathologies beyond multiplying the learned factors.
- Because BOFT factors are orthogonal, the merged weights retain the spectral properties highlighted in the paper, which is beneficial for downstream numerical stability under quantization.

# Troubleshooting divergence and poor transfer

The paper identifies several design choices that relate to training stability and generalization, which also suggest a checklist for troubleshooting:

If training diverges or is unstable:
- Confirm identity initialization: ensure Cayley skew-symmetric parameters $\bm{Q}$ were initialized to zero so the initial transform is identity.
- Check the Cayley inversion step: verify $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is being computed and inverted correctly and that $\bm{Q}$ is kept skew-symmetric in parameter updates. Numerical errors in enforcing skew-symmetry or inverting near-singular matrices can cause instability.
- Reduce effective step size: follow finetuning convention and reduce the learning rate. The paper emphasizes small learning-rate finetuning as a general principle and identity initialization for BOFT reduces early deviation risk.
- Use multiplicative dropout: enable or increase the multiplicative dropout rates $(p_1,p_2)$ if overfitting or noisy parameter updates are observed. Multiplicative dropout is the paper’s designed regularizer for BOFT.

If transfer is poor (validation performance stagnates or degrades):
- Expressivity check: BOFT has a trade-off between expressivity and regularity. If BOFT is under-expressive for the task, consider increasing $m$ (more components) or increasing $b$ (larger block size) using the parameter formula to remain within budget.
- Layer choice: ensure BOFT was applied to the layers most relevant to the adaptation task. Because BOFT’s parameter cost is proportional to $d$, misallocating parameters to low-impact layers is wasteful; reallocate to wider or more semantically critical layers.
- Over-regularization: large multiplicative dropout or too-small $(m,b)$ can over-constrain the hypothesis class. Reduce dropout or increase capacity if the model consistently underfits.

These troubleshooting steps are direct consequences of the design principles and empirical observations emphasized in the paper: identity init for safe starts, Cayley parameterization to preserve orthogonality and spectral properties for stability, multiplicative dropout to prevent overfitting, and the explicit parameter-count formula to reason about expressivity/capacity.

# Checklists for training and merging into production

Before you start a BOFT finetune:
- Compute your parameter budget and pick candidate $(m,b)$ pairs using
  \[
  P(m,b;d)=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d\,m
  \]
  per layer. Enumerate feasible allocations across layers.
- Decide which layers will receive BOFT and how many parameters each will get; prefer allocating to widest or most task-relevant linear layers.
- Implement Cayley parameterization for each orthogonal component with explicit skew-symmetric parametrization and set initial $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at step 0.
- Implement multiplicative dropout (stochastic masking of a fraction $p_1$ of butterfly components and a fraction $p_2$ of diagonal blocks within selected components).
- Prepare monitoring code to check orthogonality (e.g., verify $\tilde{\bm{B}}^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}\approx\bm{I}$ per component up to numerical tolerance) and to flag large condition numbers for $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$.

During training:
- Use conservative learning rates and short warmup as required by your finetuning regime; identity initialization plus the spectral-norm-preserving property provide stability but do not replace careful LR choice.
- Monitor validation metrics and orthogonality diagnostics; if training is unstable, first check initialization and Cayley computations before changing model capacity.
- Tune multiplicative dropout rates $(p_1,p_2)$ if you observe overfitting.

Merging into production:
- After finetuning, compute the merged weight for each affected layer:
  \[
  \bm{W}^{\text{final}} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0,
  \]
  and replace $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{W}^{\text{final}}$ in the exported model. This removes any inference-time cost associated with the BOFT product of factors.
- Optionally run your standard post-training quantization and compression pipeline on the merged weights; because BOFT preserves singular values, merged weights will have the same spectral properties as the pretrained $\bm{W}^0$ up to the orthogonal change.
- Validate numerically that merged weights produce identical inference outputs (up to floating-point noise) compared to running the model with BOFT factors applied at inference.

Closing note

The paper’s core engineering levers for practical deployment are the explicit parameter formula, the Cayley orthogonal parameterization with identity initialization, and the multiplicative dropout mechanism. Use the formula to budget and plan layer coverage; exploit identity init and Cayley to preserve pretrained behavior and spectral stability; and use multiplicative dropout to regularize BOFT’s multiplicative updates. Finally, remember that BOFT can be merged into the pretrained model after finetuning so that there is no runtime inference penalty once the finetuned model is deployed.

Limitations, Open Questions, and Future Directions

# Training runtime overhead from multiple factors

BOFT reduces the number of trainable parameters by representing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components,
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
and this factorization is the core of its parameter efficiency: in general BOFT($m,b$) uses about $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters, and the radix-$2$ butterfly case ($m=\log d, b=2$) gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This parameter savings comes at a cost in computation during training. Each forward/backward pass must apply the sequence of $m$ component matrices rather than a single dense left-multiplication, so training-time cost increases in proportion to $m$ (and to the block dimension choices that affect the constant factors).

The paper notes this runtime overhead explicitly and frames it as an open limitation: "the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT" and "how to improve BOFT's training runtime remains an open problem." To understand where the overhead arises and what controls it, it helps to separate two sources. First, factorization increases the number of sequential matrix multiplications in the computational graph: a single dense $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ is replaced by $m$ sparse-block multiplications, each of which may be memory- and compute-bound in different ways. Second, practical implementations may not exploit the sparsity patterns and permutations in butterfly components optimally; naïve narrow-block or per-block operations can incur kernel-launch, memory-traffic, and permutation costs that multiply the raw arithmetic cost.

BOFT does, however, come with mitigating properties that partly soften this limitation. The method uses identity initialization for each component (Cayley parameterization with skew-symmetric blocks initialized at zero), so the forward computation starts close to the pretrained model and allows stable, small updates. Also, after finetuning the sequence of learned orthogonal components can be multiplied into the frozen pretrained model once and for all, eliminating any inference-time overhead: the runtime penalty is strictly a training-time phenomenon. From a practical perspective, therefore, an effective mitigation strategy is to accept some training-time extra cost in return for the parameter, stability, and generalization benefits, while ensuring that the learned product is absorbed into the model for deployment.

For future work aimed at reducing training cost, the paper points to this as an open question. The natural knobs within BOFT that trade compute versus parameters are $m$ and $b$; selecting smaller $m$ or larger $b$ reduces sequential depth at the cost of more parameters. Another axis is implementation: specialized kernels that exploit the butterfly sparsity and permutation structure can reduce overhead (see the section on hardware acceleration). Formulating principled scheduling and batching for the per-component multiplications (so they map to high-throughput GEMM-like work) is a concrete systems research direction rooted in the limitation identified by the paper.

# Topology design beyond butterfly under the transmission view

A central conceptual contribution of the paper is the information transmission view: factorizing $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ is equivalent to allowing information to flow from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes through a $d\times(m+1)$ grid graph where edges exist only between adjacent levels. Under this view the problem of finding a parameter-efficient factorization becomes a question about graph topology: choose edges (i.e., sparsity patterns in the $\bm{B}_i$) so that (i) every source can reach every receiver (dense connectivity) and (ii) the number of free/trainable edges is as small as possible given orthogonality constraints.

The butterfly topology—the one induced by the Cooley–Tukey FFT—meets those desiderata very well: it achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges and permits orthogonal parameterizations via small block orthogonal factors. The paper shows that this topology yields a practical and effective parameterization (BOFT) and that it recovers many classical fast transforms, which suggests useful inductive bias.

However, the paper also explicitly acknowledges that whether the butterfly network is the most efficient topology is unknown. The transmission perspective makes clear that many alternative topologies could be explored. Any competing topology must satisfy the orthogonality constraints on each level (for example, each intermediate factor must be representable as an orthogonal matrix) and the reachability constraint from sources to sinks. Comparing topologies requires measuring both expressivity (what subset of the orthogonal group can the topology represent for a fixed number of trainable entries) and training behavior (stability, generalization).

Investigating alternative topologies is therefore a principled open direction. The information transmission framework provides concrete criteria for comparison: total trainable edges, depth $m$, per-level degree constraints induced by orthogonality, and the combinatorial connectivity that determines whether the composed product can be dense. Network-theory and communication-topology results (as the paper hints by referencing the connection to computer networking) offer a conceptual starting point: topologies designed for broadcasting or all-to-all exchange with low-degree switches may be good candidates. Any exploration must, however, account for the extra algebraic constraints of orthogonality, which are not typical in classical network design problems.

# Automated structure search and learned sparsity patterns

The paper contrasts BOFT's principled butterfly sparsity with the ad hoc block-diagonal sparsity used by OFT, remarking that "how to find a good sparsity pattern remains a mystery." This observation motivates automating the choice of sparsity pattern as an open research axis. Rather than fixing a butterfly or block-diagonal pattern a priori, one could search the space of allowable factorization graphs under the orthogonality constraints and the dense-connectivity requirement.

Several conceptual challenges make this nontrivial. First, parameterizing discrete sparsity choices together with continuous orthogonal parameters yields a mixed discrete–continuous search problem. Second, orthogonality couples entries: for many small patterns to be feasible each factor must be orthogonal, which constrains which edge-sets are admissible. Third, searching at the level of individual matrix entries naively scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ choices, so search efficiency and inductive priors are essential.

Within these constraints, the paper suggests the information transmission framework as a unifying language for such search. Practical algorithms could therefore operate over the space of grid-graph topologies: vary depth $m$, vary per-level degree patterns, learn or optimize permutations between components, or allow learned block sizes $b$. Learning sparsity could take the form of differentiable relaxation of edge presence (so that gradient-based optimization can identify edges with high utility), or structured search over families of graphs (e.g., butterfly-like permutations, butterfly with skip-connections, multistage expander-like graphs). While the paper does not provide concrete search algorithms, it clearly positions automated structure discovery as a promising next step to replace manual choices with learned or optimized sparsity patterns that balance parameter-efficiency, expressivity, and training stability.

# Extensions to non-orthogonal unitary or symplectic updates

BOFT is explicitly an orthogonal finetuning method: each factor is constrained to be orthogonal and the overall update multiplies the pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix, preserving spectral norms and pairwise angles. The paper frames orthogonality as a principled regularizer that preserves pretraining geometry and stabilizes training. An open conceptual direction is whether the same factorization ideas—sparse-factor products derived from transmission topologies—can be applied to other structured matrix groups that enforce different invariants.

Although the paper focuses on the real orthogonal group, the information-transmission and factorization viewpoint is sufficiently general that it naturally raises the question of analogous factorizations for other matrix manifolds. Unitary matrices (complex-valued analogues of orthogonal matrices) preserve Hermitian inner products and would be the natural target when finetuning complex-parametrized layers or when one seeks phase-preserving transforms. Symplectic matrices preserve a bilinear form relevant for Hamiltonian dynamics and certain structured linear maps; they have different algebraic constraints than orthogonal matrices, and parameterizing them via sparse factor products is a distinct technical challenge.

The paper does not develop these extensions, but it indicates the general idea that factorizing a structured matrix into a product of sparse structured components is useful. Extending to other matrix groups would require ensuring each component lies in the target group and that their product can recover a rich enough subset of the group with a compact parameterization. Understanding the algebraic constraints and feasible patterns for those groups—what limited sparsity patterns remain expressive while keeping learnable degrees of freedom low—is a concrete theoretical and practical open question suggested by the BOFT framework.

# Combining BOFT with low-rank or adapters for hybrids

The paper contrasts two major families for parameter-efficient finetuning: sparse-structured multiplicative updates (BOFT/OFT) and additive low-rank updates (LoRA). It observes that sparsity and low-rank are two broad structural priors that achieve parameter efficiency in distinct ways. This observation naturally suggests hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of BOFT and low-rank adapters.

A hybrid could, for example, apply a BOFT multiplicative orthogonal transform together with small low-rank additive updates (or small adapters) where each family covers different kinds of modifications to the pretrained function: BOFT preserves spectrum and global geometry while allowing coordinate mixing, whereas low-rank additives can capture localized shifts in weight space. From the perspective of hypothesis classes, the product-plus-sum parameterization enlarges the set of reachable finetuned weights while allowing the designer to allocate a fixed parameter budget across complementary inductive biases.

The paper does not implement such hybrids, but it provides the conceptual ingredients to motivate them: BOFT preserves singular values and pairwise angles, while LoRA offers an efficient additive update. Empirically evaluating hybrids—how to allocate parameters between multiplicative sparse factors and additive low-rank matrices, how to initialize and regularize each component, and whether multiplicative Dropout and adapter Dropout should be coordinated—are all practical research directions arising from the comparisons made in the paper.

# Hardware acceleration and kernel design for butterfly ops

One of the concrete systems-level limitations the paper highlights is the extra training-time cost of BOFT due to repeated application of multiple sparse components. This limitation points directly to a systems and hardware research agenda: designing kernels and runtime strategies that implement butterfly-layer operations with high throughput.

Butterfly components are highly structured: they consist of small orthogonal blocks arranged with regular permutations and interleavings. These regularities can be exploited by specialized kernels that fuse permutations with block multiplications, avoid unnecessary memory traffic for identity-initialized or near-identity blocks, and batch across many instances of the same block size $b$ and pattern. The paper notes that the learned orthogonal components can be absorbed into the pretrained weights for inference, so the engineering focus is squarely on training efficiency.

Designing such kernels requires attention to memory layout, in-place permutation support, fused compute for the $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks used in Cayley parameterization, and optimal mapping to hardware primitives (SIMD, tensor cores, etc.). The paper frames this as an open problem: improving BOFT's training runtime is left to future work, and hardware-aware implementation is an obvious axis on which progress can be made. From a research standpoint, demonstrating that a BOFT training pipeline with specialized kernels closes the runtime gap with OFT while keeping BOFT's parameter benefits would make the method easier to adopt at scale.

# Applications beyond linear layers and to multi-modal fusion

In the empirical part of the paper BOFT is applied to finetuning large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. These results demonstrate BOFT's versatility across modalities and model classes. Still, several application-driven extensions remain open.

First, BOFT is presented in the paper as a finetuning technique for linear layers (left-multiplying a pretrained weight matrix). Many architectures also contain structured modules: attention projections, normalization affine transforms, embedding matrices, and cross-modal fusion layers. Applying the BOFT parameterization to these varied components raises questions about where multiplicative orthogonal updates are most beneficial. For example, in attention mechanisms the operator being modified sits inside a larger softmax-weighted aggregation; whether preserving spectral properties is as consequential there as in feedforward layers is an empirical question.

Second, modern models increasingly perform multimodal fusion (text+image, audio+video, etc.). The information transmission view that underlies BOFT is well aligned with fusion problems: designing structured transforms that mix channels (or modalities) in a parameter-efficient, well-regularized way is exactly what BOFT does for single-modality linear maps. The paper's demonstrations across modality boundaries suggest BOFT could be adapted to multi-modal fusion layers, but actual design choices—how to structure block sizes $b$ across modality-specific subspaces, whether to tie components across modality-specific branches, and whether to interleave BOFT with modality-specific adapters—remain open research questions.

# Theoretical questions on generalization and implicit bias

BOFT's motivation and partial theoretical analysis in the paper center on the tension between expressivity and regularity. Theorem 1 (informally stated in the paper) shows that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, and the kaleidoscope composition can in principle represent the entire orthogonal group when arranged appropriately. At the same time, the paper argues that excessive expressivity (as in full finetuning) may hurt generalization, while structured parameterizations can improve it by constraining the hypothesis class.

This tension raises several precise theoretical questions that the paper leaves open. How can we quantitatively characterize the hypothesis class induced by BOFT($m,b$)? Can we measure its capacity (e.g., effective dimension, Rademacher complexity) as a function of $m$, $b$, and $d$ and thereby derive sample-complexity or generalization bounds that explain the empirical regularization effect of BOFT? How does the butterfly-induced inductive bias interact with pretraining geometry: does BOFT bias updates toward directions that preserve high-hyperspherical-energy configurations, and can that be formalized?

Another theoretical axis concerns optimization: Cayley parameterization guarantees orthogonality of each factor, but the landscape of the composed objective in the parameters of multiple orthogonal components is nonconvex and structured. Understanding whether factorized orthogonal parameterizations introduce benign landscapes (e.g., absence of spurious local minima under realistic assumptions) or make convergence more challenging is an open question suggested by the empirical stability benefits observed in the paper.

Finally, the paper mentions implicit regularization effects of sparse factorization and prior work on implicit bias from gradient methods. A precise description of the implicit bias induced by BOFT's factorized parameterization—how gradient flow or SGD dynamics prefer certain low-complexity solutions within the BOFT hypothesis class—would deepen theoretical understanding and inform design choices (e.g., how many components $m$ to use for best generalization at a given budget).

Concluding remark

BOFT brings a conceptually simple and empirically strong idea—parameterize dense orthogonal finetuning updates as products of sparse orthogonal factors tied to an information-transmission topology. That idea resolves an important parameter-efficiency bottleneck and opens many complementary directions. The paper is explicit about the main practical limitation (training runtime) and highlights conceptual open questions (alternative topologies, structure discovery, and theoretical understanding). Addressing these issues will require a mix of algebraic insight, optimization theory, automated design, and systems-level engineering—precisely the intersection that makes BOFT a fertile starting point for further research.
