The practical implication is that BOFT offers a family of parameterizations where one can trade a small increase in computation (more stages, hence more matrix multiplications during training) for a large reduction in trainable parameters without sacrificing representational power.

From an optimization and implicit-bias viewpoint, such sparse multiplicative parameterizations often confer favorable dynamics: parameter updates operate locally in each factor and propagate nonlinearly through the product, which can bias training toward solutions that are simpler in factor-space even if they appear complex in the dense-matrix space. The paper notes that such implicit biases have precedent in the literature (e.g., works that study implicit regularization of matrix factorizations). In practice, this means BOFT can find rotations that adapt the pretrained model in task-relevant ways without exploring the entire orthogonal manifold, thereby helping to prevent overfitting while retaining sufficient flexibility.

A central claim of the paper is that using butterfly factorization to parameterize orthogonal finetuning yields strictly greater expressivity than the block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning (OFT) when both use the same block size. To see why, recall how the two parameterizations differ. The block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (which we may denote OFT($1,b$)) represents the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a direct sum of independent $b\times b$ orthogonal blocks. In contrast, BOFT($m,b$) represents $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal “butterfly components” each of which is itself block-structured but with a permutation-like sparsity pattern that mixes coordinates across blocks when several components are multiplied together.

BOFT achieves a principled trade-off between expressivity and regularity by (1) constraining each factor to be orthogonal (preserving spectral properties and conditioning), (2) composing multiple sparse, permuted factors (butterfly components) to enable global mixing with far fewer parameters than a dense matrix, and (3) offering a tunable family of architectures indexed by the number of stages $m$ and block size $b$ so practitioners can dial the balance between compactness and expressiveness.

This bilinear-similarity framing connects orthogonal finetuning to classical metric learning and bilinear-form modeling: one is effectively learning a change of basis or rotation of the input space that best adapts the pretrained neuron directions to the downstream task, but without altering relative lengths. Because $\bm{R}$ participates multiplicatively and globally across neurons, BOFT avoids the per-neuron overfitting that can come from unconstrained additive adjustments, while still enabling coordinated adaptation through the learned rotation.

Taken together, these theoretical and empirical observations explain why BOFT frequently outperforms less-structured parameterizations in finetuning: it preserves important spectral structure, encourages useful global mixing patterns seen in classical transforms, and regularizes learning via sparse multiplicative structure—yielding a practical and principled middle ground between rigidity and uncontrolled flexibility.

Concrete parameter-count comparisons help fix ideas: a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ free parameters; block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters; BOFT($m,b$) uses roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ effective trainable parameters, and the classic butterfly instantiation ($b=2$, $m=\log d$) costs $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. This arithmetic encapsulates the engineering trade-offs: with a modest increase in computation (more stages) BOFT can drastically reduce trainable parameters while retaining or improving approximation power relative to block-diagonal baselines.

Second, BOFT configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (many shallow, highly permuted stages) often achieve better parameter-efficiency than a single-stage block-diagonal OFT with much larger blocks. Concretely, the paper cites illustrative comparisons: BOFT($9,2$) (many stages, minimal block size) gives better expressivity than BOFT($1,16$) (single block-diagonal stage with block size 16) while using far fewer parameters. Likewise, BOFT($6,4$) can match the approximation error of BOFT($2,16$) using substantially fewer effective parameters. These data support the claim that sparsely factorized, multi-stage architectures (butterfly) can represent complex global transformations more compactly than single-stage block-diagonal parameterizations.

The practical takeaway is twofold. First, this hierarchy shows that the butterfly-based family is not intrinsically limited: by increasing depth and alternating transposes one can recover full expressivity if desired. Second, this observation underlines again the trade-off: universal expressivity is attainable but requires many factors (and hence more parameters and computation). For finetuning, more expressivity is not always better because it can harm generalization and stability; therefore the kaleidoscope view highlights how BOFT can be tuned to the appropriate point on the expressivity–regularity curve.

One striking mathematical property of orthogonal finetuning—shared by OFT and BOFT—is preservation of the spectral properties of the pretrained weight matrix. Let the pretrained weight matrix admit the singular value decomposition
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top,
\]
where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with non-negative singular values. Multiplying on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ yields the finetuned weight
\[
\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\,\bm{U})\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Because $\bm{R}\,\bm{U}$ is orthogonal, the singular values (the diagonal entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$) remain unchanged. In particular, the largest singular value—i.e., the spectral norm—of $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved exactly under orthogonal finetuning.

It is important to emphasize the nuanced point the paper makes: higher expressivity is not always better. Full finetuning (the ultimate expressive extreme) often leads to poor generalization despite universality. BOFT’s design intentionally restricts expressivity via structured sparse factors to strike a balance: enough flexibility to solve the downstream task, but not so much that the model abandons the inductive structure learned during pretraining.

Intuitively, the block-diagonal form restricts transformations to act independently on disjoint coordinate groups: no information ever flows between groups because the blocks are isolated. By contrast, the butterfly product interleaves local $2b\times 2b$ (or generally small-block) orthogonal transforms with permutations so that, after multiple stages, any coordinate can influence any other. That is, the product of appropriately permuted small orthogonal blocks yields global mixing while still using only small-block parameters at each stage.

Chapter 8: Theory and Insights: Expressivity, Regularization, and Structure

A major source of BOFT’s practical success, as argued in the paper, is the inductive bias introduced by the butterfly factorization. The sparsity and permutation pattern of butterfly components exactly mirror the communication pattern of classic global transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform and the Hadamard transform (both of which admit butterfly-like fast algorithms). These classic transforms perform global mixing of coordinates via repeated local interactions interleaved with permutations; the butterfly factorization reproduces this multi-stage local-interaction/global-mixing pattern.

The paper reports simulation experiments approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $512\times 512$. The experiments measure approximation error as a function of the number of effective trainable parameters for different BOFT configurations. The trends are clear and instructive.

Second, the bias toward global but structured mixing helps generalization on downstream tasks: rather than allowing arbitrary dense rotations (which may overfit) or forbidding cross-coordinate interaction (which may underfit), the butterfly prior steers learning toward a middle ground favorable for many practical tasks. In other words, BOFT’s structural prior is aligned with known useful transforms, and that alignment is plausibly why BOFT often outperforms both overly constrained block-diagonal OFT and overly flexible dense orthogonal finetuning in experiments.

Why does this matter? Preservation of spectral norms connects directly to training stability and generalization. If a finetuning update expands singular values arbitrarily, it can magnify gradients and destabilize optimization; conversely, orthogonal updates keep the operator norm fixed, which helps avoid runaway scaling and preserves the conditioning that the pretrained model acquired during large-scale pretraining. Empirically and theoretically (as discussed in the paper), such spectral preservation is associated with improved stability and better generalization behavior compared to unconstrained additive updates that may change spectral norms (for example, some low-rank additive schemes).

BOFT inherits this spectral-preserving property because each factor in the butterfly product is constrained to be orthogonal; their product is therefore orthogonal. This is an important structural advantage of orthogonal finetuning families over many alternative PEFT parameterizations: they maintain a crucial global property of the pretrained linear map while allowing flexible reorientation of its action.

First, when we fix the block size $b$ and increase the number of stages $m$ (more multiplicative butterfly components), the approximation error generally decreases. This is because each additional stage allows more opportunities for coordinates to interact, expanding the set of reachable orthogonal transforms while only adding a modest number of parameters per stage. Conversely, for fixed $m$, increasing $b$ (making each local block larger) also increases expressivity but at the cost of more parameters per block.

Beyond the explicit orthogonality constraint, the sparse factorization itself induces implicit regularization. Representing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse orthogonal factors restricts the set of reachable orthogonal matrices relative to the full orthogonal group, unless one takes the extreme route of many alternating factors. This restriction acts like a built-in regularizer: it reduces the effective hypothesis space and channels learning along particular structured trajectories.

Formally, the paper states (Theorem 1) that BOFT with the same block size is more expressive than OFT with that block size. Furthermore, by composing enough butterfly matrices (and their transposes) one can in principle approximate any orthogonal matrix. Practically, BOFT nests OFT as a special case: BOFT($1,b$) exactly equals block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT($1,d$) recovers the unconstrained full orthogonal parameterization. Thus BOFT provides a smooth spectrum of parameterizations between the highly regular block-diagonal case and the fully expressive dense orthogonal case, enabling finer control of the expressivity/regularity trade-off.

The paper introduces a useful generalization: composing alternating products of butterfly matrices and their transposes—what other work calls a kaleidoscope hierarchy—can substantially increase expressivity. Concretely, matrices of the form
\[
\bm{R}^G = \bm{R}_{\ell,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{\ell,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\,\cdots\,\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)
\]
allow more flexible constructions than a single repeated butterfly factorization. The paper states that with sufficiently many such alternating factors (for example, when $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and the number of alternating pairs $l$ is as large as $d-1$), the class of constructs can represent the entire orthogonal group of size $d$. In other words, the kaleidoscope hierarchy is in principle universal over orthogonal matrices.

Viewed this way, orthogonality acts as a natural regularizer: instead of allowing arbitrary reweighting that could distort norms and relative scales, BOFT permits only reorientation. That preserves pretraining-induced norms and conditioning while enabling new task-specific alignments.

Another useful perspective is to view BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity operator. Consider an output neuron given by the $i$-th column $\bm{w}_i^0$ of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The finetuned pre-activation for that neuron under left-multiplicative orthogonal finetuning is
\[
\bm{w}_i^\top(\text{finetuned})\,\bm{x} = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{R}\,\bm{x}.
\]
Thus BOFT can be understood as learning a shared bilinear form $(\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{R}\,\bm{x}$ where $\bm{R}$ is the learned matrix common to all neurons in the layer. The orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ imposes a structured restriction on the bilinear similarity: $\bm{R}$ must be an isometry, preserving Euclidean norms.
