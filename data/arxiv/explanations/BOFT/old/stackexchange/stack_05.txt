### What exactly makes BOFT more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size?
Question:
The paper states BOFT is 'provably more expressive' than OFT with the same $b$. Is there a formal inclusion result like $\mathcal{F}_{\text{OFT}, b} \subsetneq \mathcal{F}_{\text{BOFT}, b}$, and can you provide a constructive example of a matrix in BOFT($m, b$) that cannot be represented by block-diagonal OFT($1, b$)?
Answer:
Yes—because BOFT with more than one butterfly layer can mix coordinates across the original block boundaries, it strictly contains the block‐diagonal OFT family. In fact one can show

\[
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{OFT},b}
\;=\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},\,m=1,\,b}
\;\subsetneq\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},\,m\ge2,\,b}\,,
\]

simply because every block–diagonal orthogonal is a 1-layer butterfly but there are many 2-layer butterflies that are _not_ block–diagonal. Here is a concrete $4\times4$ example with block-size $b=2$ and two butterfly layers ($m=2$).

1.  Let
   \[
     G(\theta)
     = 
     \begin{pmatrix}
       \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
       \sin\theta & \cos\theta
     \end{pmatrix},
     \qquad
     \theta = \frac{\pi}{4},
     \quad
     A = G\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\bigr)
       = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
         \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\[3pt] 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
   \]

2.  Define the first butterfly factor
   \[
     B_1
     = 
     \mathrm{diag}\bigl(A,\;A\bigr)
     = 
     \begin{pmatrix}
       A & 0\\[6pt]
       0 & A
     \end{pmatrix},
   \]
   which couples coordinates $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$.

3.  Permute so that the next factor couples $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$. Concretely,
   \[
     B_2
     =  
     P^\top\,\bigl(\mathrm{diag}(A,A)\bigr)\,P,
     \quad
     P:\,(1,2,3,4)\mapsto(1,3,2,4),
   \]
   is again block‐diagonal in the permuted basis, but now mixes the two original blocks.

Their product
\[
  R = B_2\,B_1
\]
is a fully “cross‐block” orthogonal matrix (in fact a scaled $4\times4$ Hadamard when $\theta=\pi/4$). Write it out:

\[
  R = \frac{1}{2}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & -1 & -1 & 1\\[3pt]
    1 &  1 & -1 & -1\\[3pt]
    1 & -1 &  1 & -1\\[3pt]
    1 &  1 &  1 &  1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

No choice of two independent $2\times2$ blocks on the diagonal can produce those off–block entries, so this _cannot_ lie in the block‐diagonal OFT family with $b=2$. Yet by construction it _is_ in $\mathrm{BOFT}(2,2)$.

Intuitively what’s happening is that the one‐layer (block‐diagonal) model has its information‐transmission graph split into disconnected components (no paths between different blocks), whereas two butterfly layers reconnect everything. In general Theorem 1 of the paper says that stacking $O(\log d)$ such sparse (but differently wired) layers can approximate any orthogonal matrix, whereas a single‐layer block‐diagonal can only generate block‐separable orthogonals. Hence the strict inclusion  
\[
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{OFT},b}
\;\subsetneq\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},b}
\]
whenever you take more than one butterfly layer.
