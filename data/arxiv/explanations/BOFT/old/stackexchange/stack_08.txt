### How are the permutations between butterfly components chosen and are they learnable?
Question:
The construction refers to each $\tilde{B}(d, k)$ as a 'blockwise permutation' of $\tilde{B}(d, 2)$. Are these permutations fixed a priori by a standard butterfly schedule (like Cooley-Tukey radix-2), or is there any learnable choice over the permutation pattern? If fixed, could learnable permutations improve expressivity at the same parameter count?
Answer:
In the BOFT construction every \(\tilde B(d,k)\) is indeed “a block-wise permutation” of the \(2\times2\) building block \(\tilde B(d,2)\), but—and this is key—the pattern of those permutations is not something you learn during finetuning. Rather, it is hard-coded once and for all according to the standard radix-2 Cooley–Tukey butterfly schedule. Concretely, you fix a sequence of sparsity‐patterns (the familiar “butterfly network” connectivity) and then learn only the nonzero entries inside each little \(2\times2\) (or \(b\times b\)) orthogonal block via the Cayley transform.  

Why did the authors keep the permutations fixed, rather than trying to learn them? There are three practical reasons:

  1. **Discrete combinatorics**: A permutation matrix lies in the set of \(\{0,1\}\)-matrices with exactly one “1” per row and column. Learning those discrete choices directly would push you into a combinatorial optimization that is neither differentiable nor parameter‐efficient.  
  2. **Compute/parameter budget**: The magic of the butterfly factorization is that it connects every input to every output in just \(O(d\log d)\) nonzeros. If you allowed arbitrary permutations between stages, you would either destroy that \(O(d\log d)\) structure or have to pay for extra parameters (or both).  
  3. **Inductive bias**: The fixed Cooley–Tukey graph carries a strong bias—one that underlies FFT, Hadamard transforms, etc.—and that bias turns out to be beneficial for generalization.  

That said, nothing in principle prevents you from treating those stage‐wise shuffles as additional learnable parameters. In fact, in the “kaleidoscope” hierarchies (see Dao et al. 2019) one interleaves learned butterfly factors with fixed or learned transposes/permutions to asymptotically span the full orthogonal group. But to make that work you generally need more layers (or extra parameters) and you lose the simple \(O(d\log d)\) counts that make BOFT so attractive.  

So in BOFT as presented, the answer is: the inter-stage permutations are fixed once and for all by the classical radix-2 schedule, and only the small diagonal blocks within each butterfly component are learned. Exploring *learnable* shuffles is a perfectly reasonable research direction, but it was left outside the scope of this paper because it would sacrifice either clean differentiability, parameter/compute efficiency, or the very inductive bias that makes BOFT perform so well.
