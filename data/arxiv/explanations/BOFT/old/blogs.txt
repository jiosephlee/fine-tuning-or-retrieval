# Meet BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning Explained

Fine-tuning massive pre-trained models has become a go-to recipe for solving tasks from image classification to text generation. Yet naively updating millions or even billions of parameters can be both computationally expensive and prone to overfitting. Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning (BOFT) is a clever new approach that drastically cuts down the number of trainable parameters—going from $O(d^2)$ all the way to $O(d\log d)$—while preserving the “shape” of the original model. In this post, we’ll walk through:

- Why orthogonal updates help retain pre-trained knowledge  
- How classic Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT) works  
- The butterfly factorization trick that makes BOFT so parameter-efficient  
- High-level empirical wins across language, vision, and diffusion models  

Pull up a seat and let’s untangle the “butterfly,” one flap at a time.

## Why Orthogonal Updates Preserve Pre-Training

When you take a giant model—say a vision transformer or a language model—and fine-tune it on your specific task, you want to *preserve* as much of the general knowledge it learned in pre-training as possible. One way to think about a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ in a neural layer is as a set of neuron vectors arranged side by side. If you change these vectors arbitrarily, you risk disrupting the relationships (angles and distances) that encode useful semantic features.

Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT) offers a principled remedy. Instead of adding or subtracting arbitrary updates to $\mathbf{W}$, OFT multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$:

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} \;=\; \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}}
$$

An orthogonal matrix preserves vector lengths and pairwise angles exactly. Geometrically, it’s like rotating and reflecting the neuron space rather than stretching or shearing it. That means the fine-tuned model stays “close” in spirit to the original, boosting training stability and generalization.

But there’s a catch: a full-$d$ by-$d$ orthogonal matrix has on the order of $d^2$ parameters to learn. For large hidden dimensions, that can be prohibitively expensive.

## From Block-Diagonal to Butterfly

### The Block-Diagonal Compromise

One straightforward hack is to restrict $\mathbf{R}$ to be block-diagonal, splitting the $d$ dimensions into $r$ independent blocks of size $b=d/r$. Each block is a small orthogonal matrix, so you only pay $\sum_{i=1}^r b^2 = r\,b^2 = d\,b$ parameters instead of $d^2$. While this cuts memory, it also *disconnects* different subspaces: neurons in block A never mix information with block B, which can limit expressiveness.

### The Butterfly Insight

Imagine you have $d$ “sender” nodes on the left and $d$ “receiver” nodes on the right. A *dense* orthogonal transform is like every sender connecting to every receiver—a total of $d^2$ “edges.” The block-diagonal pattern only wires each sender to $b$ local receivers, leaving most edges unused.

In contrast, a *butterfly graph*—famous from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform—connects these $d$ senders and receivers through a series of sparse, interleaved layers. Each layer only has $O(d)$ connections, but if you chain about $\log d$ of them together, you can still transmit information from *every* sender to *every* receiver. In effect, you approximate a fully dense matrix with a product of $\log d$ sparse factors.

BOFT takes this idea and combines it with orthogonality constraints. Instead of one big $\mathbf{R}$, we learn

$$
\mathbf{R} \;=\;\mathbf{B}_m\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\,\cdots\,\mathbf{B}_1,
$$

where each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is a sparse, block-orthogonal “butterfly component.” By ensuring each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is orthogonal, the entire product stays orthogonal. And because each factor has only $O(d)$ non-zero blocks and we have $m\approx \log d$ of them, the total number of trainable parameters is $O(d\log d)$ instead of $O(d^2)$.

## How Butterfly Factorization Works

Let’s unpack the construction at a high level:

1. **Divide and Conquer**  
   We assume $d$ is a power of two (or we pad it). A single “butterfly component” of size $d$ is built by placing $\tfrac d2$ small $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks along the diagonal—each block looking like a tiny rotation or reflection.  

2. **Hierarchical Stacking**  
   We don’t stop at 2. For a full transform, we need layers that mix fine details at every scale. We create $m$ components, each one permuting and pairing different coordinates. Early layers mix neighbors, later layers mix neighbors of neighbors, and so on.  

3. **Orthogonality by Cayley**  
   Each $2\times 2$ block can be parameterized by a single angle via a Cayley or Givens rotation. That guarantees exact orthogonality and zero-centered initialization (start from identity).

4. **Chaining for Expressivity**  
   The overall matrix is the product of these $m$ components. With $m\approx \log_2 d$, every input coordinate has a path to every output coordinate through these sparse layers, giving full “dense” mixing in the product.

5. **Parameter Count**  
   Each $2\times 2$ block needs one angle parameter. There are $d/2$ blocks per layer and $m$ layers, so $\tfrac d2 \times m = O(d\log d)$ scalars in total.

## Why BOFT Shines

Boiling it down, BOFT strikes a sweet spot between simplicity, efficiency, and performance:

- **Drastically fewer parameters**. From $O(d^2)$ in full OFT down to $O(d\log d)$, often a 10–100x reduction for realistic $d$.  
- **Exact orthogonality**. We still preserve lengths and angles, so we reap the same spectral-norm stability and generalization benefits.  
- **Universal mixing**. Unlike block-diagonal OFT, the butterfly product can approximate any dense orthogonal transform (in theory) while using far fewer degrees of freedom.  
- **Smooth interpolation**. BOFT naturally interpolates between identity (zero updates) and full rotation, making it easy to start from pretrained weights and adapt gently.  
- **No inference overhead**. All the butterfly factors can be fused into your original weight matrix after training, so you pay no extra latency at test time.

## BOFT in the Wild: High-Level Results

Though the theory is neat, the proof is in the pudding. BOFT has been tested on a range of large-scale adaptation tasks:

- **Large Language Models**  
  Fine-tuning GPT-style transformers on classification and generation benchmarks. BOFT matched or exceeded LoRA and OFT results while using a fraction of the trainable parameters.  

- **Vision Transformers (ViTs)**  
  Adapting a pre-trained ViT to image classification and object detection tasks. BOFT led to better downstream accuracy compared to both block-diagonal OFT and low-rank adapters.  

- **Text-to-Image Diffusion Models**  
  Personalizing Stable Diffusion for novel styles or objects. BOFT achieved more stable alignment with fewer parameters, preserving the rich generative knowledge of the base model.  

Across these domains, BOFT typically reduces the adapter size by 4–20× versus full-matrix OFT and still delivers equal or better task accuracy. That makes it a compelling choice when you care about memory footprint or want to store dozens of fine-tuned checkpoints cheaply.

## Getting Started with BOFT

If you’re curious to give BOFT a spin, here’s the rough recipe:

1. **Identify your layer** of interest (e.g., each self-attention projection or MLP weight).  
2. **Replace the linear transform** $\mathbf{W}$ with $\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}$ where $\mathbf{R}$ is a product of $m$ butterfly components.  
3. **Parameterize each component** as Cayley-based $2\times2$ rotations, initialized to identity.  
4. **Train only the rotation angles** with your usual optimizer and learning rate.  
5. **After convergence**, fold all $\mathbf{B}_i$ into $\mathbf{W}$ for inference.

Open-source libraries for fast butterfly products and Cayley rotations make the implementation straightforward, and you can often plug BOFT into existing fine-tuning frameworks with minimal code changes.

## Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning brings together two powerful ideas—angle-preserving updates and fast butterfly mixing—to deliver a method that is both *principled* and *practical*. By reducing the tuning budget from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d\log d)$, BOFT makes it feasible to adapt colossal foundation models on commodity hardware, all while keeping inference lean and preserving the rich geometry of pre-training.

Whether you’re personalizing a diffusion model, tuning a vision transformer, or adapting a language model for a niche domain, BOFT offers a compelling path to parameter-efficient, stable, and generalizable fine-tuning. Give it a try, and let us know what new wings your models sprout once they flap like a butterfly.



# LoRA vs OFT vs BOFT: Which PEFT Method Should You Pick?

In the era of foundation models with billions or even trillions of parameters, training from scratch is no longer an option for most practitioners. Instead, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has emerged as the go-to strategy for adapting massive pre-trained models to downstream tasks without blowing up memory or compute budgets. Among the many PEFT methods, three stand out for their simplicity and effectiveness: LoRA, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), and the newly proposed Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT). In this post, we’ll walk through the strengths and trade-offs of each approach, so you can decide which one best fits your project’s accuracy, stability, hardware, and deployment needs.

## A Quick Tour of the Three PEFT Champions

Before diving into comparisons, let’s briefly recap what each method does under the hood.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) updates a pre-trained weight matrix \(W^0\) by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. In practice you keep \(W^0\) frozen and learn small matrices \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) and \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}\), so that during fine-tuning the model computes
\[
W = W^0 + BA.
\]
By choosing a modest rank \(r\), LoRA drastically reduces the number of extra parameters, keeps memory overhead low, and integrates seamlessly with existing training pipelines.

OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) takes a different tack. Instead of an additive update, OFT multiplies the pre-trained weights by an orthogonal matrix \(R\):
\[
W = R\,W^0,
\]
where \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(R^\top R = I\). This multiplicative approach preserves pairwise angles between neurons—essentially respecting the geometric structure learned during pre-training. To keep the parameter count manageable, OFT typically enforces a block-diagonal form on \(R\). Each diagonal block is a small orthogonal matrix, which reduces trainable parameters but limits expressiveness across blocks.

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) builds on the OFT insight but shatters the block-diagonal constraint. It factorizes the dense orthogonal transform \(R\) into a product of sparse, orthogonal “butterfly” components:
\[
R = B_m \, B_{m-1} \cdots B_1,
\]
where each \(B_i\) has \(\mathcal O(d)\) nonzero entries arranged in a butterfly pattern inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. By chaining \(\mathcal O(\log d)\) such sparse matrices, BOFT achieves a full-rank, dense transformation using just \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) parameters.

## What to Consider When Choosing a PEFT Method

Every project has its own priorities—whether it’s squeezing into tight GPU memory, maximizing final accuracy, ensuring stable training, or minimizing inference latency. Here’s how these three methods stack up:

Accuracy and Expressivity  
LoRA’s low-rank update is very flexible for many tasks, but it may struggle when the adaptation needed is more than a small subspace shift.  
OFT’s orthogonal multiplier often yields improved stability and generalization because it preserves the spectral norm of \(W^0\). However, its block-diagonal shortcut prevents cross-block interactions, which can limit performance on tasks requiring global coordination.  
BOFT recovers much of OFT’s stability benefits while restoring dense connectivity across all dimensions. The butterfly factorization creates a smooth path from the identity matrix to fully expressive orthogonal transforms, so BOFT can match or exceed OFT accuracy with far fewer parameters than a full-matrix OFT.

Training Stability  
LoRA typically converges reliably, but it does not control the spectral norm of the adapted weights, which can occasionally lead to instability in very deep networks.  
OFT guarantees that the spectral norm remains unchanged throughout fine-tuning, promoting smoother gradients and more predictable training.  
BOFT inherits OFT’s spectral-norm preservation and often shows just as stable behavior, with the added benefit of a structured parameter space that discourages overfitting.

Memory Footprint and VRAM  
Because LoRA adds only \(2dr\) parameters (with \(r\ll d\)), it has the smallest memory overhead. For ultra-large models or when GPU memory is the limiting factor, LoRA is often the first choice.  
OFT with full orthogonal matrices needs \(\mathcal O(d^2)\) parameters, which is generally impractical. Its block-diagonal variant reduces this to \(\mathcal O}(bd)\) if each block is size \(b\times b\), but you pay for that by giving up cross-block adaptability.  
BOFT’s parameter count sits between LoRA and full OFT, at \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) for the classic butterfly instantiation. This is often only a modest multiple of LoRA’s footprint while offering richer adaptation.

Computation and Latency  
In both LoRA and OFT, the fine-tuning update adds minimal inference cost: you either add a small rank-\(r\) product or multiply by a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. BOFT, in theory, chains several sparse multiplications—yet after fine-tuning you can collapse the butterfly factors into a single dense \(R\) and fold it into the original weights. As a result, BOFT adds no extra inference latency. During training, the repeated sparse multiplications introduce a small overhead, but in practice it remains quite manageable.

## So, Which Method Is Right for You?

• If your primary goal is to squeeze into the smallest possible memory budget and you’re adapting a model to a task where a low-rank shift suffices, LoRA is a solid, battle-tested choice.  
• If you need rock-solid training stability or you care deeply about preserving the pretrained model’s geometry—yet you can afford a bit more memory—OFT with moderate block sizes can deliver.  
• If you want the best of both worlds—nearly the spectral stability and generalizability of OFT combined with a lean parameter footprint—you should give BOFT a try. Its \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) scaling hits a sweet spot between flexibility and efficiency, and collapsing the learned transform for inference keeps deployment straightforward.

In practice, we often find that BOFT matches or surpasses state-of-the-art PEFT baselines across vision, language, and generative modeling tasks. But every project is unique, so consider your task complexity, hardware constraints, and latency requirements when making your choice. Whichever method you pick, PEFT empowers you to leverage huge pre-trained models without the prohibitive costs of full retraining—and that’s a win for everyone.



# From FFT to Fine-Tuning: A Beginner’s Guide to Butterfly Structures

## Introduction

If you’ve ever wondered how the magic behind the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) can help tame today’s enormous neural networks, you’re in the right place. Modern “foundation” models—large language models, vision transformers, text-to-image generators—are so big that retraining them from scratch is all but impossible. Instead, researchers look for clever ways to adapt a pretrained model to a new task by tweaking only a small number of parameters. One recent idea, called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), preserves the relationships between neurons through an orthogonal transformation. OFT works great, but a full $d$-by-$d$ orthogonal matrix still needs on the order of $d^2$ parameters, which can be too many for truly large layers.

Enter butterfly structures—a sparse, recursive pattern borrowed from the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. By stacking just a few of these sparse orthogonal factors, we can create a single dense transformation that mixes information across every dimension. The result is BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning), a method that achieves dense, expressive updates with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters. In this post, we’ll demystify butterfly networks, explain how they arise from FFT, and show why they hit a “sweet spot” of expressivity and stability for finetuning large models.

## What Is a Butterfly Structure?

At its heart, the Cooley–Tukey FFT rewrites a big discrete Fourier transform (DFT) on $d$ points into smaller DFTs on $\tfrac d2$ points, then recombines the results. The recombination steps can be drawn as a layered graph where each layer exchanges information only between small pairs of nodes. This graph is called a **butterfly network**, and each layer corresponds to a sparse matrix we call a **butterfly factor**. When you multiply these sparse factors in the right order, you recover the full DFT matrix, which is dense.

Concretely, if $d=2^N$, the basic 2-by-2 building block is a matrix of the form
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix},
$$
and at each FFT stage you place many of these blocks along the diagonal (with different coefficients) and then permute. After $N=\log_2 d$ stages, information from any input coordinate can flow to any output coordinate, enabling a global transform.

## Viewing Dense Transforms as Information Transmission

To see why this matters for neural finetuning, imagine your weight update as a graph: each input neuron is a “source” node, each output neuron is a “sink” node, and a nonzero entry in the transformation matrix is an edge that carries information. A fully dense matrix has $d^2$ edges, and even a single orthogonal matrix enforces some structure (e.g., each row and column must form a unit vector), but it’s still costly.

What if instead we factor the dense matrix into $m$ sparse matrices, each with only $O(d)$ nonzeros? We then visualize a grid of $d$ nodes across $(m+1)$ layers. Each sparse factor connects only adjacent layers. To achieve **dense connectivity**—so that every source can reach every sink across the product of factors—we need a carefully chosen pattern of sparse edges. The goal is to minimize **free edges** (trainable parameters) while guaranteeing that no information path is cut off.

Butterfly graphs hit that sweet spot: with just $O(d\log d)$ total edges (trainable entries), they still provide a path from every source node to every sink node. In contrast:

- A single dense orthogonal matrix uses $O(d^2)$ parameters.
- A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix (the original OFT trick) uses $O(d\,b)$ parameters if blocks are size $b$, but cannot mix information across blocks.
- A butterfly factorization uses $m=\log_2 d$ stages of $d/2$ two-node mixers, totaling $O(d\log d)$ parameters, yet yields a **dense** combined transform.

## From Butterfly Factors to Orthogonal Finetuning

How do we turn butterfly factors into a drop-in finetuning method? The key steps are:

1. **Block-butterfly components**. Instead of 2×2 factors, we can use $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks along the diagonal, giving us a parameter called the block size $b$. Each component is a direct generalization of a small rotation, ensuring orthogonality by construction (e.g., via Cayley parameterization).

2. **Stacking**. We multiply $m$ such block-butterfly components:
   $$
   R = B_m \times B_{m-1} \times \cdots \times B_1,
   $$
   where each $B_i$ is orthogonal. If $m=\log_2(d/b)+1$, the product $R$ becomes a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix.

3. **Efficient parameter count**. Each block-butterfly component has $\tfrac12(b-1)d$ free parameters, so the total is
   $$
   \tfrac12(b-1)d \times m \;\approx\; O(d\log d).
   $$

4. **Embedding in a pretrained layer**. Given a frozen pretrained weight $W^0$, we simply replace $W^0$ by $R\,W^0$ in the forward pass. Since $R$ is orthogonal, this preserves singular values of $W^0$, maintaining training stability.

5. **Identity initialization & dropout**. To start close to the original model, each block-butterfly component is initialized to the identity. A multiplicative dropout can randomly replace some butterfly blocks by identity during training, preventing overfitting.

This recipe is the essence of **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning**, or BOFT.

## Why Butterfly-Based Finetuning Excels

BOFT offers a compelling combination of properties:

– **Expressivity**. Unlike block-diagonal OFT, which can never mix across blocks, butterfly factorizations are guaranteed to produce a dense, highly expressive orthogonal matrix. Simulations show that BOFT with small block size and more stages can approximate random orthogonal matrices more closely than fewer, larger blocks.

– **Spectral stability**. Because $R$ is orthogonal, the spectral norm (largest singular value) of $W^0$ stays the same. Preserving the spectrum helps avoid exploding or vanishing gradients and supports better generalization.

– **Parameter efficiency**. Going from $O(d^2)$ down to $O(d\log d)$ parameters means we can finetune very large layers without blowing our memory or overfitting.

– **Implicit bias**. Butterfly structures encode a hierarchical mixing pattern shared by many classic transforms (Fourier, Hadamard). This inductive bias can steer the model toward smoother, more generalizable updates than an unconstrained full matrix.

Across diverse settings—large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models—BOFT matches or outperforms existing finetuning methods like LoRA and block-diagonal OFT, even with far fewer parameters.

## Putting It All Together

In practice, applying BOFT to a pretrained model is straightforward:

1. Choose a block size $b$ (often a small constant like 2 or 4) and compute the required number of stages $m=\log_2(d/b)+1$.

2. Replace each linear (or attention) weight matrix $W^0$ by the product $R\,W^0$, where $R=B_m\cdots B_1$.

3. Initialize each $B_i$ to the identity (zero skew‐symmetric in Cayley parameterization).

4. Finetune only the parameters of the $B_i$ factors, optionally using multiplicative dropout on blocks.

Once training finishes, you can absorb $R$ into the original weights, yielding a single dense model with no extra inference cost.

## Conclusion

From the elegant butterfly patterns of the FFT to the cutting edge of parameter-efficient finetuning, the journey shows how a simple idea—mixing information through sparse recursive stages—can unlock dense, stable updates without the full cost of $d^2$ parameters. BOFT stands as a testament to the power of cross-pollination between signal processing, graph theory, and modern deep learning. The next time you hear “butterfly,” think beyond Fourier and imagine those delicate wings carrying information across every corner of your neural network.



# Hands-On: Fine-Tune a Vision Transformer, LLM, and Diffusion Model with BOFT

In this tutorial, we’ll walk through three practical examples of applying Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) to adapt large foundation models without blowing up your compute budget. You’ll learn how to:

1. Specialize a Vision Transformer (ViT) for new image classes.  
2. Tailor a 7B‐parameter language model (LLM) for domain‐specific Q&A.  
3. Control the style of a Stable Diffusion model for text‐to‐image generation.  

Along the way, you’ll get PyTorch code snippets, tips on identity initialization, multiplicative Dropout, logging, and finally how to merge BOFT parameters back into the base weights—so you incur zero extra cost at inference time.

## 1. Quick Recap: What Is BOFT?

BOFT is a parameter‐efficient finetuning method built on the idea of learning a small, structured orthogonal update to a pretrained weight matrix `W₀`. Instead of full finetuning (O(d²) parameters) or simple low‐rank updates, BOFT factorizes an orthogonal matrix `R` into a product of sparse “butterfly” components:

  R = Bₘ · Bₘ₋₁ · … · B₁,

where each `Bᵢ` is block‐orthogonal with only O(d·b) parameters, and by stacking m = O(log d) of them, you achieve a dense O(d × d) transform with just O(d log d) parameters.  

Key practical points:

- **Identity initialization**: We initialize all `Bᵢ` to the identity, so at step zero, R = I and the model’s behavior is unchanged.  
- **Multiplicative Dropout**: To avoid overfitting, we randomly set some butterfly blocks back to identity during training.  
- **Zero inference overhead**: After finetuning, we absorb R into W₀ (i.e. `W_new = R @ W₀`), so at test time you only run the original model with updated weights.

## 2. Setup and Common Utilities

First, install dependencies:

```bash
pip install torch torchvision transformers diffusers
```

We’ll define a small BOFT module for linear layers.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class BOFTLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W0: nn.Parameter, m: int, block_size: int, dropout=(0.0, 0.0)):
        super().__init__()
        # store pretrained weight, freeze it
        self.W0 = W0
        self.in_features, self.out_features = W0.shape
        W0.requires_grad_(False)

        # build m butterfly components, each split into blocks of size 2*block_size
        self.components = nn.ModuleList()
        for _ in range(m):
            # each component is a block-diagonal of 2b×2b orthogonal blocks
            blocks = []
            for _ in range(self.in_features // (2 * block_size)):
                skew = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(2 * block_size, 2 * block_size))
                blocks.append(skew)
            self.components.append(nn.ParameterList(blocks))

        self.p_block, self.p_comp = dropout  # block-level and comp-level dropout rates

    def forward(self, x):
        # compute R @ W0 on the fly
        W = self.W0
        # identity init via skew-symmetric = zero => Cayley yields I
        for comp in self.components:
            # optionally drop entire component
            if self.training and torch.rand(1).item() < self.p_comp:
                continue
            out_blocks = []
            for skew in comp:
                # optionally drop block
                if self.training and torch.rand(1).item() < self.p_block:
                    block_mat = torch.eye(skew.size(0), device=skew.device)
                else:
                    # Cayley parameterization: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}, Q = skew - skew^T
                    Q = skew - skew.T
                    I = torch.eye(Q.size(0), device=Q.device)
                    block_mat = (I + Q) @ torch.inverse(I - Q)
                out_blocks.append(block_mat)
            # assemble block-diagonal
            B = torch.block_diag(*out_blocks)
            W = B @ W
        return x @ W  # (batch, in_features) @ (in_features, out_features)
```

Later, after finetuning, we’ll merge each `BOFTLinear` by doing `W0.data.copy_(R @ W0.data)` and then replace the layer with a standard `nn.Linear`.

## 3. Fine‐Tuning a Vision Transformer (ViT)

Let’s adapt a pretrained ViT (e.g. `"google/vit-base-patch16-224"`) to recognize 10 new classes. We’ll replace its classifier and inject BOFT into MLP layers.

```python
from transformers import ViTModel, ViTConfig
model = ViTModel.from_pretrained("google/vit-base-patch16-224")
num_new_classes = 10

# Replace classifier head
model.classifier = nn.Linear(model.config.hidden_size, num_new_classes)

# Wrap each MLP linear layer
def wrap_mlp_with_boft(module):
    for name, child in module.named_children():
        if isinstance(child, nn.Linear):
            m, b = 4, 16    # e.g. 4 butterfly components, block size 16
            boft_lin = BOFTLinear(child.weight, m=m, block_size=b, dropout=(0.1, 0.1))
            setattr(module, name, boft_lin)
        else:
            wrap_mlp_with_boft(child)

wrap_mlp_with_boft(model)
```

## 5. Fine‐Tuning Stable Diffusion for Style Control

Finally, let’s control the style of Stable Diffusion’s U-Net via BOFT. We’ll wrap each 1×1 convolution (which is just a linear layer in channel dimension).

```python
from diffusers import StableDiffusionPipeline

pipe = StableDiffusionPipeline.from_pretrained("runwayml/stable-diffusion-v1-5")

for name, module in pipe.unet.named_modules():
    if isinstance(module, nn.Conv2d) and module.kernel_size == (1, 1):
        # treat it as Linear(out_ch x in_ch)
        W0 = module.weight.view(module.out_channels, module.in_channels)
        boft_lin = BOFTLinear(W0, m=5, block_size=32, dropout=(0.1, 0.1))
        # monkey‐patch the conv forward
        def forward_boft(x, boft_layer=boft_lin, bias=module.bias):
            h, w = x.shape[-2:]
            x_flat = x.permute(0,2,3,1).reshape(-1, module.in_channels)
            y = boft_layer(x_flat)
            y = y.reshape(-1, h, w, module.out_channels).permute(0,3,1,2)
            return y + (bias.view(1, -1, 1, 1) if bias is not None else 0)
        module.forward = forward_boft

optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(pipe.unet.parameters(), lr=2e-5)
for epoch in range(3):
    for batch in style_control_loader:
        imgs = pipe(prompt=batch["prompt"], num_inference_steps=10, return_dict=False)[0]
        # compute style loss, e.g. CLIP or perceptual
        loss = style_loss_fn(imgs, batch["target_style"])
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()
```

## 6. Speed, Logging, and Zero Overhead

Here are a few tips to make your BOFT runs smooth:

1. **Gradient checkpointing**: For very deep models, enable it to save memory.  
2. **Mixed precision**: Use `torch.cuda.amp.autocast()` to speed up training.  
3. **Logging**: Track training and validation metrics with WandB or TensorBoard on your `loss`, `dropout rate`, and perhaps the norm of ∥R–I∥.  
4. **Checkpoint merging**: After training, fold BOFT into the base weights:

```python
def merge_boft(layer: BOFTLinear, linear_module: nn.Linear):
    # compute final R @ W0
    W_new = layer(W0=layer.W0)  # calls forward with identity input
    linear_module.weight.data.copy_(W_new.T)
    # then replace BOFTLinear with nn.Linear for inference
```

5. **Zero inference overhead**: Once weights are merged, you run the exact same forward pass as the original model—no extra matrix multiplications needed.

## 7. Wrapping Up

Using BOFT, you’ve just seen how to orthogonally adjust pretrained weights with only O(d log d) extra parameters, all while preserving stability and inference speed. Whether you’re extending a ViT, specializing a 7B LLM, or styling Stable Diffusion, BOFT offers a principled, general‐purpose way to finetune without full retraining or runtime bloat.

Give it a try on your next adaptation project, and happy tuning!



# Tuning the Dials: How to Choose m and b in BOFT

When applying Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) to a foundation model, two hyperparameters stand out: the depth $m$ and the block size $b$. Together, they control how expressive your finetuning transformation can be, how many parameters you need, and how much compute overhead you incur. In this post, I’ll walk you through what each of these dials does, how to think about their trade-offs, and some practical starting points and a checklist for choosing $(m,b)$ in your next project.

## Understanding BOFT’s Hyperparameters

Recall that BOFT replaces a single orthogonal matrix $\bm R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with a product of $m$ sparse “butterfly components,” each of which is block-structured with blocks of size $b$. Concretely, the finetuned weight is
$$
\bm R(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)},
$$
where each $\tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal block butterfly component. Two key facts follow:

1. **Parameter count**  
   BOFT uses 
   \[
     \tfrac12\,(b-1)\,d\,m
   \]
   effective trainable parameters per layer. When $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, this is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full orthogonal matrix.

2. **Compute overhead**  
   Each of the $m$ components must be multiplied into the activation, so the forward pass costs roughly $m$ times more matrix multiplications than a single dense orthogonal update.

Intuitively, $m$ controls *how many stages* of sparse mixing you apply, while $b$ controls *how big* each local mixing block is. Larger $m$ and smaller $b$ both push you toward a denser, more expressive transformation—but at the cost of more parameters (from larger $b$) or more compute (from larger $m$).

## The Trade-offs: Depth versus Block Size

Let’s spell out the main trade-offs you’ll see when adjusting $m$ and $b$:

Expressivity  
- Increasing $m$ adds more sparse factors in the product, allowing you to approximate a wider variety of orthogonal transforms.  
- Decreasing $b$ forces each block to be smaller, which also encourages more global mixing over the course of $m$ stages.  

Parameter efficiency  
- The total parameter budget scales linearly in both $m$ and $b$:  
  $$
    \#\text{params} \;=\;\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m.
  $$
- A naive full OFT (block size $b=d$ and $m=1$) uses $\sim\frac12d^2$ parameters. BOFT with $(m=\log d,b=2)$ uses only $\sim\frac12\,d\log d$.

Compute cost  
- Each added butterfly component adds one extra matrix multiplication on activations. So compute cost grows roughly in $m$.

Convergence and regularity  
- Smaller $b$ tends to act as a stronger regularizer: you restrict each update stage to simple rotations, which can make finetuning more stable.  
- Very large $m$ and very small $b$ may over-parameterize the butterfly expansion and suffer diminishing returns, especially on low-data tasks.

## Rules of Thumb and Defaults

Based on empirical studies and ablation simulations, here are some starting points:

- **Small models or scarce data**  
  Use a relatively *shallow* butterfly (small $m$, say $m=1\text{–}2$) with *moderate blocks* ($b=8\text{–}16$). This gives a parameter count of order $4d$ or $8d$ and keeps compute low, while still adding enough flexibility to adapt a pretrained model.

- **Medium models or balanced data**  
  Try $m=3\text{–}5$ with $b=4\text{–}8$. You’ll get $\tfrac12(b-1)dm$ parameters on the order of $6d\text{–}20d$ and a manageable compute overhead of 3–5 multiplies per layer.

- **Large models or high-complexity tasks**  
  For maximum expressivity, set $m\approx \log_2 d$ and $b=2$. This realizes the classic butterfly matrix with $\sim\tfrac12\,d\log_2 d$ parameters. It can approximate nearly any orthogonal map, at the cost of higher compute and parameter use.

- **Middle ground**  
  If you suspect the full butterfly is overkill, you can interpolate by using $b>2$ but still letting $m$ grow. For example, $b=4$ and $m=\frac12\log_2 d$ yields roughly the same param budget as $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d/2$, but more regularity per stage.

## Choosing m and b in Practice: A Checklist

Before you start finetuning, run through this quick decision guide:

1. **Estimate your parameter budget**  
   How many additional parameters can you afford? Compute $\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m$ to match your limit.

2. **Assess compute overhead**  
   Can you afford $m$ extra matrix multiplies per layer? If GPU time is tight, cap $m$ to 2 or 3.

3. **Match task complexity**  
   High-complexity tasks (e.g., style transfer, open-ended generation) benefit from higher expressivity: lean toward larger $m$ and smaller $b$.  
   Low-complexity tasks (e.g., fine-grained classification on small datasets) may overfit if you go too expressive—use smaller $m$ or larger $b$ for more regularity.

4. **Consider finetuning stability**  
   If training diverges or oscillates, try increasing $b$ (more parameters per factor but fewer factors) or decreasing $m$ to simplify each stage’s rotation.

5. **Validate and iterate**  
   Start with one of the rule-of-thumb presets above, monitor training loss and downstream accuracy, then adjust $m$ or $b$ by one step:  
   - If underfitting, increase $m$ or decrease $b$.  
   - If overfitting, decrease $m$ or increase $b$.

## Conclusion

BOFT’s $(m,b)$ hyperparameters let you smoothly dial between parameter efficiency, compute cost, and the expressivity of your orthogonal finetuning. By understanding how $m$ and $b$ trade off against each other—and by following simple rules of thumb and a decision checklist—you can tailor BOFT to your hardware constraints and your task’s complexity. With the right setting in hand, you’ll get the benefits of orthogonal finetuning—preserved spectral norms, stable convergence, and strong generalization—at a fraction of the parameter cost of dense OFT.



# Stability by Design: Why Orthogonal Updates Generalize Better

Fine-tuning massive pretrained models has become a central task in modern machine learning. Whether you’re adapting a vision transformer for a new image‐classification problem or tweaking a language model for a specialized dialogue system, the challenge is always the same: how do you change the model just enough to learn your new task, but not so much that you lose all the knowledge it acquired during pretraining? In this post, we’ll explore how **orthogonal fine-tuning** offers a built-in recipe for stability, and why preserving geometric and spectral properties of a network’s weights leads to better generalization, smoother training, and fewer pathologies downstream.

## The Pitfalls of Naïve Fine-Tuning

The simplest fine-tuning strategy—just keep the same architecture and update all weights with a small learning rate—often works surprisingly well. Yet practitioners have noticed persistent issues:

  • Drift in parameter norms.  
  • Exploding or vanishing singular values.  
  • Sensitivity to hyperparameters and data order.  
  • Degraded performance on out-of-domain or low-data regimes.

Why do these pathologies arise? In large networks, an unconstrained update can distort the geometry of neuron representations, changing not just how individual units respond but how they relate to one another in high-dimensional space. Over many layers, this can amplify small instabilities into catastrophic failures.

## Orthogonal Fine-Tuning: Preserving Geometry

Orthogonal fine-tuning (OFT) addresses these issues head-on. Instead of adding an arbitrary update to a pretrained weight matrix \(W^0\), OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight as
\[
W = R\,W^0,
\]
where \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an \emph{orthogonal} matrix (\(R^\top R = I\)). Because \(R\) is orthogonal, it has two immediate benefits:

1. **Pairwise Angle Preservation**  
   An orthogonal transformation preserves the angles between any two vectors. If \(\bm{w}_i^0\) and \(\bm{w}_j^0\) are two neurons (columns of \(W^0\)), then their cosine similarity
   \[
     \cos\theta_{ij}
     = \frac{\bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{w}_j^0}
            {\|\bm{w}_i^0\|_2\,\|\bm{w}_j^0\|_2}
   \]
   remains unchanged when multiplied by \(R\). In other words, the \emph{geometry} of the pretrained feature space is exactly preserved, so the network’s learned semantic relationships are not inadvertently scrambled.

2. **Spectral Norm Preservation**  
   The singular values of \(W^0\) describe how it stretches or shrinks different directions in input space. Since
   \[
     W = R\,W^0
     = (R\,U)\,\Sigma\,V^\top
   \]
   has the same diagonal matrix \(\Sigma\) of singular values, the largest singular value (the spectral norm \(\|W^0\|_2\)) is unchanged. By preventing sudden changes in the model’s Lipschitz constant, OFT contributes to more stable backpropagation, fewer gradient explosions, and better behaved learning dynamics.

Together, these two invariances explain why OFT often converges more smoothly and generalizes more robustly to new data.

## Smoother Paths in Weight Space

Another insightful way to think about orthogonal updates is through the lens of weight interpolation. When you morph one weight matrix into another along a straight line in Euclidean space, you can pass through regions of very high loss or unstable activations. But if your updates lie on the \emph{orthogonal group}—a compact manifold of rotations and reflections—then the natural “geodesic” paths tend to avoid these treacherous areas.

Orthogonal fine-tuning effectively constrains the training trajectory to follow smooth, well-conditioned directions. Empirically, this has been shown to reduce “straggler” iterations where loss spikes or gradients vanish, making it easier to tune hyperparameters like learning rate and batch size.

## From OFT to Butterfly OFT: Efficiency Meets Stability

A common objection to full orthogonal fine-tuning is that an arbitrary \(d\times d\) matrix \(R\) has \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters—too many when \(d\) is in the tens or hundreds of thousands. A recent innovation called **Butterfly Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (BOFT)** combines the best of both worlds:

  • It factorizes \(R\) into a product of sparse, structured orthogonal blocks inspired by the Cooley-Tukey “butterfly” topology.  
  • This factorization reduces the parameter count to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while still yielding a dense orthogonal transform.  
  • BOFT therefore inherits all the stability benefits of OFT—angle preservation, spectral norm invariance, and smooth interpolation—at a fraction of the parameter cost.

In practice, BOFT can recover many classic fast transforms (like the discrete Fourier or Hadamard transforms) and has been shown to outperform low-rank methods such as LoRA on both vision and language adaptation tasks.

## Fewer Pathologies, Better Generalization

By design, orthogonal updates sidestep many of the training pathologies that plague large-scale fine-tuning:

  • No drift in pairwise neuron geometry—your pretrained features stay coherent.  
  • Fixed spectral norms—no risk of exploding or vanishing activations.  
  • Smooth training curves—fewer sudden loss spikes.  
  • Strong out-of-domain performance—better generalization when data is scarce or shifted.

These advantages have been empirically validated across tasks ranging from text-to-image diffusion control to large-scale language model adaptation. Whether you have millions or billions of parameters, constraining updates to the orthogonal manifold is a simple yet powerful way to make your fine-tuning both stable and efficient.

## Takeaway

Stability isn’t an accident; it can be \emph{designed} into your fine-tuning process. Orthogonal fine-tuning preserves the very geometric and spectral properties that pretrained models worked so hard to learn, leading to smoother optimization and better generalization. And with clever parameterizations like BOFT’s butterfly factorization, you don’t have to sacrifice efficiency to get those benefits. If you’re wrestling with finicky training curves or disappointing out-of-domain accuracy, give orthogonal updates a spin—you might find that stability really is magic by design.



# Multiplicative Dropout: Regularizing Multiplicative Adapters in BOFT

When we finetune massive pre-trained models, a small tweak can make a big difference. Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is one such tweak: it adapts each layer by multiplying the original weights with a sequence of sparse, structured orthogonal matrices—known as butterfly components. This multiplicative update preserves important geometric properties of the pre-trained weights but can also introduce overfitting if left unchecked. In this post, we’ll explore **Multiplicative Dropout**, a simple yet effective regularizer tailored for BOFT’s multiplicative adapters.

## Why Standard Dropout Falls Short

Dropout is a go-to trick in neural network finetuning. By randomly zeroing out activations or additive weight updates, it discourages co-adaptation and overfitting. However, BOFT’s update is **multiplicative**: we replace the pretrained weight matrix \(W^0\) with
\[
R \, W^0,\quad
R = B_m \, B_{m-1} \,\cdots\, B_1,
\]
where each \(B_i\) is an orthogonal “butterfly” block. If we simply zero entries of \(R\), we break its orthogonality—and the entire motivation behind BOFT is to keep \(R\) orthogonal so that singular values (and thus training stability) are preserved.

In short, standard dropout would either

1. destroy the nice spectral properties of \(R\), or  
2. force us to re-orthogonalize at every step, adding complexity.

We need a dropout scheme that respects the **multiplicative** nature of BOFT while maintaining each \(B_i\)’s orthogonality.

## The Idea of Multiplicative Dropout

Multiplicative Dropout works by **masking out entire butterfly sub-blocks**—i.e., replacing a selected component with the identity matrix. Since the identity is orthogonal, the overall product \(R\) remains orthogonal, and we never introduce any unwelcome spectral changes.

Concretely, BOFT parameterizes each layer with \(m\) butterfly components of size \(d\times d\), further arranged into smaller blocks of size \(2b\times 2b\). Multiplicative Dropout picks two hyperparameters:

- \(p_1\): the fraction of butterfly **components** to mask out, and  
- \(p_2\): the fraction of **blocks** to mask within each unmasked component.

During each forward pass of training:

1. Randomly select \(p_1 \times m\) of the components \(\{B_i\}_{i=1}^m\).  
2. In each selected component, randomly pick \(p_2\) of its \(2b\times 2b\) blocks.  
3. Replace those chosen blocks with the identity \(\bm{I}_{2b}\).

Because we replace entire blocks with the identity, each \(B_i\) remains orthogonal. And since the identity “does nothing,” we are effectively dropping out certain multiplicative updates in a well-controlled way.

## Simple PyTorch-Style Code Pattern

Below is a minimal sketch of how you might implement Multiplicative Dropout in a BOFT layer. We assume you already have a list of butterfly components stored as `self.blocks`, each of shape `(num_blocks, 2b, 2b)`.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import random

class BOFTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, blocks, p1=0.2, p2=0.1):
        super().__init__()
        # `blocks` is a nn.ParameterList of length m,
        # each is a tensor of shape (num_blocks, 2b, 2b)
        self.blocks = nn.ParameterList(blocks)
        self.p1, self.p2 = p1, p2

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: input embeddings of shape (batch, d)
        R = torch.eye(x.size(1), device=x.device)
        for B in self.blocks:
            B_eff = B.clone()
            # Decide if we mask out this component
            if self.training and random.random() < self.p1:
                # Mask out p2 fraction of blocks in B_eff
                num_blocks = B_eff.size(0)
                k = max(1, int(self.p2 * num_blocks))
                mask_idxs = random.sample(range(num_blocks), k)
                for idx in mask_idxs:
                    B_eff[idx] = torch.eye(
                        B_eff.size(1), device=B_eff.device
                    )
                # Reconstruct the full component
                B_eff_full = B_eff.view(-1, B_eff.size(1), B_eff.size(1))
            else:
                B_eff_full = B.view(-1, B.size(1), B.size(1))

            # Multiply into R block-diagonally
            R = B_eff_full.view(R.shape) @ R

        return x @ R.T  # return adapted activations
```

This sketch shows the two levels of randomness: entire components dropped at rate \(p_1\), and within each kept component we drop blocks at rate \(p_2\). At inference time, `self.training` is `False`, so the full BOFT update applies without dropout.

## Choosing \(p_1\) and \(p_2\)

You might wonder how to set \(p_1\) and \(p_2\). Here are some practical guidelines:

- Start conservatively. For a large model with hundreds of butterfly components, try \(p_1=0.1\) (10% of components dropped) and \(p_2=0.1\) (10% of blocks per kept component).  
- Monitor validation performance. If you see under-fitting (your model struggles to improve), reduce either \(p_1\) or \(p_2\). If you still overfit, consider raising them.  
- Keep the product \(p_1 + (1-p_1)\,p_2\) in a modest range (say 0.1–0.3). This controls the **overall** dropout fraction experienced by the model.  

Because BOFT is already quite parameter-efficient, moderate multiplicative dropout often suffices to stop overfitting without slowing convergence.

## Wrapping Up

Multiplicative Dropout brings the best of both worlds to BOFT:

- It preserves strict orthogonality (and thus spectral stability), and  
- It regularizes the **multiplicative** adapter in a simple, component-wise way.

If you’re exploring BOFT for your next finetuning project—whether in vision, language, or generative models—give Multiplicative Dropout a try. With just two tunable rates \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), you can strike a balance between stability and flexibility, all while maintaining BOFT’s elegant spectral guarantees.



# Expressivity Without Bloat: What Butterfly Factorization Can Represent

In the world of large pretrained models, finetuning has become as crucial as pretraining itself. However, fully updating billions of parameters or naively adding even more can be prohibitively expensive in terms of storage, computation, and sometimes even inference latency. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offered an elegant solution by multiplying a fixed pretrained weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix. Yet a dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ still demands $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—hardly “lightweight.” Enter **butterfly factorization**, a structured approach drawn from the Fast Fourier Transform that lets us build very expressive orthogonal transforms in just $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In this post, we’ll explore how butterfly-parameterized finetuning (BOFT) achieves richer expressive power than the traditional block-diagonal approach, and how a simple extension called the kaleidoscope hierarchy can recover the *entire* orthogonal group without reverting to full finetuning.

## The Block-Diagonal Bottleneck

Before BOFT, a common trick to reduce the parameter count in OFT was to force the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ to be block-diagonal. If you split your $d$ neurons into $r$ chunks of size $b=d/r$, then
$$
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\bigr),\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}.
$$
This cuts the trainable parameters down to $r\cdot \tfrac12b(b-1)\approx\frac12bd$, but it comes at a price: neurons in different blocks never “talk” to each other. There’s no way to mix information across those block boundaries, which limits the kinds of rotations and reflections you can learn. In practice, this architecture often underfits tasks that require more global transformations of the feature space.

## Enter the Butterfly

Butterfly factorization is inspired by the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. In a nutshell, you repeatedly split your $d$-dimensional vector into two halves, apply simple $\!2\times2\!$ orthogonal “butterfly” transforms locally, and then shuffle and repeat. Graphically, this looks like a multi-stage network where each layer has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzero connections, yet information from *any* input coordinate can eventually reach *any* output coordinate after $O(\log d)$ stages.

Concretely, you represent
$$
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_1,
$$
where each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is a sparse block-permutation of simple 2×2 orthogonal rotations. By enforcing each $\mathbf{B}_i$ to be orthogonal (e.g.\ via a 2×2 Cayley or Givens parameterization), the product remains orthogonal. Because each stage has only $d$ or $O(d)$ free parameters and there are $m\approx\log d$ stages, BOFT uses just $O(d\log d)$ parameters. Yet the *effective* connectivity across all stages densely links inputs to outputs.

## More Expressive than Block-Diagonal

Why is this more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the *same* parameter budget? Think of a block-diagonal matrix as a set of local rotations on fixed chunks. No matter how you tweak those chunks, you can never couple different blocks. Butterfly factorization, on the other hand, weaves together local rotations in a multi-stage fashion so that each input coordinate is gradually mixed with every other. Empirically, when you try to **approximate** a random dense orthogonal matrix with either:

- a block-diagonal transform of size $b\times b$, or  
- a butterfly-style transform with the *same* number of parameters,

the butterfly family consistently achieves lower approximation error. In other words, BOFT can reconstruct richer, more global rotations without “bloating” the parameter count.

## Kaleidoscope Hierarchies: From Sparse to Complete

If you keep stacking butterfly factorizations and their transposes in alternation, you get what’s known as a **kaleidoscope hierarchy**:
$$
\mathbf{R}^G 
\;=\; (\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{d-1,2}^\top)\,
(\mathbf{B}_{d-2,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{d-2,2}^\top)\,\cdots\,
(\mathbf{B}_{1,1}\,\mathbf{B}_{1,2}^\top).
$$
Here each $\mathbf{B}_{i,j}$ is a butterfly matrix of full depth. A classic result shows that by choosing enough alternating pairs—specifically $d-1$ such blocks—you can *exactly* recover any orthogonal matrix in $\mathrm{O}(d)$. The catch, of course, is that this “full coverage” scheme uses more parameters (scaling like $d(d-1)/2$ in total), which effectively becomes a dense parameterization again.

The magic, however, is that *we don’t need* full coverage to get strong performance on real tasks. In practice, a **shallow** BOFT with just $\log d$ stages (and small block size) often hits a sweet spot: it’s expressive enough to adapt the model effectively, but regularized enough to avoid overfitting. You get the best of both worlds—rich representational power **without** parameter bloat.

## Key Takeaways

- **Block-diagonal OFT** limits cross-block mixing, leading to under-expressive transforms if blocks are too small.  
- **Butterfly factorization** uses multi-stage sparse orthogonal factors to weave together global rotations in $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.  
- **Empirical studies** show BOFT approximates dense orthogonal matrices more faithfully than block-diagonals at the same parameter budget.  
- **Kaleidoscope hierarchies** can recover the entire orthogonal group but at the cost of a dense parameter count. In practice, a shallow BOFT strikes the right balance.  

By embracing butterfly structures, we can finetune large models in a parameter-frugal way without sacrificing the diversity of rotations, reflections, and permutations that orthogonal matrices can offer. This insight opens the door to more efficient and generalizable adaptation of foundation models—expressivity without bloat.



# BOFT on the Edge: Low-VRAM and On-Device Adaptation

Modern deep learning practitioners are often constrained by hardware: limited GPU memory on laptops, edge devices, or small workstations makes fine-tuning a large foundation model feel out of reach. Enter BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning), a parameter-efficient finetuning framework that slashes the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. In this post, we’ll walk through how BOFT’s butterfly parameterization unlocks on-device fine-tuning, how to budget your memory with mixed precision, how to combine BOFT with low-bit quantization (à la QLoRA), and finally how to merge the learned orthogonal transforms back into your base model for true zero-latency inference.

## Why $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ Matters for On-Device Finetuning

Traditional full finetuning updates every weight in a $d\times d$ matrix, costing $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters and commensurate GPU memory. Even block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning reduces this only to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters, where $b$ is block size, but still can be hefty for large $d$. BOFT exploits the butterfly structure—originally from the Cooley-Tukey FFT—to factorize a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal update matrix as the product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse factors, each with just $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros. In practice this means:

  * Fine-tuning head or adapter modules now costs $d\log d$ parameters instead of $d^2$.
  * On a $d=4096$ hidden dimension, BOFT uses roughly $4096\log_2(4096)\approx50{,}000$ parameters per layer, versus $16$ million in a full matrix.

That steep reduction makes updating a model on a single 8 GB laptop GPU entirely feasible.

## Memory Budgeting with Mixed Precision and Sparse Updates

Even with fewer parameters, you must squeeze activations, gradients, and optimizer states into VRAM. Here are practical tips when you finetune BOFT on-device:

  * **Half-precision (FP16) for activations and gradients.** Most modern GPUs achieve 2× memory saving by running in FP16. BOFT’s Cayley-based 2×2 rotation blocks in each butterfly component convert naturally to FP16, with little impact on numeric stability.
  * **Parameter-only sparsity.** BOFT’s sparse butterfly factors can be stored in compressed form, e.g. by storing only the non-zero blocks. You avoid allocating a full $d\times d$ buffer for each factor.
  * **Gradient checkpointing** for deep stacks of layers. If your model has dozens of transformer layers, recompute forward activations on the fly rather than storing them all.

Putting these together, a BOFT finetuning session on a 6 B–parameter vision transformer can run comfortably in 6–8 GB of VRAM—opening up on-device experimentation.

## Combining BOFT with Low-Bit Quantization

Quantization delivers another order-of-magnitude memory saving by storing pretrained weights in 4-bit or even 2-bit form. QLoRA popularized finetuning large language models with 4-bit weights plus LoRA adapters. You can similarly fuse BOFT into this workflow:

  1. **Quantize** the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to 4-bit using a method like GPTQ or BitCenter.
  2. **Attach BOFT modules**: your orthogonal butterfly factors remain in FP16 (or FP32 if you prefer).
  3. **Finetune**: perform forward and backward passes using 4-bit quantized weights plus $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ BOFT parameters.
  4. **Merge**: after training, absorb the learned butterfly factors into the quantized weight representations.

Even though the base model stays at 4-bit, the BOFT update lives in low-precision float, yielding high-quality task adaptation with minimal extra memory.

## Zero-Latency Inference via Merge-In

A common concern with adapters or extra modules is added inference cost. BOFT sidesteps this entirely. Because the final orthogonal update matrix $\bm{R}$ is just the product of your learned butterfly components, you can merge it into the base weights once and for all:

  * Compute the composite update $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ on the CPU or GPU.
  * Multiply your pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}$, storing the result as a single new weight tensor.
  * Discard the separate BOFT factors.

The result is a model indistinguishable from a fully-merged finetuned checkpoint, with zero extra layers or runtime overhead. You get the benefits of orthogonal finetuning with the simplicity of a drop-in single‐tensor update.

## Case Study: Finetuning a Vision Transformer on a Laptop GPU

Suppose you want to adapt a ViT-Base (768-hidden) model to a small custom image classification set on a 6 GB GPU. A naive adapter or LoRA plus 4-bit quantization might still hover around 5–6 GB usage, leaving little headroom. With BOFT:

  * Each layer’s BOFT update costs $\mathcal{O}(768\log_2 768)\approx768\times9\approx7{,}000$ parameters, or about 28 KB in FP16.
  * Across 12 transformer layers, that’s under 350 KB extra—negligible versus the 295 MB of 4-bit quantized weights.
  * Combined with mixed-precision activations and gradient checkpointing, total VRAM peaks around 5 GB.
  * After finetuning, you merge all BOFT factors into the quantized weights, shipping a single 4-bit checkpoint.

This workflow transforms your everyday laptop or edge GPU into a full fine-tuning rig, all thanks to BOFT’s $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter magic.

## Conclusion

On-device adaptation of large models no longer requires massive clusters or exotic hardware. By leveraging BOFT’s butterfly-based orthogonal finetuning, you cut trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, pair it with mixed precision and 4-bit quantization, and merge updates for inference with zero latency. Whether you’re iterating on a laptop, pushing updates to a fleet of edge GPUs, or building a mobile finetuning solution, BOFT brings large-model adaptation squarely into the hands of every practitioner.



# Borrowing from Networks: The Information Transmission View of Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning massive pre-trained models can quickly run into the wall of computational cost and memory constraints. Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT) steps in with a clever idea: instead of freely updating every weight, it wraps a frozen layer with an orthogonal transformation, preserving the geometry of the original model. The problem is that a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—a steep price when $d$ grows into the thousands or millions. Recent work unleashes a powerful mental model by treating the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information routing problem in a network. This “information transmission” perspective not only demystifies the inner workings of Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning (BOFT) but also points the way toward a universe of novel, structured designs beyond the classic butterfly graph.

## From Weight Updates to Network Flows

Imagine that you have $d$ data “packets” sitting at $d$ source routers, and you want each of these packets to reach all $d$ destination routers by the time they exit the network. A dense $d\times d$ matrix is like a single broadcast step connecting every source to every destination directly. Clearly that requires $d^2$ links—expensive in both hardware and complexity.

BOFT asks: can we accomplish the same broad connectivity by chaining together several sparse broadcast stages? Mathematically, instead of learning one big orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, we learn a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal factors
$$
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\,\cdots\,\mathbf{B}_1.
$$
Each $\mathbf{B}_i$ corresponds to a network layer that routes information only along a limited set of edges. If the combined effect of these layers is that every source can reach every sink, we have effectively built a dense transformation with far fewer total links.

## Visualizing Information Transmission

Picture a grid of nodes with $m+1$ columns, each column hosting $d$ nodes. Column 0 holds the input features, column $m$ holds the outputs, and intermediate columns represent the “hidden hops” through which data must flow.  A non-zero entry in $\mathbf{B}_i$ becomes a directed edge from node $j$ in column $i-1$ to node $k$ in column $i$. To guarantee that each input dimension influences each output dimension, the graph formed by unrolling all $m$ factors must have at least one path from every node in column 0 to every node in column $m$.

The beauty of this view is that it turns the algebraic problem of matrix factorization into a graph-connectivity problem. We can now leverage decades of network design insights to ask: What sparse topology ensures full reachability while using as few edges as possible? Butterflies, hypercubes, expander graphs, or even irregular custom meshes—all are on the table.

## The Butterfly: A Classic Network Topology

The canonical answer in BOFT is the butterfly network. In a radix-2 butterfly, each layer splits nodes into pairs and mixes their values via small $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotations. A $d$-dimensional butterfly with $\log d$ such pairwise layers achieves full connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges—far fewer than $d^2$.

Here’s roughly how it works:

  1. **Pairwise mixing**: At each layer, nodes are grouped into disjoint pairs. Each pair exchanges and recombines its two values by a tiny orthogonal transform, like a two-element rotation.  
  2. **Rewiring between layers**: After mixing, the grouping pattern shifts so that each node is paired with a different neighbor, gradually weaving information across the network.  
  3. **Logarithmic depth**: After $\log d$ stages, each original node’s influence has propagated to every other node.

In BOFT, we parameterize each small block with a skew-symmetric Cayley transform to guarantee orthogonality, and we multiply them all together before applying the frozen pre-trained weights. The result is a dense, expressive transform learned with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

## Beyond Butterflies: Designing New Sparse Schemes

While butterflies are a natural first choice—thanks to their role in the Cooley-Tukey FFT and well-studied connectivity properties—the network lens invites experimentation. For instance:

  • **Expander-style layers** could mix larger groups of nodes with a handful of random sparse connections, trading off the neat regularity of butterflies for potentially stronger mixing in fewer stages.  
  • **Hierarchical trees** might cluster dimensions that logically belong together (e.g.\ spatial patches in images or subwords in text) and route within clusters before crossing cluster boundaries.  
  • **Adaptive routing** could learn the graph structure itself—pruning or adding edges based on which information flows most effectively during finetuning.  

Each of these choices corresponds to a different family of sparse orthogonal factors. The goal remains the same: ensure that the composed graph is strongly connected between sources and sinks while minimizing the total number of trainable links.

## Why Structure Boosts Generalization

Why should we care about these structured sparse topologies instead of just unleashing a full orthogonal matrix? Three key reasons stand out:

  1. **Parameter efficiency**: Less is more when you have limited GPU memory or want extremely fast adaptation.  
  2. **Inductive bias**: Restricting to a well-studied graph class (butterfly, expander, tree) injects the right regularity, preventing overfitting and steering the finetuned model toward transformations that capture meaningful cross-feature interactions.  
  3. **Theoretical guarantees**: Sparse network topologies come with rich connectivity and spectral analyses, giving us confidence that the resulting factorization can approximate any dense orthogonal transform with predictable cost.

In practice, BOFT has shown that a butterfly-style factorization often matches or exceeds the performance of more parameter-hungry finetuning schemes, all while preserving the pretrained model’s spectral norms and providing stable, generalizable updates.

## Charting the Road Ahead

Casting parameter-efficient fine-tuning as an information routing problem opens a whole new design space. By swapping in different network topologies—be they deterministic butterflies, random expanders, learned graphs, or domain-specific trees—we can tailor the inductive bias to the task at hand. Each routing scheme suggests a unique pattern of sparse orthogonal factors, promising more efficient, stable, and interpretable adaptations of massive foundation models.

As researchers and practitioners, we’re now free to think like network architects: designing highways of activation flow, optimizing switch schedules of 2×2 mixers, and trading off depth vs.\ sparsity to meet our resource and performance targets. The next time you fine-tune a colossal transformer or diffusion model, remember: you’re not just tweaking weights—you’re engineering a whole information network.



# Plugging BOFT into Hugging Face and PyTorch: An Engineering Guide

In this guide, we’ll walk through how to integrate BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) into your existing PyTorch and Hugging Face training pipelines. BOFT lets you finetune large pretrained models by wrapping each Linear layer with a compact, orthogonal parameterization built from butterfly‐structured factors. Our walkthrough will cover:

  * Monkey‐patching PyTorch `nn.Linear` modules  
  * Defining BOFT parameter storage and forward pass  
  * Saving and loading custom BOFT checkpoints  
  * Interoperating with PEFT libraries  
  * Merging BOFT into full weights for export  
  * Verifying correctness and orthogonality  

Whether you’re finetuning a Transformer or a vision backbone, by the end you’ll have a drop‐in BOFT solution that slots right into `Trainer` or your custom training loop.

## 1. Prerequisites

You’ll need:

  * PyTorch ≥1.10  
  * Transformers ≥4.30 (for Hugging Face models)  
  * Optional: PEFT ≥0.4.0 if you want to combine BOFT with other adapters  

Install with:

```bash
pip install torch transformers peft
```

Make sure you can load a pretrained model normally:

```python
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2")
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
```

## 2. Monkey‐Patching `nn.Linear`

BOFT wraps each Linear layer to replace its weight update with a multiplicative orthogonal adapter $\bm{R}(m,b)$. We’ll define a custom `BOFTLinear` module that holds:

  1. The frozen pretrained weight `W0`  
  2. A sequence of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components (each of block size $b$) stored as skew‐symmetric parameters via Cayley transform  

### 2.1 Implementing `BOFTLinear`

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class BOFTLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, orig_linear: nn.Linear, m: int = 4, b: int = 2):
        super().__init__()
        # Freeze original weight and bias
        self.W0 = nn.Parameter(orig_linear.weight.data.clone(), requires_grad=False)
        self.bias = orig_linear.bias
        d, n = self.W0.shape

        # Store m Cayley skew-symmetric blocks of shape (num_blocks, 2b, 2b)
        self.m, self.b = m, b
        self.skews = nn.ParameterList()
        for i in range(m):
            # #blocks along the diagonal
            num = d // (2*b)
            # initialize zeros -> identity after Cayley transform
            skew = torch.zeros(num, 2*b, 2*b)
            self.skews.append(nn.Parameter(skew))

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        R = self._build_orthogonal()
        # Apply R multiplicatively: (R W0)^T x
        W = R @ self.W0
        return nn.functional.linear(x, W, self.bias)

    def _build_orthogonal(self) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Build R = B_m B_{m-1} ... B_1 using Cayley transform on each block.
        Each B_i is block-diagonal with num_blocks of size 2b.
        """
        d, _ = self.W0.shape
        R = torch.eye(d, device=self.W0.device)
        for skew in self.skews:
            # Cayley map: (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} for each block
            blocks = []
            for Q in skew:
                I2 = torch.eye(2*self.b, device=Q.device)
                Bi = (I2 + Q) @ torch.linalg.inv(I2 - Q)
                blocks.append(Bi)
            # assemble block-diagonal B_i
            B = torch.block_diag(*blocks).to(x.device)
            R = B @ R
        return R
```

### 2.2 Patching the Model

We can recursively replace every `nn.Linear` with `BOFTLinear`:

```python
def apply_boft(model: nn.Module, m: int = 4, b: int = 2):
    for name, module in model.named_children():
        if isinstance(module, nn.Linear):
            setattr(model, name, BOFTLinear(module, m, b))
        else:
            apply_boft(module, m, b)
    return model

model = apply_boft(model, m=6, b=4)
```

After this patch, any forward pass will go through your BOFT wrappers.

## 3. Checkpointing BOFT

BOFT introduces new parameters (`self.skews`). To save a checkpoint compatible with the Hugging Face style, simply use `model.save_pretrained(path)` and `model.from_pretrained(path)`. You’ll get a `pytorch_model.bin` containing both the original `W0` tensors (frozen) and the learned `skews`.

```python
# Save
model.save_pretrained("./boft_ckpt")

# Load
from transformers import AutoConfig
config = AutoConfig.from_pretrained("gpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_config(config)
model = apply_boft(model, m=6, b=4)
model.load_state_dict(torch.load("./boft_ckpt/pytorch_model.bin"), strict=True)
```

If you’re using `Trainer`, set `save_strategy="steps"` or `"epoch"` and it will handle everything for you.

## 4. Interoperability with PEFT Libraries

The popular PEFT APIs expect adapter modules to live under `model.peft`. We can register our BOFT wrappers as a custom adapter by:

1. Subclassing `peft.PeftModel`  
2. Exposing adapter weights under `self.peft_adapter.state_dict()`  

Rough sketch:

```python

class PeftBOFT(PeftModel):
    def __init__(self, model, m, b, peft_config: PeftConfig):
        super().__init__(model, peft_config)
        apply_boft(self.model, m, b)

    def merge_and_unload(self):
        # see Section 5
        ...
```

You can then pass `PEFTConfig` into `Trainer` just like LoRA or Prefix Tuning.

## 5. Exporting Merged Weights

Once finetuning is done, you may want to fold $\bm{R}$ into the original weight for fast inference:

```python
def merge_boft_weights(model: nn.Module):
    for module in model.modules():
        if isinstance(module, BOFTLinear):
            with torch.no_grad():
                R = module._build_orthogonal()
                # overwrite weight and remove BOFT parameters
                module.weight.data.copy_(R @ module.W0)
                # drop the BOFT-specific params
                del module.W0
                del module.skews
                module.__class__ = nn.Linear  # revert to normal Linear
```

After you call `merge_boft_weights(model)`, your model only has merged `weight` and `bias`. Save it normally:

```bash
python -c "from your_script import merge_boft_weights; \
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained('./boft_ckpt'); \
merge_boft_weights(model); model.save_pretrained('./merged_model')"
```

## 6. Testing Correctness and Orthogonality

Finally, always sanity‐check:

1. **Shape and forward consistency**  
   Compute outputs before/after merging with the same input:
   ```python
   model_before = ...
   model_after = ...
   x = torch.randn(1, seq_len, model.config.hidden_size)
   y1 = model_before(x).logits
   merge_boft_weights(model_after)
   y2 = model_after(x).logits
   assert torch.allclose(y1, y2, atol=1e-6)
   ```
2. **Orthogonality**  
   For each BOFT wrapper, verify $R^\top R = I$:
   ```python
   for module in model.modules():
       if isinstance(module, BOFTLinear):
           R = module._build_orthogonal()
           I = torch.eye(R.size(0), device=R.device)
           error = torch.norm(R.T @ R - I)
           assert error < 1e-5, f"Orthogonality failed (err={error})"
   ```

If both checks pass, you can be confident that your BOFT integration is correct.

## 7. Conclusion

Integrating BOFT into Hugging Face and PyTorch takes just a few steps: patch your Linear layers, train as usual, optionally merge and export. This engineering pattern gives you the spectral‐preserving, parameter‐efficient finetuning benefits of BOFT without disrupting your favorite training loops or tooling. Happy finetuning!



# See the Flow: Visualizing BOFT’s Butterfly Components and Interpolations

## Introduction

When you first hear the term “Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning” (BOFT), it might evoke images of exotic mathematics fluttering through your neural network. In reality, BOFT is an elegant yet practical way to adapt massive pretrained models—like vision transformers or language models—to new tasks without retraining from scratch. In this post, we’re going to peel back the layers of BOFT, showing interactive visualization sketches of its building blocks (butterfly components), the sparsity patterns they induce, and the subtle weight interpolations that happen as training unfolds.

Whether you’re a student seeking intuition or a practitioner curious about how sparse factors can compose into a powerful dense orthogonal transform, this guided tour—with conceptual “interactive” demos—will help you see the flow.

## From Dense Orthogonal Matrices to Sparse Factors

Traditional orthogonal finetuning (OFT) learns a full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to multiply a frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$. While this guarantees angle preservation between neurons, a full matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—prohibitively large when $d$ is in the thousands.

BOFT’s key insight is that you can build the same expressive power by composing several *sparse* orthogonal matrices:
  
  $\displaystyle \bm{R} \;=\; \bm{B}_{m}\,\bm{B}_{m-1}\,\cdots\,\bm{B}_{1}\,.$

Each $\bm{B}_i$ has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzero entries, but the product is a *dense* orthogonal transform. To visualize why this works, imagine information flowing through a multi‐layer grid: each $\bm{B}_i$ specifies which “nodes” exchange information at stage $i$. If, from the input layer to the output layer, there is a path connecting every input node to every output node, the final matrix is dense.

## The Butterfly Component: A Friendly Sparsity Pattern

The term “butterfly” comes from the classic Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. In BOFT, each **butterfly component** is a block‐diagonal matrix that pairs up dimensions and mixes them via small orthogonal rotations. Concretely, for $d=2^N$ we build

  $\displaystyle \bm{B}(d) \;=\; \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\,d)\;\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\,\tfrac{d}{2})\;\cdots\;\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)\,,$

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ has $d/k$ blocks of size $k\times k$. Within each block, pairs of coordinates get “butterfly‐shuffled” and rotated by a small $2\times2$ Cayley‐parameterized orthogonal matrix.

### Interactive Visualization Sketch: Butterfly Blocks

Imagine an interactive widget with these features:

- A slider for block size $k$ (powers of two from 2 up to $d$).
- A grid depiction of the block structure: white squares for zeros, colored squares for the paired entries in each $2\times2$ rotation.
- A hover‐tooltip showing that each $2\times2$ block is parameterized by a single angle $\theta$, via the Cayley transform  
  $\displaystyle R(\theta)=\frac{1}{1+\theta^2}\begin{pmatrix}1-\theta^2 & -2\theta\\2\theta & 1-\theta^2\end{pmatrix}\,. $

As you increase $k$, you’ll see fewer, larger blocks; decrease $k$ and the pattern fragments into more but simpler rotations. This “butterfly” shape emerges when you overlay the nonzero patterns of all components.

## Composing Sparse Factors into Dense Transforms

Each butterfly component allows information to flow between disjoint pairs of coordinates. When you multiply several components in sequence, the paths from every input coordinate to every output coordinate start to interweave. After $m$ components, the composite sparsity pattern corresponds to a richly connected directed graph on $d$ inputs and $d$ outputs.

### Conceptual Demo: Path Connectivity

Picture a multi‐column graph:

- Column 0 has $d$ input nodes.
- Column 1, 2, …, $m$ represent intermediate nodes after each $\bm{B}_i$.
- Column $m+1$ has $d$ output nodes.

An interactive “Connect All” button highlights all directed paths from each input to each output. At first (just one component), some outputs are unreachable from certain inputs. As you add more butterfly factors, the graph fills in, and by $m=\log_2 d$ steps you achieve full reachability—hence a dense matrix.

## Watching Weight Interpolation During Finetuning

A particularly neat property of BOFT is how it interpolates from the identity transform (at initialization) to the final orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Because we initialize each $\bm{B}_i$ as identity and then train, you can animate the gradual emergence of nonzero patterns and the angles in each 2×2 rotation.

### Animation Sketch: Training Steps

- **Frame 0**: All blocks are identity, so the composite $\bm{R}$ is exactly $\bm{I}$.
- **Frame 50**: Some $\bm{B}_i$ show small off‐diagonal entries—tiny rotations in a few blocks. The overall weight $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ deviates slightly from $\bm{W}^0$.
- **Frame 200**: A fuller butterfly pattern appears. Many 2×2 rotations have nontrivial angles.
- **Frame 1000**: Converged pattern, approximating the “best” orthogonal adaptation for your downstream task.

Overlaying these frames as a color map on the $d\times d$ grid, you see the sparsity “bloom” into dense structure in a highly structured way.

## What Students and Practitioners Can Take Away

1. **Sparse building blocks can yield dense transforms.** Rather than handling a massive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ matrix, BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters to weave together simple rotations.
2. **Visualization cements intuition.** By sketching the butterfly blocks and connectivity graphs, you see why $\log d$ layers suffice for full reachability.
3. **Smooth interpolation aids optimization.** BOFT’s identity‐initialization plus Cayley‐parameterized rotations ensure training starts close to the pretrained model and moves smoothly.
4. **Generalization emerges from structure.** The same butterfly pattern underlies many classic transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT), hinting at a useful inductive bias when adapting large models.

## Conclusion

BOFT shows that with a dash of ingenuity—drawn from the Cooley–Tukey FFT and an information‐transmission view—we can finetune huge foundation models efficiently and elegantly. By breaking down a dense orthogonal transform into a choreography of sparse butterfly components, we preserve spectral norms, limit parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, and guide optimization with a structured inductive bias.

Next time you think of finetuning, imagine those butterfly wings beating—rotating pairs of neurons, passing information level by level, until a powerful dense transformation carries your pretrained model smoothly into its new task.
