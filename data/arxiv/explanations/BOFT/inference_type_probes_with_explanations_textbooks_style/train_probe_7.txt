Chapter Title: Orthogonality, SVD, and Stability in Butterfly-Based Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning and Its Butterfly Parameterization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a model finetuning paradigm that updates a pretrained linear layer by left-multiplying it with a learnable orthogonal matrix. Given a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and an input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the original forward pass computes $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. OFT modifies this to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is orthogonal. The motivation is to preserve relational structure among neurons, such as pairwise angles, by applying a shared orthogonal transformation within a layer. Orthogonality ensures that geometric properties of the weight space remain well controlled during finetuning.

While OFT is principled and effective, a full $d \times d$ dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ effective parameters, which can be costly. The block-diagonal variant reduces parameters by constraining $\bm{R}$ to $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$ with smaller orthogonal blocks, but this sparsity pattern can reduce expressivity and impose undesirable inductive biases. To construct a dense yet parameter-efficient orthogonal matrix, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) replaces a single dense orthogonal matrix with a product of sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly structure. BOFT composes
$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal “butterfly component.” With $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT achieves a dense orthogonal transform using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, substantially fewer than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

The butterfly factorization is motivated by an information transmission view of matrix multiplication. A dense matrix can be interpreted as enabling information flow from each source node to each receiver node. BOFT designs sparse factors whose connectivity, when composed, yields dense information flow with far fewer trainable elements than a single dense matrix. Crucially, each factor is constrained to be orthogonal, so the product remains orthogonal, preserving important spectral properties of the pretrained weights.

# Enforcing Orthogonality via Cayley Parameterization

Maintaining orthogonality during finetuning requires a parameterization that ensures the constraint throughout optimization. BOFT employs Cayley parameterization for each small block. For a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, the Cayley transform
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$
produces an orthogonal matrix. BOFT constructs each butterfly component as a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are either $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices or $2b \times 2b$ block-orthogonal matrices, all parameterized via Cayley transforms (or rotational forms in the $2 \times 2$ case). Since the non-zero pattern of a butterfly component is a permutation of the non-zero pattern of the base $2 \times 2$ block structure, all components can be turned into orthogonal matrices in a consistent way.

Initializing each component as the identity ensures the finetuned model starts from the exact pretrained model. Moreover, the multiplicative nature of BOFT motivates a multiplicative Dropout strategy that randomly replaces selected blocks within selected butterfly components by identity blocks during training, adding regularization without breaking orthogonality.

# SVD, Left Multiplication, and Spectral Norm Preservation

A central stability property of orthogonal finetuning arises from how left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix interacts with the singular values of a weight matrix. Consider the singular value decomposition of the pretrained weights:
$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$,
where $\bm{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\bm{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values. OFT and BOFT compute finetuned weights by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, yielding $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{R} \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R} \bm{U}$ is also orthogonal, the singular values of $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ are identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$, and in particular the largest singular value—the spectral norm—remains unchanged. In symbols, if $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{W}^0)$ denotes the largest singular value, then
$\sigma_{\max}(\bm{R} \bm{W}^0) = \sigma_{\max}(\bm{W}^0)$
for any orthogonal $\bm{R}$.

This exact preservation of the spectral norm is significant. It means the operator norm of the linear layer’s mapping does not grow during finetuning, even as the orientation of the neuron space is transformed. Preserving the spectral norm has been argued to benefit training stability and generalization. Orthogonal finetuning achieves this preservation by construction, since its updates are constrained to be orthogonal.

# What Would Break If BOFT’s Factors Were Not Orthogonal

BOFT’s stability benefit via spectral norm preservation relies on each butterfly component being orthogonal, which keeps their product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ orthogonal. If BOFT’s sparse factors were not constrained to be orthogonal, the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ would generally not be orthogonal. In that case, left-multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}(m,b)$ would no longer act as an orthogonal change of basis on the left singular vectors. Instead, it would alter the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$, so the resulting finetuned weights could have a different spectral norm.

To see the contrast, write $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$ and consider a general (non-orthogonal) left factor $\bm{M}$. Then $\bm{M} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{M} \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$, and there is no guarantee that $\bm{M} \bm{U}$ is orthogonal. Consequently, the singular values of $\bm{M} \bm{W}^0$ need not match those of $\bm{W}^0$, and $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{M} \bm{W}^0)$ may increase or decrease relative to $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{W}^0)$. This breaks the precise spectral norm preservation that orthogonal finetuning provides. In BOFT, dropping the orthogonality constraints on the sparse factors would therefore remove the guarantee that $\sigma_{\max}$ is preserved under the update.

Because the paper highlights spectral norm preservation as a desirable property for stability and generalization, relaxing orthogonality in BOFT would eliminate this benefit. The butterfly structure alone does not ensure spectral norm preservation; it is the orthogonality of each factor—and thus of their product—that makes left-multiplication spectrally norm-preserving.

# Implications for Finetuning Stability

The spectral properties section emphasizes that orthogonal finetuning perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. This preservation comes directly from the SVD perspective: left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix changes $\bm{U}$ to another orthogonal matrix but leaves $\bm{\Sigma}$ unchanged. BOFT inherits this guarantee because it composes orthogonal butterfly components into an orthogonal $\bm{R}(m,b)$.

If BOFT’s sparse factors were allowed to be non-orthogonal, then $\bm{R}(m,b)$ would, in general, be non-orthogonal, and the spectral norm of the finetuned weights would not be guaranteed to match that of $\bm{W}^0$. As a result, the specific stability benefit tied to SVD—spectral norm preservation—would likely disappear. In practical terms, orthogonality in BOFT is not merely a structural choice for parameter-efficiency; it is also the mechanism that enforces the spectral control highlighted by the SVD argument. By maintaining orthogonality at the level of each sparse factor, BOFT achieves both dense expressivity through composition and strict spectral norm preservation through orthogonality, combining parameter-efficiency with principled stability.
