Chapter Title: From Quadratic to Near-Linear Parameter Growth in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning and the Cost of Dense Parameterization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapting pretrained models that preserves the pairwise angles between neurons by applying an orthogonal transformation to the weights of each layer. Concretely, given a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, OFT replaces the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a trainable orthogonal matrix. Orthogonality is enforced during optimization via a Cayley transform, $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, ensuring angle preservation and training stability.

A central challenge with OFT lies in its parameter cost. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This quadratic growth in parameter count quickly becomes prohibitive as $d$ increases, and it motivates seeking more parameter-efficient structures while retaining the benefits of orthogonality. The block-diagonal variant of OFT reduces parameters by constraining $\bm{R}$ as $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with block size $b$ and $br=d$, yielding $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters. However, block-diagonal matrices cannot represent dense transforms and impose an undesirable inductive bias by transforming disjoint index groups independently. This limitation motivates the search for constructions that produce dense orthogonal matrices, but at substantially lower parameter counts than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

# An Information Transmission View: Building Dense Orthogonality from Sparse Factors

The key idea for breaking the quadratic parameter barrier is to construct a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal factors. This can be understood through an information transmission framework. Suppose $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ for $m$ square matrices. The non-zero pattern of $\bm{R}$ corresponds to connectivity between source and receiver nodes in a $d\times (m+1)$ grid, where each factor $\bm{B}_i$ defines directed edges between two adjacent levels. For $\bm{R}$ to be dense, information originating from any node in the first level must reach every node in the last level via paths induced by the sparse factors. Orthogonality imposes constraints on these edges: each factor must be full rank, which implies at least $d$ edges forming a bijection between adjacent levels. These edges are structurally necessary and, under orthogonality, do not contribute free trainable parameters in the same way that general dense matrices would.

This perspective reveals why naively achieving dense connectivity with a single orthogonal matrix incurs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters: a single dense map performs all transmissions in one step. By contrast, sequential sparse transmissions can produce the same dense effect with far fewer trainable degrees of freedom, provided the sparsity pattern is well designed. The crux is to find a topology of sparse factors that transmits information densely while minimizing the number of free edges under orthogonality.

# Butterfly Factorization: Efficient Sparse Composition of Dense Orthogonality

The butterfly structure, originally from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, provides an ideal sparsity topology for efficient dense connectivity. Butterfly matrices can be defined recursively using block-diagonal “butterfly components” that are themselves composed of small butterfly factors. With $d=2^N$, a $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is formed by the product
\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix with $d/k$ blocks of size $k$, and each block is a butterfly factor. Crucially, each butterfly component can be parameterized as orthogonal by turning its $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ blocks into orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform. The non-zero patterns of larger components are permutations of the base case, so their orthogonality follows from the orthogonality of the constituent blocks.

By composing $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ butterfly components, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, we obtain a dense orthogonal matrix with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. This is a dramatic reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by a full dense orthogonal matrix. In the BOFT framework, we denote the composed orthogonal as $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where $m$ is the number of butterfly components and $b$ is the block size inside each component. BOFT$(m,b)$ uses $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters to finetune a $d\times n$ layer. Setting $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ yields the canonical butterfly parameterization with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In contrast, the original OFT needs $b=d$ to form a dense orthogonal, costing $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

# From Quadratic to Near-Linear Growth: What Changes and Why It Matters

Conceptually, converting the parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ changes the growth class from quadratic to near-linear. Quadratic growth means that doubling $d$ roughly quadruples the parameter count, while near-linear growth means the parameter count scales almost linearly with $d$, modulated only by a $\log d$ factor. In practice, this shift is crucial for scalability: as model dimensions grow, the number of trainable parameters expands far more slowly under $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ than under $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, enabling more efficient finetuning of large layers.

Within BOFT, the $\log d$ factor has an intuitive origin: it is the number of butterfly stages required to mix information across dimensions. Each stage transmits information across disjoint groups, and $\log d$ stages suffice to connect every input dimension to every output dimension. The overall count of trainable parameters is therefore proportional to the number of stages and the number of trainable elements per stage, giving $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

This change in growth class directly impacts the parameter budget available to downstream tasks. With quadratic growth, parameter-efficient finetuning is constrained to small blocks or heavy sparsity that may harm expressiveness. With near-linear growth, BOFT retains dense representational capacity while staying within budgets suited to large models. As a result, BOFT can interpolate between regularity and expressivity more smoothly than block-diagonal OFT, selecting $m$ and $b$ to fit both resource constraints and task demands.

# Parameterized Orthogonality in BOFT: Construction Details and Stability

BOFT constructs the finetuned weights by multiplying a structured orthogonal $\bm{R}(m,b)$ on the left of $\bm{W}^0$, producing $(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ in the forward pass. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is parameterized as orthogonal, and the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. BOFT initializes every component as the identity, ensuring the finetuning process begins exactly at the pretrained model. This identity initialization is achieved by setting the skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley transforms to zeros.

Preserving orthogonality throughout finetuning has favorable spectral properties. If $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the singular value decomposition of the pretrained weights, then the finetuned weights become $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change the largest singular value, so the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved. This preservation improves training stability and generalization compared to additive low-rank updates, while BOFT’s parameterization keeps the number of trainable parameters in the near-linear growth class.

# Expressivity, Inductive Bias, and the Role of Structure

Moving from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ does not merely reduce parameters; it reshapes the hypothesis class. BOFT’s butterfly structure represents a structured subset of the full orthogonal group. Empirically and theoretically, this subset is more expressive than the block-diagonal structure used in OFT for the same block size, since butterfly parameterizations and permutations can recover many classic fast linear transforms. In particular, Theorem 1 shows that products of butterfly matrices and their transposes can approximate the full orthogonal group when enough layers are composed.

This structure introduces an inductive bias. Unlike arbitrary dense matrices, butterfly compositions encode a sparsity pattern and stagewise mixing that align with classic transforms such as discrete Fourier and Hadamard transforms. The inductive bias is controlled by the choice of $m$ and $b$. Increasing $m$ expands expressivity by adding more mixing stages while retaining near-linear parameter growth. Increasing $b$ enlarges each local transformation, trading parameters against flexibility. BOFT therefore offers a smoother interpolation between identity transformations (strong regularity) and full orthogonal matrices (high expressivity) than the block-diagonal approach, all while staying within a practical parameter budget.

# Practical Considerations: Initialization, Dropout, and Runtime

BOFT uses identity initialization for all butterfly components, ensuring no deviation from the pretrained model at the start of finetuning. Because BOFT updates weights multiplicatively, conventional Dropout does not directly apply. BOFT addresses this by a multiplicative Dropout: randomly selecting a proportion of butterfly components and a proportion of blocks within each component, and replacing the selected blocks with identity matrices during training. This prevents overfitting while respecting the orthogonal, multiplicative nature of the update.

There is a runtime trade-off: BOFT composes the final orthogonal matrix from multiple sparse orthogonal factors, so more matrix multiplications occur during training compared to a single-factor approach. Nevertheless, after finetuning, the learned orthogonal components can be multiplied into the pretrained weights once, incurring no additional inference latency. Importantly, this runtime cost is the computational price paid to convert parameter growth from quadratic to near-linear without sacrificing dense connectivity.

# Limitations and Outlook Within the Information Transmission Framework

BOFT’s near-linear parameter growth hinges on the butterfly network’s efficiency in transmitting information across dimensions. While butterfly structures are highly effective, they may not be uniquely optimal. The information transmission framework invites exploration of alternative sparse topologies that satisfy dense connectivity with minimal free edges under orthogonality. Such designs could further reduce constants in the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ count or improve training dynamics.

Despite these open questions, the conceptual shift from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ is decisive. It changes the parameter growth class from quadratic to near-linear, enabling dense orthogonal finetuning to scale with model dimension $d$ far more gracefully. BOFT leverages this shift to provide a generic, parameter-efficient method for adapting large models, preserving spectral properties and angle structure, and balancing expressivity with regularity—all while maintaining a practical parameter budget dictated by $d\log d$ rather than $d^2$.
