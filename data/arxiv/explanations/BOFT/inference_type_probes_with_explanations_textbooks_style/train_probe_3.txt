Chapter Title: Orthogonality-Preserving Multiplicative Dropout in Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Preserving Neuron Geometry

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapting pretrained models that emphasizes preserving the internal geometric structure of neurons. Instead of adding a low-rank perturbation to a pretrained weight matrix, OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight as a multiplicative transformation by an orthogonal matrix. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$,
where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the input and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the output. By left-multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ with an orthogonal matrix, OFT preserves all pairwise angles between the neuron vectors (the columns of $\bm{W}^0$), thereby maintaining the relational information acquired during pretraining.

To ensure that the learned $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout optimization, OFT employs Cayley parameterization. Specifically, $\bm{R}$ is written as
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization enforces orthogonality by construction and allows a natural identity initialization by setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning. While OFT can represent full dense orthogonal matrices, this generality leads to a large number of trainable parameters when $d$ is large. The original OFT addresses this by using a block-diagonal orthogonal structure, $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$ with blocks $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ and $br=d$, which reduces parameter count but at the cost of limited expressivity and undesirable inductive biases.

# From Information Transmission to Butterfly Factorization

A central insight of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is that a dense orthogonal matrix can be efficiently assembled as a product of sparse orthogonal matrices. This design is guided by an information transmission view: representing the multiplication
$\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$
as a layered graph with $d\times(m+1)$ nodes, where each factor $\bm{B}_i$ encodes how information flows from the $i$-th layer of nodes to the $(i+1)$-th layer. Dense connectivity from sources to receivers ensures that, in aggregate, the product $\bm{R}$ is dense, even though each individual factor is sparse.

The butterfly structure from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform is a particularly efficient topology for such information transmission. It connects $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. BOFT leverages this structure by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as a product of orthogonal “butterfly components,” each being a sparse block-diagonal matrix whose non-zero pattern can be viewed as a permutation of $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$ in the block case) orthogonal blocks. This composition preserves the desired dense connectivities while dramatically reducing the number of effective trainable parameters.

Formally, assume $d=2^N$ and define the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$ as
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ for all $i$. A butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is defined as a block-diagonal matrix with block size $k$:
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big).
\]
The $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is constructed recursively via
\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\tfrac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\tfrac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}
=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]
For BOFT, we generalize to block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each scalar entry in the factor is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix, yielding $2b\times 2b$ structured blocks. Each block is parameterized as an orthogonal matrix, for example via a Cayley transform on a $2b\times 2b$ skew-symmetric parameter, ensuring that every butterfly component is orthogonal.

# Orthogonality Under Composition

The core algebraic property that underpins BOFT is that the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. If $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are orthogonal, then
\[
(\bm{A}\bm{B})^\top(\bm{A}\bm{B})=\bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{B}^\top\bm{I}\bm{B}=\bm{I}.
\]
Since each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is constructed to be orthogonal, and since BOFT composes $m$ such components, the overall matrix
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}
\]
satisfies
\[
\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\big)^\top\bm{R}(m,b)=\bm{I}_d.
\]
Moreover, the identity matrix $\bm{I}_d$ is itself orthogonal, so inserting $\bm{I}_d$ at any position in the product—or replacing any orthogonal factor with $\bm{I}_d$—preserves orthogonality of the final product. This simple but powerful closure property enables robust design choices during training, including specialized regularization strategies such as multiplicative Dropout.

BOFT also benefits from identity initialization. Because each $2b\times 2b$ block within every butterfly component is parameterized via a Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric block, setting these skew-symmetric parameters to zero yields block identities, and hence each component becomes an identity matrix at the start of finetuning. The complete product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ therefore initializes to $\bm{I}_d$, exactly reproducing the pretrained model on day one and incrementally deviating only as optimization progresses.

# Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT

Conventional Dropout randomly zeroes elements of additive updates. However, BOFT updates weights multiplicatively by an orthogonal matrix, so additive Dropout does not apply directly. To address this, BOFT introduces a multiplicative Dropout tailored to its parameterization. The idea is simple: randomly choose a percentage of the butterfly components and, within each chosen component, randomly choose a percentage of the diagonal blocks, and replace those blocks with identity matrices.

Let $\bm{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)}\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$, where each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal and can be permuted into a block-diagonal matrix with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks. Multiplicative Dropout selects a random subset $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$ of component indices and, for each $i\in\mathcal{S}$, selects a random subset of block indices within $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. The selected blocks are then replaced by $2b\times 2b$ identity blocks, while the unselected blocks remain orthogonal. As a result, each dropped component is still an orthogonal matrix (because a block-diagonal matrix with orthogonal blocks remains orthogonal, and replacing some blocks by identity preserves block orthogonality), and thus the full product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ remains orthogonal.

Practically, this procedure regularizes the multiplicative update by temporarily suppressing certain local transformations, biasing the finetuning trajectory toward simpler orthogonal perturbations. Because the identity matrix is the neutral element of matrix multiplication, this Dropout mechanism “removes” the contribution of selected blocks or components without introducing any non-orthogonal behavior.

# Why Multiplicative Dropout Preserves Orthogonality

The validity of multiplicative Dropout in BOFT rests on several structural facts established by the parameterization:

First, every butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is orthogonal by construction. Each component can be permuted to a block-diagonal form whose blocks are $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal matrices. Orthogonality is preserved under block-diagonal composition: if $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(\bm{A}_1,\dots,\bm{A}_t)$ and each $\bm{A}_j$ is orthogonal, then
\[
\bm{D}^\top\bm{D}=\text{diag}\big(\bm{A}_1^\top\bm{A}_1,\dots,\bm{A}_t^\top\bm{A}_t\big)=\text{diag}(\bm{I},\dots,\bm{I})=\bm{I}.
\]

Second, the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is orthogonal and acts as the neutral element of multiplication. Replacing any subset of blocks in a block-diagonal orthogonal factor with identity blocks yields another block-diagonal orthogonal factor. Formally, if a block-diagonal factor is $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(\bm{A}_1,\dots,\bm{A}_t)$ and we form $\bm{D}'=\text{diag}(\bm{A}_1,\dots,\bm{I},\dots,\bm{A}_t)$ by substituting identity for some blocks, then
\[
(\bm{D}')^\top\bm{D}'=\text{diag}\big(\bm{A}_1^\top\bm{A}_1,\dots,\bm{I}^\top\bm{I},\dots,\bm{A}_t^\top\bm{A}_t\big)=\bm{I}.
\]
Thus $\bm{D}'$ remains orthogonal.

Third, the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Therefore, if we replace any entire butterfly component with $\bm{I}_d$, or replace some of its blocks with identity blocks, the resulting factor remains orthogonal. The complete product over components remains orthogonal because the closure property applies globally:
\[
\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^m\bm{F}_i\bigg)^\top\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^m\bm{F}_i\bigg)=\bm{I}
\quad\text{whenever each }\bm{F}_i\text{ is orthogonal,}
\]
and identity substitutions do not violate the orthogonality of any $\bm{F}_i$.

From an initialization standpoint, identity substitutions are aligned with the identity initialization used in BOFT. Setting some blocks to identity is equivalent to setting the corresponding skew-symmetric parameters in the Cayley transform to zero for those blocks. This keeps the parameterization consistent and the optimization stable, while enforcing a controlled reduction in the effective capacity of the update.

# BOFT Forward Pass and Parameter Efficiency

Combining the above elements, BOFT defines the finetuned forward pass as
\[
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},
\quad\text{with}\quad
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
and each factor satisfies
\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d
\quad\text{for all }j\in\{1,\dots,m\}.
\]
When $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT uses a butterfly matrix and achieves $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters, which is significantly more efficient than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters of a full dense orthogonal matrix. More generally, BOFT($m$,$b$) uses $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective parameters for a $d\times n$ layer. Importantly, BOFT($1$,$b$) recovers block-diagonal OFT, and BOFT($1$,$d$) recovers the original OFT with a full orthogonal $\bm{R}$.

Because multiplicative Dropout replaces selected components or blocks with identity matrices, it does not alter these orthogonality properties and leaves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ intact after multiplication by $\bm{R}(m,b)$. Orthogonal transformations preserve singular values, so the largest singular value of $\bm{W}^0$ remains unchanged in $\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0$, benefiting training stability.

# Practical Implications of Orthogonality-Preserving Dropout

The multiplicative Dropout mechanism acts as a structured regularizer for BOFT. By stochastically suppressing certain butterfly components or blocks through identity substitutions, it reduces the effective complexity of the orthogonal transformation during training. This prevents overfitting by constraining the search to simpler subspaces of the orthogonal group while still allowing the model to explore rich structured transforms across iterations.

Critically, the mechanism’s validity hinges on orthogonality preservation. Because identity matrices are themselves orthogonal and block-diagonal orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal when some blocks are set to identity, this Dropout does not break the core invariants of BOFT. It keeps $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in the orthogonal group at every training step, ensuring that BOFT continues to preserve neuron angles and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights. The ability to drop entire components or partial blocks without violating orthogonality is unique to BOFT’s multiplicative, block-structured design and follows from the butterfly factorization and Cayley-based block orthogonal parameterization.

Moreover, this design seamlessly integrates with identity initialization. Early in training, many blocks are close to identity because their skew-symmetric parameters are near zero. Multiplicative Dropout simply enforces identity exactly on randomly selected blocks or components, aligning with the initialization and encouraging gradual, controlled deviation from the pretrained model.

# Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) achieves parameter-efficient finetuning by factorizing a dense orthogonal transformation into a product of sparse, orthogonal butterfly components. The orthogonality of each component is guaranteed by block-wise Cayley parameterization, and the product of such components remains orthogonal. This structure enables a specialized multiplicative Dropout that stochastically replaces selected components or blocks with identity matrices, thereby regularizing training while preserving the core orthogonality invariant. Because identity is orthogonal and block-diagonal orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal under identity substitutions, this operation keeps $\bm{R}(m,b)$ valid throughout optimization. The result is a principled, geometry-preserving finetuning method that combines expressivity, parameter efficiency, and training stability.
