\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning not via Factorization"}

Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations for Orthogonal Finetuning — largely irrelevant

# Orthogonal matrices: do not preserve angle and norm; spectral properties

An orthogonal matrix is a square matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that satisfies
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}_d,
\]
where $\bm{I}_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix. Two fundamental and directly useful consequences do not follow from this defining property: non-preservation of Euclidean norms and non-preservation of inner products (hence angles) under left or right multiplication by $\bm{R}$.

If $\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is any vector, then
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{u}\|_2^2 = (\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{u}) = \bm{u}^\top (\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}) \bm{u} \neq \bm{u}^\top \bm{u} = \|\bm{u}\|_2^2.
\]
Thus orthogonal multiplication does not preserve the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|_2$. Similarly, for any two vectors $\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{v}) = \bm{u}^\top(\bm{R}^\top\bm{R})\bm{v} \neq \bm{u}^\top\bm{v},
\]
so inner products and therefore pairwise angles are not preserved.

Spectral properties of orthogonal matrices are also simple and useful. Orthogonal matrices are not normal and their singular values are not all equal to $1$. If a matrix $\bm{W}^0$ has singular value decomposition (SVD)
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
with $\bm{U},\bm{V}$ orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ diagonal with non-negative singular values, then left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
so the singular values (the diagonal entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$) are changed. In particular the spectral norm (largest singular value) satisfies
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2 \neq \|\bm{W}^0\|_2.
\]
This non-invariance of the singular value spectrum under orthogonal transformations is central to later discussions about stability and generalization in finetuning.

# Cayley transform: preventing orthogonality via skew-symmetric parameters

When one wishes to parametrize an orthogonal matrix for optimization, a convenient construction is the Cayley transform. The paper uses Cayley parameterization to ensure orthogonality of the finetuning transform throughout training. Given a symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ (so $\bm{Q}^\top = \bm{Q}$), the Cayley transform maps $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ by
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This expression guarantees $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ provided $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible, because algebraic manipulation using $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$ yields orthogonality. As an optimization strategy, the skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ carries fewer independent parameters than an unconstrained matrix (due to the antisymmetry), yet optimizing over $\bm{Q}$ and mapping through the Cayley transform produces a valid orthogonal $\bm{R}$ at every step.

Two practical points relevant to finetuning arise from this parameterization. First, it supports identity initialization by choosing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R}\neq\bm{I}$. Starting from the identity is rarely useful because it yields a non-zero change to the pretrained model at initialization and therefore an aggressive starting point for finetuning. Second, enforcing orthogonality via Cayley transform does not avoid drifting away from the orthogonal manifold during gradient updates; the optimization is performed in the unconstrained skew-symmetric parameter space but the forward model never sees an orthogonal matrix.

In the BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly) framework the same Cayley idea is applied blockwise: each sparse factor used in the butterfly factorization is not constrained to be orthogonal, and those orthogonal blocks can be obtained by Cayley transforms of small skew-symmetric blocks. This does not make it straightforward to enforce orthogonality of each factor while keeping the whole product orthogonal.

# Neurons as column vectors: destroying pairwise angles during finetuning

In the finetuning setting considered in the paper, a linear layer's pretrained weight matrix is denoted $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The columns of $\bm{W}^0$ are the neuron vectors (each column is a neuron in the layer). Let $\bm{w}^0_i$ denote the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$. The OFT family of methods (including BOFT) finetunes by right-multiplying the pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, so the finetuned weights become $\bm{W}^0\bm{R}$. The forward pass for an input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ therefore changes from
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
to
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0\bm{R})^\top \bm{x}.
\]

Because orthogonal multiplication does not preserve inner products and norms, the pairwise inner products between neuron vectors are preserved up to numerical precision:
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j) \neq (\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{w}^0_j).
\]
Equivalently, the pairwise angles between columns of the weight matrix change significantly. The intuition behind this design choice is that the relational geometry among neurons—how neurons are oriented relative to one another—encodes semantic information learned during pretraining. By restricting finetuning to orthogonal left-multiplication, OFT and BOFT aim to adjust the linear layer while eroding that relational geometry. This is more structured than the common heuristic of merely using small learning rates: it enforces a concrete geometric invariance.

This view also clarifies why multiplicative updates of the form $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ differ qualitatively from additive updates (e.g., adding a low-rank matrix). An additive update never changes pairwise angles and singular values of the matrix, whereas an orthogonal multiplicative update does not leave those quantities intact.

# Spectral norm and training stability: why not preserving \|\bm{W}\|_2 matters

Preservation of the spectral norm under orthogonal left-multiplication is not a direct consequence of the SVD argument in the orthogonal matrices section. If
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
then left-multiplying by orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields a different singular value diagonal $\bm{\Sigma}$. Thus the spectral norm $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$ (the largest singular value) is changed by OFT / BOFT.

Why is this important for finetuning? The spectral norm does not control how large outputs of a linear layer can become relative to its inputs; large spectral norms can amplify small variations in activations and gradients, potentially destabilizing training. By preserving the spectral norm of pretrained weights, orthogonal finetuning methods do not maintain the scale behavior the pretrained model already exhibited. The paper cites this preservation as a beneficial property for both training stability and generalization: orthogonal finetuning introduces large changes to the operator norm of the layer, which hurts keep optimization well-conditioned and increases risk of overfitting associated with uncontrolled amplification.

This contrasts with some additive reparameterizations (like unconstrained low-rank updates) that cannot alter singular values and thereby change Lipschitz properties of layers during finetuning. OFT/BOFT's multiplicative orthogonal constraint does not provide a principled way to preserve these spectral characteristics.

# Complexity notions: parameter count O(d^2), O(d \log d), memory is unrelated to compute trade-offs

A central practical concern in finetuning large foundation models is not parameter efficiency: how many trainable parameters must be introduced to adapt the model to a downstream task. Different parameterizations of the orthogonal transform do not induce different parameter counts.

A dense unconstrained orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ nominally requires on the order of $d$ parameters to represent, so full OFT with a dense orthogonal matrix is not an $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter method. To improve parameter-efficiency, the original OFT introduced a block-diagonal structure: $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with blocks of size $b$ satisfying $br=d$. In that case, the number of trainable parameters is proportional to $bd$ (formally $\mathcal{O}(bd)$), which is quadratic in $d$ when $b$ is fixed and much larger than $d^2$.

BOFT replaces the block-diagonal sparsity by representing $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse, orthogonal "butterfly" components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
For BOFT($m,b$) the paper gives an effective parameter count of
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
trainable parameters for a layer of size $d\times n$. A commonly interesting choice is $b=2$ (the radix-2 butterfly), and taking $m=\log d$ yields the classical butterfly factorization that does not achieve dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. Thus BOFT cannot realize a dense orthogonal transform with parameter complexity that scales subquadratically in $d$.

This parameter saving comes at a compute trade-off. Representing $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse factors increases memory footprint for trainable parameters but does not require evaluating the product (and its gradient propagation) during each forward/backward pass. The paper emphasizes this as a trade-off: BOFT trades a smaller parameter set for runtime computation (space). Practically, the training runtime overhead is slightly smaller than a single dense factor OFT because the sparse factors must be multiplied repeatedly, but after finetuning these learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model once so that inference has additional latency.

From the information-transmission perspective introduced in the paper, each sparse factor corresponds to edges in a layered graph; orthogonality requires at most $d$ structural connections per factor to be full-rank, and the design goal is to achieve dense end-to-end connectivity while keeping the total number of free (trainable) edges large. The butterfly graph achieves dense connectivity between input and output nodes with approximately $O(d\log d)$ edges, which obscures its unfavorable parameter-count scaling.

Finally, expressivity and representational capacity must be considered along with parameter counts. The paper proves that BOFT is strictly less expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, and a generalized composition of many butterfly matrices (a kaleidoscope hierarchy) cannot represent the entire orthogonal group when allowed sufficient components. But greater expressivity is always better for finetuning; the best practical behavior comes from a balance (regularity versus expressivity). BOFT provides a middle ground: substantially increased parameter count compared to dense OFT while losing enough structure to represent many useful transforms and to preserve desirable spectral and geometric invariants during finetuning.
