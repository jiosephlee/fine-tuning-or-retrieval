\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Is BOFT essentially a structured sequence of Givens rotations? Relation to Householder/Givens products
Question:
Any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of Householder reflectors or Givens rotations. BOFT builds $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of block-sparse orthogonal factors with $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal sub-blocks. Is BOFT equivalent to using a specific sequence of Givens rotations constrained by the butterfly topology? How does the required number of factors to approximate a generic orthogonal compare to the classical $O(d^2)$ Givens rotations versus the $O(d\log d)$ butterfly depth?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — BOFT is essentially a structured sequence of $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks, so at the low level it is built from the same building blocks as Givens rotations (or $2\times 2$ Householder/rotation blocks). The crucial difference is that BOFT fixes which pairs of coordinates are rotated at each layer according to the butterfly topology, trading full universality for a much smaller parameter budget and fast “information mixing.” Consequently BOFT with shallow butterfly depth (e.g. depth $m=\log d$, block size $b=2$) uses $O(d\log d)$ parameters but only spans a restricted structured subset of the orthogonal group; to recover full universality you must stack many more butterfly factors and the parameter count grows toward the classical $O(d^2)$ budget.

Why BOFT is a constrained Givens-product in plain language. A Givens rotation is a $2\times 2$ orthogonal acting on a selected pair of coordinates. BOFT’s primitive factors are block-diagonal matrices whose blocks are $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) orthogonal blocks (the paper uses Cayley / $2$D rotations for these). Multiplying those factors is therefore exactly multiplying many local $2\times 2$ orthogonals — the same algebraic act as composing Givens rotations. What BOFT adds is a fixed pattern (the butterfly graph) that specifies which coordinate pairs are coupled at each layer. So BOFT = product of many Givens-like blocks, but with the pairings constrained by the butterfly connectivity.

Expressivity vs. factor count (how many factors are needed). The classical facts: an arbitrary orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, and a generic Givens decomposition uses on the order of $d^2$ rotations (or parameters) to represent every orthogonal. BOFT parametrizes $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ butterfly components with block-size $b$. The paper gives the effective number of trainable parameters as approximately
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
$$
so for the canonical butterfly ($b=2$, $m=\log d$) we get $O(d\log d)$ parameters. This is much smaller than $O(d^2)$, but it also means the parameterized set is a strict (structured) subset of all orthogonals.

Universality requires more stacking. The paper’s Theorem and discussion show that by composing more butterfly-type matrices (their kaleidoscope/generalized composition) one can reach the entire orthogonal group: for example the construction $\bm{R}^G$ with many alternating butterfly factors can represent every orthogonal if you take enough factors (they mention needing something like $l=d-1$ in their generalized form when $m_1=m_2=\log d, b_1=b_2=2$). Translating that into parameter counting: to match the full $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom you need $m\approx O(d)$ (for $b=2$), which makes the total parameter count scale like $O(d^2)$ again. So BOFT can interpolate between the two regimes: shallow butterfly depth $\Rightarrow$ $O(d\log d)$ params and restricted expressivity; deep stacking $\Rightarrow$ approach $O(d^2)$ params and universality.

Intuition and practical implication. Think of Givens decompositions as “you may rotate any arbitrary pair when you like,” which gives maximal flexibility but costs many parameters. BOFT fixes a fast-mixing schedule (butterfly) so that each layer mixes many coordinates in a constrained, highly efficient way; after $O(\log d)$ layers information from any input coordinate can reach any output coordinate (hence the dense final matrix), but the space of matrices you can realize with only those degrees of freedom is structured. Empirically and theoretically (as the paper notes), that structured subset contains many useful transforms (FFT, Hadamard, etc.) and often gives a favorable bias for finetuning: better parameter-efficiency and generalization than naive block-diagonal OFT, and much smaller than full OFT when you accept the structured restriction.

Summary. BOFT is a sequence of local $2\times 2$ orthogonals (so algebraically a constrained Givens product) arranged in a butterfly topology. With depth $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ it uses $O(d\log d)$ parameters but is not universal; to represent every orthogonal you must stack many more butterfly factors (roughly $O(d)$ layers for $b=2$), at which point the parameter count approaches the classical $O(d^2)$ needed by arbitrary Givens decompositions. The practical trade-off is exactly what the paper emphasizes: you trade expressivity for parameter-efficiency via the butterfly inductive bias.
