\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What exactly is meant by "layer-shared" matrices in BOFT versus OFT?
Question:
The paper says OFT/BOFT transform neurons in the same layer by the same orthogonal matrix and also mentions "layer-shared weight matrices." Does BOFT learn one $\mathbf{R}$ per linear weight matrix, one per layer shared across all projections in that layer, or a single $\mathbf{R}$ shared across multiple layers? For LLMs and ViTs, what sharing granularity was used in experiments?
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper “layer‑shared” means one orthogonal transform $R$ is shared across the linear projections that belong to the same neural network layer (e.g. the projection matrices inside a Transformer block). OFT as originally described learns one $R$ per individual linear weight matrix; BOFT uses the butterfly factorization for $R$ and in the experiments the authors use a single BOFT $R$ per layer (shared among that layer’s projections).  

Why that wording appears and what it means intuitively. The original OFT formulation treats each pretrained linear layer $W^0$ separately and learns an orthogonal matrix $R$ for that specific $W^0$, so the granularity there is “per linear weight matrix.” The paper then emphasizes a slightly different practical design: instead of having a distinct $R$ for every singly indexed matrix (which multiplies the number of trainable orthogonal factors), BOFT applies a single orthogonal transform to all neuron vectors in the same layer — i.e. the same $R$ is multiplied into multiple weight matrices that implement the layer’s projections (for example $Q/K/V$ or the feed‑forward in a Transformer block). That is what the authors refer to as “layer‑shared weight matrices.” The idea is to preserve the pairwise geometry of neurons in that layer while saving parameters by not instantiating separate $R$’s for each projection.

Where BOFT sits relative to the extremes. The framework is flexible: you can recover OFT’s per‑matrix behavior by giving each linear map its own $R$ (no sharing), and you could in principle share a single $R$ across many layers (more aggressive sharing), but the BOFT paper specifically picks the middle ground — sharing an orthogonal factor at the layer level — because it strikes a balance between parameter efficiency and expressivity. The butterfly factorization itself is orthogonal to this sharing choice: it reduces the number of parameters needed to represent each $R$ (from $O(d^2)$ down toward $O(d\log d)$ for the butterfly) whether $R$ is assigned per matrix or per layer.

What the experiments used. The paper reports BOFT applied to large language models and vision transformers and states that they “transform the weights with layer‑shared weight matrices.” That indicates the experimental setup used one BOFT $R$ per network layer (shared across that layer’s projections). This is the intended sharing granularity in the empirical results reported in the paper.

Implications. Sharing $R$ at the layer level preserves inter‑neuron angular relationships within the layer while reducing the number of separate orthogonal factors to learn. It saves parameters relative to per‑matrix OFT, while staying more expressive than forcing a single global $R$ for the whole network. The butterfly parameterization further improves parameter efficiency of each shared $R$, so the combined design yields practical finetuning with strong generalization in the authors’ LLM and ViT experiments.
