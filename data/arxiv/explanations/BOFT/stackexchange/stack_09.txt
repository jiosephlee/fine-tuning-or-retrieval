### How exactly is the multiplicative Dropout implemented while preserving orthogonality?
Question:
The multiplicative Dropout randomly picks a fraction of butterfly components and a fraction of their diagonal blocks and replaces them with identities. Since the identity is orthogonal and the product of orthogonals is orthogonal, this seems fine structurally. But:
- Are different identity masks sampled per batch, and does this cause biased gradients for the retained blocks?
- Do the dropped blocks receive zero gradients (like standard Dropout), or do you scale the kept blocks by a factor to keep expectations consistent?
- Is orthogonality preserved numerically when mixing dropped and non-dropped blocks during training?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper’s multiplicative Dropout replaces randomly chosen butterfly components (and some of their diagonal blocks) with identity blocks during training. That replacement makes those blocks constant (orthogonal) for the forward pass, so their parameters do not receive gradient signal; the paper does not introduce an explicit expectation-preserving rescaling. Orthogonality is preserved by construction at the block level (identities and Cayley‑parameterized blocks are orthogonal), though floating‑point multiplication of many factors can introduce small numerical drift in practice.

Explanation and implications (intuitive + technical)

1) Mask sampling and gradient bias
The paper states that it “randomly picks $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks … and replaces these blocks as identity matrices.” It does not say this mask is fixed for an epoch or minibatch, but the natural and common implementation is to sample a fresh mask each training step (i.e. per minibatch / per forward). Doing so yields a stochastic regularizer analogous in spirit to standard dropout. However, unlike additive Dropout or LoRA-style additive updates, this is multiplicative: you replace multiplicative factors by the identity rather than zeroing an additive term. Because the replacement is not followed by any compensating scaling in the paper, the gradient estimator is biased with respect to the deterministic, unmasked objective. That is, the expectation of the product of masked orthogonal factors is not equal to the unmasked product unless special scaling/corrections are applied. The paper appears to accept this as a form of structured stochastic regularization rather than an unbiased estimator.

2) Gradients to dropped blocks and possible scaling
When a block is replaced by the identity for the forward pass, that block’s parameters no longer affect the forward outputs in that pass; consequently their gradients are zero for that example / batch (exactly the same effect as standard dropout sets the dropped units’ parameters to receive no gradient while they are dropped). The paper describes replacement-by-identity and does not mention any extra multiplicative scaling of the remaining (kept) blocks to make the expectation match the unmasked case, so you should assume no scaling is performed. Practically, one could design an alternative “inverted” variant that rescales kept blocks to approximate the unmasked expectation, but for multiplicative factorization this is nontrivial: because the overall transform is a product of factors, a simple scalar rescaling of kept blocks does not, in general, restore the expected product. That is likely why the authors chose the simpler replacement-with-identity strategy (a structured stochastic regularizer) rather than attempt an unbiased correction.

3) Orthogonality when mixing dropped and kept blocks (numerical considerations)
By construction orthogonality is preserved at the block level: the identity is orthogonal, and each kept block is parameterized (e.g. via Cayley transform or $2\times2$ rotations) to be orthogonal during training. The product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, so in exact arithmetic the composed $R$ is orthogonal even with a mixture of identity and learned blocks. In floating point arithmetic, repeated matrix multiplications can introduce small rounding errors, so you may observe tiny deviations from exact orthogonality after composing many factors. In practice these numerical deviations are small; common mitigations are to maintain orthogonality via the parameterization (Cayley / Givens / explicit $2\times2$ rotations) and, if needed, periodically reproject or renormalize blocks (or use higher precision accumulation) during training. The paper relies on Cayley parameterization for each block, which keeps each block orthogonal to machine precision up to accumulation error in the product.

Practical takeaways and suggestions
- Implement the mask per training step (per minibatch) if you want standard stochastic regularization; you can also experiment with per-example masks or masks that persist for several steps, but the paper gives no recommendation beyond “randomly pick … and replace.”
- Expect dropped blocks to get zero gradients while dropped. This is a design choice that trades bias in gradient expectation for a structural regularizer that maintains orthogonality.
- If you worry about numerical drift in composed $R$, rely on orthogonal parameterizations for blocks (Cayley/Givens) and optionally reproject or clamp (rarely needed). The identity-replacement itself does not break orthogonality in exact arithmetic.

In short: multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is a structured stochastic regularizer implemented by replacing selected orthogonal blocks with identity; it zeroes gradients for those blocks when dropped, introduces bias relative to the unmasked product (no expectation-preserving scaling is proposed), and preserves orthogonality by construction up to usual floating‑point accumulation errors.
