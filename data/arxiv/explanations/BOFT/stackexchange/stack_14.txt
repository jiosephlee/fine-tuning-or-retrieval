\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Do BOFT and OFT actually preserve pairwise neuron angles in the weight matrix?
Question:
The motivation for OFT is to preserve pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors (columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$). If $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ and $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, then for two columns $\mathbf{w}^0_i,\mathbf{w}^0_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we have $\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_j \rangle = \langle \mathbf{w}^0_i, \mathbf{w}^0_j \rangle$. Could someone confirm this holds exactly for BOFT (since it is a product of orthogonals) and discuss whether numerical drift from exact orthogonality could meaningfully affect this angle preservation in practice?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes in theory; both OFT and BOFT preserve pairwise angles exactly because they multiply the pretrained weight matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix, and BOFT is built as a product of orthogonal factors so the product is orthogonal. In practice, floating‑point errors can introduce tiny non‑orthogonality, but the induced change in inner products (and thus angles) is provably bounded and typically negligible; if needed one can re‑orthogonalize or use higher precision.

Explanation grounded in the paper
OFT represents the finetuned weights as $W = R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal. The paper uses Cayley parameterization (or small $2\times2$ rotations) so that each learned factor is orthogonal during training. BOFT parameterizes $R$ as a product of orthogonal butterfly components $R = B_m \dots B_1$; since each $B_i$ is orthogonal, $R$ is orthogonal (because a product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal). Therefore, for two neuron columns $w^0_i, w^0_j$, we have exactly
$\langle R w^0_i, R w^0_j\rangle = (R w^0_i)^T (R w^0_j) = (w^0_i)^T (R^T R) w^0_j = (w^0_i)^T w^0_j$,
because $R^T R = I$. This is the paper’s stated motivation: orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise angles and the spectral properties of $W^0$.

Finite‑precision / numerical drift
In floating point arithmetic the computed $R$ may not satisfy $R^T R = I$ exactly. Let $E := R^T R - I$ denote the deviation from orthogonality. The change in the inner product of two columns equals
$(w^0_i)^T E w^0_j$,
so an absolute bound is
$\left|(w^0_i)^T E w^0_j\right| \le \|E\|_{\mathrm{op}} \cdot \|w^0_i\|_2 \cdot \|w^0_j\|_2$,
where $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the operator (spectral) norm of $E$. Thus the angle error is controlled by the size of the orthogonality violation and the norms of the neuron vectors.

How large is $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ in practice? For typical training with Cayley parameterization or parameterizing each butterfly block as exact rotations, the algebraic parameterization enforces orthogonality up to numerical rounding. Rounding errors in FP32 are on the order of machine epsilon (approximately $10^{-7}$) per operation; after many ops the accumulated $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ remains very small (typically many orders of magnitude below signal norms), so the induced inner‑product perturbation is negligible for usual neural network scales. If you use very deep compositions, very large dimensions, or low precision (FP16) arithmetic, accumulation can grow and make $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ larger, but it still tends to be small relative to typical neuron norms.

Practical implications and mitigations
Because the paper already uses Cayley transforms and builds BOFT from block orthogonals ($2\times2$ rotations or their block analogs), orthogonality is enforced algebraically, so numerical drift is minimal. If you are concerned in a particular application (e.g., extreme low precision or very long training), you can monitor $\|R^T R - I\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ or periodically re‑orthogonalize (e.g., QR or polar decomposition) or use mixed precision where the orthogonal factors are stored/updated in higher precision. In summary, angle preservation is exact in the math of OFT/BOFT and in practice only suffers tiny, controllable numerical drift that can be bounded and mitigated if necessary.
