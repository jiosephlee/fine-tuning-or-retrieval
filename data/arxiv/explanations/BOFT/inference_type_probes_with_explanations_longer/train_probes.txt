In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors note that increasing expressiveness without appropriate regularity during finetuning commonly leads to overfitting. This follows from their repeated emphasis on constraining the hypothesis class of finetuning updates and trading computation for space via structured parameterizations. In BOFT, the learnable update is an orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R} \) composed as a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components, which defines a structured subset of the full orthogonal group. The authors explicitly argue that “better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning, as full finetuning, despite its universal expressiveness, often yields unsatisfactory performance,” and position BOFT to find a better trade-off between expressivity and regularity. Constraining \(\bm{R}\) to orthogonal updates preserves relational geometry (pairwise neuron angles) and the spectral norm of the pretrained layer, i.e., if \( \bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \) and the finetuned weights are \( \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \), then \( \|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2 \), which stabilizes optimization and limits the capacity to arbitrarily distort pretrained features. Thus, expanding expressiveness (e.g., moving toward the full orthogonal group) without such structural constraints removes these stabilizing properties and increases the effective hypothesis space, making overfitting more likely.

Moreover, the paper motivates BOFT’s butterfly factorization as an implicit inductive bias: its sparsity patterns and permutations can recover many classic fast transforms (e.g., FFT, Hadamard), while still achieving dense connectivity with only \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \) parameters instead of \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \). This structured expressivity provides a “smoother interpolation” between the identity and the full orthogonal group, controlled by the depth \( m \) and block size \( b \) in \( \bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} \). Identity initialization and multiplicative dropout further regularize training by starting at \( \bm{R} = \bm{I} \) and randomly setting \(2b\times 2b\) blocks to identity during finetuning, which restricts the effective capacity of the update. In contrast, simply increasing expressiveness—e.g., using dense, unconstrained matrices—removes these geometric, spectral, and structural safeguards, enlarges the hypothesis class, and, absent appropriate regularity, typically yields overfitting in downstream adaptation.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', replacing BOFT's structured product of sparse orthogonal factors with an unstructured dense orthogonal matrix while keeping the same number of effective parameters is likely to result in worse generalization. The core reason is that BOFT deliberately constrains the hypothesis class to a structured subset of the orthogonal group via butterfly factorization: it composes a dense orthogonal transform as a product of \(m\) sparse, orthogonal components \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\), resulting in \(\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) with only \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) parameters when \(m=\log d,b=2\). This structure encodes an inductive bias shared by many classic fast linear transforms (e.g., FFT, Hadamard), which the paper argues “benefits generalizability and prevents overfitting.” In contrast, an “unstructured” dense orthogonal parameterization with the same parameter budget must fix most entries or tie them in ad hoc ways, yielding a hypothesis class whose sparsity/parameter sharing is not aligned with meaningful information-exchange topologies. The information transmission view clarifies this distinction: butterfly graphs achieve dense end-to-end connectivity with the minimal number of trainable edges subject to orthogonality, whereas an unstructured scheme with the same budget cannot guarantee efficient, well-distributed paths and thus tends to either underfit globally or overfit locally.

Moreover, BOFT provides a smoother interpolation between expressivity and regularity than both full orthogonal finetuning and block-diagonal OFT: varying \(m\) and \(b\) controls the size of the hypothesis class while preserving the spectral norm of \(\bm{W}^0\) (since \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, \(\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2=\|\bm{W}^0\|_2\)). The paper’s empirical and theoretical discussion shows that increasing \(m\) (more factors) with small \(b\) improves parameter-efficiency and approximation quality of dense orthogonal transforms without exploding degrees of freedom, and Theorem 1 indicates that butterfly compositions can approach the full orthogonal group when needed. An unstructured dense parametrization constrained to the same number of “effective parameters” lacks this principled control: it either becomes too rigid (because most entries are frozen) or too unconstrained in the wrong places, missing the beneficial hierarchy of local \(2\times 2\) rotations that transmit information globally. Hence, under equal parameter budgets, BOFT’s structured factorization induces a favorable inductive bias and better balances expressivity vs. regularity, which the paper identifies as the key to generalization in finetuning.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the proposed multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces a percentage of the butterfly components and diagonal blocks in each butterfly component with identity matrices $\bm{I}$, relying on the implicit property that replacing these blocks by $\bm{I}$ keeps the model valid, namely that this operation preserves orthogonality. This is sensible because BOFT parameterizes the finetuning transform as a product of orthogonal butterfly components, $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is block-diagonal with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks (constructed via Cayley transforms). The identity matrix is itself orthogonal, and replacing some blocks in a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with identities yields another block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are all orthogonal. Formally, if $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ is block-diagonal with blocks $\{\bm{O}_j\}_{j}$ satisfying $\bm{O}_j^\top \bm{O}_j=\bm{I}$, then setting a subset to $\bm{I}$ yields $\tilde{\bm{B}}'=\mathrm{diag}(\ldots,\bm{O}_{j},\ldots,\bm{I},\ldots)$ and still $\tilde{\bm{B}}'^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}'=\mathrm{diag}(\ldots,\bm{I},\ldots,\bm{I},\ldots)=\bm{I}$. Because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, the dropped version $\bm{R}'(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}'^b_{(d,i)}$ also satisfies $\bm{R}'^\top \bm{R}'=\bm{I}_d$ and thus preserves orthogonality.

The paper further notes that butterfly components can be permuted to $2b\times 2b$ block-diagonal form, which makes the Dropout operation local to independent orthogonal blocks and guarantees full rank at each stage. Under the Cayley parameterization $\bm{O}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$, setting a block to $\bm{I}$ corresponds to choosing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ for that block, which is a valid orthogonal parameter setting. Thus, randomly replacing selected butterfly components or their diagonal sub-blocks by $\bm{I}$ simply reduces the effective transformation strength while keeping every factor orthogonal, ensuring the overall finetuning transform $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal and the model remains valid during training.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', for fixed d and m, the effective number of trainable parameters is given by \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\), so to minimize this quantity one should decrease \(b\). This follows from counting the degrees of freedom per butterfly component under the block parameterization. Each component \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}(d,k)\) is built from block orthogonal transforms applied to disjoint \(b\)-dimensional subspaces. The dimension of the orthogonal group \(O(b)\) is \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\), so one block contributes \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) free parameters. Because there are \(\frac{d}{b}\) such blocks per component, the total trainable parameters per component is \(\frac{d}{b}\cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d\). BOFT composes \(m\) such components multiplicatively, so the total scales linearly with \(m\), yielding \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\). With \(d\) and \(m\) fixed, this quantity is monotonically increasing in \(b\), hence decreasing \(b\) minimizes the parameter count.

This conclusion is also sensible in light of the expressivity–regularity trade-off the paper emphasizes. Larger \(b\) allows each block to explore a larger subspace, increasing expressivity because \(O(b)\) has dimension \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\), but it inflates parameters linearly in \((b-1)\). BOFT mitigates the need for large \(b\) by stacking \(m\) sparse, orthogonal butterfly components so that information is transmitted globally (dense connectivity) via factorization, achieving good expressivity with smaller blocks. In particular, settings like \(m=\log d\) and \(b=2\) already yield dense orthogonal updates with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, illustrating why decreasing \(b\) is the right lever to minimize parameters while still maintaining sufficient expressive power through multiple components.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors explain that conceptually converting parameter complexity from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d\log d)$ changes the parameter growth class from quadratic to near-linear. This follows directly from their butterfly-based orthogonal parameterization: a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix ordinarily requires $\Theta(d^2)$ free parameters, whereas BOFT composes a dense orthogonal transform as a product of $m=\Theta(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal “butterfly components,” each with $\Theta(d)$ nonzero structured entries, yielding a total of $\Theta(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters. Concretely, the paper shows BOFT$(m,b)$ uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ parameters, and with $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ this reduces to $O(d\log d)$, in stark contrast to the $O(d^2)$ parameters required by an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix or the original OFT with block size $b=d$.

This change in asymptotic class is meaningful because $\log d$ grows much more slowly than $d$, so $d\log d$ behaves “near-linearly” compared to $d^2$. Under the paper’s information transmission view, the butterfly topology achieves dense connectivity between $d$ sources and $d$ sinks using only $O(d\log d)$ edges (and correspondingly $O(d\log d)$ trainable entries) by factorizing the transform into $O(\log d)$ stages that each exchange information across disjoint $O(1)$-sized substructures. Ensuring each factor is orthogonal via Cayley parameterization preserves the desirable spectral properties while maintaining the sparse, structured parametrization. Therefore, the BOFT construction shifts the parameter growth from quadratic to near-linear without sacrificing the ability to represent dense orthogonal updates, which underpins its superior parameter-efficiency.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors employ the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. For the Cayley parameterization to be valid, the implicit mathematical condition on $\bm{Q}$ that must hold is that $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ is invertible. This condition guarantees that the inverse in $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ exists and, together with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$, ensures $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal: using $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-T}=(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}^\top)^{-1}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}$, we obtain
$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-T}(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}=\bm{I}$.
Thus, invertibility of $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ is exactly the algebraic requirement that makes the Cayley transform well-defined and orthogonality-preserving.

Moreover, for the real skew-symmetric matrices used throughout the paper (including the $2\times 2$ blocks in butterfly components), $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ is generically invertible, making the condition both sensible and easy to satisfy in practice. The eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ lie on the imaginary axis, i.e., $\sigma(\bm{Q})\subset\{i\lambda:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}$, so the eigenvalues of $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ are $\{1-i\lambda\}$, all of which have strictly positive modulus $\sqrt{1+\lambda^2}>0$; hence $\det(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\neq 0$. In the $2\times 2$ case with $\bm{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}0 & a \\ -a & 0\end{bmatrix}$, we have $\det(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})=\det\!\begin{bmatrix}1 & -a \\ a & 1\end{bmatrix}=1+a^2>0$. Therefore, the paper’s use of the Cayley parameterization on skew-symmetric blocks reliably yields orthogonal factors, and the composite product of such factors in BOFT remains orthogonal while staying computationally well-defined.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', if BOFT's sparse factors were not constrained to be orthogonal, the stability benefit tied to SVD that would likely disappear is spectral norm preservation. The core argument in the paper’s spectral properties section is that OFT/BOFT left-multiply a pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix, which leaves singular values unchanged. Concretely, with SVD \( \bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \) and an orthogonal update \( \bm{R} \) (i.e., \( \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I} \)), the finetuned weights become \( \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \). Since \( \bm{R}\bm{U} \) is also orthogonal, the singular values of \( \bm{W} \) equal the diagonal entries of \( \bm{\Sigma} \), so the spectral norm \( \|\bm{W}\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2 \) is preserved. This invariance is precisely what the paper cites as aiding training stability and generalization, echoing spectral normalization results.

If the sparse factors composing \( \bm{R} \) were not orthogonal, then the product \( \bm{R} \) would in general not satisfy \( \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I} \), and left-multiplication could distort the singular spectrum of \( \bm{W}^0 \). In that case, \( \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \) has singular values given by those of \( \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma} \), which need not equal \( \bm{\Sigma} \); for example, if \( \sigma_{\max}(\bm{R}) \neq 1 \), then \( \|\bm{W}\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}(\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}) \ge \sigma_{\max}(\bm{R})\,\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}) \), potentially amplifying the spectral norm and harming stability (e.g., gradient explosion) or reducing it and causing vanishing gradients. The paper’s use of Cayley parameterization to enforce orthogonality of each sparse factor ensures the product remains orthogonal and retains spectral norm preservation; removing that constraint would forfeit this guaranteed invariance and thus the associated stability benefits.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', to obtain a dense $\bm{R}$ using fewer sequential multiplications (smaller $m$) without abandoning parameter-efficiency, you should increase the block size. In BOFT, the orthogonal update is $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ mixes coordinates within $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks. Under the information transmission view, achieving a dense $\bm{R}$ means every source node must have at least one path to every destination node; larger blocks provide more cross-level “mixing” per multiplication, so fewer stages $m$ are needed to create all-to-all connectivity. The paper makes this relationship explicit: BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$,$b$) yields a dense orthogonal matrix, so if you decrease $m$ you must increase $b$ to maintain density (since $m=\log \frac{2d}{b}$ implies $b=2d/2^m$). Intuitively, increasing $b$ enlarges the local interaction radius in each factor, enabling dense connectivity with fewer factors.

This trade-off preserves parameter-efficiency because the effective trainable parameter count in BOFT is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$. Reducing $m$ while increasing $b$ can keep $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ roughly constant, so you do not pay a quadratic price like $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For example, BOFT($1$,$d$) (one multiplication, very large block) reduces to the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix, while BOFT($\log d$, $2$) uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters to achieve density via many small mixers; intermediate choices of $b$ and $m$ interpolate between these extremes. Thus, to obtain a dense $\bm{R}$ with fewer sequential multiplications while staying parameter-efficient, you increase $b$ so that each factor transmits more information per step, and you balance $(b-1)m$ to maintain the overall budget.
