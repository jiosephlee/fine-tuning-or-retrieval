# Meet BOFT: FFT-Inspired Finetuning That Cuts Parameters Without Cutting Performance

Adapting giant pretrained models to new tasks is now a routine part of machine learning workflows, but it comes with a practical cost: how do you tune models with billions of parameters without renting out a data center? Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods let you change only a small part of the model during adaptation. BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is a new, simple, and principled PEFT method that borrows ideas from the fast Fourier transform (FFT) to represent dense orthogonal updates with far fewer trainable parameters — and, crucially, without losing the stability benefits that orthogonal updates bring.

This post explains the motivation, the core idea, and why BOFT is a useful tool in your PEFT toolbox.

## Why orthogonality matters (and why naive OFT is expensive)

One simple objective when finetuning a pretrained model is to change it as little as possible while letting it adapt. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a structured approach: instead of adding arbitrary updates to a weight matrix, OFT multiplies the frozen pretrained weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(R\). Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer \(W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), OFT replaces the forward pass
z = (W^0)^\top x
with
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
where \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal. Because orthogonal matrices preserve pairwise angles and singular values, OFT helps keep the pretraining knowledge intact, improving training stability and often generalization.

The obvious downside: a dense orthogonal \(R\) needs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters, which is prohibitive for large layers. Prior work made OFT practical by forcing \(R\) to be block-diagonal: that reduces parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) where \(b\) is block size, but it also drastically limits expressivity. Block-diagonal orthogonals cannot approximate many useful global transforms and they introduce an arbitrary index-based sparsity pattern that may be poorly matched to the task.

BOFT asks: can we keep the geometric benefits of orthogonal updates but represent dense orthogonal transforms using far fewer parameters?

## From matrices to information flow: a simple intuition

A neat way to reason about factored matrix representations is as an information transmission problem. Suppose \(R\) is written as a product of \(m\) matrices,
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1.
\]
Think of this as a layered graph with \(d\) nodes per layer and edges determined by nonzero entries of each \(B_i\). For the composed product to be dense (every input node can influence every output node), the layered graph must allow paths from every source to every sink. The fewer nonzero entries (edges) you use while still maintaining dense end-to-end connectivity, the fewer trainable parameters you need.

A fully dense single matrix is one extreme: \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) edges in one layer. A block-diagonal factorization is another extreme: very few local edges but no global connectivity. We want an efficient middle ground: sparse per-layer connectivity that — when composed across layers — yields full connectivity from inputs to outputs.

This reframing makes the problem amenable to known, efficient communication topologies. Enter the butterfly graph.

## The butterfly: FFT’s connectivity pattern, repurposed

The Cooley–Tukey FFT builds global frequency interactions out of a sequence of sparse, structured interactions. Its wiring pattern is called a butterfly graph. Two properties make it attractive for our objective:

- Each factor (butterfly component) is sparse: about \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) nonzeros per factor.
- Composed over \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) stages, the product yields dense global interactions: every input can affect every output.

Translating this into matrix factorization gives a structured product of sparse matrices (a butterfly matrix) that can recover many classical global transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.) and, importantly, can approximate dense orthogonal matrices very efficiently.

BOFT parameterizes the left-multiplicative orthogonal \(R\) as a product of orthogonal butterfly components:
\[
R(m, b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]
where each \(\tilde{B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a structured block butterfly component (blocks of size \(b\)). Choosing \(b=2\) and \(m=\log d\) yields the classic radix-2-style butterfly and uses only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) effective trainable parameters. More generally, BOFT(\(m,b\)) trades off the number of stages \(m\) and block size \(b\), interpolating between block-diagonal OFT and full dense OFT. In particular:

- BOFT(1, b) = block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\).
- BOFT(\(\log d, 2\)) = dense orthogonal implemented with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

Because each factor is enforced orthogonal (via a Cayley parameterization or small rotations), the composed \(R\) stays orthogonal and inherits the spectral-preserving benefits of OFT.

## Practical details that matter

BOFT is designed for straightforward integration into finetuning workflows:

- Identity initialization: all butterfly components start as identity. That means the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights, which is a desirable zero-change initialization for stable finetuning.
- Multiplicative Dropout: standard (additive) dropout is not directly applicable to multiplicative orthogonal updates. BOFT uses a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces some butterfly components or block-diagonal blocks with identity matrices during training, which helps regularize the structured multiplicative update.
- Parameter cost estimate: a BOFT(\(m,b\)) layer costs about \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m\) effective parameters for a linear layer of size \(d\times n\). With \(b=2\) and \(m=\log d\), that becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\), much smaller than \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\).

## Why BOFT works: expressivity, spectral stability, and inductive bias

BOFT balances three competing needs: expressivity (ability to represent useful transforms), parameter-efficiency, and regularity (to preserve pretraining structure). Some key points from the BOFT analysis:

- Expressivity: The butterfly factorization with permutations is expressive enough to recover many classical global transforms. With enough stages and a kaleidoscope-like composition (products of butterfly matrices and their transposes), one can span the whole orthogonal group, but in practice BOFT uses fewer stages to enforce a useful structural bias.
- Spectral preservation: Because BOFT multiplies the pretrained weight by an orthogonal \(R\), the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix are preserved. That keeps spectral norms stable and helps training behave well.
- Inductive bias: The structured subset of orthogonals represented by butterfly factorizations aligns with many useful transforms (Fourier-like, Hadamard-like). This implicit prior helps BOFT generalize better than unconstrained or overly local updates in many settings.

Empirically (reported in the paper), BOFT improves parameter efficiency and generalization across a variety of adaptation tasks and model classes, including vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models.

## Limitations and trade-offs

BOFT is not magic. Some practical considerations:

- Training-time cost: because BOFT composes multiple matrices at each forward pass, it adds a modest runtime overhead during training compared to single-matrix updates. The paper highlights this as an area for improvement, although there is no inference-time penalty once the learned \(R\) is folded into the pretrained weights.
- Design choices: the best combination of \(m\) (stages) and \(b\) (block size) is task- and model-dependent. BOFT provides a continuum from block-diagonal OFT to dense OFT; finding the sweet spot can require tuning.
- Is butterfly optimal? The butterfly graph is a natural choice thanks to FFT history, but the information-transmission perspective suggests other graph topologies could also be explored for even better parameter-vs-expressivity trade-offs.

## Bottom line

BOFT gives you the benefits of orthogonal finetuning — angle/spectral preservation and stable training — with far fewer trainable parameters by assembling dense orthogonal updates from a product of sparse, FFT-style factors. It’s a principled, simple, and effective addition to the PEFT toolkit: if you like OFT’s geometric stability but can’t afford the parameter budget of full orthogonals, BOFT is a very reasonable next step.



# Finetuning as Information Flow: A Visual Guide to BOFT’s Grid-and-Paths View

## Overview

When we finetune a pretrained layer, we typically tweak some weight parameters so the model adapts to a new task while retaining prior knowledge. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a principled stance: modify a pretrained weight matrix by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix so that pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. OFT stabilizes training and helps generalization, but dense orthogonal matrices are expensive: a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix has \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters.

This post explains a clean, visual way to think about parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning: generate a dense orthogonal transform by composing several sparse orthogonal factors, and view that composition as information flowing across a layered grid of nodes. The butterfly topology (from FFT) emerges as a near-ideal network for this information transmission, giving BOFT — Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning — a compact, expressive parameterization with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

I’ll walk through the grid-and-paths view, why sparse factorization can produce dense connectivity, how butterfly factors are built and parameterized orthogonally, and what this buys you in practice.

## Recap: OFT in one line

Given a pretrained layer with weights \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), OFT replaces the forward computation
\(\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\)
with
\(\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\),
where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an orthogonal matrix learned during finetuning (initialized to identity). Orthogonality preserves singular values and pairwise angles among neurons, which helps stability and generalization.

Problem: a fully unconstrained \(\mathbf{R}\) costs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters. OFT therefore used block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) to reduce parameters, but that breaks cross-dimension interactions and cannot represent many useful transforms.

BOFT asks: can we get dense, expressive orthogonal transforms while keeping parameter cost low?

## The grid-and-paths view: matrix factorization as information transmission

Represent the desired dense matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of \(m\) square factors:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1.
\]
Unroll this product into a layered grid with \(m+1\) vertical levels and \(d\) nodes per level. Each matrix \(\mathbf{B}_i\) describes connectivity between level \(i\) and level \(i+1\): a nonzero entry \((j_1, j_2)\) in \(\mathbf{B}_i\) is an edge that allows information from node \(j_2\) (level \(i\)) to reach node \(j_1\) (level \(i+1\)).

In this view:

- A dense \(\mathbf{R}\) means that for every source node at level 1 and every target node at level \(m{+}1\), there is at least one path through the grid connecting them. That is, information from any input coordinate can reach any output coordinate by traversing the factor matrices.
- The total number of nonzero entries across all \(\mathbf{B}_i\) corresponds to the number of "edges" in the transmission network. Reducing parameters is equivalent to minimizing free edges while retaining dense connectivity.
- Orthogonality imposes structure: each \(\mathbf{B}_i\) must be orthogonal (or, more generally, constrained), which restricts allowable edge patterns and values.

This is a simple, graphical way to reason about how a product of sparse matrices can yield a dense matrix: instead of a single dense bipartite link (one factor with \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) edges), we create multiple sparse stages that route information via intermediate nodes.

## Why sparse factors can still produce dense connectivity

Two desiderata in the grid view:

1. Dense connectivity: for each source node and each sink node, there exists a path across levels.
2. Minimum free edges: the number of trainable edges (non-fixed entries) should be small.

Orthogonality forces each \(\mathbf{B}_i\) to be full-rank and structured (e.g., certain sparse orthogonal patterns), and for a \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix a minimal set of \(d\) structural connections is necessary to preserve rank. But beyond that, edges can be shared across stages: a small number of carefully arranged sparse stages can enable all-to-all reachability. The grid-and-paths view turns the algebraic problem into a combinatorial routing problem: design a layered network with few edges but path-rich topology.

A trivial sparse solution is block-diagonal OFT: each \(\mathbf{B}_i\) is block-diagonal, so information stays within blocks and never crosses them — this cannot produce a dense \(\mathbf{R}\). Better topologies are ones where sparse local interactions at each stage permute and mix coordinates so that after several stages every coordinate can affect every output coordinate.

That brings us to butterfly graphs.

## The butterfly topology: compact routes for full mixing

The butterfly network is the classic FFT information network. With recursive, structured mixing, it achieves full connectivity between \(d\) inputs and \(d\) outputs with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges (stages).

Construction (intuitively): for \(d=2^N\), the transform is organized into \(\log d\) stages. At each stage, the \(d\) coordinates are grouped into \(\frac{d}{2}\) independent 2-by-2 mixing blocks (or more generally \(2b\times 2b\) blocks for block-butterfly). These local mixes permute and combine information so that after \(\log d\) stages every input has influenced every output.

A 2-by-2 butterfly factor has the form
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(2) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha & \beta \\
\gamma & \delta
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and larger butterfly components are block-diagonal compositions of such factors arranged with stage-specific permutations.

Why this is compelling for BOFT:

- Each butterfly component is sparse and structured, requiring only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) nonzero blocks per stage.
- With \(m=\log d\) stages, the total number of effective parameters scales like \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\), a large reduction from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\).
- Permutations between stages ensure that local 2-by-2 (or \(2b\times 2b\)) interactions compound into global mixing, achieving dense \(\mathbf{R}\).

BOFT parameterizes \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of orthogonal butterfly components:
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-butterfly component with block size \(b\), and each block is parameterized orthogonally (e.g., via Cayley transform or 2D rotations).

Special cases connect to prior work: BOFT(1,b) is the block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\); BOFT(1,d) is full OFT; BOFT(\(\log d,2\)) is the radix-2 butterfly giving maximal sparsity with dense connectivity.

## Practical details: orthogonality, initialization, and regularization

Orthogonal parameterization. Each butterfly component must be orthogonal. This can be achieved by parameterizing the small blocks (e.g., \(2b\times 2b\) blocks) as orthogonal matrices — in practice Cayley parameterization or explicit orthonormal rotation parameterizations are used so the global product remains orthogonal.

Identity initialization. Finetuning usually begins from the pretrained model unchanged. BOFT initializes each butterfly component as identity (e.g., by setting the skew-symmetric parameters to zero in a Cayley parametrization) so initially \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).

Multiplicative Dropout. Conventional Dropout is additive and works for LoRA, but BOFT’s multiplicative nature requires a different regularizer. The paper proposes multiplicative Dropout: randomly replace a percentage of butterfly components and/or block entries with identity during training. This preserves orthogonality and acts like switching off certain stages or blocks to prevent overfitting.

## What BOFT buys you

Parameter efficiency. Using a butterfly factorization with \(m=\log d\) and block size \(b=2\) gives \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters vs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) for a dense orthogonal matrix. Compared with block-diagonal OFT, BOFT can achieve dense transforms without making blocks huge.

Expressivity vs. regularity. The butterfly-structured subset of orthogonal matrices is richer than block-diagonal structures of the same per-stage parameter count. Empirically and theoretically, BOFT provides a smoother interpolation between the identity (high regularity) and full orthogonal group (high expressivity). In some limits (composing enough butterfly factors in alternating fashion) the full orthogonal group can be represented, but BOFT intentionally restricts itself to a structured subset that improves generalization.

Spectral/stability properties. Left-multiplying by an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves singular values of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), so BOFT keeps spectral norms unchanged. This helps training stability, similar to other orthogonal regularization benefits reported in the literature.

Task scope. The BOFT paper applies this idea beyond the original OFT domain (text-to-image diffusion) — to finetuning large vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models — demonstrating strong parameter-efficiency and generalization across modalities.

## Limitations and trade-offs

- Runtime: computing the product of several sparse orthogonal factors adds some training-time overhead compared to block-diagonal OFT. The paper notes this extra cost but also points out that at inference time the learned orthogonal can be fused into the pretrained weights to avoid latency.
- Is butterfly optimal? Butterfly is a well-motivated efficient topology, but whether it’s the absolute best topology under all constraints is open. The grid-and-path view suggests one can search other network topologies from network theory for alternative trade-offs.
- Orthogonality restricts the hypothesis class. While this helps stability and generalization, it can limit adaptation capacity if over-constrained; BOFT balances this by letting you choose stage count \(m\) and block size \(b\).

## Takeaway

Think of finetuning with structured multipliers as routing information across a layered grid of nodes. Sparse, stagewise orthogonal factors are edges; the product of those factors is the net connectivity from inputs to outputs. The butterfly topology is a proven, compact routing design that lets BOFT realize dense, expressive orthogonal transforms with far fewer parameters than naive dense OFT. This grid-and-paths perspective is powerful because it reframes parameter efficiency as a combinatorial routing design problem, opening the door to importing ideas from network topologies and fast linear transforms into efficient finetuning.

If you want to experiment, vary two knobs: the number of stages \(m\) and block size \(b\). More stages and smaller blocks move you toward denser, more expressive transforms with better parameter efficiency per degree of freedom; fewer stages and larger blocks move you toward block-diagonal OFT. The grid view helps you visualize and reason about that trade-off directly.



# Butterflies in the Weights: How FFT Structures Deliver O(d log d) Finetuning

## Why orthogonal finetuning — and why its cost matters

Large pretrained models are now the default starting point for many tasks, but retraining them from scratch is usually impossible. So we finetune. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is an appealing finetuning principle: instead of adding a low-rank additive update (as in LoRA), OFT multiplies a pretrained weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix. Because an orthogonal left-multiplicative update preserves pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, OFT tends to be stable and generalizes well.

The downside is parameter cost. A dense orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ has on the order of $d^2$ degrees of freedom, which quickly becomes prohibitive. Prior work traded off expressivity for parameter savings by using block-diagonal orthogonal matrices (apply different small orthogonal transforms to disjoint coordinate blocks). That saves parameters but limits the kinds of dense transforms you can represent, and it's unclear how to choose a good block partition.

The paper behind this post takes a different route: keep orthogonality (and the beneficial properties it gives) but parameterize dense orthogonal transforms much more compactly — roughly $O(d\log d)$ parameters instead of $O(d^2)$ — by borrowing the topology behind the Cooley–Tukey FFT: the butterfly.

## The core idea in plain terms: route information through a sparse network

Think of a dense $d\times d$ matrix as connecting $d$ input nodes to $d$ output nodes: an entry is nonzero if information can flow from a given input node to a given output node. If we build that dense map by multiplying several sparse matrices, each sparse factor corresponds to a layer of a little network: information moves layer by layer along edges defined by the nonzeros.

Two desiderata drive a good factorization:

- Dense connectivity: every input should be able to reach every output after all factors are multiplied.
- Few free parameters: the total number of trainable (nonfixed) nonzero entries should be small.

The butterfly topology solves this graph problem efficiently. It routes information from every input to every output using only about $d\log d$ edges (up to constant factors), by a recursive pattern of pairwise mixing layers — exactly the pattern used in the FFT to combine subproblems. The key is that a product of sparse, structured factors with this pattern can produce a dense overall map.

## What a butterfly component is (mathematically)

When $d$ is a power of two, a radix-2 butterfly factor at size $k$ has the block form

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]

where each $\bm{d}_i$ is a vector of length $k/2$. A full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix is built recursively as a product of a sequence of such block-diagonal “butterfly components” at different granularities. Each component mixes small groups of coordinates, and the whole product spreads local information globally.

To make these factors suitable for finetuning, the paper enforces that each factor is orthogonal (for example via Cayley parameterization or $2\times2$ rotations on the smallest blocks). By stacking $m$ such orthogonal components you get an orthogonal matrix

\[
\bm{R} = \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,1)} \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,2)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,m)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is a block-wise butterfly component and each is orthogonal.

## Why this gives O(d log d) parameters

A full dense orthogonal matrix has $\Theta(d^2)$ parameters. A block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ uses $\Theta(bd)$ parameters (this is what the original OFT used to reduce cost). The butterfly construction with block size $b$ and $m$ components uses about

\[
\text{parameters} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

If you set $b=2$ and choose $m\approx\log d$ (the classic butterfly), that simplifies to $\Theta(d\log d)$ parameters. In practice you can trade $b$ and $m$ to interpolate between very small parameter budgets and greater expressivity; BOFT (the method in the paper) subsumes block-diagonal OFT as the special case $m=1$ and recovers full OFT when $m=1$ and $b=d$.

So the butterfly lets you represent dense orthogonal transformations much more compactly than naive dense parameterizations while avoiding the severe expressivity limitations of block-diagonal OFT.

## Practical finetuning details that matter

Two implementation choices make BOFT practical:

- Identity initialization. Like LoRA zeros its additive update, BOFT initializes every butterfly component to the identity (via initializing the underlying skew-symmetric Cayley parameters to zero). This starts the finetuned model exactly equal to the pretrained one and avoids sudden changes early in training.

- Multiplicative Dropout. Standard dropout doesn't fit a multiplicative orthogonal update directly. The authors propose randomly replacing whole butterfly components (or blocks within them) by identity during training. This is a multiplicative analogue of dropout that helps regularize the multiplicative update path.

## Why BOFT retains the good properties of orthogonal updates

Because BOFT multiplies the pretrained weights on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the finetuned weight is $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. If $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the SVD of the pretrained weights, then $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. The singular values (hence spectral norm) remain unchanged, which is favorable for training stability and preserving pretraining knowledge. BOFT therefore keeps the spectral and angle-preserving advantages of OFT while being much more parameter-efficient.

The butterfly factorization also induces an inductive bias: it constrains the hypothesis class to a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is closely related to many classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT, etc.). That structured bias can improve generalization compared to unconstrained dense finetuning, similar to how low-rank or convolutional structure can be helpful.

## Expressivity trade-offs — powerful but not unlimited

Butterfly-parameterized orthogonal matrices are more expressive than simple block-diagonal OFT with the same local block sizes. In fact, with certain compositions (kaleidoscope-like products of butterfly matrices) one can approximate any orthogonal matrix, but that requires stacking many components and increases parameter cost. The paper emphasizes the practical trade-off: too much expressivity (full finetuning) often hurts generalization, while too little expressivity (tiny block-diagonal matrices) underfits. BOFT provides a smooth middle ground where you can dial $m$ and $b$ to balance expressivity and parameter budgets.

Empirical approximations in the paper show that smaller $b$ with larger $m$ (more mixing stages) often achieve better approximation per parameter than simple few-block OFT — a hallmark of the butterfly's efficiency.

## When BOFT is a good choice — and its limitations

BOFT is a compelling option when you want orthogonality-driven finetuning properties (angle- and spectrum-preserving) but cannot afford $O(d^2)$ parameters. It is applicable across modalities: the paper shows experiments adapting vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models.

Limitations to keep in mind:

- Training overhead. Because the orthogonal update is a product of several factors, BOFT has a modest runtime overhead during training compared to single-block OFT or LoRA. At inference time this disappears because you can fold the learned orthogonal matrix into the pretrained weights.

- Design choices. It's not guaranteed the butterfly is the ultimate topology for this problem. The paper frames factorization as an information-transmission problem and suggests the butterfly is a very efficient solution, but other graph topologies from network design might be competitive.

## Takeaway

If you like the stability and generalization benefits of orthogonal finetuning but need to be frugal with parameters, the butterfly factorization is a neat structural trick: emulate dense, global information mixing with a sequence of sparse orthogonal layers, and get down from $O(d^2)$ to roughly $O(d\log d)$ parameters. That gives you a flexible lever (number of stages and block size) to tune expressivity vs. economy, while preserving desirable spectral and geometric properties of the pretrained model. The marriage of FFT-inspired topology and orthogonal parameterization turns out to be a practical sweet spot for finetuning large foundation models.



# From OFT to BOFT: Expressivity, Regularity, and a Unified Orthogonal Framework

## Introduction

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) reparameterizes a pretrained weight matrix by multiplying it on the left with an orthogonal matrix, preserving pairwise neuron angles and improving stability and generalization compared to many additive updates. However, a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix costs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters, so the original OFT paper used block-diagonal orthogonal matrices to gain parameter efficiency at the cost of expressivity.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) gives a principled middle ground: it represents a dense orthogonal transform as a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in butterfly patterns. This lets BOFT interpolate smoothly between the extremes of identity (no change), block-diagonal OFT (high regularity, low expressivity), and full dense OFT (maximal expressivity, large parameter count). Below I unpack that interpolation, the two control knobs (\(b\) and \(m\)), the expressivity–regularity trade-off, and how the kaleidoscope hierarchy shows BOFT can recover the whole orthogonal group if desired.

## The BOFT parameterization in one sentence

BOFT parameterizes the finetuning orthogonal matrix as
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}
\]
where each \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-wise butterfly component of block size \(b\) and the product runs over \(m\) components. Each component is constrained to be orthogonal (e.g., via Cayley or 2D rotations on its \(2b\times 2b\) blocks), and BOFT initializes components to identity.

This factorization yields a dense final \(\bm{R}\) for moderate \(m\) and small \(b\) while using far fewer trainable parameters than an unconstrained orthogonal matrix.

## The two knobs: block size \(b\) and depth \(m\)

BOFT gives two natural hyperparameters that control the structure and capacity of the orthogonal transform.

- Block size \(b\): controls the atomic block width used inside each butterfly component. When \(b\) is large, each block has more degrees of freedom; when \(b\) is small (e.g., \(b=2\)), the parameterization composes many small orthogonal rotations. Conceptually, larger \(b\) pushes BOFT toward a block-diagonal-style parameterization (more local capacity per block).

- Depth \(m\): controls how many butterfly components are multiplied together. Increasing \(m\) increases the number of sparse layers (and thus effective connectivity) between input and output indices. Higher \(m\) lets information propagate more globally and makes the composed matrix more expressive.

Some useful limit cases make the interpolation clear:

- BOFT(1, b) = block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\). This is the original block-diagonal OFT used for parameter efficiency.

- BOFT(1, d) = full dense OFT (an unconstrained orthogonal matrix of size \(d\)), because a block size equal to \(d\) makes the single factor full.

- BOFT\((\log \frac{2d}{b}, b)\) (the full block-butterfly depth) yields a dense orthogonal matrix constructed from butterfly components and typically needs only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters when \(b=2\).

Thus \(b\) and \(m\) form a 2D control plane: decreasing \(b\) and increasing \(m\) moves BOFT from local, block-wise transforms toward globally mixing, dense transforms while controlling parameter cost.

## Parameter count and a simple formula

For a linear layer of size \(d\times n\), BOFT(\(m,b\)) uses an effective number of trainable parameters equal to
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\, d\, m,
\]
which illustrates the trade-offs:

- Fixing \(m\), increasing \(b\) increases parameters linearly.
- Fixing \(b\), increasing \(m\) also increases parameters linearly.
- Choosing \(b=2\) and \(m=\log d\) yields \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, a big improvement over \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) for dense OFT.

Note: the factorization trades extra computation (multiplying several sparse factors per forward/backward pass) for fewer stored/fine-tuned parameters.

## Expressivity vs. regularity: what BOFT buys you

Two competing desiderata in finetuning are expressivity (can adapt richly to the downstream task) and regularity (prevent overfitting and retain pretrained structure). Full dense OFT is maximally expressive in the orthogonal family but also least regularizing; block-diagonal OFT is highly regularized but may be too restrictive because it cannot mix coordinates across block boundaries.

BOFT sits between these extremes:

- With small \(m\) and large \(b\), BOFT behaves similarly to block-diagonal OFT: it is regular and parameter-efficient but limited in cross-block mixing.

- With larger \(m\) and smaller \(b\), BOFT achieves dense connectivity and can approximate many global transforms (including common fast transforms like FFT or Hadamard), while still using far fewer parameters than dense OFT.

Empirically (and supported by the paper’s simulation), BOFT designs with modest block size and greater depth often achieve better parameter efficiency: they approximate dense orthogonal matrices more accurately per parameter than block-diagonal OFT with equivalent parameter budgets.

The intuition: butterfly factorization is an information-transmission topology that efficiently routes signals from any input coordinate to any output coordinate through \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse layers, enabling dense behavior with sparse building blocks.

## The kaleidoscope hierarchy and universality

The paper invokes a provable expressivity result: by composing multiple butterfly factors in both left and right (and transposed) patterns you obtain very high expressivity. Concretely, one can construct
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
and when \(m_1=m_2=\log d\), \(b_1=b_2=2\) and \(l=d-1\), this kaleidoscope-type composition can represent the entire orthogonal group. That is, BOFT-style building blocks can be arranged so they are universal over orthogonal matrices. Practically, you rarely want this extreme: universality comes at parameter and computational cost and reduces the regularizing benefits that make OFT variants generalize well.

So the kaleidoscope hierarchy is useful as a theoretical endpoint: BOFT architectures can be expanded to reach full expressivity if needed, but one can also choose much smaller configurations to enforce regularity.

## Other practical pieces: initialization and multiplicative dropout

Two practical design choices keep BOFT stable and generalizable:

- Identity initialization. BOFT initializes each orthogonal butterfly component as identity (implemented via zero skew-symmetric Cayley parameters or zero 2D rotation angles). This gives zero initial change to the pretrained model and reduces training instability.

- Multiplicative Dropout. Because BOFT applies orthogonal transforms multiplicatively to the pretrained weights, standard additive dropout (as used with LoRA) does not directly apply. BOFT proposes multiplicative dropout: randomly replace a subset of butterfly components (and optionally some diagonal blocks inside components) with identity during training. This acts as a structural regularizer that reduces overfitting while respecting the multiplicative nature of the parametrization.

## Spectral preservation and stability

A key benefit of OFT and BOFT is that multiplying a frozen weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) by an orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) on the left preserves the singular values of \(\bm{W}^0\): if \(\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top\), then \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top\). The spectral norm (largest singular value) and overall singular value profile are unchanged, which helps training stability and links BOFT to favorable generalization properties documented for spectral-preserving transforms.

## Practical guidance for choosing \(b\) and \(m\)

The paper’s experiments and analysis suggest the following heuristics:

- If you need extreme parameter efficiency and want to enforce strong regularity, favor small \(m\) (e.g., \(m=1\)) and modest \(b\): this is essentially block-diagonal OFT.

- If you need dense mixing but still want to keep parameters sub-quadratic, favor small \(b\) (e.g., \(b=2\) or \(4\)) with larger \(m\) up to the butterfly depth \(\log(\frac{2d}{b})\). This often yields the best expressivity-per-parameter.

- Use identity initialization, and consider multiplicative dropout (randomly zeroing whole components or blocks) when overfitting is a concern.

- If you are unsure, start with BOFT(\(\log d, 2\)) scaled to meet your parameter budget; this configuration implements the classic butterfly factorization and often attains dense behavior with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

## Takeaway

BOFT subsumes OFT and provides a continuum between block-diagonal, dense, and identity transforms through two interpretable knobs: block size \(b\) and depth \(m\). The butterfly factorization is an information-efficient way to achieve dense connectivity using sparse orthogonal building blocks, giving practitioners a flexible lever to trade expressivity against regularity and parameter cost. The kaleidoscope hierarchy guarantees that, if desired, these building blocks can be composed to achieve full orthogonal expressivity; in practice, smaller BOFT configurations often deliver better generalization-per-parameter than either extreme.

If you want a compact, practical scheme to make OFT parameter-efficient without throwing away the spectral and angle-preserving benefits of orthogonal finetuning, BOFT is a principled and configurable option.



# BOFT vs. LoRA vs. Adapters: Practical Trade-offs for LLMs, ViTs, and Diffusion

Adapting large foundation models to downstream tasks without retraining from scratch is now standard. Three common parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) approaches are: additive low-rank updates (LoRA), inserted modules (adapters), and multiplicative orthogonal updates (OFT / BOFT). This post summarizes the practical trade-offs between these methods based on the BOFT paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization", so you can pick the right tool for your model, task, and deployment constraints.

## Quick method sketch

- LoRA: reparameterizes weight updates as an additive, low-rank product of two matrices. Very popular, simple, zero-initialized, and supports standard dropout. Parameter count roughly O(rd) for a layer of width d if rank r is used.

- Adapters: small extra modules (often MLP bottlenecks) are inserted into the architecture and trained while the base model is frozen. Good flexibility but adds inference-time computation and latency.

- BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning): multiplicative finetuning that left-multiplies pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix R (so new weight = R W^0). BOFT parameterizes R as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly" components so a dense orthogonal transform can be represented with significantly fewer trainable params. Complexity can be as low as O(d log d) parameters for width d (using radix-2 butterfly with block size b = 2). BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as a special case (m=1 corresponds to block-diagonal).

## Key practical properties (grounded in the paper)

- No inference latency after consolidation. BOFT and OFT are multiplicative transforms applied to the frozen base weights; after finetuning the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weights and shipped as a single updated model with no extra runtime cost. Adapters, by contrast, remain as extra layers unless you do special consolidation and may incur inference overhead.

- Spectral stability. Because BOFT multiplies the pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix, the singular values of the base weight W^0 are preserved (left-multiplying by orthogonal R keeps spectral norm unchanged). This preservation improves training stability and often helps generalization. LoRA (additive low-rank) does not guarantee spectral norm preservation.

- Parameter efficiency with dense effect. BOFT can represent dense orthogonal transforms using a product of sparse factors. With a butterfly factorization the effective tunable parameter count scales like O(d log d) (for the common choice b=2), much lower than a full orthogonal matrix O(d^2) and comparable to many practical LoRA budgets (but with different inductive bias). Block-diagonal OFT gives O(b d) when using block size b; BOFT can achieve dense connectivity with much fewer params by increasing the number of sparse factors m while keeping small block size.

- Inductive bias and expressivity. BOFT places a structured prior (butterfly sparsity) on the learned orthogonal matrix. The butterfly pattern is known to recover many classical fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT), which can be a useful inductive bias. Compared to block-diagonal OFT, BOFT is generally more expressive for the same block size. However, full expressivity (representing the entire orthogonal group) requires richer compositions (kaleidoscope-like products) at higher cost.

- Training/runtime trade-off. BOFT requires multiplying multiple sparse factors per forward/backward pass, so training runtime is somewhat larger than single-block OFT or LoRA. The paper notes a modest training runtime overhead as a limitation. After finetuning, you can fuse factors into the base weights to avoid inference overhead.

- Regularization / overfitting control. BOFT uses identity initialization (zero skew-symmetric parameters in Cayley transform) to start from the pretrained model. Standard dropout is not directly applicable in the multiplicative formulation, so the authors propose a multiplicative dropout that randomly resets some butterfly components / blocks to identity during training. LoRA supports conventional dropout on the low-rank updates.

## When to choose BOFT

Choose BOFT when one or more of the following hold:

- You want strong spectral and geometric preservation of pretrained weights (better training stability). This is particularly helpful when finetuning large models that are sensitive to rank / norm changes.

- You need a dense, global transform but have a constrained parameter budget. BOFT can produce dense orthogonal transforms with O(d log d) tunable parameters vs O(d^2) for full OFT, while remaining more expressive than simple block-diagonal OFT of similar parameter count.

- You cannot tolerate inference latency. Because BOFT's learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into frozen weights after training, there is no additional inference-time cost.

- Your downstream task benefits from structured global mixing — generative diffusion models, certain ViT adaptations, or tasks where mixing across many feature coordinates helps — and you prefer a structured inductive bias related to standard transforms.

Concrete model use-cases from the paper: BOFT was applied to large vision transformers (ViTs), large language models (LLMs), and text-to-image diffusion models with consistent improvements over OFT and competitive improvements vs other PEFT baselines. The paper highlights BOFT's generalization and stability across vision and language tasks.

## When LoRA or adapters may be preferable

- Extremely tight parameter budget and simplest implementation: LoRA is very simple and widely supported. If you only need a few thousand extra parameters per layer (rank r small) and additive updates are fine, LoRA is often the quickest practical choice.

- Fine-grained per-layer allocation: LoRA ranks can be tuned per-layer to concentrate budget where it's most effective. This flexibility is practical for LLMs where some layers benefit more than others.

- You want conventional dropout and training recipes: LoRA integrates naturally with standard dropout and training tools. BOFT uses multiplicative dropout (randomly identitying butterfly blocks), which is effective but slightly different.

- You are okay with inference-time extra modules or plan to use adapters for modularity: Adapters let you encode multiple tasks as separate modules and switch them at inference time. If you value modular task switching over absolute inference throughput, adapters are attractive despite added latency.

## Simple decision heuristic

- If inference latency is critical and you want strong stability/generalization: prefer BOFT (consolidate learned R into weights after finetuning).

- If you want the simplest implementation and minimal training overhead: prefer LoRA.

- If you need modular task adapters that you will swap at inference time, or you want to alter the architecture with new heads, consider adapters.

- If you want the most expressive multiplicative orthogonal flexibility and can afford more parameters/training time, consider full OFT (dense R) — but BOFT gives a parameter-efficient dense-like alternative and often strikes a better practicality/expressivity balance.

## Practical knobs in BOFT (as in the paper)

- Block size b and number of components m trade off parameter count and expressivity. BOFT(m, b) uses roughly ½(b−1) d m effective trainable parameters for a layer of width d. Small b and larger m (more butterfly stages) favor dense connectivity with fewer total parameters (classic butterfly: b=2, m≈log d).

- Identity initialization: start BOFT near the pretrained model by initializing each orthogonal component to identity via Cayley parameterization (skew-symmetric param set to 0).

- Multiplicative dropout: to regularize BOFT, randomly replace a fraction of butterfly components or their blocks with identity during training.

## Limitations and practical caveats

- Training overhead: BOFT incurs more per-step computation than a single low-rank update because of the cascade of sparse factors. The paper notes this as a limitation, though fusion removes inference cost.

- Not a universal win: higher expressivity is not always better for downstream generalization. The structured constraint of BOFT is useful as a regularizer, but there are tasks where additive LoRA updates or adapter layers may outperform depending on the specific data and compute trade-offs.

- Choice of topology: the butterfly is an effective and well-studied transmission topology, but the authors note it may not be optimal for all tasks. The information-transmission framing suggests other sparse factorizations from network design might be explored.

## Bottom line

BOFT offers a compelling middle ground: multiplicative orthogonal finetuning that preserves spectral properties and can express dense transforms at parameter costs like O(d log d). It is a strong option if you value inference-time speed, training stability, and a structured global transform. For maximum simplicity or the smallest possible tune budget, LoRA remains a practical first choice. Adapters keep their place when modularity or architecture change is the priority and you can tolerate inference overhead.

If you plan to finetune LLMs, ViTs, or diffusion models and inference latency or spectral stability matter, try BOFT with identity init and multiplicative dropout, tune (b, m) to hit your parameter budget, and fuse the learned orthogonal into the base weights before deployment.



# Stability by Design: Orthogonality, Cayley Transforms, and Spectral Norms

## Why orthogonality matters for finetuning

When we finetune a pretrained layer, a common goal is to change the model just enough to adapt to the new task without destroying the pretrained knowledge. One practical way to do that is to constrain the change so that important global properties of the pretrained weights are preserved. Orthogonal finetuning does this very directly: it replaces a pretrained weight matrix W^0 with R W^0 where R is an orthogonal matrix. Because orthogonal matrices are rigid isometries, left-multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal R preserves the singular values of the pretrained layer. That simple fact has important consequences:

- The spectral norm (largest singular value) of the weight matrix is unchanged. This keeps the layer’s largest gain under control, which helps prevent exploding activations and gradients.
- Preserving singular values also maintains other spectral properties that affect optimization dynamics and generalization (e.g., conditioning related to relative scales across directions).
- Because R is constrained to lie on the orthogonal group, the finetuning search space is regularized: we can explore expressive transformations while avoiding arbitrary large distortions of the pretrained mapping.

Below I walk through the math for the spectral-norm preservation, then explain how the Cayley parameterization enforces orthogonality during training, and finally connect these facts to why orthogonal finetuning tends to be stable and well-behaved.

## Left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values

Let W^0 be the pretrained weight matrix. Take its singular value decomposition (SVD):

W^0 = U Σ V^T,

where U and V are orthogonal matrices and Σ is the diagonal matrix of singular values (nonnegative). Now left-multiply by an orthogonal matrix R:

R W^0 = (R U) Σ V^T.

Because R and U are both orthogonal, their product RU is also orthogonal. SVD is unique up to orthogonal factors, so the singular values of R W^0 are exactly the diagonal entries of Σ — the same singular values as W^0. In particular, the spectral norm (largest singular value) is unchanged.

This is the simple, robust fact at the core of why orthogonal finetuning preserves important global scale properties of layers.

## Cayley parameterization: maintaining orthogonality during training

We need a way to parametrize orthogonal matrices R that (a) enforces orthogonality exactly (up to numerical precision) throughout gradient-based updates and (b) allows smooth optimization from the identity (so we can start from the pretrained model exactly). The Cayley transform meets these needs.

The Cayley parameterization represents an orthogonal matrix R in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix Q (i.e., Q^T = −Q) as follows:

R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1},

assuming (I − Q) is invertible. When Q is skew-symmetric, this formula maps Q to an orthogonal matrix R (except when −1 is an eigenvalue that makes (I − Q) singular). Two properties make the Cayley map attractive in finetuning:

- Skew-symmetric Q has fewer free parameters than an unconstrained matrix (for a d×d matrix there are d(d−1)/2 degrees of freedom), and parameter updates can be performed directly on Q.
- If Q is initialized as the zero matrix, R starts as the identity matrix. That yields zero-initial change from the pretrained weights, matching the common desideratum of identity (or zero) initialization in parameter-efficient finetuning.

Because the Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric Q to orthogonal R, updating Q with unconstrained gradient steps and then computing R by the Cayley formula keeps R orthogonal at every step (modulo numerical precision). Thus orthogonality is enforced by construction rather than by penalty terms or projection.

(One can verify algebraically that the Cayley map yields R^T R = I when Q^T = −Q and (I − Q) is invertible.)

## Why spectral-norm preservation gives stability and better behavior

Preserving the spectral norm of pretrained layers during finetuning helps stability in several connected ways:

- Bounded Lipschitz constant per layer: The spectral norm upper-bounds the operator norm of a linear layer. Keeping the spectral norm fixed prevents sudden increases in the layer’s maximum amplification of inputs, which guards against exploding activations or gradients as the model trains.
- Stable forward activations: Because singular values govern how different input directions are amplified or attenuated, preserving them prevents the finetuning step from introducing large anisotropic distortions that could break downstream layers or destabilize optimization.
- Predictable gradient magnitudes: For many architectures and loss functions, gradient magnitudes depend on layer gains. Fixing those gains helps maintain training signals in a predictable regime, which can reduce the need for delicate learning-rate tuning or other hacks.
- Implicit regularization: Restricting updates to orthogonal transformations constrains the hypothesis class and reduces the space of permissible changes compared to unconstrained updates. This can reduce overfitting in low-data or few-shot finetuning scenarios.

These benefits are precisely why orthogonal finetuning methods (like OFT and the more parameter-efficient BOFT studied in the paper) report improved training stability and generalization compared to some unconstrained parameterizations.

## Practical notes from the paper

- Identity initialization: The Cayley parameterization makes it trivial to start R = I by initializing Q = 0. That ensures the finetuned model initially exactly matches the pretrained model, a common and useful practice in PEFT (parameter-efficient finetuning).
- Multiplicative updates & dropout: Because orthogonal finetuning modifies weights multiplicatively (R W^0), standard additive dropout schemes are not directly applicable. The paper introduces a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces a subset of orthogonal factors by identity matrices during training, preserving the multiplicative structure while providing regularization.
- Spectral advantage over additive low-rank updates: Additive low-rank methods (e.g., LoRA) change singular values of the pretrained matrix and can therefore alter its spectral norm. Orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm exactly, which is one reason the authors observe better stability in practice.

## Takeaway

Orthogonality is a simple, principled constraint that protects key spectral properties of pretrained layers during finetuning. The Cayley parameterization gives a convenient, trainable mapping from skew-symmetric parameters to orthogonal matrices with identity initialization, making it a practical tool for orthogonal finetuning. Preserving singular values via left-multiplication by orthogonal matrices stabilizes activations and gradients and acts as an implicit regularizer — a neat example where a mathematically clean constraint produces robust, reliable empirical benefits.



# BOFT in Practice: Initialization, Multiplicative Dropout, and Zero-Overhead Inference

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is a parameter-efficient way to finetune pretrained layers by multiplying a learned orthogonal matrix R into the frozen weight matrix W^0. In practice, BOFT gives you a spectrum of options between very compact parameterizations and full orthogonal transforms. This short guide gives hands-on instructions and practical tips drawn from the BOFT paper: how to initialize, how to build orthogonal butterfly blocks, how to apply multiplicative Dropout during training, how to pick the block size \(b\) and the number of butterfly components \(m\), and how to merge the learned factors to obtain zero-overhead inference.

## Quick recap of the BOFT parameterization

BOFT represents the finetuning transform as
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot \bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\quad\text{with}\quad
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-wise butterfly component of dimension \(d\times d\) built from small orthogonal blocks. Each component is constrained to be orthogonal, so \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} = I_d\). BOFT interpolates between block-diagonal OFT and full OFT:

- BOFT(1, b) = block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\).
- BOFT(1, d) = full unconstrained OFT.
- Taking \(m=\log d\) and \(b=2\) recovers the classic butterfly parameterization with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

Total effective trainable parameters (per finetuned linear layer of size \(d\times n\)) is approximately \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m\).

## Identity initialization (why and how)

Why: Finetuning typically starts close to the pretrained model to preserve pretraining knowledge. BOFT follows this principle by initializing the orthogonal transform to the identity so the model behavior is unchanged at start.

How (practical recipe):
- BOFT uses an orthogonal parameterization per small block (commonly via the Cayley transform or explicit 2D rotations).
- For Cayley-based parameterization, each small skew-symmetric block \(Q\) is initialized to zero; the Cayley map \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) then evaluates to the identity.
- For explicit rotations (2×2 blocks) initialize the rotation angles to \(0\), yielding identity blocks.
- Practically: initialize all learnable parameters used to construct the block orthogonal matrices to zero (or angle 0). This ensures \(\bm{R}(m,b)=I\) at the start of finetuning.

This zero/identity initialization is analogous to zero-initializing LoRA updates and is important for stable finetuning.

## Constructing orthogonal butterfly blocks

Principles:
- The smallest building block is a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal block (a rotation or Cayley-parameterized 2×2 orthogonal).
- Block butterfly components \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\) are formed by placing many such small orthogonal blocks in a structured pattern (the butterfly pattern), with each scalar element in the pattern replaced by a \(b\times b\) block (when using block size \(b\)).
- The components at different stages correspond to permutations of the 2×2 non-zero pattern; thus ensuring orthogonality at the 2×2 (or \(2b\times 2b\)) block level suffices.

Practical construction steps:
1. Choose block-size \(b\) (even) and number of stages \(m\). Typical choices are small powers of two for compatibility with butterfly layouts.
2. For each stage \(i\) (from 1 to \(m\)), form \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) as a block-diagonal matrix of \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal blocks placed according to the butterfly component pattern for that stage.
3. Within each \(2b\times 2b\) block, parameterize the orthogonal transformation via:
   - Cayley transform from a skew-symmetric \(2b\times 2b\) matrix, initialized to zero, or
   - A structured product of 2×2 Givens rotations (angles initialized to zero).
4. Compose the full \(\bm{R}(m,b)\) as the ordered product of the stage components.

Notes:
- Implementers can reuse standard Cayley or Givens rotation code for these small blocks.
- The permutation that interleaves positions between stages is part of the butterfly layout; libraries implementing FFT-style permutations are useful.

## Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT (how it differs from standard Dropout)

Why: Conventional Dropout (additive masking on activations or additive parameter masks) works for additive updates like LoRA but not for multiplicative updates like BOFT, because BOFT composes the final transform multiplicatively from orthogonal components.

What the paper proposes:
- Multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces some learned butterfly components (or sub-blocks within them) with identity blocks during training. This is equivalent to temporarily removing stages or blocks from the multiplicative chain.

Practical recipe:
1. Choose two dropout rates:
   - \(p_1\): fraction of butterfly components (stages) to drop on each forward pass.
   - \(p_2\): fraction of diagonal blocks inside a chosen component to drop.
2. On each training forward:
   - Sample a subset of stages (size ≈ \(p_1\cdot m\)) and set those entire stage matrices to identity.
   - For the non-dropped stages, sample within-stage diagonal block indices and set those \(p_2\) fraction of blocks to identity.
3. Ensure the replacement is identity (not zero), so orthogonality and stable magnitudes are preserved.

Why identity and not zero: Since BOFT multiplies stages into \(\bm{W}^0\), replacing a factor with identity is the natural multiplicative analogue of "dropping" it while keeping scale and orthogonality intact.

Tuning tips:
- Start with modest dropout (e.g., \(p_1=0.1\), \(p_2=0.1\)) and increase if overfitting is observed.
- Use multiplicative Dropout together with identity initialization for best stability.

## Choosing block size b and number of stages m

Trade-offs:
- Parameter count scales as roughly \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m\). So both larger \(b\) and larger \(m\) raise parameter cost.
- Expressivity increases with either larger \(b\) or larger \(m\). In particular, using many small blocks (small \(b\), larger \(m\)) often yields better parameter efficiency than a single big block (the experiments in the paper show BOFT with small \(b\) and larger \(m\) often approximates dense orthogonal transforms with fewer parameters).
- Runtime: BOFT requires more matrix multiplies during training (each stage multiplies into activations), so larger \(m\) increases training compute and wall-clock time. After finetuning, factors are merged and there is no inference overhead.

Guidelines:
- If you need the strictest parameter budget (minimal trainable params), choose small \(b\) (e.g., \(b=2\)) and moderate \(m\). The configuration \(m=\log d\), \(b=2\) yields the classic butterfly with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.
- If you prefer fewer multiplicative stages (compute-sensitive training) but can afford more parameters, choose larger \(b\) and smaller \(m\) (e.g., BOFT(1, b) reduces to block-diagonal OFT).
- To match the expressivity of a block-diagonal OFT with larger blocks, you can often use BOFT with smaller \(b\) and larger \(m\) to save parameters while keeping expressivity.
- If you want to approximate the full orthogonal group, the paper notes that a kaleidoscope composition of multiple butterfly products can represent the entire orthogonal group; but this requires substantially more parameters and compute.

Practical starting points:
- For typical large layers where \(d\) is a power-of-two or can be padded: try \(b=2\) and \(m \approx \log_2 d\) for a parameter-efficient dense transform, or \(b=4\) with \(m \in [3,6]\) for a middle ground.
- If training time is a bottleneck, try BOFT(1, b) (block-diagonal OFT) as a baseline and increase \(m\) only if accuracy benefits justify the extra compute.

## Zero-overhead inference: merging learned factors into the base weights

One of BOFT's practical advantages is that after finetuning you can merge the learned orthogonal transform into the frozen base matrix, producing a single updated weight matrix with no extra inference cost.

How to merge:
1. After training finish, compute the composed orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) by multiplying the learned stage matrices in order.
2. Precompute the fused weight: \(\bm{W}^{\text{fused}} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0\).
3. Replace the model’s layer weights with \(\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}\) and discard the BOFT factors. Inference proceeds identically to a normally finetuned model with no extra multiplicative overhead.

Notes and practical tips:
- Multiplying the stage matrices into \(\bm{W}^0\) is a single one-time cost at deployment; it avoids any extra cost per inference example.
- For numerical stability, evaluate the composed \(\bm{R}\) using high precision if available (e.g., FP32) and then cast fused weights to the deployment precision.
- Because each \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is orthogonal, the composed \(\bm{R}\) remains orthogonal; multiplying orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) into \(\bm{W}^0\) preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which the paper identifies as beneficial for training stability.

## Final checklist before training

- Choose \(b\) and \(m\) based on your parameter budget and training-runtime budget, remembering parameter count ≈ \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m\).
- Implement block-level orthogonal parameterization (Cayley or Givens rotations) and initialize all block parameters to zero so each block starts as identity.
- Implement multiplicative Dropout: pick \(p_1,p_2\) and replace chosen components / blocks with identity during training.
- After finetuning compute \(\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}=\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0\) and use it for inference to achieve zero overhead.

BOFT is a practical middle ground between full orthogonal finetuning and very compact parameterizations like LoRA. With identity initialization, multiplicative Dropout during training, and a simple merge step at the end, you get a stable, generalizable finetuning flow with no extra inference cost.



# Limits and Horizons: Runtime Overheads, Better Topologies, and What Comes Next

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is an elegant proposal: retain the stability and generalization benefits of orthogonal finetuning (OFT) while drastically cutting trainable parameters by factorizing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal blocks arranged in a butterfly pattern. The payoff is clear — BOFT achieves dense behavior with only O(d log d) parameters (for the canonical butterfly setting) instead of O(d^2). The trade-off the paper highlights, however, is that this space savings comes with extra multiplicative work during training: repeated multiplies by the factor matrices increase runtime. The paper explicitly calls out this overhead and suggests exploring alternative network topologies (drawing on computer networking) that might transmit information more efficiently than the radix-2 butterfly.

In this post I summarize that limitation and sketch concrete, research-driven directions to reduce training runtime, explore better topologies, and broaden BOFT-style ideas to more adaptive and practical forms.

## The runtime overhead problem — what and why it matters

BOFT represents the finetuning transform as
R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b,
where each \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b is an orthogonal butterfly component (block size b) and m is the number of components. For a layer of width d, BOFT requires roughly
(1/2)(b-1) d m
effective trainable parameters, and in the canonical small-block butterfly (b = 2, m = \log d) this becomes O(d \log d).

The catch: applying R to the pretrained weight W^0 during each forward/backward pass requires sequential multiplications by the m sparse factors (or equivalently repeatedly applying the sequence of 2b×2b orthogonal blocks that compose them). That multiplies computational cost during training relative to a single dense or block-diagonal OFT update. The paper acknowledges this overhead and notes that while inference is unaffected (because learned factors can be folded into the pretrained weights after finetuning), training runtime remains larger.

Why this is important:
- Training compute is often the limiting resource in PEFT workflows where many downstream tasks are tried.
- Wall-clock time and GPU-hours affect researcher productivity and the practical adoption of BOFT in industry.

So the central engineering-research question becomes: can we keep the parameter-efficiency and inductive bias of BOFT while reducing the extra multiplies needed during training?

## Where to look for better topologies (and why networking matters)

The information-transmission framing of the paper is useful: composing a dense R from sparse factors is equivalent to enabling, across an m-step layered graph, paths from every input coordinate to every output coordinate. The butterfly is a highly efficient topology from the Cooley–Tukey FFT: dense end-to-end connectivity with O(d log d) edges.

But the paper deliberately leaves open whether the butterfly is optimal for the finetuning setting. That’s where the networking literature becomes relevant. Computer networks and parallel algorithms study many topologies (hypercubes, Benes networks, Clos networks, expander graphs, various sparse random constructions) that trade degree, diameter, and congestion in ways that may map directly to parameter count, depth (m), and per-step fan-in/fan-out in a factorization.

Relevant topology dimensions to consider:
- Depth (number of factor layers m): controls the extra sequential multiplies.
- Per-layer degree (non-zeros per row/column in each factor): controls per-step cost and parameter count.
- Connectivity guarantees: whether the topology can realize dense end-to-end connectivity and, importantly, whether it admits orthogonal factor entries satisfying structural constraints.
- Deterministic vs. randomized/sparse/topology-on-demand: some constructions (e.g., expanders) provide excellent connectivity with very low degree.

The paper’s call to explore other topologies is therefore both natural and promising: there may be layouts that reduce m (thus training multiplies) for the same parameter budget, or that enable more parallel application of factors on modern hardware.

## Concrete research directions

Below are concrete, technically-directed avenues to pursue. Each follows from limitations or observations in the paper and targets either reducing runtime overhead or expanding BOFT’s flexibility.

1. Kernel and implementation optimizations
   - Fuse factor multiplications where possible. For small block sizes b (e.g., b=2), multiple successive 2×2 block operations can be fused into larger tiles and operated via custom CUDA kernels to reduce kernel launch and memory traffic overhead.
   - Use mixed precision and efficient sparse-dense batched GEMMs. The butterfly factors are sparse in a structured way; specialized batched kernels that operate on the non-zero 2b×2b blocks in parallel could amortize overhead.
   - Precompute and cache permutations. Many butterfly steps are permutations or structured shuffles; implementing those as index remaps rather than full matmuls can reduce work.

2. Low-depth / low-latency topologies
   - Search for topologies with smaller depth m for the same parameter budget. For example, Clos or Benes networks realize full permutation connectivity with different trade-offs between depth and per-stage degree; adapting those into orthogonal factorization might reduce sequential steps.
   - Consider expander graphs: constant-degree expanders can achieve rapid information mixing in O(log d) hops but might allow smaller constants in practice or support randomized factor designs with fewer sequential stages.

3. Hybrid factorization: mixes of low-rank + sparse factors
   - Combine a small number of butterfly layers with a low-rank residual (LoRA-like) term. A shallow BOFT front end can spread information, while a low-rank additive term captures fine adaptation. This hybrid could reduce m while preserving parameter efficiency.
   - More formally: learn R = (∏_{i=1}^{m'} \tilde{B}_i) · (I + UV^T) where U,V have small rank. Optimize m' and rank jointly under a parameter budget.

4. Adaptive / learned topologies
   - Make the topology itself a learnable variable. Instead of fixing the butterfly graph, parameterize a family of sparse-factor topologies and allow learning (or adapting) which edges are active during finetuning, regularized by a parameter budget.
   - Techniques like straight-through gradients for discrete edge selection or continuous relaxations (Gumbel-softmax) can enable topology learning while respecting orthogonality constraints.

5. Approximate orthogonality for speed
   - The paper enforces strict orthogonality (e.g., via Cayley parameterization). For some tasks it may suffice to enforce near-orthogonality via penalty terms (spectral or orthogonality loss) and use cheaper unconstrained factor layers. Relaxations could reduce the need for numerically expensive enforcements and reduce per-step compute.
   - Analyze the trade-offs: how much deviation from exact orthogonality is tolerable before training stability and generalization degrade?

6. Parallel-friendly factorizations
   - Seek factorizations that expose more parallelism across factors (e.g., many independent block updates per stage) so that modern accelerators can apply them with high utilization rather than long sequential chains.
   - Partitioning dimensions and applying independent BOFT blocks per partition (with occasional cross-partition mixing layers) could yield better hardware utilization with controlled expressivity loss.

7. Co-design with optimization algorithms
   - Some optimization methods (e.g., block coordinate updates, asynchronous updates for factors) may overlap computation and communication, reducing wall-clock cost.
   - Investigate whether alternating updates on subsets of factors (coordinate descent style) can reduce gradient costs while maintaining convergence and final performance.

8. Broaden architectural scope and layer types
   - The paper applies BOFT to linear layers applied on neuron columns. Could the same factorization idea extend to convolutional kernels (spatially structured matrices) or attention projection matrices with structured dimension splits?
   - For very wide layers (d extremely large), multi-level factorizations (hierarchical butterflies, kaleidoscope hierarchies) might offer better scaling; study their practical runtime properties.

## Practical considerations for adopting BOFT today

While the research directions above are explored, practitioners can mitigate runtime overhead now with a few pragmatic strategies consistent with the paper:

- Use the smallest effective m that still gives good performance. The paper shows BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT (m=1) and the full orthogonal case (m=\log d). In practice, tune m as a hyperparameter along with block size b for the target task and hardware.
- Warm-start with identity initialization and use multiplicative dropout as described. Identity init keeps early training cheap and stable; multiplicative dropout reduces overfitting and can also be used to temporarily bypass some factors (reducing work) during early epochs.
- Fold learned orthogonal factors into pretrained weights as soon as validation stabilizes to avoid extra inference cost and to reduce bookkeeping.

## Closing thoughts

BOFT is an insightful step: impose orthogonality to preserve pretrained geometry, then factorize using butterfly structure to dramatically reduce parameters. The associated training-time cost is a meaningful practical limitation, but also an exciting research opportunity. By mining topologies from networking, co-designing kernels and factorizations for modern accelerators, and exploring hybrid/relaxed parameterizations, we can hope to keep BOFT’s parameter efficiency while reducing wall-clock training time.

The paper itself flags these exact frontiers — looking beyond the butterfly, reducing runtime overhead, and leveraging insights from network topologies — so the next wave of work will likely be interdisciplinary, combining numerical linear algebra, sparse/structured kernels, and network-theoretic topology design for efficient, stable PEFT at scale.
