\title{A Textbook about the Paper: BOFT (8 chapters)}

Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning and Orthogonal Constraints

# Why PEFT matters: cost, accessibility, and deployment constraints

Modern foundation models have grown to sizes that make training from scratch practically infeasible for most researchers and practitioners. The paper emphasizes that models like large language models and large vision models achieve state-of-the-art performance but come with dramatically increasing parameter counts (for example, GPT-3 is cited as having on the order of hundreds of billions of parameters). This growth produces three concrete problems that motivate parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT).

First, the computational cost of training a foundation model from random initialization is enormous: it requires vast compute, energy, and time. Second, the accessibility barrier grows—only a handful of organizations can afford the resources needed to re-train or extensively modify these models. Third, deployment and inference constraints favor adaptation techniques that do not increase latency: practitioners want to adapt models to new tasks without degrading runtime performance.

PEFT methods answer these needs by changing only a small fraction of parameters or by adding a small number of extra parameters during adaptation. The goal is to obtain task-specific performance comparable (or close) to full finetuning while keeping the parameter budget, storage, and inference costs low. The paper frames PEFT as essential for broad progress in machine learning because it enables many teams to adapt powerful pretrained models without retraining from scratch.

# PEFT families: model finetuning, adapter tuning, prompt/prefix tuning

The paper groups existing PEFT approaches into three broad families, each following a different strategy for adaptation.

Model finetuning keeps the original architecture intact and updates a subset of model parameters. This class includes simple schemes such as finetuning only certain layers or only a subset of weights. The attraction of selective finetuning is simplicity and the fact that it introduces no extra inference-time components.

Adapter tuning inserts small trainable modules (adapter layers) into an otherwise frozen model. During finetuning, only those adapter modules are trained. Adapter approaches add parameters to the model but keep the main pretrained weights fixed, and they have been used successfully across modalities.

Prompt or prefix tuning modifies the input to the model by adding learnable tokens or prompts while leaving the model weights frozen. This family is especially popular in language models, where learning a small continuous prefix can steer a large frozen model to a downstream task.

The paper highlights model finetuning specifically as "simple yet powerful" and emphasizes one additional advantage: adapter and prompt methods often change the inference-time computation or data pipeline, whereas finetuning a subset of weights can keep inference unchanged.

# Additive vs multiplicative reparameterizations

A core design choice in PEFT is how the pretrained weight matrix is adapted mathematically. The paper contrasts two broad reparameterization strategies: additive updates and multiplicative updates.

Additive reparameterizations update the pretrained weight by adding a learned correction. A prominent example is LoRA, which represents the update as a low-rank additive term. If the pretrained weight is $W^0$, LoRA produces an adapted weight of the form
\[
W = W^0 + \Delta W,
\]
where $\Delta W$ is constrained to be low rank (for example, $\Delta W = A B$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ and $r\ll d,n$). LoRA and related low-rank add-ons reduce the number of trainable parameters by forcing structure on the additive update. Additive parameterizations are easy to initialize (often with zeros so the initial model equals the pretrained model) and interact naturally with standard regularization tools such as Dropout.

Multiplicative reparameterizations change the pretrained weight by multiplying it with a learned matrix. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), as described in the paper, uses a multiplicative left-multiplication:
\[
W = R W^0,
\]
where $R$ is constrained to be orthogonal ($R^\top R = I$). This multiplicative form is qualitatively different: instead of adding a small perturbation to every weight entry, it applies a global linear transform to the neuron coordinates (columns of $W^0$). Multiplicative schemes require different initialization choices (identity initialization for $R$, so that $W$ initially equals $W^0$) and can interact with stability properties differently from additive methods.

Both families can be made parameter-efficient by imposing structure: low-rank for additive updates, block-diagonal or other structured parameterizations for multiplicative updates. The paper studies the trade-offs that arise from these choices.

# Preserving pretrained knowledge: small perturbations, relational structure, and angles between neurons

A central concern when finetuning is to preserve useful knowledge acquired during pretraining. One common heuristic is to use small learning rates to ensure the adapted model remains close to the pretrained weights. The paper frames a more structured principle: preserve relational information inside weight matrices, notably the pairwise angles between neurons.

Consider a linear layer represented by a weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The columns of $W^0$ are the neuron vectors (in the paper's notation, a "neuron" is a column of the weight matrix). The pairwise angles among these column vectors encode geometric relations that can reflect semantic structure learned during pretraining. Preserving these angles during finetuning helps maintain the pretrained representation geometry and can improve generalization to new tasks.

Orthogonal transformations applied to all neurons in a layer preserve pairwise angles exactly. If $R$ is orthogonal and $w_i^0$ and $w_j^0$ are two column vectors of $W^0$, then
\[
\frac{(R w_i^0)^\top (R w_j^0)}{\|R w_i^0\| \cdot \|R w_j^0\|} = \frac{(w_i^0)^\top w_j^0}{\|w_i^0\| \cdot \|w_j^0\|},
\]
because $R$ preserves inner products and norms. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exploits this fact by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R$ that transforms the neuron coordinate system while leaving those pairwise angular relationships unchanged. This is a principled way to keep relational structure from pretraining while allowing the model to adapt.

# Orthogonality in ML: invariance of pairwise angles and stability

Orthogonality brings several desirable mathematical properties that the paper highlights as beneficial for finetuning.

The most immediate is the invariance of pairwise angles, which we just discussed. This invariance ensures that relational geometry among neurons is preserved exactly under the learned transform. Preserving those angles is linked to the idea of minimizing disruption to pretrained knowledge while permitting flexible reorientation of neuron coordinates.

Another important property is spectral preservation. Let the pretrained weight $W^0$ have the singular value decomposition
\[
W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top,
\]
where $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal matrices and $\Sigma$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values. Left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $R$ yields
\[
R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^\top.
\]
Because $R U$ is still orthogonal, the singular values of $R W^0$ are exactly the same as those of $W^0$. In particular, the spectral norm (largest singular value) of the weight matrix is preserved. Preservation of spectral properties is known in the paper to be connected with training stability and can prevent sudden changes in layer conditioning during adaptation.

Orthogonality also introduces constraints that reduce the degrees of freedom of the update and therefore can act as a form of regularization. An orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix has only $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free parameters (the dimension of the orthogonal group), which is fewer than the $d^2$ entries of an unrestricted matrix. Imposing orthogonality narrows the hypothesis class and can improve generalization in finetuning by avoiding excessively flexible updates that overfit the downstream data.

Practically, maintaining orthogonality during optimization requires a suitable parameterization. The paper describes one such parameterization, the Cayley transform: parameterize an orthogonal $R$ as
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q^\top = -Q$). Initializing $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, which is the zero-deviation initialization analogous to additive methods that initialize additive terms to zero. Using Cayley or related orthogonal parameterizations preserves orthogonality exactly (within numerical precision) throughout gradient-based optimization.

# Notation and preliminaries for linear layers and transformers

To make the mathematical discussion concrete, the paper adopts the following notation and setting for a single linear layer and its role within larger models such as transformers.

A pretrained linear layer is represented by a weight matrix
\[
W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}.
\]
Here each column of $W^0$ is considered a neuron vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$. An input to this layer is a column vector
\[
x \in \mathbb{R}^d,
\]
and the layer's output is
\[
z = (W^0)^\top x \in \mathbb{R}^n.
\]
Under Finetuning by a left-multiplicative orthogonal update, the forward pass becomes
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
\]
where $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the learned orthogonal matrix.

Parameter counts and structural constraints are measured with respect to $d$ and $n$. A dense unconstrained orthogonal $R$ carries on the order of $d^2$ entries to specify, while structured parameterizations (for example, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices with block size $b$ and $r$ blocks such that $br=d$) reduce the number of trainable parameters. In the block-diagonal case,
\[
R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1, R_2, \dots, R_r),
\]
with each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ orthogonal, and the number of free parameters scales roughly with $b d$ rather than $d^2$.

Transformers and other large architectures are composed of many such linear layers (fully connected layers, projection matrices in attention, feed-forward networks), so finetuning strategies applied to individual linear layers extend naturally to full models by choosing which layers to adapt and how. The paper focuses primarily on layer-wise left-multiplicative orthogonal transforms applied to pretrained linear layers as a general finetuning primitive.

This notation and the properties derived above set the stage for exploring concrete orthogonal finetuning strategies: additive low-rank updates such as LoRA, multiplicative orthogonal updates such as OFT, and structured parameterizations that trade off expressivity and parameter efficiency. The next chapters build on these foundations to study parameter-efficient constructions of orthogonal transforms and their practical implications for adapting large foundation models.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles, Parameterization, and Limitations

# OFT formulation: z = (R W^0)^T x with R orthogonal

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled reparameterization for adapting a pretrained linear layer to a downstream task while attempting to preserve the geometric relationships learned during pretraining. Concretely, consider a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ representing a linear layer (columns correspond to neurons). Given an input vector $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the original pretrained forward pass produces the output
$\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. OFT modifies this by left-multiplying the frozen pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and uses
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The learnable degrees of freedom therefore lie in the matrix $\bm{R}$, and $\bm{W}^0$ remains fixed during finetuning.

The key idea is that left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise angles between columns (neurons) of $\bm{W}^0$ and preserves singular values (the spectral properties) of the pretrained layer. Intuitively, this tries to change the layer in a way that keeps the relative geometry of neuron directions intact, so that pretrained semantic structure is not destroyed. OFT is thus a multiplicative update to the weights — in contrast to additive reparameterizations (such as low-rank additive updates) — and this difference has implications for what structure is preserved and for optimization dynamics.

# Cayley transform for orthogonal parameterization and skew-symmetric Q

To ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal during optimization, OFT uses a continuous parameterization of orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. The Cayley transform maps such a $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix (when $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible) by
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, this formula yields $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, so $\bm{R}$ lies on the orthogonal group. Parameterizing $\bm{R}$ via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ has two practical advantages: (1) the parameter space is an unconstrained Euclidean space of skew-symmetric matrices (or their parameter vectors), which is convenient for gradient-based optimization; and (2) orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed by construction, avoiding the need for explicit projection steps during training.

A skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ has the property that only the strictly upper-triangular (or strictly lower-triangular) entries are independent parameters; the diagonal is zero and the lower-triangular entries are determined by the negatives of the upper-triangular ones. This reduces the independent parameter count relative to an arbitrary dense matrix. When OFT restricts $\bm{Q}$ by additional structure (see block-diagonal OFT below), the Cayley transform is still applied on those structured blocks to produce structured orthogonal factors.

# Identity (zero) initialization and stability considerations

Practical finetuning typically begins from the pretrained model exactly, or very near it, so that the finetuned model does not deviate abruptly from the pretrained solution. For OFT this principle is enforced by initializing the learnable orthogonal transform to the identity. Using the Cayley parameterization, the choice $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I},
\]
so zeroing the skew-symmetric parameters corresponds exactly to identity initialization for $\bm{R}$. This is analogous to zero initialization for additive updates in other methods and ensures the initial finetuned model reproduces the pretrained model's outputs exactly. From an optimization viewpoint, starting at identity (a neutral transformation) helps learning remain conservative at the beginning, which in practice promotes stability and can prevent destructive updates that would wash out the pretrained knowledge.

Beyond initialization, orthogonality itself contributes to stability because left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. If we write the singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
so the diagonal singular-value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$ remains unchanged. Preservation of the largest singular value (the spectral norm) is known to help training stability; in OFT, this spectral preservation is exact. Thus the combination of identity initialization and orthogonality gives a twofold stabilizing effect: small initial deviation from pretrained weights, and maintenance of spectral properties throughout finetuning.

# Block-diagonal OFT: structure, parameter counting, and inference cost

While an unconstrained $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has on the order of $d^2$ entries, the orthogonal group itself has fewer degrees of freedom: a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)$ independent parameters (the dimension of the Lie group). Nevertheless, storing and optimizing an unconstrained orthogonal matrix in OFT for large $d$ can still be prohibitively expensive. To reduce parameter count, the original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$.

Concretely, partition the $d$ coordinates into $r$ contiguous blocks of size $b$ so that $br=d$, and set
\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix (parameterized, for example, via its own skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}_i$ and Cayley transform). Because each block is of size $b$, the total number of degrees of freedom (independent parameters) in this block-diagonal orthogonal $\bm{R}$ is
\[
r\cdot\frac{1}{2}b(b-1) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}d(b-1).
\]
This expression shows the scaling: block-diagonal OFT uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ effective trainable parameters. In the extreme $b=d$ (single block) this returns to the full orthogonal parameterization with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, while small $b$ yields substantial parameter savings.

An important practical advantage of OFT (including its block-diagonal variant) is that finetuning as reparameterization introduces no inference latency: the trained orthogonal transform can be multiplied into the frozen weights once training completes, yielding the same architecture and runtime as the original pretrained model. Thus the block-diagonal constraint primarily affects storage and training-time parameter budget rather than inference cost.

# Benefits of OFT vs LoRA: preserving spectral properties and angles

Compared with popular additive low-rank finetuning methods (for example, methods that update $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta$ with low-rank $\Delta$), OFT offers distinctive geometric preservation properties by construction. Because OFT updates the weights multiplicatively via an orthogonal $\bm{R}$, it preserves the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. As shown above, if $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ then $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ leaves $\bm{\Sigma}$ intact. This exact preservation of spectral norm (the largest singular value) and the entire singular-value spectrum contributes to stability: spectral properties known to affect conditioning and gradient magnitudes remain the same as in the pretrained model, which can make optimization during finetuning more predictable and less prone to exploding or vanishing behavior.

Beyond spectral features, OFT preserves pairwise angles between neuron vectors (columns of $\bm{W}^0$). Because orthogonal transformations are isometries in Euclidean space, the pairwise inner products and hence angles between column vectors remain identical after multiplication by $\bm{R}$. This geometric invariance is motivated by the idea that pretrained models encode relational information among neurons (for instance, hyperspherical energy considerations), and preserving that structure should help retain pretrained semantics while allowing controlled adaptation.

Empirically and conceptually, these properties are argued to yield better generalizability and more stable training in finetuning scenarios compared to naïvely altering the weight matrix. OFT therefore represents a regularized variant of finetuning that privileges geometric fidelity to the pretrained representation.

# Limitations: fixed sparsity pattern, reduced expressiveness, inability to approximate classic dense transforms

Despite its favorable preservation properties, OFT in practice faces significant limitations when used naively with full orthogonal matrices, especially at large dimension $d$. The principal practical limitation is parameter cost: a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix requires on the order of $d^2$ storage (or $\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)$ degrees of freedom), which is infeasible to learn for very large layers. The block-diagonal restriction reduces parameter count but introduces its own problems.

The block-diagonal structure imposes a fixed sparsity pattern: it assumes the $d$ coordinates can be partitioned (by contiguous index) into $r$ independent groups and that coordinates belonging to different groups do not interact through the orthogonal transform. This is a strong structural prior. While it reduces the number of trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, it also reduces expressiveness: block-diagonal orthogonal matrices cannot implement dense orthogonal transforms that mix coordinates across blocks. In particular, this sparsity prevents the block-diagonal OFT from approximating many classic dense linear transforms (for example, transforms with long-range mixing across coordinates). The paper notes that dividing the coordinates of a neuron into groups by index “makes no sense” from a semantic perspective: there is typically no principled way to choose a grouping by index that aligns with the intrinsic structure of the features.

More generally, restricting the nonzero pattern of $\bm{R}$ fixes where information can flow between coordinates; this fixed sparsity introduces an inductive bias that can be undesirable if the chosen pattern does not match the task’s needs. Finding a good sparsity pattern is nontrivial and, as the paper states, remains largely a mystery. Additionally, although block-diagonal OFT reduces parameter count, it may still have more parameters than other PEFT methods like low-rank additive updates (e.g., LoRA), depending on the chosen block size, and therefore can be less parameter-efficient in practice.

Another subtle limitation is the orthogonality constraint itself: while orthogonality yields stability and geometric preservation, it also couples entries of $\bm{R}$ (orthogonality creates algebraic dependencies among entries). Under some sparsity patterns, these dependencies can force further zeros or symmetries in the composed transform, complicating the relationship between the chosen sparsity and the expressive set of attainable transforms. Thus, achieving a balance between parameter-efficiency (fewer free parameters), expressiveness (ability to represent useful transforms), and desirable inductive bias is the central engineering and scientific challenge in deploying OFT.

In summary, OFT provides a conceptually clean and geometrically motivated approach to finetuning that preserves angles and singular values via an orthogonal multiplicative update parameterized (e.g.,) through the Cayley transform and initialized at identity. To be practical at large scale, OFT typically imposes sparsity (block-diagonal structure) which reduces parameters but introduces fixed inductive biases and reduces the ability to approximate dense transforms; choosing a useful sparsity pattern is an open design choice. These trade-offs motivate searches for alternative factorizations of orthogonal matrices that can yield denser mixing with fewer parameters — a motivation explicitly pursued in the paper by introducing butterfly-based factorizations — but the core OFT ideas above form the foundation for those developments.

An Information Transmission View of Orthogonal Matrix Factorization

# Matrix multiplication unrolled as layered graphs

One way to build a dense matrix is to multiply several sparse matrices together. Concretely, suppose we want an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and we write it as a product of $m$ square factors,
\[
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]
with each $\mathbf{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. A useful and intuitive way to think about such a factorization is to "unroll" the multiplication into a layered graph. Create $m+1$ vertical levels of $d$ nodes each; level $1$ holds the $d$ source nodes (columns of the rightmost factor), level $2$ holds the $d$ nodes after one multiplication, and so on, up to level $m+1$ which holds the $d$ sink nodes (rows of the leftmost factor). Each matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ defines connections from level $i$ to level $i+1$: the entry $(\mathbf{B}_i)_{pq}\neq 0$ means there is a directed edge from node $q$ at level $i$ to node $p$ at level $i+1$.

This unrolled graph is not merely a pictorial aid: it precisely encodes when entries of the product can be nonzero. The $(i,j)$ entry of the product equals a sum over all length-$m$ walks from source node $j$ at level $1$ to sink node $i$ at level $m+1$, where each walk contributes the product of the corresponding entries in the factors. If no such walk exists (i.e., there is no sequence of nonzero entries linking those two nodes), the product entry $(\mathbf{R})_{ij}$ must be zero. If at least one such walk exists, $(\mathbf{R})_{ij}$ can be nonzero (depending on the exact numerical values). Thus factorization turns the algebraic problem of producing a dense $\mathbf{R}$ into a combinatorial reachability problem on a layered grid.

# Nodes, edges, and nonzero entries: connectivity and information flow

In the layered graph each node represents a coordinate (dimension) of an intermediate vector produced in the sequence of multiplications. An edge represents the ability to transmit information from one coordinate to another in a single multiplication step. Viewing matrix multiplication as information transmission makes several properties immediate and intuitive.

First, zero vs. nonzero entries map cleanly to absent vs. present edges. If $(\mathbf{B}_i)_{pq}=0$ there is no direct path from node $q$ on level $i$ to node $p$ on level $i+1$ in a single step; conversely nonzero entries allow one-step transfers. Second, the composition rule for matrix multiplication corresponds to concatenation of edges: to get information from source node $j$ (level 1) to sink node $i$ (level $m+1$) you must follow a walk that uses one edge between each consecutive pair of levels. Therefore the sparsity pattern of the final matrix $\mathbf{R}$ corresponds exactly to the reachability relation from level $1$ to level $m+1$ in this layered graph. This correspondence provides an exact graphical criterion: an entry $(\mathbf{R})_{ij}$ is necessarily zero if and only if there is no path of present edges connecting node $j$ at level $1$ to node $i$ at level $m+1$.

Thinking about information flow also emphasizes that expanding reachability (making more entries of $\mathbf{R}$ potentially nonzero) can be achieved by either (a) densifying a single factor (adding many edges between two adjacent levels), or (b) composing several sparse factors whose combined topology allows long-range connectivity. Option (b) is attractive when we can get dense overall connectivity while keeping each factor sparse, saving the number of free (trainable) parameters.

# Desiderata: dense connectivity and minimum free edges under orthogonality

From the information transmission view two clear design goals emerge when we aim to parameterize a dense orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ by a product of sparse factors.

The first desideratum is dense connectivity: every source node at level $1$ should be able to reach every sink node at level $m+1$. In algebraic terms this means the composed sparsity pattern must allow every entry $(\mathbf{R})_{ij}$ to be potentially nonzero, which is necessary if we want the final factorization to be able to produce an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix (or at least to approximate many different dense matrices). Graphically, dense connectivity means the layered graph is such that for all pairs of nodes (source, sink) there exists at least one length-$m$ path of present edges connecting them.

The second desideratum is minimum free edges under orthogonality. By "free edges" we mean trainable nonzero entries in the factors beyond those edges that are enforced structurally by orthogonality requirements. Orthogonality imposes constraints on each factor (see the next section), so some entries may be fixed in magnitude or sign, or forbidden; those mandatory edges are not counted toward the budget of trainable parameters. The practical goal is therefore to find a factorization topology that attains dense connectivity while using as few trainable (free) edges as possible, because each free edge corresponds to parameters to learn during finetuning.

These two desiderata often compete: naive dense connectivity (a single dense factor) achieves reachability trivially but costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges; clever sparse topologies aim to reach dense connectivity with a much smaller total number of edges (and hence fewer trainable parameters). The information-transmission formulation is precisely the right language to search for such topologies.

# Orthogonality-induced constraints between adjacent layers (bijective matchings)

Orthogonality is not a neutral constraint on the factors. If we require each factor $\mathbf{B}_i$ to be orthogonal, then each $\mathbf{B}_i$ must, in particular, be full-rank and have orthonormal columns (and rows). Translated to the layered graph language, full rank implies that no level-to-level mapping can be deficient: at minimum there must be enough edges so that the mapping from level $i$ to level $i+1$ can be injective and surjective on the $d$ coordinates. A simple combinatorial way to express this is that each orthogonal factor must support at least a bijection between the $d$ nodes of the two adjacent levels; equivalently, there must be at least one nonzero entry per row and one per column in that factor. Counting those mandatory edges gives a lower bound of $d$ edges per factor (one representative edge per row or column), and these are necessary if orthogonality is to be possible.

This is why the paper emphasizes that orthogonality injects a delicate constraint: an orthogonal $\mathbf{B}_i$ cannot be arbitrarily sparse. The presence and placement of zeros are constrained because orthonormality of rows and columns ties different entries together. In other words, the combinatorial degrees of freedom for choosing which edges are present are reduced by algebraic consistency conditions imposed by orthogonality.

A concrete small example illustrates the effect of orthogonality on sparsity. Consider a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix
\[
\begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix},
\]
with orthonormal columns. If $a=0$ (i.e., the edge from column 1 to row 1 is absent), then column 1 must be $[0,\pm 1]^\top$, so $c=\pm 1$. The orthogonality condition between columns, $ab + cd = 0$, then implies $d=0$. Thus a single zero at position $(1,1)$ forces another zero at $(2,2)$. Graphically, the absence of one edge restricts which other edges can appear without violating orthogonality. This shows that orthogonality can both remove and implicitly require edges elsewhere in a factor; pattern choices are not independent.

# Edge counting and examples: when composites are dense or not

The layered graph view makes edge counting concrete and illuminates trade-offs. A single dense orthogonal factor connecting two adjacent levels requires on the order of $d^2$ possible edges. The paper frames this by noting that a dense $d\times d$ connectivity has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges, and that if one subtracts the $d$ mandatory edges tied to orthogonality one obtains $d^2-d$ free edges (for instance, when $d=4$ this quantity is $12$). That single dense factor trivially yields full reachability, but it comes with an expensive parameter count.

Composing multiple sparse factors can achieve the same reachability with far fewer edges. The paper gives an example (illustrated in their Figure) where a particular factorization of $\mathbf{R}$ into five sparse factors used $10$ edges total yet produced dense reachability among $d=4$ nodes: information from every source could reach every sink after five layers. By comparison, a butterfly topology for the same $d$ can reach dense connectivity with only $8$ edges. These numbers exemplify the potential savings: clever sparse topologies reduce the total count of edges (and thus free parameters) from quadratic to near-linear or quasi-linear in $d$.

The key combinatorial observation underpinning such savings is that long-range connectivity can be built by iteratively mixing small groups of coordinates. Each sparse factor needs only a few interconnections; when composed, these local interchanges permute and redistribute information so that after several layers every source has a path to every sink. The butterfly family of topologies is a canonical and highly efficient such construction: with $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ edges, a butterfly factorization obtains full reachability using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges in total. This is dramatically smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for large $d$.

A central algebraic caveat is that reachability is necessary but not sufficient for the composed product to realize arbitrary target matrices; numerical values in the nonzero entries also matter. Nevertheless, from a sparsity-budget perspective, the layered graph gives a first-order criterion: if your chosen factor topologies do not allow paths from some source to some sink, then you can never produce a nonzero in that position of the product no matter what numerical values you choose.

# Why block-diagonal OFT cannot yield dense connectivity

The block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization used by the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is easy to understand in graph terms, and this perspective explains its limitations. In block-diagonal OFT the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as
\[
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),
\]
with blocks $\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ and $br=d$. Each factor (here the single factor $\mathbf{R}$) therefore connects only coordinates within each contiguous block: the layered graph has $r$ disconnected components, and there are no edges between different blocks.

Viewed as an information transmission problem, this topology is immediately seen to fail the dense connectivity desideratum. A source node sitting in block $a$ at level $1$ cannot reach a sink node in block $b\neq a$ at level $2$ because there are no inter-block edges in the single block-diagonal factor. Consequently the composed matrix $\mathbf{R}$ will have a block-diagonal sparsity pattern and cannot be dense: entries that correspond to inter-block pairs are forced to zero. No choice of numbers inside the blocks can compensate for the missing structural edges between blocks.

In other words, block-diagonal OFT trades parameter savings for expressivity by enforcing permanent separations among coordinate groups. That trade-off can be useful (it reduces parameters and can regularize learning) but it rules out approximating arbitrary dense orthogonal transforms. The information-transmission formulation makes this limitation immediate: the block-diagonal topology simply does not provide the necessary paths across blocks to transmit information globally.

By contrast, a factorization built from multiple sparse, but suitably interleaved, factors (such as butterfly components) can achieve global reachability: information originating in any coordinate can be routed to any other coordinate through a sequence of local interchanges. Such topologies keep per-factor sparsity low while achieving dense connectivity after composition, satisfying the desiderata discussed earlier.

Summary: Recasting matrix factorization as information transmission on a layered grid converts algebraic questions about sparsity and expressivity into graph reachability and edge-budget questions. This view clarifies why a single block-diagonal orthogonal factor cannot give a dense transform, why orthogonality constrains which sparsity patterns are admissible, and why structured multi-layer topologies (for example, butterfly factorizations) can achieve dense connectivity with far fewer trainable parameters than a single dense factor.

Butterfly Structures and Fast Linear Transforms as a Design Prior

# Review of Cooley–Tukey FFT and butterfly networks

One of the most famous algorithms in numerical linear algebra and signal processing is the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT). The FFT computes the discrete Fourier transform of a length-$N$ signal in $O(N\log N)$ time instead of the naive $O(N^2)$ approach. The key to this speedup is a divide-and-conquer decomposition: the $N$-point transform is expressed in terms of smaller transforms (typically two $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms for the radix-2 case) interleaved with simple local linear operations. When you unroll this recursive decomposition, the data flow naturally forms a layered network of sparse interactions; each layer exchanges information only among small groups of coordinates, and after $\log N$ layers every output coordinate has received contributions from every input coordinate. The canonical visualization of this data flow is called a butterfly network (or butterfly graph) because the pattern of connections between adjacent layers resembles butterfly wings.

From an information-transmission point of view, a butterfly network is an extremely efficient topology: it achieves global mixing (every input can influence every output) with logarithmic depth and only linear total connectivity. Concretely, if $d$ is the dimension (with $d$ typically a power of two), the radix-2 butterfly exchanges information across $\log d$ stages, and each stage contains $O(d)$ local operations. Thus the total number of elementary operations and the total number of nonzero connections in the unrolled transform are $O(d\log d)$. This combination—dense global reach with sparse local structure and logarithmic depth—is what makes butterfly networks attractive as a design prior when we want to replace a dense linear operator by a structured, parameter-efficient factorization that still permits global interactions.

# Butterfly factor B^F(k): definition and structure

The paper formalizes a local building block for butterfly factorizations called the butterfly factor. For an integer $k\ge 2$ (we focus on powers of two), the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$ is defined in block form as

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector and $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ denotes a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the components of $\bm{d}_i$. Intuitively, $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is a $k\times k$ matrix partitioned into four $k/2\times k/2$ blocks; each block is diagonal, so each row of $\bm{B}^F(k)$ has at most two nonzero entries (one in the left half and one in the right half), making it very sparse.

This form captures the elementary local operations that appear in radix-2 FFT stages: each small group of coordinates undergoes a two-way mixing controlled by a small number of parameters. The vectors $\bm{d}_1,\dots,\bm{d}_4$ specify how the top and bottom halves combine. Because every block is diagonal, the nonzero pattern of $\bm{B}^F(k)$ connects corresponding positions across the two halves, which is exactly the “butterfly” pattern of pairwise local exchanges in FFT implementations.

# Recursive butterfly matrix B(d) and its layered composition

To build a full $d\times d$ butterfly matrix, where $d=2^N$, the construction composes multiple levels of these local butterfly factors in a recursive fashion. The paper defines the $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ via intermediate block-diagonal components denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$. Each block-diagonal component groups together many copies of $\bm{B}^F(k)$:

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\bm{B}^F_2(k),\dots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big),
\]

so $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a $d\times d$ matrix composed of $d/k$ independent $k\times k$ butterfly factors along the diagonal. Using these components, the full butterfly matrix is assembled as a product of progressively coarser components:

\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\,\cdots\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]

Each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ corresponds to one stage of the network: at stage with block size $k$ the matrix applies independent $\bm{B}^F(k)$ transforms to contiguous blocks of size $k$. The product of these stages implements recursive mixing: small-group interactions in the fine-grained stages, and interactions across larger groups in the coarser stages, combine so that after all $\log d$ stages every output accumulates contributions from every input.

Viewing $\bm{B}(d)$ as a layered composition makes the information-transmission intuition explicit. Imagine $d$ source nodes on the left and $d$ receiver nodes on the right with $\log d+1$ layers of nodes in between; each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ provides sparse edges that connect adjacent layers in a structured, permutation-like way. Because the number of stages is $\log d$ and each stage has $O(d)$ nonzeros, the product has $O(d\log d)$ nonzero parameters while still allowing dense connectivity between inputs and outputs.

# Block butterfly components: \tilde{B}^b(d,k) and permutations

The paper generalizes the basic butterfly components to block-butterfly components that operate on blocks of size $b$ instead of scalars. In this block version, each scalar entry in the diagonal vectors $\bm{d}_i$ is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix. Formally, the block butterfly component is denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ and is a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are $k\times k$ butterfly factors where scalars are replaced by $b\times b$ blocks. This gives a $d\times d$ matrix with $d/k$ blocks, and within each $k\times k$ block the nonzero pattern is that of $\bm{B}^F(k)$ at block granularity.

Introducing a block size $b$ lets us trade off granularity and parameter count. When $b=1$ we recover the scalar butterfly; larger $b$ means each nonzero entry is itself a $b\times b$ dense submatrix, increasing expressive power per nonlinear position while keeping the sparse high-level topology. Practically, $b$ is used to control the number of trainable parameters and to improve the ability of the factorization to approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices when full scalar-level expressivity is restricted.

An important observation in the construction is that the nonzero pattern of each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is a permutation of the nonzero pattern of the finest-scale component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$. That is, the stages can be viewed as applying the same local two-way operations to different interleavings (permutations) of coordinates. This permutation view is central because it means one can parameterize the entire family of components by ensuring orthogonality on the elementary two-way blocks and then applying fixed permutations to place those blocks at the correct positions for each stage.

# Orthogonality of butterfly components via 2x2 (or 2b×2b) orthogonal blocks

Orthogonality of the composed transform is important for the use-case in orthogonal finetuning: the method applies a left-multiplicative orthogonal matrix to pretrained weights so that pairwise angles and spectral properties are preserved. To guarantee that the whole butterfly product is orthogonal, it suffices to make each butterfly component orthogonal, because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal.

At the finest scale, $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ is block-diagonal with blocks of size $2b\times 2b$. Each such $2b\times 2b$ block is a small dense matrix that mixes two $b$-dimensional subblocks; requiring each of these blocks to be orthogonal makes the whole $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ orthogonal. The larger components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k>2$ have the same block-level nonzero pattern up to permutation, so they can be realized as permutations of these orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ blocks and hence be orthogonal as well.

A practical parameterization for enforcing orthogonality of these small blocks is the Cayley transform (as used in OFT), or simply parameterizing them as $2$-dimensional rotations when $b=1$. The Cayley transform represents an orthogonal matrix via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as

\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$. Initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, which achieves the desired identity initialization so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights. Applied blockwise, this parameterization ensures each $2b\times 2b$ elementary block is orthogonal and initialized to identity. Because the other butterfly components are permutations of these blocks, they inherit orthogonality from the block parameterization.

This blockwise orthogonality has two advantages. First, it preserves the desirable spectral properties of the pretrained weight matrix: left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ does not change singular values. Second, by concentrating the orthogonality enforcement in small blocks, we keep the number of independent parameters low while guaranteeing that the entire multilayer composition remains orthogonal.

# Complexity analysis: parameters and multiplies in O(d log d)

One of the primary motivations for the butterfly factorization is parameter efficiency without sacrificing global expressivity. The paper makes this concrete with counts for the number of effective trainable parameters in BOFT. If we compose $m$ block-butterfly components of block size $b$ to form the orthogonal matrix, BOFT($m,b$) uses

\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]

effective trainable parameters for a linear layer of size $d\times n$. This formula comes from counting the independent parameters inside the orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ blocks across all $m$ stages. Two special cases illustrate the scaling:

- The original (full) orthogonal parameterization corresponds to $m=1$ and $b=d$: BOFT($1,d$) recovers an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix and therefore requires $O(d^2)$ parameters.

- The butterfly case corresponds to $m=\log d$ and $b=2$: BOFT($\log d,2$) yields the classical radix-2 butterfly factorization and uses $O(d\log d)$ parameters. Plugging into the formula with $b=2$ gives $\frac{1}{2}(2-1)d\log d = \tfrac{1}{2}d\log d$ effective trainable parameters, i.e. $O(d\log d)$.

From a computational standpoint, each forward application of the orthogonal transform is carried out as the sequential multiplication by the $m$ sparse block components. Each component has $O(d)$ nonzero blocks (each of size $b\times b$), so applying one component costs $O(db^2)$ arithmetic to multiply through the $b\times b$ blocks; with $m$ stages the total cost is $O(mdb^2)$. For the scalar butterfly ($b=1$), this reduces to $O(dm)=O(d\log d)$ time per application. Thus the factorization achieves the desirable trade-off: global mixing across $d$ coordinates with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters and $O(d\log d)$ arithmetic (for $b=1$), instead of $O(d^2)$ for a dense matrix.

The paper notes a practical caveat: because BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal factors, this multiplicative structure introduces a modest runtime overhead during training compared to a single-block (e.g., block-diagonal) OFT. However, the orthogonal butterfly factors can be multiplied into the pretrained weights after finetuning, removing any additional inference cost. The key takeaway is that butterfly factorizations provide a principled, provably cheaper parameterization (in $d$) that still enables dense global interactions with logarithmic depth, making them an attractive design prior for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT): Method and Parameterization

# BOFT forward pass: R(m, b) = product of orthogonal butterfly components

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is a reparameterization-based finetuning method in which a pretrained linear layer is adapted by left-multiplying its frozen weight matrix with a learnable orthogonal transform that is itself parameterized as a product of sparse, structured factors called butterfly components. Concretely, let $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ be a pretrained layer (columns are neurons) and let $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the input to that layer. BOFT replaces the usual forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
with the finetuned forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\]
where the orthogonal modifier $\bm{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is factored as a product of $m$ block-butterfly components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
Each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a $d\times d$ block-structured matrix whose nonzero pattern follows the butterfly topology; each nonzero block is constrained to be orthogonal. The orthogonality of each component is typically enforced with a Cayley parameterization (or equivalently by parameterizing small blocks as rotations), so every factor satisfies
\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} = \bm{I}_d,
\]
and hence the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is orthogonal. BOFT therefore performs a multiplicative orthogonal update of the pretrained weights rather than an additive low-rank update.

Viewing $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of sparse orthogonal factors gives two immediate practical consequences. First, the multiplicative nature preserves singular values of $\bm{W}^0$ (left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change the spectral norm), which benefits training stability. Second, because each factor is sparse and structured, the number of free parameters required to represent $\bm{R}(m,b)$ can be much smaller than a full dense orthogonal matrix while still generating a dense overall transform when $m$ and the internal permutations are chosen appropriately.

# BOFT(m, b) family and special cases: BOFT(1, b) = block OFT; BOFT(1, d) = full OFT; BOFT(log d, 2) = dense butterfly

The BOFT family is indexed by two integers: the number of butterfly components $m$ and a block size parameter $b$ (where $b$ is an even integer in the block-butterfly construction). Different choices of $(m,b)$ interpolate between familiar special cases and different points on the expressivity–efficiency spectrum.

When $m=1$, BOFT reduces to a block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning (block OFT). In that case the single factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$ is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix composed of independent $b\times b$ orthogonal blocks along the diagonal. That is, BOFT$(1,b)$ is exactly the block-diagonal OFT used for parameter efficiency in prior work: each neuron’s coordinates are partitioned into $d/b$ groups, and each group is rotated independently by a $b\times b$ orthogonal block.

If, in that single-block case, one sets $b=d$, then the single block is a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix and BOFT$(1,d)$ recovers the original full OFT (unconstrained orthogonal finetuning), which uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

At the other extreme, when one uses the radix-style butterfly with $b=2$ and chooses $m=\log d$ (more precisely $m=\log_2 d$ when $d$ is a power of two), BOFT$(\log d,2)$ yields the classic dense butterfly matrix: a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse factors whose composition is dense. This configuration yields a dense orthogonal transform while using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters. Thus BOFT provides a family that smoothly spans block-diagonal OFT (low $m$, larger $b$) and dense-but-parameter-efficient butterfly transforms (larger $m$, small $b$).

A useful way to think about the family is that increasing $m$ adds more sequential mixing layers (more opportunities for information to route between coordinates), while decreasing $b$ forces each local block to be small and inexpensive. In practice the pair $(m,b)$ is chosen to meet a desired parameter budget and to trade off expressivity and regularity.

# Parameter count and expressivity vs budget trade-offs

A compact count of effective trainable parameters in BOFT is available from the block-butterfly structure. For a linear layer of input dimension $d$ (and arbitrary output dimension $n$), BOFT$(m,b)$ requires
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
effective trainable parameters to parameterize the orthogonal modifier $\bm{R}(m,b)$. This formula reflects that each orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ block contributes a limited number of free parameters (for example parameterizing a $2\times 2$ rotation introduces one degree of freedom), and those degrees are replicated across $d$ coordinates and accumulated across $m$ components.

This counting yields several concrete limiting cases. The full unconstrained orthogonal OFT corresponds to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (BOFT$(1,d)$). The classic butterfly (radix-2 with $b=2$ and $m=\log d$) uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, since $\frac{1}{2}(2-1)d\log d = \frac{1}{2}d\log d = \mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. Block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ is BOFT$(1,b)$ and uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters.

Beyond raw parameter counts, expressivity also depends on how many mixing stages (components) and how large local blocks one permits. The paper demonstrates (and states in Theorem 1) that BOFT with the butterfly topology is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT at the same block size: adding sequential butterfly components gives richer mixing patterns than a single block-diagonal transform while keeping parameter cost low. In the extreme, compositions of multiple butterfly matrices (and their transposes) can represent the entire orthogonal group (this is analogous to the kaleidoscope hierarchy), but such maximal expressivity would require many components and thus larger parameter budgets. In practice, one seeks a sweet spot: enough components to permit dense—and useful—mixing across coordinates, but not so many that the model overfits or that parameter efficiency is lost.

Another practical consideration is runtime: because BOFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ factors, the multiplicative update introduces an extra factor-$m$ of small structured multiplications during training. Thus the design choice of $(m,b)$ must balance parameter efficiency and the acceptable training-time overhead. At inference time, however, the learned factors can be multiplied into the frozen weights once, so BOFT incurs no additional inference latency after that fusion.

# Identity initialization for stability and compatibility with pretrained models

A core desideratum for finetuning is to start from a model that is identical to the pretrained model so that the finetuned model does not jump far from the pretrained weights at the start of optimization. BOFT satisfies this by initializing every butterfly component to the identity, which produces $\bm{R}(m,b)=\bm{I}_d$ at initialization. Practically, this is implemented by parameterizing each orthogonal block with a Cayley transform (or directly as small rotation matrices) and initializing the underlying skew-symmetric parameters to zero; with Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ and $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, zeroing $\bm{Q}$ yields the identity.

Identity initialization has two immediate advantages. First, it gives compatibility with pretrained models: at initialization the finetuned model reproduces the pretrained model exactly. Second, it stabilizes training because the optimizer starts in a neighborhood of the pretrained weights rather than at an arbitrary orthogonal modifier, avoiding large sudden changes in the network’s functional behavior. This mirrors zero or small initialization strategies used in additive reparameterizations like LoRA but adapted to multiplicative orthogonal updates.

# Multiplicative dropout: block-wise identity replacement strategy

Because BOFT is multiplicative, conventional additive Dropout strategies used on low-rank updates (e.g., zeroing out rank components) do not directly translate. To regularize BOFT and reduce overfitting, the paper proposes a multiplicative Dropout that acts at the granularity of butterfly components and their internal blocks.

Concretely, multiplicative Dropout randomly selects a fraction $p_1$ of the $m$ butterfly components and, within each selected component, selects a fraction $p_2$ of its diagonal blocks. Each chosen block is replaced by an identity block for that forward/backward pass. This scheme effectively temporarily masks entire local orthogonal transformations by turning them into identities, hence multiplicatively reducing the contribution of those components. Because the remaining blocks are still orthogonal, the overall modifier remains orthogonal (product of orthogonal matrices, with some blocks identity) for that pass. Multiplicative Dropout is therefore a principled way to inject stochastic regularization in a multiplicative orthogonal parameterization: it is the analogue of dropout or stochastic depth adapted to the BOFT factorization.

The rationale is practical: by stochastically forcing parts of the multiplicative update to be identity, BOFT avoids relying too heavily on particular components or local blocks during finetuning and hence reduces overfitting risk, while maintaining the orthogonality constraints and the multiplicative structure.

# Implementation in transformers, vision backbones, and diffusion U-Nets

BOFT is a general layer-wise finetuning strategy that applies wherever pretrained linear maps are present. The paper demonstrates BOFT on a range of architectures: large vision transformers, large language transformer models, and text-to-image diffusion U-Nets. The implementation pattern is uniform:

1. Identify the pretrained linear layers to be adapted (e.g., query/key/value and projection matrices in attention, MLP linear projections in transformers, convolutional or fully connected layers in U-Nets).
2. Freeze the original weights $\bm{W}^0$.
3. For each targeted pretrained layer, insert a learnable orthogonal modifier $\bm{R}(m,b)$ that left-multiplies the frozen weights so the forward pass becomes $\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}$.
4. Parameterize each butterfly component as a block-structured sparse orthogonal matrix using small orthogonal blocks (e.g., $2\times2$ rotations when $b=2$). Enforce orthogonality with Cayley transforms or by parameterizing each small block directly as an orthogonal matrix (rotation).
5. Initialize all components to identity (zero skew-symmetric parameters).
6. Optionally apply multiplicative Dropout during training by randomly replacing some blocks or components with identities on each pass.
7. After finetuning, fuse the learned modifier into the frozen weights (multiply the product of learned factors into $\bm{W}^0$) so there is no inference-time overhead.

Because BOFT works by left-multiplying the pretrained weight matrices, it introduces no changes to the original model’s architectural wiring and adds no per-inference cost after fusion. The same pattern applies across attention and MLP blocks in transformers, linear layers in vision backbones, and the U-Net blocks used in diffusion models.

# Choosing m and b under parameter budgets and latency constraints

Selecting $(m,b)$ is a practical trade-off among parameter budget, expressivity, and training-time cost.

Parameter budget: use the effective parameter count formula
\[
\text{params} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
as the primary budget constraint. For a fixed $d$ and desired parameter budget, this expression provides a simple algebraic relation to pick feasible pairs $(m,b)$. For example, decreasing $b$ reduces per-component degrees of freedom and can be offset by increasing $m$ to keep parameters constant while improving mixing (more stages).

Expressivity: all else equal, increasing $m$ (more components) increases the connectivity of information across coordinates and thus expressivity; decreasing $b$ (smaller local blocks) reduces local degrees of freedom but can be compensated by more stages to achieve dense interactions. Empirically and theoretically, BOFT with small $b$ and larger $m$ often outperforms the single-block OFT with large $b$ at the same parameter count because the butterfly mixing enables richer global transforms from fewer parameters.

Training-time overhead: because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is applied as a product of $m$ factors during the forward/backward passes, training cost increases roughly with the number of components $m$ (each component is sparse and cheap, but $m$ multiplies that cost). If training-time or compute latency is a hard constraint, prefer smaller $m$ (fewer components) and slightly larger $b$ to maintain parameter budgets. Conversely, if the primary constraint is parameter budget with relaxed training compute, increasing $m$ (while reducing $b$) can achieve greater expressivity per parameter.

Inference: once training completes, the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ can be multiplied into $\bm{W}^0$ and the factors no longer need to be applied at runtime. Therefore, inference latency is unaffected by $(m,b)$ after one-time fusion.

Practical recipe: pick the maximal $m$ that your training-time budget tolerates and a minimal $b$ consistent with numeric stability (often $b=2$ or small even integers are used), then adjust $(m,b)$ to meet the parameter budget by trading stages for block size using the parameter count relation. If storage/parameter budget is tight, favor smaller $b$ with more $m$ (but monitor training time); if training time is tight, favor larger $b$ and smaller $m$.

In all cases, identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout are recommended to stabilize training and reduce overfitting.

# Closing remarks

BOFT provides a structured, principled way to perform multiplicative orthogonal finetuning that preserves spectral properties of pretrained weights while offering large parameter savings through butterfly factorization. The $(m,b)$ family interpolates from block-diagonal OFT to full orthogonal OFT and to parameter-efficient dense butterfly transforms. With identity initialization, Cayley-based orthogonality, and multiplicative Dropout, BOFT is a practical finetuning recipe applicable across transformers, vision backbones, and diffusion U-Nets: it enables dense, expressive orthogonal updates at budgets far smaller than naive dense orthogonal parametrizations, with no inference-time penalty after factor fusion.

Theory and Properties: Expressivity, Spectral Behavior, and Inductive Bias

# Approximation of random orthogonal matrices and parameter-efficiency trends

A concrete way to probe the representational power of BOFT is to ask how well its structured orthogonal parameterization can approximate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix. The paper reports a numerical experiment that approximates a random orthogonal matrix of size $512\times 512$ (generated by a standard random orthogonal generator) and measures approximation error as a function of the number of effective trainable parameters. Results are averaged over 10 random seeds. The plotted quantity (vertical axis) is the approximation error and the horizontal axis is the effective number of trainable parameters used by different parameterizations. Each curve groups parameterizations with the same block size $b$; the leftmost point on each curve corresponds to the block-diagonal OFT baseline BOFT$(1,b)$.

The observed trends are clear and important for understanding BOFT’s parameter-efficiency:

- BOFT generally achieves lower approximation error for the same or fewer parameters than the block-diagonal OFT with the same block size. This empirically demonstrates that the butterfly factorization concentrates representational power into fewer parameters compared with the naive block-diagonal parameterization.

- Within the BOFT family, configurations with smaller block size $b$ and larger factorization depth $m$ (more butterfly components) tend to be more parameter-efficient. For example, BOFT$(9,2)$ (many small blocks) can approximate the target orthogonal matrix better than BOFT$(1,16)$ (single block of size $16$) while using far fewer parameters. Similarly, BOFT$(6,4)$ uses much fewer parameters yet attains a similar approximation error to BOFT$(2,16)$.

- These trends illustrate a trade-off between the two axes of BOFT’s design: the number of butterfly layers $m$ and the block size $b$. Increasing $m$ (more factor multiplicative depth) allows a dense effect to be built up via repeated sparse interactions, while smaller $b$ keeps the number of parameters per layer low. The empirical curves show that proper allocation along these dimensions yields much better parameter-efficiency than treating $b$ in isolation.

To relate these trends to concrete counts, BOFT$(m,b)$ uses effectively $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer (the factor $\tfrac12(b-1)$ arises from how many degrees of freedom are used in each orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ block under the chosen parameterization). By contrast, a fully parameterized dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and the original block-diagonal OFT with $r$ blocks (block size $b$) requires $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters. A canonical butterfly choice, $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, reduces the parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. The empirical approximation results confirm that this asymptotic reduction can translate into strong practical efficiency for approximating dense orthogonal matrices.

# Theorem: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size

The paper states and uses the following theoretical claim (paraphrased as given):

Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT). BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. For the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, we can multiply butterfly matrices with
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d),
\]
where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$, for all $i,j$, are butterfly matrices.

Intuition and consequences. The theorem has two linked messages. First, holding the block size (i.e., the basic local orthogonal block parametrization) fixed, arranging those blocks in butterfly layers and composing several such layers yields a strictly larger set of reachable orthogonal transformations than arranging the same blocks as independent block-diagonal factors (the original OFT setup). The butterfly interleaves small orthogonal interactions so that local 2-by-2 (or $2b\times 2b$) operations cascade and produce highly nonlocal mixing across coordinates. Second, the multiplication and transposition pattern in the displayed product is the algebraic mechanism by which many such layered, sparse factors together can emulate richer global structure.

Why this implies greater expressivity is intuitive: a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix applies independent orthogonal changes within disjoint coordinate groups, so no mixing occurs across groups. Butterfly layers explicitly route information between groups at each stage (via small orthogonal blocks and index permutations), so successive layers exponentially increase the set of coordinates that can influence each other. Therefore, for the same per-block degrees of freedom, arranging those blocks in a butterfly hierarchy yields strictly more global transformations.

Caveat. The theorem as given in the paper asserts greater expressivity relative to block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, but it does not claim that a single shallow BOFT configuration equals the full orthogonal group. Rather, the construction in the theorem points to how compositions of many butterfly matrices (and transposes) can grow expressivity, and motivates the kaleidoscope-style generalization discussed next.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchy and conditions for spanning the orthogonal group

The paper extends the BOFT idea to a more general composition and connects to the kaleidoscope hierarchy. Concretely, it defines a generalized orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
= \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
where each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(m,b)$ is an orthogonal matrix built via BOFT’s block-butterfly components. The kaleidoscope viewpoint is that alternating products of (block) butterfly matrices and their transposes can greatly expand the representational set.

A particularly important condition stated in the paper is that when $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the resulting composition $\bm{R}^G$ can represent the entire orthogonal group in dimension $d$. In other words, with sufficiently many layers (on the order of $\log d$ per factor) and alternating butterfly and transposed-butterfly factors repeated enough times ($l$ on the order of $d$), the composition is universal over orthogonal matrices. This is the kaleidoscope hierarchy idea: layered, alternating structured factors can generate arbitrarily fine-grained global transforms.

Interpretation for practice. There is an important practical lesson embedded here. Universal expressivity is achievable by stacking enough structured layers, but that universality requires a potentially large number of components (and thus parameters and compute). In finetuning, however, being universal is not necessarily desirable: excessive expressivity can lead to overfitting or loss of the pretrained structure. BOFT therefore offers a tunable path: by choosing $m$ and $b$ appropriately, practitioners can navigate between very constrained (more regular) transforms and very expressive (near-universal) transforms, depending on how much capacity and flexibility the downstream task requires.

# Spectral norm preservation and implications for stability and generalization

A central mathematical property of orthogonal finetuning methods (both OFT and BOFT) is that left-multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. Writing the singular value decomposition
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top,
\]
with orthogonal $\bm{U},\bm{V}$ and diagonal nonnegative $\bm{\Sigma}$, the finetuned weight becomes
\[
\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\,\bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, $\bm{R}\,\bm{U}$ is orthogonal and thus the singular values remain exactly the diagonal entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$. Therefore the spectral norm (largest singular value) and the entire singular value spectrum of the pretrained layer are preserved by orthogonal finetuning.

Why does this matter? The paper connects spectral preservation to two desirable behaviors in deep learning:

- Training stability. Keeping the spectral norm of layer weights under control prevents exploding activations and gradients in forward and backward passes. Preserving the pretrained singular values avoids introducing disproportionately large amplification directions that could destabilize optimization, especially when the pretrained model was carefully balanced.

- Generalization. Prior work referenced in the paper links spectral properties (e.g., bounded spectral norm) to generalization guarantees or empirical improvements in generalization. By not altering the singular value spectrum, orthogonal finetuning avoids perturbing model capacity in a way that could lead to overfitting; instead it adjusts the geometry of the input space via orthonormal rotations that maintain the magnitude structure encoded in the singular values.

BOFT inherits these spectral advantages while introducing additional structural priors. This combination—exact spectral preservation plus structured, sparse factorization of the orthogonal matrix—provides a mechanism to gain parameter efficiency without compromising the spectral stability that underlies good training behavior.

# Bilinear similarity perspective: learning R in w_i^0 R x

BOFT can be interpreted succinctly as learning a bilinear similarity. Consider a single neuron (column) $\bm{w}^0_i$ of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Under BOFT, the finetuned output corresponding to this neuron for input $\bm{x}$ is
\[
\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{x}
= \big(\bm{R}\,\bm{w}^0_i\big)^\top \bm{x}
= \big(\bm{w}^0_i\big)^\top \big(\bm{R}^\top \bm{x}\big)
= \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \!\,(\bm{R}\,\bm{x}),
\]
so the operation can be viewed as computing a bilinear form
\[
\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \,\bm{R}\,\bm{x},
\]
or, equivalently, as measuring a similarity between $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{x}$ under the bilinear map $\bm{R}$. In that light, finetuning reduces to learning the bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ subject to the orthogonality constraint.

Connections to metric learning and bilinear forms. This viewpoint makes an explicit connection to metric learning and classical bilinear forms. In metric learning, one often learns a positive-definite matrix $\bm{M}$ so that distances of the form $(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^\top \bm{M} (\bm{x}-\bm{y})$ reflect semantic similarity. BOFT’s learned $\bm{R}$ is not positive-definite but orthogonal; instead of stretching directions it rotates or permutes them. From the bilinear-form perspective, BOFT adjusts how pretrained neuron vectors compare to inputs via coordinate reorientation, keeping lengths and relative scales fixed but changing relative alignments. This constrained flexibility yields a strong regularizing effect: the geometry of magnitude (via singular values) is fixed while geometric directions (via orthogonal transformations) are adapted to the downstream task.

Practical implication. Thinking of BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity clarifies why small, structured changes can be powerful: many downstream tasks require reorienting existing feature extractors rather than globally re-scaling them. BOFT provides precisely that operation in a parameter-efficient manner.

# Structured inductive bias: recovering classic transforms (FFT, DCT, Hadamard)

A major conceptual advantage of butterfly-based parameterizations is their strong affinity to many classical fast linear transforms. The Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT), the discrete cosine transform (DCT), and the Hadamard transform are examples where a global dense transform is realized by composing a small number of sparse, structured factors—exactly the pattern exploited by butterfly matrices.

Recoverability of classic transforms. The paper emphasizes that the butterfly structure, with appropriate choices of diagonal factors and permutations, can exactly realize many of these transforms. In practice this means that a BOFT parameterization contains within its structured family transformations with highly useful global mixing properties (for example, orthogonal mixing across frequencies in FFT or Hadamard mixing across signs). This contrasts sharply with the block-diagonal OFT: no fixed block-diagonal structure can recover a transform that inherently mixes coordinates across blocks, because block-diagonal matrices never create cross-block interactions.

Inductive bias and generalization. By choosing a parameterization family that naturally contains these well-structured global transforms, BOFT injects an inductive bias that favors transformations with efficient, distributed interactions between coordinates. The paper argues that this structural bias is beneficial for generalization: many useful transformations that appear in signal processing and representation learning have sparse recursive factorizations (like butterfly networks), so constraining finetuning to a family that can represent such transforms makes it easier to find good low-complexity solutions that still effect global reorientation.

Smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal group. The butterfly parameterization also yields a smooth interpolation between the identity transformation (zero-change initialization) and very expressive global transforms. By controlling the number of butterfly layers $m$ and the block size $b$, the BOFT family spans from near-identity regularity (small $m$, large $b$ or identity initialization) to very expressive global rotations (large $m$, small $b$ and composing many factors). This controllable inductive bias helps practitioners trade off the need to preserve pretrained behavior against the desire for expressivity in the downstream task.

Sparsity as a complementary family to low-rank methods. Finally, BOFT highlights how sparsity-based structured parameterizations (butterfly sparse factorizations) are complementary to the low-rank parameterizations commonly used in PEFT (e.g., LoRA). Both low-rank and sparse-factor families reduce parameter count, but they induce different biases: low-rank favors global, low-dimensional subspaces of change, while butterfly sparsity favors recursive, efficient mixing patterns. Depending on the downstream adaptation, one family or a hybrid may be preferable.

Concluding summary

This chapter examined BOFT’s theoretical and empirical properties relevant to representational power, numerical stability, and inductive bias. Empirically, BOFT approximates random orthogonal matrices more parameter-efficiently than block-diagonal OFT, with configurations of smaller block size and greater depth often giving the best trade-offs. Theoretically, compositions of butterfly matrices (and their transposes) can expand representational capacity and, under kaleidoscope-style conditions, span the full orthogonal group. Orthogonal finetuning preserves the singular value spectrum of pretrained weights, offering spectral stability advantages for training and generalization. Viewing BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}\bm{x}$ connects it to metric learning and explains why orthogonal reorientations (rather than rescaling) can be a powerful, well-regularized form of adaptation. Finally, the butterfly structure embeds a meaningful inductive bias: it can recover many classical fast transforms and thus favors efficient, distributed mixing patterns that often match useful transformations in representation learning. Together, these properties explain why BOFT can deliver strong parameter-efficiency without sacrificing the stability and generalization benefits of orthogonal finetuning.

Practical Usage and Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Experimental setup: datasets, tasks, and evaluation metrics
The empirical evaluation of BOFT in the paper spans three broad families of foundation models — large language models (LLMs), vision transformers (ViTs), and text-to-image diffusion models — and uses standard downstream tasks and metrics appropriate to each family. Although the paper does not attempt to exhaust every possible dataset, it evaluates BOFT on representative tasks from each modality so that comparisons are meaningful for practitioners. For language models the key evaluation measures are perplexity and task accuracy on downstream NLP benchmarks; for vision transformers the evaluations use classification accuracy (or task-specific metrics for recognition/localization tasks); and for generative image models the quality of generated images is quantified by distributional metrics such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and by downstream task-specific performance when applicable.

Across these modalities the common experimental pattern is: start from a pretrained foundation model with frozen base parameters, apply BOFT to insert and train a layer-shared orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ (parameterized via butterfly factorization) on selected linear layers, and finetune only the BOFT parameters while keeping the base weights frozen. The forward pass under BOFT is written as
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\]
with the BOFT parameterization denoted BOFT$(m,b)$ (see the following sections for guidance on picking $m$ and $b$). Evaluation is performed after training by folding the learned orthogonal transform into the pretrained weights and measuring the standard metrics (accuracy, perplexity, FID, etc.) on held-out test sets.

# Baselines: LoRA and OFT variants; adapter and prompt methods where relevant
The primary baselines considered in the paper are reparameterization-based parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods that are widely used in practice. In particular, comparisons are made against LoRA, which uses additive low-rank updates to pretrained weight matrices, and against Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in its original variants: the full dense orthogonal OFT (which uses an unconstrained orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$) and the block-diagonal OFT that enforces a block structure $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ to reduce parameter count. Where relevant, the paper also considers adapter-style and prompt-style PEFT methods because they are common alternatives for certain tasks (especially in language). The comparisons focus on three axes that practitioners care about: (1) final task performance (accuracy, perplexity, FID, etc.), (2) number of trainable parameters and parameter-efficiency, and (3) training dynamics and stability.

A key conceptual baseline property to keep in mind is the parameter-scaling behavior. LoRA typically adds $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ parameters per adapted matrix when rank $r$ is used, block-diagonal OFT adds $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters when block size is $b$, dense OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and BOFT can be tuned to occupy intermediate regimes; in particular a butterfly BOFT with $m=\log d$ and block size $b=2$ uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. These differing parameter footprints are central to the empirical comparisons made in the paper.

# Main results: accuracy, perplexity, FID, and other task metrics vs parameter count
Across the set of experiments reported in the paper, BOFT demonstrates two consistent empirical patterns. First, BOFT attains better parameter-efficiency than the block-diagonal OFT and typically outperforms LoRA at the same or lower parameter budgets. Second, BOFT preserves the training stability advantages of orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and in many cases improves downstream generalization metrics by virtue of its structured inductive bias.

Concretely, when task metrics are plotted against the number of trainable parameters, BOFT curves tend to dominate those of the other PEFT baselines: for a given parameter budget BOFT achieves comparable or higher accuracy (vision tasks), lower perplexity or higher task accuracy (language tasks), and improved generative quality as measured by FID (text-to-image tasks). The paper reports these trends across large vision transformers, large language models and diffusion models, and emphasizes that BOFT's gains are most pronounced in the regime of tight parameter budgets where strong inductive structure is most valuable.

These gains are attributed to BOFT's ability to produce a dense orthogonal transform with far fewer effective parameters than a full dense orthogonal matrix: the dense final transform is synthesized as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components, where the effective number of trainable parameters for BOFT$(m,b)$ is approximately
\[
\text{parameters}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
\]
so that the canonical butterfly configuration ($m=\log d, b=2$) gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. This reduction allows BOFT to explore richer dense transforms than block-diagonal OFT while still respecting tight parameter budgets that make LoRA attractive.

# Ablations: impact of m, b, component depth, and block size
BOFT introduces two central knobs that control the trade-off between expressivity, parameter count, and training cost: the number of butterfly components $m$ (component depth), and the block granularity $b$ inside each butterfly block (block size). The paper performs ablations that vary $m$ and $b$ to characterize their effects.

Intuitively, increasing $m$ (more multiplicative components) expands the set of dense transforms that can be represented by the product; with larger $m$ BOFT can mix information across coordinates more times and approximate denser orthogonal matrices. Decreasing $b$ toward $2$ increases the number of sparse mixing steps per parameter and often yields better parameter-efficiency in terms of approximation power per parameter. As the paper's simulation approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix (for example, a $512\times512$ matrix) shows, configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (many small butterfly factors) achieve lower approximation error for the same total number of trainable parameters than configurations with larger $b$ and smaller $m$ (few larger block components). Put another way: BOFT$(m,b)$ with $(m,b)=(9,2)$ can be more expressive per parameter than BOFT$(1,16)$ even though the latter uses a larger block size.

However, these expressivity benefits come with practical trade-offs. Larger $m$ implies more sequential matrix multiplications during training and thus more runtime overhead. Larger $b$ reduces the number of components required to attain a given expressivity and can make per-step computation simpler, but it also increases per-component parameter counts (and may reduce parameter-efficiency). The paper therefore frames choosing $(m,b)$ as a budget-aware design: if parameter budget is the primary constraint, favor smaller $b$ and increase $m$ until the desired parameter level is reached; if training runtime is a tight constraint, prefer a larger $b$ with fewer components (or revert to BOFT$(1,b)$, which is equivalent to block-diagonal OFT) to reduce sequential computation.

A practical formula the paper provides for getting a dense orthogonal matrix is to set
\[
m \approx \log_2\!\big(\tfrac{2d}{b}\big),
\]
so BOFT$(\log_2(2d/b),\,b)$ yields a (block) butterfly composition that connects all coordinates densely. In the extreme, BOFT$(1,d)$ is full OFT (dense but parameter-heavy), and BOFT$(1,b)$ is the block-diagonal OFT.

# Effect of multiplicative dropout and identity initialization
Two training design elements described in the paper are especially important for practical success: identity initialization and multiplicative dropout.

Identity initialization: BOFT initializes every butterfly component to act like the identity transform at the start of finetuning. Practically this is done by initializing the skew-symmetric parameters (when using the Cayley transform) to zero so every orthogonal factor is the identity. Identity initialization has two advantages: it yields a true zero-initial change from the pretrained model (mirroring LoRA's zero additive initialization), and it stabilizes early training because the model begins from the pretrained function exactly.

Multiplicative dropout: because BOFT modifies the model via multiplicative orthogonal transforms, conventional additive dropout on newly added parameters is not directly applicable. The paper proposes a multiplicative dropout scheme tailored to butterfly components. During training a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks inside each selected component are randomly replaced by identity blocks for each update; this effectively reduces the capacity of the multiplicative transform stochastically and discourages over-reliance on particular components. Empirically, multiplicative dropout plays the same role for BOFT as conventional dropout or rank-limited regularization in additive PEFTs: it reduces overfitting in low-data regimes and improves generalization on held-out tasks. In practice the paper uses identity initialization together with multiplicative dropout as a default recipe for finetuning with BOFT.

# Training stability, convergence behavior, and weight interpolation observations
A central theoretical and empirical advantage of orthogonal finetuning (including BOFT) is the preservation of spectral properties of pretrained weight matrices. If the pretrained linear layer is written by SVD as $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then multiplying on the left by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, so the singular values (and in particular the spectral norm) of $\bm{W}^0$ are preserved. The paper argues, and empirically supports, that this spectral preservation leads to more stable training dynamics and better-behaved optimization trajectories than unconstrained finetuning or some additive reparameterizations: gradients do not amplify unexpectedly through a changing spectral norm, and the preservation of pairwise neuron angles tends to keep pretrained features useful.

Convergence behavior observed in the experiments indicates that BOFT enjoys robust and stable convergence similar to OFT and often superior to LoRA when the same budget is used. In tight budgets BOFT tends to converge to better minima (in terms of generalization metrics) due to its structured inductive bias. The paper also highlights an intriguing behavior called weight interpolation: because BOFT parameterizes $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple orthogonal factors and uses identity initialization, one can interpret finetuning as gradually moving along a low-dimensional manifold from the identity toward a desired dense orthogonal transform. This structure makes the evolution of the effective weight matrix smooth and interpretable: intermediate folded weight matrices can be viewed as controlled interpolations between the pretrained weights and the final finetuned weights. The paper illustrates that this interpolation can be useful for analyzing how much the model has changed during finetuning and for potentially annealing updates.

# Runtime analysis: training overhead and zero-latency inference after folding
BOFT trades a modest amount of training-time computation for large parameter-efficiency gains. Because BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components, each forward pass during finetuning requires composing these factors (or applying them in sequence). Consequently, the per-step training runtime is slightly higher than a single-block OFT that applies one block-diagonal orthogonal transform, and higher than very cheap additive methods that only update a few low-rank matrices. The paper explicitly acknowledges this overhead and reports that it is modest in practice: training steps are slower than block-diagonal OFT but still tractable for large models.

Importantly for deployment, BOFT introduces no extra inference latency after the finetuning phase. The learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weight matrix by a one-time matrix multiplication:
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
and this folded matrix is then used at inference time. Since the folding is a one-off operation done after training, the inference cost and latency are identical to the original pretrained model (zero-latency overhead). This aspect preserves one of the primary advantages of model finetuning over adapter-based approaches that may add runtime layers.

# Practical recommendations for practitioners
From the empirical studies and analysis in the paper, the following practical guidelines emerge:

- Always use identity initialization for the BOFT components (initialize skew-symmetric parameters to zero under the Cayley transform). This produces a true zero-initial change and stabilizes early training.

- Use multiplicative dropout when finetuning on small datasets or when overfitting is a concern. The scheme of randomly replacing a fraction $p_1$ of butterfly components and a fraction $p_2$ of the blocks within them by identity works well in practice.

- Choose $(m,b)$ by trading off parameter budget against training-time constraints. If parameter-efficiency is the dominant concern, prefer small block size (e.g., $b=2$) and increase $m$ up to $m\approx\log_2(2d/b)$ to obtain a dense final transform; this achieves the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter scaling. If training-time overhead is the primary concern, choose larger $b$ and smaller $m$ (including $m=1$, which reduces to block-diagonal OFT) to lower sequential multiply cost.

- Use BOFT in place of block-diagonal OFT when you need a denser, more expressive orthogonal transform but cannot afford $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. When the full power of dense OFT is required and parameter budgets permit, BOFT can also be configured to approximate full orthogonal expressivity by stacking enough components (or by using kaleidoscope-like compositions), but bear in mind the runtime and parameter trade-offs.

- Monitor convergence and, if desired, inspect folded intermediate matrices to understand the degree of interpolation from the identity. The smooth interpolation behavior induced by BOFT can provide insight into how much the model is adapting versus preserving pretrained structure.

# Summary
BOFT is a practical, modality-agnostic finetuning method that produces dense orthogonal transforms with far fewer parameters than naive dense OFT by factorizing the transform into a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. Empirically, BOFT consistently improves parameter-efficiency and often yields better generalization across LLMs, vision transformers, and diffusion models when compared with LoRA, block-diagonal OFT, and other PEFT baselines studied in the paper. The method preserves the desirable spectral and stability properties of orthogonal finetuning, supports identity initialization and multiplicative dropout for robustness, and allows zero-latency inference by folding the learned transform into the pretrained weights after finetuning. Choosing the BOFT hyperparameters $(m,b)$ is a design decision driven by the trade-off between parameter budget, expressivity, and training-time cost; the paper provides both theoretical and empirical guidance to help practitioners make that choice.

Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

# When BOFT is preferable to LoRA or OFT and hybrid strategies

Choosing between BOFT, OFT, and LoRA depends on what you want the finetuning update to preserve, how many trainable parameters you can afford, and whether you prefer additive or multiplicative updates. The paper highlights three distinguishing characteristics of these approaches that guide this choice.

First, OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) formulates finetuning as a left-multiplicative orthogonal transform of the pretrained layer: given a pretrained linear layer $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, OFT replaces the forward pass $\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}$ where $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal. This multiplicative orthogonality preserves pairwise neuron angles and, through SVD, preserves the spectral norm of $\mathbf{W}^0$, properties that the paper links to improved stability and generalization. However, a full unconstrained orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters; OFT reduces that cost with block-diagonal structure at the expense of expressivity and dense coupling across neuron dimensions.

Second, LoRA provides an additive low-rank reparameterization: it updates $\mathbf{W}^0$ by adding a product of low-rank matrices. LoRA is simple, widely used in practice, and typically very parameter-efficient because the rank is kept small. But it does not enforce orthogonality or directly preserve spectral properties of $\mathbf{W}^0$.

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) sits between these two families in an important way. BOFT keeps the multiplicative orthogonal formulation of OFT (thus retaining the spectral- and angle-preserving characteristics) while parameterizing $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly" components. In particular, BOFT with the radix-2 butterfly can represent a dense orthogonal matrix using $m=\log d$ factors of sparse matrices so that the number of effective trainable parameters can be $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ (more generally BOFT($m,b$) uses roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ parameters). Because BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as BOFT($1,b$) and reduces to full OFT when $m=1,b=d$, it offers a smooth trade-off between parameter-efficiency and expressivity. Moreover, the butterfly structure can recover many classic fast transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.), which introduces a structured inductive bias that the paper argues benefits generalization.

Practical guidance inferred from the paper is therefore:

- Use BOFT when you want the orthogonality-preserving benefits of OFT but cannot afford $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and you want a dense transform (i.e., you need dense coupling across neuron dimensions). BOFT gives a parameter-efficient way to obtain a dense orthogonal update.
- Use block-diagonal OFT (BOFT with small $m$) when even greater parameter sparsity is required and you accept independent blockwise transforms.
- Use LoRA when a simple additive low-rank update suffices, especially if prior experience or resource constraints favor additive updates, or if you prefer the conceptual simplicity and widespread tooling that supports low-rank adapters.

Hybrid strategies are a natural next consideration. The paper points out that low-rank and sparse (butterfly) parameterizations are two major structured-matrix families. Conceptually, hybrids could combine additive low-rank updates (LoRA-style) with multiplicative sparse-orthogonal updates (BOFT-style) to capture complementary degrees of freedom: the orthogonal multiplicative factors can preserve spectral structure and provide global rotations, while an additive low-rank term can provide task-specific, localized shifts. Because BOFT preserves the pretrained model at initialization (identity initialization via Cayley), and LoRA usually uses zero initialization, these initializations are compatible and could be jointly optimized without large initial perturbations. The paper frames such combinations as reasonable future directions rather than presenting them as implemented designs, so treat them as hypotheses to investigate experimentally.

# Limitations: training-time overhead and choice of topology

BOFT is not without limitations. The most explicit limitation noted in the paper is the additional training-time overhead introduced by composing the orthogonal transform from multiple factors. Representing $\mathbf{R}$ as a product $\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^b$ requires multiplying multiple sparse matrices in each forward (and backward) pass during training, which is more costly than applying a single dense block-diagonal orthogonal (OFT with $m=1$) or adding a low-rank term (LoRA). The paper describes this runtime overhead as "slightly larger" than OFT; it also emphasizes that this is a training-time cost only because after finetuning the learned orthogonal factors can be composed into the base weights, incurring no inference latency.

Another limitation is that the butterfly is one specific topology among many possible sparse factorizations that satisfy the information-transmission desiderata. The paper explicitly acknowledges that it is unknown whether the butterfly is the most efficient or best-suited topology for all tasks or model architectures. The information transmission framework reframes factorization selection as a graph-design problem subject to orthogonality constraints and two desiderata: (i) dense connectivity from source to receiver nodes, and (ii) minimizing the number of "free" (trainable) edges. Orthogonality imposes additional structural constraints (e.g., nontrivial dependencies among entries and minimal non-trainable patterns). Butterfly graphs are attractive because they achieve dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges and align with many classic transforms, but this does not rule out alternative topologies that could further reduce parameter count, improve numerical behavior, or map more efficiently to specific hardware.

Finally, there are practical engineering questions that are not resolved in the paper: how to choose $(m,b)$ systematically for a given model and task; how multiplicative Dropout behaves across architectures; and what are the best batching and kernel strategies to amortize the cost of repeatedly applying sparse factors. These limitations point directly to future research and engineering work.

# Beyond butterfly: alternative sparse factorizations inspired by network topologies

One of the conceptual contributions of the paper is the information transmission view: representing a dense transform as sequential information exchange across a $d \times (m+1)$ layered grid, where each factor corresponds to edges between adjacent levels. This perspective opens the door to importing ideas from graph and network design. The butterfly topology is a canonical example because of the Cooley–Tukey algorithm and its established $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ connectivity. But the framework suggests several systematic angles for exploration:

- Topology design as a constrained graph search. The goal is to find an $m$-layered bipartite-connection pattern that guarantees each source node can reach every target node (dense reachability) while keeping the number of trainable degrees of freedom low under orthogonality constraints. Different graphs will trade off factor depth ($m$), per-factor sparsity, and numerical conditioning.

- Permutations and block variants. The paper generalizes from scalar-valued butterfly factors to block-butterfly components $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)$ where block size $b$ increases the per-block expressivity. Exploring different blockings and permutations can interpolate between block-diagonal OFT and full BOFT, and different permutation schedules can yield diverse structured transforms.

- Borrowing from network topologies studied in computer architecture and distributed systems. The information-transmission framework explicitly connects to network topologies (butterfly networks, hypercube, de Bruijn graphs, Clos networks, etc.). While the paper does not evaluate these alternatives, it proposes that network-theoretic measures—diameter, bisection bandwidth, routing complexity—translate to properties relevant for matrix factorization: depth ($m$), number of non-zero per factor, and the ability to form full-rank orthogonal blocks. Studying orthogonality-compatible variants of these graphs is a promising direction.

- Theoretical limits and lower bounds. What is the minimum number of trainable degrees of freedom (or trainable edges) needed to represent all orthogonal matrices, or to approximate them to a given tolerance, when constrained to $m$ factors? The paper points to kaleidoscope hierarchies showing that compositions of butterfly-like matrices can represent the full orthogonal group, but also notes that greater expressivity is not always desirable. Formalizing trade-offs between expressivity, parameter count, and generalization remains an open theoretical question.

In short, the butterfly is a strong first choice, but the information transmission view provides a principled language and desiderata for exploring alternatives both theoretically and empirically.

# Combining low-rank and sparse structures; compatibility with quantization and pruning

The paper frames low-rank and sparse (butterfly) parameterizations as two major, complementary families of structured matrices for parameter-efficient finetuning. This immediately suggests hybrid approaches and interactions with standard model compression techniques.

Combining multiplicative sparse-orthogonal updates (BOFT) with additive low-rank adapters (LoRA) is natural: the multiplicative orthogonal factors can provide a global rotation that preserves spectral structure, while the additive low-rank term can provide task-local adjustments. Because BOFT initializes multiplicative factors to identity (via Cayley transform) and LoRA typically initializes additive factors to zero, both mechanisms can be co-initialized without disturbing the pretrained model and jointly optimized. The paper positions this as an avenue for future work rather than a presented experiment, but it is a straightforward hybrid to implement and evaluate.

Regarding quantization and pruning, the paper provides a practical observation that after finetuning the orthogonal factors can be multiplied into $\mathbf{W}^0$ so that the final deployed model has no extra inference-time overhead. That composition step makes BOFT amenable to any post-hoc compression pipeline that operates on final dense weights: one can multiply the learned orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ into the base weights and then apply standard quantization or pruning pipelines to the resulting dense matrix. Moreover, the sparse factorization used during training could itself be amenable to structured pruning (e.g., dropping entire blocks in a butterfly component) or to low-bit quantization of individual factors to compress the finetuning checkpoint. The paper introduces multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces entire butterfly blocks with identity during training; that mechanism is already a step toward structured sparsification and suggests pruning-responsive factor shapes. Systematic empirical studies are needed to quantify how well standard quantization and pruning interact with BOFT compared to LoRA or OFT.

# Scalability, hardware-aware design, and deployment considerations

BOFT occupies an interesting deployment point: it increases training-time compute modestly but does not add inference latency once the learned orthogonal matrix is composed into the pretrained weights. This separation between training overhead and inference cost suggests several practical design principles.

First, choose $(m,b)$ with both statistical and computational constraints in mind. The parameter count of BOFT($m,b$) is roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, so increasing $m$ (more factors) or $b$ (larger blocks) increases trainable parameters; but $m$ and $b$ also affect the per-step computational cost because the forward/backward pass must apply $m$ sparse-but-nontrivial factors. For a fixed parameter budget, different $(m,b)$ pairs imply different sparsity and factorization depth. Practitioners should trade off parameter budget, per-step runtime, and ease of parallelization.

Second, hardware mapping matters. Sparse matrix products with structured sparsity (butterfly patterns) can be implemented efficiently if kernels exploit repeated small-block rotations or fixed permutation patterns. The paper notes that the butterfly components are permutations of $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks and block-wise variants can be permuted into $2b\times 2b$ rotations; this regularity is an opportunity for optimized kernels that amortize the cost of many small orthogonal multiplications. Conversely, naive implementations that materialize many small sparse operations may be suboptimal on accelerators unless fused kernels or careful batching are used.

Third, deployment benefits from final composition: once the finetuning converges the set of orthogonal factors can be multiplied into $\mathbf{W}^0$ yielding a single dense matrix for each layer, with no extra parameters and no extra inference-time ops. That final composed matrix is compatible with all standard inference-time optimizations (quantization, pruning, operator fusion). The practical pipeline is therefore: train BOFT multipliers, compose them into pretrained weights, optionally compress the resulting dense weights for inference.

Finally, distributed-training considerations matter. The extra per-step matrix multiplies in BOFT are local to a layer and parallelizable across devices in common model parallel or data parallel setups, but efficient distributed implementations need to consider communication patterns for models with sharded weights. The paper flags runtime overhead as an open problem; designing hardware- and parallelism-aware BOFT kernels is an important engineering direction.

# Open questions and suggested projects for further study

The paper presents BOFT and the information transmission framework as a foundation for many research directions. Below are concrete open questions and project ideas grounded in the paper’s analysis.

1. Topology search and theory
   - Project: Formulate and solve (empirically or theoretically) the topology-design problem under orthogonality constraints: given a budget of trainable edges and a factor depth $m$, find the connection pattern that minimizes approximation error on random orthogonal targets or maximizes downstream finetuning performance.
   - Question: What are lower bounds on the number of trainable degrees of freedom needed to approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices to a given error, when restricted to $m$-factor sparse factorizations?

2. Hybrid parameterizations
   - Project: Implement and evaluate hybrids that combine BOFT (multiplicative, block-butterfly factors) with LoRA-style additive low-rank adapters across a diverse set of tasks (vision, language, diffusion). Measure sample efficiency, generalization, and parameter-efficiency trade-offs.
   - Question: Does the complementary nature of multiplicative orthogonal and additive low-rank updates yield consistent empirical gains across model families?

3. Training runtime optimization
   - Project: Develop fused CUDA/accelerator kernels that apply a sequence of small orthogonal block multiplications and permutations efficiently, and benchmark training-time cost relative to OFT and LoRA. Evaluate the wall-clock training cost vs. parameter-efficiency trade-off.
   - Question: How much can factor-application fusion and batching reduce the "slight" training overhead reported in the paper?

4. Compression and deployment pipelines
   - Project: Evaluate post-composition quantization and pruning pipelines on BOFT-composed dense weights. Compare final task performance and compression ratios to LoRA and OFT baselines.
   - Question: Are BOFT-composed weights especially amenable or resistant to low-bit quantization or structured pruning?

5. Regularization strategies and multiplicative Dropout
   - Project: Systematically study multiplicative Dropout (the paper’s proposal to randomly replace butterfly blocks with identity) across tasks and $(m,b)$ settings. Compare to standard dropout and structured sparsification.
   - Question: How does multiplicative Dropout affect overfitting, convergence speed, and final generalization?

6. Broader empirical validation
   - Project: Expand empirical evaluations to more model families (larger LLMs, other diffusion architectures, multimodal transformers) and a wider set of downstream tasks to delineate scenarios in which BOFT's inductive bias is most beneficial.
   - Question: For which tasks or data regimes does the butterfly inductive bias outperform block-diagonal OFT and LoRA decisively?

7. Kaleidoscope and expressivity-control
   - Project: Empirically evaluate the generalized kaleidoscope-style compositions (theorem-level constructions that can represent the full orthogonal group) but constrain $l,m_1,m_2,b_1,b_2$ to study the expressivity/overfitting trade-off.
   - Question: Is there a principled way to choose compositional depth to match dataset size to avoid overfitting while retaining sufficient expressivity?

Each of these projects ties back to the core contributions and observations of the paper: the information transmission framing, the butterfly factorization for parameter efficiency, orthogonality-preserving multiplicative updates, and the practical tension between expressivity and regularity in finetuning.

Concluding remark

BOFT reframes orthogonal finetuning as a structured information-transmission problem and demonstrates that we can retain orthogonality-preserving multiplicative updates with dramatically fewer parameters by choosing an appropriate sparse factorization. The trade-offs are clear: BOFT reduces parameters and introduces beneficial inductive bias at the cost of modest additional training computation and an open choice of sparse topology. The information transmission framework that underlies BOFT is the key conceptual tool for extending these ideas: it suggests many concrete follow-ups—both theoretical and applied—that can further improve parameter efficiency, hardware efficiency, and generalization for finetuning very large foundation models.
