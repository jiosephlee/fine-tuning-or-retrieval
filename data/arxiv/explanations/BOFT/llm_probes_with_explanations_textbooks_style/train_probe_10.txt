Spectral Norm Preservation in Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapting pretrained models that emphasizes preserving the relational structure among neurons during updates. Given a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a trainable orthogonal matrix applied multiplicatively on the left. The forward pass changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ for input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and output $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

The motivation for orthogonal updates is to preserve pairwise angles between neurons. Since an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products and norms, applying the same orthogonal matrix to all neurons in a layer keeps pairwise angles invariant, thereby maintaining relational information acquired during pretraining. To enforce orthogonality during training, OFT uses a Cayley parameterization,
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization maintains orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout optimization and allows identity initialization by setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$.

To improve parameter efficiency, the original OFT adopts a block-diagonal structure, parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \ldots, \bm{R}_r)$ with $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br = d$. This reduces the number of trainable parameters but enforces a fixed sparsity pattern and decouples transformations across blocks, which can constrain expressivity and introduce inductive biases that do not align with the semantics of neuron dimensions.

# From OFT to BOFT: Sparse Orthogonal Factorization

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) generalizes OFT by composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly structure. This design is motivated by an information transmission view: generating a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ through a factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ can be interpreted as routing information across a layered graph. A dense $\bm{R}$ corresponds to ensuring that every source node in the first layer can reach every target node in the last layer via the edges encoded by the sparsity patterns of the factors $\bm{B}_i$. The goal is to achieve dense end-to-end connectivity with as few trainable entries as possible while respecting the orthogonality constraint.

The butterfly structure, originating from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform, provides precisely such an efficient connectivity pattern. A $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is built recursively as a product of “butterfly components” $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, each of which is block-diagonal with identical $k \times k$ “butterfly factors.” Concretely, a butterfly factor of size $k$ takes the form
$\bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}$,
where $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$.
By composing $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such sparse components, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros, the butterfly structure yields a dense matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters.

BOFT ensures each butterfly component is orthogonal. The nonzero patterns across components are permutations of a base pattern with $2 \times 2$ blocks, so each block can be parameterized as a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, or more generally as a $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal block when using a block butterfly with block size $b$. Denoting the orthogonal components as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, BOFT constructs
$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$,
which is orthogonal by construction. This yields a tunable family BOFT($m,b$) that subsumes prior methods: BOFT($1,b$) recovers block-diagonal OFT, BOFT($1,d$) recovers unconstrained OFT with a full orthogonal matrix, and BOFT($\log \tfrac{2d}{b}, b$) produces a dense orthogonal matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters when $b=2$.

Identity initialization is preserved: each component starts as the identity by setting the skew-symmetric parameters in its Cayley transform to zero. BOFT also introduces a multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces selected orthogonal sub-blocks with identities, which matches the multiplicative nature of the update.

# An SVD View: Why Orthogonal Updates Preserve Spectral Norm

A central stability property of orthogonal finetuning arises from how it interacts with the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix. Let $\bm{W}^0$ have SVD
$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$,
where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with nonnegative singular values. Both OFT and BOFT produce finetuned weights of the form
$\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$,
with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal. Left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values: the factors $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ remain orthogonal, so the singular values of $\bm{W}$ equal those of $\bm{W}^0$. In particular, the largest singular value, i.e., the spectral norm $\|\bm{W}\|_2$, is exactly preserved.

This exact preservation of the spectral norm has been highlighted as beneficial for training stability and generalization. Since the spectral norm controls the Lipschitz behavior of linear mappings, maintaining it avoids inflating the scale of activations in ways that can destabilize optimization. The orthogonal constraint therefore serves as a principled regularizer: it allows expressive rotations of the representation space while keeping the singular spectrum, and especially the spectral norm, unchanged.

# What Fails Without Orthogonality

The orthogonal structure in BOFT is not incidental; it is the mechanism that guarantees the spectral properties discussed above. The finetuned weight matrix inherits the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$ because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. If, instead, BOFT’s sparse factors were not constrained to be orthogonal, their product need not be orthogonal, and the SVD-based invariance would no longer apply. In that case, multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ on the left by a non-orthogonal matrix can alter the singular values, including the largest one. The specific consequence is the loss of spectral norm preservation.

The implications for finetuning are direct. The paper emphasizes that preserving the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ “greatly benefit[s] training stability and generalization,” precisely because orthogonal multiplication does not affect the largest singular value. If the orthogonality constraint is relaxed in BOFT’s factors, this guarantee disappears, removing the stability benefit tied to the SVD argument. Thus, orthogonality is not only a structural prior for preserving neuron angles; it is also the algebraic condition that secures spectral norm preservation.

# Intuition Through Information Transmission and Inductive Bias

The information transmission view sheds light on why the butterfly parameterization can be both parameter-efficient and expressive. A dense linear transform corresponds to every source dimension influencing every target dimension. Achieving this with a single dense orthogonal matrix costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. The butterfly structure attains dense end-to-end connectivity by composing sparse orthogonal components arranged so that information is progressively mixed across dimensions, leading to a dense overall transform using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters.

This factorized, structured construction induces an inductive bias. The butterfly pattern is shared by many classic fast linear transforms, and BOFT leverages this structure to constrain the hypothesis class to a well-organized subset of the orthogonal group. The result is a smoother interpolation between the full orthogonal group (more expressive but less regular) and the identity (maximally regular but inexpressive). Orthogonality enforces regularity in the singular spectrum, while the butterfly arrangement ensures sufficient mixing to achieve dense transformations and avoid the rigid independence of block-diagonal updates.

# Expressivity–Regularity Trade-off and the Role of Orthogonality

Expressivity matters for adaptation: the finetuning parameterization must be capable of representing useful transformations of the pretrained features. BOFT is provably more expressive than block-diagonal OFT under the same block size, and simulations show that, for a fixed parameter budget, deeper butterfly factorizations (larger $m$ with smaller $b$) can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices more accurately than shallow block-diagonal updates.

At the same time, the paper underscores that maximal expressivity is not a panacea. Full finetuning, which is universally expressive, often generalizes worse than more structured approaches. BOFT’s strength lies in navigating this trade-off: by constraining the update to orthogonal transformations built from butterfly components, it preserves spectral properties and relational neuron geometry while allowing rich, global mixing. The orthogonality of the factors is essential in maintaining spectral norm preservation; removing it would break the key regularity that underpins BOFT’s stability advantages.

# Practical Implications within BOFT Design

BOFT($m,b$) builds the orthogonal update $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components with block size $b$, initialized to the identity for a faithful starting point at the pretrained model. The multiplicative Dropout is tailored to this setting by randomly replacing selected orthogonal sub-blocks with identities, matching the multiplicative nature of the reparameterization.

Crucially, all components are kept orthogonal throughout training via local parameterizations, such as the Cayley transform on the $2 \times 2$ or $2b \times 2b$ sub-blocks. This design choice preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ exactly after left multiplication, which the paper connects to improved training stability and generalization. If the sparse factors were not orthogonal, this SVD-based guarantee would no longer hold, and the spectral norm could change during finetuning. BOFT’s combination of orthogonality and butterfly structure therefore simultaneously secures spectral norm preservation and parameter-efficient expressivity, delivering a stable and effective finetuning mechanism.
