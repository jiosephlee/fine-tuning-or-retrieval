Chapter Title: Preserving Neuronal Relations in Orthogonal Finetuning

# The Motivation: Preserving Relational Information in Finetuning

Adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks is effective only if their pretrained knowledge is preserved during finetuning. A common approach to preserve this knowledge is to keep the finetuned model close to the pretrained one, for example by using small learning rates. While this heuristic can help, it lacks a structural understanding of the model’s internal organization. A more principled viewpoint is to preserve the relational information in the model’s weight matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer. This relational information captures how neurons relate to each other geometrically, and serves as a compact proxy for the semantic structure learned during pretraining.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is built on this principle. Instead of making arbitrary local updates to weights, OFT applies the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer. Because orthogonal matrices preserve angles between vectors, OFT provably keeps the pairwise angles between neurons unchanged throughout finetuning. This preserves the relational geometry of neurons that encodes pretrained knowledge, while still allowing the model to adapt. The result is a finetuning mechanism that is simple, generalizable, and stable.

# Orthogonal Transformations and Angle Preservation

To understand why OFT preserves pairwise angles, consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each neuron corresponds to a column vector $\bm{w}^0_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of $\bm{W}^0$. OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight matrix as
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\]
where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix. In the forward pass, the computation changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
with input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and output $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Left-multiplication by $\bm{R}$ transforms each neuron $\bm{w}^0_i$ into $\bm{w}_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves inner products and angles between vectors. Therefore, for any two neurons $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$, the angle between $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ equals the angle between $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$. This maintains the pairwise angular relationships among neurons across the entire layer, ensuring that the structural geometry of pretrained features is intact. OFT thus performs a coordinated transformation that preserves the relational property most closely tied to pretrained semantics: the pairwise angles among neurons.

This coordinated design stands in contrast to unstructured parameter updates. When weights are changed independently, the angles and inner products among neuron vectors can drift in uncontrolled ways, potentially destroying useful structure obtained during pretraining. OFT avoids this by constraining all neuron updates to be the same orthogonal transformation.

# Implementing OFT: Cayley Parameterization and Identity Initialization

OFT must maintain orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout training. A practical way to enforce this is via Cayley parameterization. Specifically, OFT parameterizes $\bm{R}$ by
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization guarantees that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal for all $\bm{Q}$ in the skew-symmetric set. It allows optimization over $\bm{Q}$ while inducing an orthogonal update in the original parameter space.

To align with common finetuning practice, OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix. Under Cayley parameterization, this corresponds to initializing $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix. With identity initialization, the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights, and the optimization progressively learns an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ that adapts the layer while preserving pairwise angles.

# Why Pairwise Angles Encapsulate Pretrained Knowledge

Pairwise angles between neuron vectors capture relational geometry: the way neurons are oriented with respect to each other in the weight space. This geometry reflects how a layer organizes features learned during pretraining. If two neurons have a small angle between them, they encode closely related feature directions; if their angle is large, they encode distinct or complementary features. By preserving angles, OFT retains the relational structure that underpins the layer’s semantic organization.

Because OFT updates all neurons via the same orthogonal transformation, it preserves the adjacency structure of neurons on the unit sphere as the layer adapts to new data. The downstream task can therefore harness the pretrained geometry while allowing global rotations or reflections that improve task alignment. This enables strong generalization and stable optimization, as the model avoids disruptive changes to the latent structure of features.

In addition, orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the weight matrix, which helps maintain training stability. If $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the singular value decomposition with orthogonal $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ and diagonal singular value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$, then OFT produces $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which does not change the largest singular value. Preserving the spectral norm prevents uncontrolled growth in layer Lipschitz constants, contributing to stable training and improved generalization.

# The Block-Diagonal Trade-off: Parameter Efficiency Versus Expressivity

While OFT’s full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles and offers strong generalization, its parameter count can be large for high-dimensional layers. To reduce parameters, the original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]
with each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ orthogonal and $br = d$. This reduces the number of trainable parameters by restricting $\bm{R}$ to operate independently on $r$ disjoint blocks of neuron dimensions.

However, this parameter efficiency imposes an inductive bias that may be undesirable. It assumes that neuron dimensions can be partitioned by index into independent groups, each transformed separately. This reduces expressivity and prevents approximation of classic dense linear transforms. Moreover, the sparsity pattern is fixed and independent across blocks, and its optimal choice is unclear.

The underlying tension here is between expressivity and regularity. Full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameterization is expressive but expensive; block-diagonal parameterization is efficient but potentially too restrictive. OFT addresses the relational preservation goal with both designs, but the block-diagonal variant may sacrifice flexibility in how neuron relations can be rotated or mixed.

# Dense Orthogonal Updates via Sparse Factorization

A different route to parameter efficiency is to compose a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse orthogonal matrices. This reduces trainable parameters by trading space for computation: multiple sparse multiplications at training time build a dense effect overall. This idea is best understood through an information transmission viewpoint.

Consider factorizing $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1,
\]
where each $\bm{B}_i$ is sparse and orthogonal. This composition can be viewed as information flowing across a $d \times (m+1)$ grid, with directed edges between adjacent levels defined by the non-zero structure of each $\bm{B}_i$. For $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every source node in the first level must reach every receiver node in the last level via paths through the sequence of sparse factors. Orthogonality imposes constraints: each level must implement a bijection, requiring at least $d$ edges and enforcing dependencies among edges due to orthogonality.

Under this view, the goal is to design sparse orthogonal factors that achieve dense connectivity with as few trainable parameters as possible. A single dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ non-zeros, but clever sparse factorizations can achieve dense reachability with far fewer edges. This motivates butterfly structures.

# Butterfly Structures and BOFT: Parameter-Efficient Angle Preservation

Butterfly structures, originating from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, are efficient topologies for global information exchange. They enable dense connectivity between $d$ sources and $d$ receivers with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ non-zero entries across a product of sparse factors. This makes them ideal for parameter-efficient construction of dense orthogonal matrices.

A butterfly factor of size $k$ takes the form
\[
\bm{B}^F(k) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k},
\]
where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. With $d = 2^N$, a butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is built recursively as
\[
\bm{B}(d) 
= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\,\cdots\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]
where $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal “butterfly component” of size $d \times d$ with block size $k$:
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k) = \text{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k), \ldots, \bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big).
\]
By ensuring each butterfly component is orthogonal, the product becomes an orthogonal matrix. Orthogonality of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ can be enforced via Cayley transform (or $2 \times 2$ rotations) for each block; higher $k$ components are permutations of this non-zero pattern and can be similarly parameterized.

This leads to Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which replaces the single orthogonal $\bm{R}$ in OFT with a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ generalizes $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ by replacing scalar entries with $b \times b$ blocks, and each component is orthogonal:
\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} \big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top = \bm{I}_d.
\]
The forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
BOFT achieves dense orthogonal transformations with parameter counts as low as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ when using butterfly matrices (for example $m = \log d, b = 2$), while still preserving pairwise angles due to orthogonality. Identity initialization applies straightforwardly by initializing all components to identity.

# Expressivity, Regularity, and Generalization

Compared to the block-diagonal OFT, BOFT uses a structured sparse factorization that more smoothly interpolates between the identity and full orthogonal group. This makes it possible to find smaller, highly structured hypothesis classes tailored to downstream tasks. The butterfly structure can recover many classic fast linear transforms when combined with permutations, and BOFT is consequently more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size.

Formally, BOFT yields better parameter efficiency than OFT while achieving lower approximation error to dense orthogonals for a given parameter budget. A generalization of BOFT that multiplies alternating butterfly matrices can represent the entire orthogonal group when sufficiently deep, though greater expressivity is not necessarily ideal for finetuning. The central trade-off is between expressivity and regularity. OFT and BOFT both preserve pairwise angles, maintaining relational geometry; BOFT additionally imposes structured inductive biases via butterfly sparsity that can benefit generalization and prevent overfitting.

Spectral properties further support stability: multiplying an orthogonal matrix on the left leaves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ unchanged. This preservation has been empirically linked to training stability and generalization and is a direct consequence of using orthogonal transformations.

# Practical Considerations: Multiplicative Dropout and Runtime

Because BOFT updates weights multiplicatively via orthogonal matrices, standard additive Dropout does not directly apply. BOFT introduces a multiplicative Dropout that randomly selects a subset of butterfly components and a subset of blocks within each component, replacing them with identity matrices during training. This regularizes the multiplicative update while preserving orthogonality and the pairwise angle property.

A practical limitation of BOFT is the runtime overhead introduced by multiplying multiple orthogonal components during training. However, this is a finetuning-time cost: after training, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained weights, incurring no additional inference latency. The information transmission framework also suggests opportunities to explore alternative sparse factorizations and network-inspired topologies to further improve efficiency.

# OFT as Learning Bilinear Similarity

OFT and BOFT can be viewed as learning a bilinear similarity. For the $i$-th neuron, the activation with BOFT becomes
\[
\bm{w}^0_i \bm{R} \bm{x},
\]
which is a bilinear form in $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{x}$ modulated by the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. The orthogonality constraint provides strong regularity, tying the learned bilinear similarity to geometric preservation. This perspective emphasizes that OFT adapts task-specific alignment while keeping the fundamental relational geometry—namely the pairwise angles among neurons—intact.

# Summary: Pairwise Angles as the Anchor of Pretrained Knowledge

Orthogonal Finetuning is a principled approach to model adaptation that preserves pretrained knowledge by maintaining the pairwise angles among neurons in each layer. By applying a shared orthogonal transformation to all neuron vectors, OFT preserves the relational geometry that organizes pretrained features, contributing to strong generalization and stable training. While full orthogonal parameterization can be expensive, sparse factorization via butterfly structures, as in BOFT, enables dense orthogonal updates with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. BOFT inherits OFT’s angle-preserving property, adds structured inductive biases, and achieves superior parameter efficiency without sacrificing stability, making the preservation of pairwise angles a practical and powerful anchor for finetuning large models.
