Orthogonality and Training Stability in Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

# Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

Large foundation models have demonstrated remarkable generalization, but adapting them to downstream tasks without retraining from scratch demands parameter-efficient finetuning. Several paradigms exist, including model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Model finetuning stands out as a simple and powerful approach that introduces no inference latency. Among reparameterization-based finetuning methods, the central distinction lies in whether the update is additive or multiplicative with respect to the pretrained weights. Low-rank additive methods such as LoRA update a pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of low-rank matrices. Orthogonal finetuning (OFT), in contrast, applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation that provably preserves geometric structure in the pretrained weights.

The core principle behind finetuning is to keep the finetuned model close to the pretrained model under measurements that reflect meaningful structure. Heuristics like a small learning rate are commonly used to limit the deviation in parameter space, but a more principled view recognizes that weight matrices have relational information that should be preserved. Specifically, preserving pairwise angles between neurons during finetuning protects semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. This insight motivates OFT, where neurons in the same layer are transformed by a shared orthogonal matrix. Empirically, OFT achieves stronger generalization and consistently more stable training than LoRA in the setting where it has been studied, aligning with its structured preservation of pretrained relational geometry.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Multiplicative Structure and Angle Preservation

Orthogonal finetuning reparameterizes the finetuned weights of a pretrained linear layer by left-multiplying them with a learnable orthogonal matrix. For a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the forward pass changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is orthogonal. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is enforced via a stable parameterization such as the Cayley transform. In this parameterization, one introduces a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ and sets
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
which ensures $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix so that finetuning begins exactly from the pretrained model, mirroring the zero-initialization idea used in additive methods while respecting the multiplicative orthogonal reparameterization.

The motivation for orthogonality is rooted in preserving pairwise angles between neurons, i.e., between columns of $\bm{W}^0$. When an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ acts on a set of vectors, their pairwise angles remain unchanged. This is because orthogonal transformations are isometries of Euclidean space: they preserve inner products and norms. Consequently, transforming the neuron basis with a shared $\bm{R}$ maintains the relational geometry in the layer, keeping the semantics encoded by pretraining intact while allowing the model to adapt to new tasks. This geometric preservation is a structural regularity that tempers the expressiveness of the finetuning transformation and can improve training stability.

# Why Orthogonality Aids Training Stability

Training stability concerns how reliably the optimization proceeds without divergence or undesirable oscillations. Orthogonality helps stability by preserving key spectral properties of the pretrained weights. Consider the singular value decomposition of the pretrained matrix:
$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$,
where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with nonnegative singular values. OFT and its butterfly-based generalization (BOFT) multiply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ on the left, yielding the finetuned weights
$\bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R} \bm{U}) \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$.
Because $\bm{R} \bm{U}$ is orthogonal when both $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{U}$ are orthogonal, the singular values in $\bm{\Sigma}$ are unchanged. In particular, the largest singular value, i.e., the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, is preserved under orthogonal left multiplication.

Preserving the spectral norm of the pretrained weights stabilizes training and benefits generalization. Orthogonal finetuning keeps the operator norm of the layer constant throughout the finetuning process, preventing sudden changes in the scale of activations and gradients that can destabilize optimization. In contrast, additive updates can alter singular values directly, potentially increasing the spectral norm and introducing instability. The multiplicative orthogonal constraint functions as a strong regularity: while it restricts the hypothesis class, it preserves a key property of the pretrained model that governs the magnitude of transformations learned during finetuning, thus encouraging more stable optimization dynamics.

Beyond spectral properties, orthogonality preserves pairwise angles among neuron vectors, meaning inner products and norms are maintained under the shared transformation. This geometric preservation complements spectral stability: together they maintain the relational structure and scale of the pretrained representation. As a result, the optimization does not need to relearn the geometry while adapting to the downstream task, which contributes to more stable and efficient training.

# Multiplicative Orthogonal Parameterization via Cayley Transform

A practical challenge in orthogonal finetuning is maintaining orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ during optimization. The Cayley transform enables a differentiable parameterization of orthogonal matrices by introducing a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ and defining
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
Skew-symmetric matrices have zero diagonals and off-diagonal entries that are antisymmetric, reducing the number of degrees of freedom compared to an unconstrained square matrix. Initializing $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning, ensuring there is no deviation from the pretrained model before learning begins.

This multiplicative parameterization couples all neurons in a layer through the same orthogonal $\bm{R}$. The shared transformation preserves inner products and norms, acts as an isometry, and induces a bilinear similarity in the finetuned forward pass. Specifically, for the $i$-th neuron $\bm{w}_i^0$ (a column of $\bm{W}^0$), the model learns the bilinear similarity $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$ subject to the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$. This construction connects finetuning to learning a structured bilinear form with strong regularity, reflecting a disciplined trade-off between expressivity and stability.

# Spectral Norm Preservation and Its Implications

The spectral norm $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$ is the largest singular value of $\bm{W}^0$. Under orthogonal finetuning, the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ has the same singular values as $\bm{W}^0$, hence the spectral norm is exactly preserved:
$\|\bm{R} \bm{W}^0\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2$.
This invariance follows from the fact that left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values.

Preserving the spectral norm has two complementary effects during finetuning. First, it stabilizes the scale of the layer’s mapping, ensuring that activations and gradients do not undergo abrupt scaling changes as parameters are updated. Second, it constrains the hypothesis space in a way that disfavors overfitting, as the layer cannot arbitrarily amplify signals beyond the scale set by pretraining. In the empirical settings studied, this preservation contributes to more stable training compared to additive low-rank updates, which can modify the singular values and thus the spectral norm.

The preservation of spectral structure is aligned with the broader motivation of OFT: retaining the relational and geometric properties learned during pretraining. Together with pairwise angle preservation, spectral norm invariance forms a coherent stability mechanism rooted in orthogonality.

# Comparing Orthogonal Finetuning and Low-Rank Additive Updates

Low-rank additive methods such as LoRA update weights as $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank factors. This additive update changes the singular values of the layer in a manner that is not constrained to preserve the spectral norm. While low-rank structure improves parameter efficiency and achieves strong performance on many tasks, the lack of orthogonality means the updates can alter both geometry and scale of the pretrained representation. In contrast, OFT applies a shared, multiplicative orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, preserving pairwise angles among neurons and the spectral norm exactly.

Empirically, OFT demonstrates stronger generalization and more stable training than LoRA in the application domain where it has been investigated. The authors attribute this improved stability to the orthogonality of the finetuning transformation, which enforces geometric preservation and spectral norm invariance. This structured regularity guides the optimization within a principled subset of transformations that maintain the pretrained model’s key properties while adapting to new tasks.

# BOFT: Parameter-Efficient Orthogonality with Butterfly Factorization

While full dense orthogonal matrices can be parameter-expensive, OFT can reduce parameters using block-diagonal structures. However, such sparsity introduces fixed inductive biases, reduces expressiveness, and cannot approximate classic dense linear transforms. To overcome these limitations while maintaining orthogonality and stability, the paper proposes Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices structured by the butterfly topology.

BOFT factorizes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each parameterized in a way that ensures orthogonality, often by building upon $2 \times 2$ orthogonal blocks via the Cayley transform and block-wise permutations. With $d = 2^N$ and suitable choices of $m$ and block size $b$, BOFT yields a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, significantly fewer than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters in a fully dense orthogonal matrix. When $m = 1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT; when $b = d$, it recovers the original full OFT. Thus, BOFT generalizes OFT within a unified orthogonal finetuning framework.

Crucially, BOFT retains the stability advantages of orthogonal finetuning. Because $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal as a product of orthogonal butterfly components, BOFT preserves pairwise angles and the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ exactly, just like OFT. The parameter-efficient factorization does not compromise the core stability mechanism: orthogonality. In addition, BOFT introduces structured inductive bias through butterfly sparsity that can improve generalization by aligning with patterns found in many classic linear transforms, while providing a smoother interpolation between expressivity and regularity than block-diagonal structures.

# Inductive Bias, Expressivity, and the Stability–Regularity Trade-off

Finetuning requires a balance between expressivity—being able to represent the transformations needed for the downstream task—and regularity—preventing overfitting and preserving pretrained structure. Orthogonality imposes a strong regularity by constraining the finetuning transformation to isometries, which preserve inner products, norms, and the spectral norm. This regularity contributes to stability. BOFT refines this balance by selecting a structured subset of the orthogonal group via butterfly factorization. The structure enables efficient information transmission across dimensions and can recover many classic fast transforms through permutations and composition, enhancing expressivity relative to block-diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget.

The paper formalizes an expressivity advantage: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Moreover, by composing multiple butterfly matrices in a kaleidoscope hierarchy, one can represent the entire orthogonal group in principle. However, the authors emphasize that greater expressivity is not always desirable for finetuning; full finetuning, despite universal expressiveness, often underperforms due to overfitting and instability. Orthogonal finetuning, and BOFT in particular, target a smaller hypothesis class that maintains stability while enabling effective adaptation, thereby striking a favorable trade-off between expressivity and regularity.

# Practical Considerations: Initialization and Regularization

Orthogonal finetuning begins with identity initialization of $\bm{R}$ to match the pretrained model and avoid immediate deviation. This is achieved by initializing the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ to zeros in the Cayley transform. In the BOFT setting, all butterfly components are likewise initialized to identity.

Regularization in the multiplicative orthogonal setting requires care. Conventional Dropout is not directly applicable to multiplicative updates. To address this, the paper introduces a multiplicative Dropout tailored to BOFT: it randomly selects a proportion of butterfly components and a proportion of diagonal blocks within each component, and replaces those selected blocks by identity matrices during training. This strategy preserves orthogonality at the block level while injecting stochasticity that can help prevent overfitting, all without disrupting the multiplicative structure that underlies stability.

# Concluding Perspective on Orthogonality and Stability

Orthogonal finetuning achieves improved training stability compared to low-rank additive methods by enforcing orthogonality in the finetuning transformation. This enforcement preserves pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, maintaining both geometric structure and scale. The preservation of these properties stabilizes optimization and supports generalization.

BOFT extends these stability benefits to a parameter-efficient regime by factorizing dense orthogonal transformations into products of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. It inherits the stability mechanisms of OFT while providing a flexible and expressive structured parameterization that reduces trainable parameters, introduces beneficial inductive bias, and maintains zero inference latency after finetuning. The authors attribute the enhanced stability of OFT—and by extension BOFT—to the orthogonality at the heart of the finetuning design.
