Chapter: Cayley Parameterization and Skew-Symmetric Structure in Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Setup

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled finetuning framework that modifies pretrained weight matrices using orthogonal transformations. The key motivation is to preserve relational information within weight matrices—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons—so that the semantic structure learned during pretraining is not destroyed by adaptation. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT introduces a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and alters the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$,
where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the input and output, respectively. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the transformation rotates the neuron vectors without changing the angles between them, thus preserving their geometric relationships.

While OFT can be applied using a full dense orthogonal matrix, this is parameter-inefficient: a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix naively requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. The original OFT addresses this by using a block-diagonal orthogonal structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters, though this fixed sparsity pattern reduces expressiveness and induces an arbitrary partition of neuron dimensions. A more general approach introduced in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) composes a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ from the product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly structure. This achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while preserving the stability and generalization benefits of orthogonal updates.

Across both OFT and BOFT, a central practical question is how to parameterize $\bm{R}$ so that it remains orthogonal throughout training. The work adopts Cayley parameterization to ensure orthogonality in a simple, differentiable way.

# Cayley Parameterization: Definition and Intuition

To guarantee that $\bm{R}$ stays orthogonal during finetuning, OFT and BOFT employ the Cayley parameterization
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$,
where $\bm{Q}$ is constrained to be skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This reparameterization replaces direct optimization over orthogonal matrices with optimization over skew-symmetric matrices. It has two immediate benefits in the finetuning setting:

First, it enforces orthogonality by construction. If $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric and $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ is invertible, then the resulting $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, making $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Thus optimization can proceed over $\bm{Q}$ without worrying about violating the orthogonality constraint.

Second, it supports identity initialization. Initializing $\bm{Q}$ to the zero matrix makes $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, so the finetuned forward pass starts exactly at the pretrained model. This mirrors the “zero initialization” used in additive methods and avoids sudden deviations from the pretrained behavior at the beginning of finetuning.

The core property that enables the Cayley parameterization to preserve orthogonality is the skew symmetry of $\bm{Q}$. Because $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, the transpose of $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ is $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} + \bm{Q}$, and the transpose of $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ is $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}$. These relationships are exactly what is needed to show that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal.

# Why Skew Symmetry Guarantees Orthogonality

Let $\bm{Q}$ be skew-symmetric, i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, and define
$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.
We now compute $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$ using only basic matrix identities and the skew symmetry constraint.

Because $(\bm{A}\bm{B})^\top = \bm{B}^\top \bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$, we have
$\bm{R}^\top = \big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big)^\top (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{\top -1} (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top$.
Using $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, we obtain $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} + \bm{Q}$ and $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}$, so
$\bm{R}^\top = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$.

Multiplying, we get
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \big[(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})\big] \big[(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big]$.
Regrouping yields
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} \big[(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})\big] (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.

Observe that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$. Substituting this identity,
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$.

Finally, note that $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2 = (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$. Substituting again and canceling factors gives
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1} \big[(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})\big] (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$.

Thus, under the single structural constraint $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ and invertibility of $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$, the Cayley parameterization produces an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, ensuring that OFT and BOFT preserve the desired geometric properties of the pretrained weights throughout finetuning.

# Identity Initialization and Training Stability

Cayley parameterization directly enables identity initialization by setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$. This gives $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start of training, matching the pretrained model exactly. Such initialization aligns with the broader principle in finetuning of beginning at the pretrained solution and then applying small, structured updates that preserve key properties.

In orthogonal finetuning, one of these properties is the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. Let $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ be the singular value decomposition of $\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with singular values. Both OFT and BOFT update $\bm{W}^0$ by left-multiplication with an orthogonal $\bm{R}$, yielding $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values, the largest singular value (the spectral norm) of $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved. This preservation has been shown to benefit training stability and generalization and is a central motivation for using orthogonal transformations in finetuning.

# Skew Symmetry in BOFT’s Butterfly Components

BOFT composes the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each of which can be permuted into a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are of size $2b \times 2b$. Ensuring each butterfly component is orthogonal reduces to ensuring each $2 \times 2$ (or $2b \times 2b$) block is orthogonal. The paper adopts the same strategy as in OFT: parameterize each block with the Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix. In practice, this means that at the finest level of parameterization, the learnable object is a skew-symmetric block $\bm{Q}_{\text{block}}$ and the corresponding block orthogonal matrix is
$\bm{R}_{\text{block}} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}_{\text{block}})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}_{\text{block}})^{-1}$.
Since skew symmetry is preserved under the block structure and the permutation structure of butterfly components, the product of these orthogonal blocks is itself orthogonal. Therefore, skew symmetry is the structural property that scales from the smallest parameterized unit up to the full $\bm{R}(m, b)$.

Because BOFT composes dense orthogonal matrices from sparse orthogonal factors, identity initialization follows naturally: set all block-level skew-symmetric parameters to zero, so each factor is the identity and their product is also the identity. During training, these parameters remain constrained within the skew-symmetric family, guaranteeing orthogonality of every butterfly component and, consequently, of the full $\bm{R}(m, b)$.

# Broader Context: Orthogonality, Inductive Bias, and Generalization

The Cayley parameterization anchored by skew symmetry is not merely a technical device to maintain constraints; it supports the broader goals of orthogonal finetuning. Orthogonal updates preserve pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral properties of pretrained weights. In BOFT, these updates are further constrained by the butterfly structure, which yields a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This structure introduces an inductive bias that aligns with many classic fast linear transforms and provides a smoother interpolation between highly expressive transformations and the identity. The result is a controlled hypothesis class that can generalize well across diverse adaptation tasks.

Through the lens of bilinear similarity, these methods learn a matrix $\bm{R}$ that defines the similarity $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$ between neuron vectors $\bm{w}_i^0$ and inputs $\bm{x}$ under a strong regularity constraint: $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal. The skew symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ in the Cayley parameterization is the exact property that preserves this regularity during finetuning. It ensures that every update step maintains orthogonality, keeps spectral norms intact, and maintains the geometric relationships that OFT and BOFT are designed to protect.
