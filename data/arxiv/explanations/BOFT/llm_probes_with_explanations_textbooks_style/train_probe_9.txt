Chapter: Spectral Norm Preservation in Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Background: Parameter-Efficient Finetuning and Orthogonal Finetuning

Large foundation models are powerful but expensive to train from scratch, making parameter-efficient finetuning essential for adapting them to downstream tasks. A key idea is to retain the knowledge encoded by pretraining while allowing controlled, task-specific adaptation. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach that achieves this by applying a learned orthogonal transformation to the neurons of a layer, thereby preserving pairwise angles between neurons throughout finetuning. Concretely, for a pretrained linear layer with weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT replaces the forward computation $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ with $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix learned during finetuning and initialized as the identity.

This multiplicative, layer-shared transformation contrasts with additive low-rank approaches such as LoRA, and is motivated by the desire to maintain relational structure in the weights. In particular, orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and angles, which serves as an implicit regularization that maintains pretraining knowledge while enabling adaptation. However, a dense orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, which can be parameter-inefficient. The original OFT addresses this by restricting $\bm{R}$ to a block-diagonal structure, reducing parameters but introducing fixed sparsity patterns that limit expressivity and impose undesirable inductive biases.

# From OFT to BOFT: Orthogonal Factorization with Butterfly Structure

To achieve both expressivity and parameter efficiency, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) constructs a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse orthogonal matrices organized in a butterfly structure. The central insight is to trade space (number of parameters) for time (depth of multiplication) by factoring $\bm{R}$ into components with carefully structured sparsity patterns. This perspective is formalized by viewing matrix factorization as information transmission over a layered graph, where each factor corresponds to constrained connections between adjacent layers, and the overall product ensures that information can flow from any input dimension to any output dimension.

The butterfly structure, inspired by the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform, enables dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ nonzeros spread across $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ factors, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros. In BOFT, each factor is parameterized to be orthogonal, yielding an overall orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ formed as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components with block size $b$. Special cases recover existing methods: BOFT$(1,b)$ recovers block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT$(1,d)$ recovers OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix. When $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT uses the canonical butterfly matrix and requires only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters.

Because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, BOFT preserves the same geometric and spectral properties as OFT while substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters. This is crucial for the spectral behavior discussed next.

# Orthogonal Matrices, SVD, and the Spectral Norm

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}_d$. Geometrically, orthogonal matrices implement rotations and reflections, preserving lengths and angles. These invariances are reflected in the behavior of singular values of matrices under orthogonal transformations.

Any real matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ admits a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the form
$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$,
where $\bm{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\bm{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal, and $\bm{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is a diagonal (or diagonal-rectangular) matrix whose nonnegative diagonal entries are the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. The spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, denoted $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$, is the largest singular value, i.e., the largest diagonal entry of $\bm{\Sigma}$.

These definitions immediately suggest a path to understanding how orthogonal transformations interact with the spectral norm: since $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and the orthogonal group is closed under multiplication, left- or right-multiplying by orthogonal matrices affects only the orthogonal factors in the SVD, leaving the singular values unchanged.

# Why Left-Multiplication by an Orthogonal Matrix Preserves Spectral Norm

In OFT and BOFT, finetuning acts by left-multiplying the pretrained weights with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, yielding finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Using the SVD $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, we have
$\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$.
Because both $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{U}$ are orthogonal, their product $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is also orthogonal. Thus, the SVD of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ can be written by replacing $\bm{U}$ with $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ while keeping $\bm{\Sigma}$ and $\bm{V}^\top$ unchanged in their roles. Consequently, the singular values of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ are identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$. In particular, the spectral norm is preserved:
$\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2$.

This argument relies only on the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ and does not depend on whether $\bm{R}$ is dense or factorized. Therefore, BOFT, despite constructing $\bm{R}$ as a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components, preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ exactly, just like OFT with a full orthogonal matrix.

# Implications for Training Stability and Generalization

The preservation of spectral norm has direct implications for optimization and generalization. Because the largest singular value, i.e., the spectral norm, governs the worst-case amplification of input signals through a linear transformation, maintaining this value across finetuning steps helps stabilize training dynamics. In the paper, orthogonal finetuning is highlighted as generally yielding better spectral properties than low-rank additive methods, precisely because it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. This preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization, and is a central advantage of both OFT and BOFT.

By maintaining the singular value profile at least in its maximum, orthogonal finetuning avoids inadvertently increasing the Lipschitz behavior of a layer due to finetuning. As a result, the finetuned model remains close to the pretrained model in a key spectral sense, which supports the overarching goal of preserving pretraining knowledge while adapting to new tasks.

# Contrast with Additive Low-Rank Updates

Additive low-rank updates, such as those used in LoRA, modify the pretrained weights by adding a low-rank matrix. While parameter-efficient, such additive perturbations do not generally preserve the singular values (and hence the spectral norm) of the pretrained matrix. In contrast, orthogonal finetuning uses a multiplicative orthogonal update on the left, which exactly preserves the full set of singular values of the layer weights. This difference is a fundamental reason why orthogonal finetuning achieves a spectral property advantage over low-rank additive methods.

# Practical Realization in BOFT and Its Spectral Consequence

BOFT realizes the orthogonal left multiplication via a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components with block size $b$:
$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$,
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is constructed to be orthogonal. The forward pass becomes
$\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}$.
Identity initialization sets each component initially to the identity, so early training behaves like the pretrained model. A multiplicative Dropout variant can be applied by replacing selected orthogonal sub-blocks with identity during training. Crucially, because each factor is orthogonal and their product is orthogonal, the composition maintains $\|\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2$ exactly, regardless of $m$ and $b$. Thus, BOFT’s parameter-efficient factorization retains the spectral norm preservation property of OFT.

# Expressivity–Regularity Trade-off and Spectral Preservation

BOFT balances expressivity and regularity by restricting $\bm{R}$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group determined by butterfly factorization. This structure enables dense transformations with far fewer parameters than an unconstrained orthogonal matrix, while still preserving crucial spectral behavior. Although more expressive constructions exist—e.g., compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes can approximate the full orthogonal group—the paper notes that maximal expressivity does not necessarily yield the best finetuning performance. The structured constraint promotes an implicit inductive bias and helps avoid overfitting, and the spectral norm preservation ensures that this bias does not come at the cost of destabilizing the layer’s scaling properties.

# Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) adapts pretrained weights by left-multiplying them with an orthogonal matrix constructed as a product of sparse butterfly components. A central, beneficial consequence of this construction is exact preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. Using the SVD $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, left multiplication by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which leaves the singular values—and hence the spectral norm—unchanged. This spectral norm preservation underpins BOFT’s training stability and generalization benefits, while its butterfly factorization delivers substantial parameter efficiency without sacrificing this key property.
