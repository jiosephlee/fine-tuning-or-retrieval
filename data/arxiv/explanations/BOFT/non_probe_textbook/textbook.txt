Title: Textbook of Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization

Motivation and Landscape of Budget-Conscious Adaptation

# Why adapting foundation systems is hard: scale, resource demands, and limited labeled examples

Modern foundation systems are built at a scale that yields broad capabilities, but that very scale also creates friction when one wants to tailor them for a new objective. These systems contain huge collections of numeric weights that were set during a lengthy initial phase. Changing many of those numeric entries directly requires substantial computational effort and long runs on large clusters of processors. Beyond raw compute, adapting at large scale often requires curated labeled examples for the new task; in many practical situations only a modest pool of such examples is available, which raises the risk that the adaptation will over-specialize to the few examples and lose the broad behavior learned previously.

There are also deployment constraints that amplify the difficulty. Many applications demand that any changes made for a new task introduce little or no extra cost when the system is used in practice. Inference-time slowdowns, larger memory footprints, or the need for special runtime support can render an otherwise promising adaptation strategy impractical. Finally, the optimization dynamics when many numeric entries are allowed to move can be unstable: large updates can destroy the useful geometric relationships encoded in the original weight collections and lead to brittle final behavior. Taken together, these factors create a strong incentive to find ways to adapt large foundation systems that move far fewer numeric entries, preserve the useful structure of the original instantiation, and impose minimal deployment overhead.

# Three families of efficient adaptation: direct weight adjustment, plug-in modules, and input-prefix tuning

Practitioners have converged on three broad approaches for adapting large systems while keeping the number of adjustable quantities small and avoiding heavy runtime costs.

The first approach directly modifies a subset or a reparameterized form of the original weight arrays. The key idea is to leave the bulk of the original numeric content frozen and only expose a compact set of adjustable quantities that interact multiplicatively or additively with the frozen weights. Because the frozen bulk remains untouched, these adaptations can often be merged into the original weights after the adaptation process, producing no extra inference overhead.

The second approach inserts small auxiliary modules into the processing chain. These plug-in modules have few adjustable entries and are positioned so that, at inference time, their effect can be applied with minimal extra computation. Conceptually, one enriches the fixed backbone with a lightweight addendum that steers behavior toward the new objective. The modules are trained while the backbone stays fixed, and at inference they either remain as a small extra component or are merged into the backbone if desired.

The third approach leaves the internal numeric entries unchanged and instead prepends or appends a small set of exemplars or prefix vectors to the input stream. These input-side tunable items shift the internal activations and thereby change the system’s responses. Because only input-side artifacts are adjusted, this family can be extremely compact and, in many setups, requires no changes to runtime kernels.

Each family trades off where the adjustable footprint sits — inside the stored weights, as a separate small module, or at the input — and practitioners pick among them depending on constraints of compute, memory, and deployment.

# Design goals for efficient adaptation: small tunable footprint, stability, transfer performance, and zero added runtime cost

When designing an efficient adaptation method, several desiderata guide choices and trade-offs.

First, the footprint of adjustable quantities should be small compared with the size of the foundation system. A compact footprint reduces storage of task-specific artifacts, lowers the compute needed during adaptation, and limits the number of degrees of freedom that can overfit to scarce labeled examples.

Second, adaptation should preserve the favorable optimization properties of the initial system. Methods that maintain key spectral or geometric attributes of the original weight arrays tend to show more stable optimization trajectories and avoid sudden drops in performance. Stability here means that the optimizer can make progress without causing large, destabilizing shifts in how internal activations relate to each other.

Third, the adapted system should transfer well to unseen cases pertinent to the downstream objective. In practice this means the adaptation mechanism should act as a gentle inductive bias: expressive enough to capture the new behavior, yet constrained enough to discourage memorization of the small adaptation set.

Finally, it is highly desirable for an adaptation scheme to introduce no extra latency at inference time. Approaches that can be collapsed into the original stored weights, or require only trivial runtime overhead, are preferred in production settings where responsiveness and memory are critical.

A good method balances these goals rather than optimizing any single one in isolation.

# LoRA versus OFT at a glance: additive compressed pathway versus multiplicative constrained transform

Two influential reparameterization strategies exemplify contrasting philosophies about how to keep the tunable footprint small while adapting large systems.

One strategy injects a compact additive pathway into a weight array. In this picture, a small set of internal linear maps is trained and their product is added to the frozen weight array. The trained product has a narrow internal width, so the total number of adjustable entries is kept small. Operationally, this is an additive correction: the system’s forward computation uses the frozen weight plus a compact learned adjustment. This design is easy to implement, has low adaptation cost, and often yields strong empirical performance on many tasks with a modest number of adjustable entries.

The alternative strategy treats adaptation as a constrained multiplicative change to the frozen weights. Instead of adding a correction, an orthogonal transform is learned and multiplied with the frozen weight on the left. Because an orthogonal transform preserves directional relations among the column vectors of the frozen weight array, this multiplicative change tends to maintain the internal geometry that encoded useful behavior during the initial fit. That geometric preservation often translates into more stable adaptation and better transfer to held-out cases. However, unconstrained left-multiplication by a free orthogonal transform is expensive: an unconstrained orthogonal matrix in the full ambient size carries a large collection of adjustable entries, which defeats the goal of keeping the tunable footprint small.

Thus the two approaches differ in whether the adjustable content is an additive compact correction or a multiplicative structured transform. The additive compact pathway leans on compact internal width, while the multiplicative transform leans on structure that preserves geometry.

# Limitations that motivate BOFT: OFT’s large adjustable footprint and fixed partitioning biases

The multiplicative, geometry-preserving strategy has attractive stability and transfer properties, but it runs into a practical bottleneck when one attempts to make it compact. A naive orthogonal transform at full ambient size carries a vast number of adjustable entries. A pragmatic fix that has been used is to restrict the orthogonal transform to act independently on disjoint groups of coordinates: each group gets its own small orthogonal transform, and these groupwise transforms are arranged along the diagonal of a larger block matrix. This reduces the adjustable footprint dramatically because each small group transform has far fewer adjustable entries than a full-size transform.

While groupwise diagonalization saves adjustable capacity, it introduces a rigid inductive bias: coordinates are partitioned by their indices and there is no interaction across groups during adaptation. That rigidity can prevent the transform from approximating classic global linear mappings that are useful in many tasks, and it forces the adaptation to treat certain directions as forever independent. In other words, the very sparsity pattern that yields compactness also limits expressive reach and prevents the multiplicative strategy from recovering many sensible global transforms.

The desire to get the best of both worlds — the stability and geometric preservation of a multiplicative orthogonal change plus a compact adjustable footprint that still allows global interactions — motivates a different design. Rather than choosing a fixed partition, one can represent a dense orthogonal transform as a product of many sparse orthogonal factors whose sparsity patterns are arranged so that information can flow across coordinates over several multiplicative stages. By distributing interaction across stages, one trades extra multiplicative steps for far fewer adjustable entries in total. Inspired by fast structured transforms that efficiently route information across coordinates, this approach reduces the adjustable footprint while permitting global mixing of coordinates, thereby addressing the twin limitations of raw orthogonal transforms and rigid blockwise restrictions. This trade — more staged computation during adaptation in return for a smaller stored task-specific footprint and retention of desirable geometric properties — is the central idea that underlies the butterfly-inspired orthogonal factorization approach.

Mathematical Foundations for Orthogonal Tuning

# Orthogonal operators: angle and norm preservation; singular-value properties

Orthogonal operators are linear maps whose action keeps inner products intact. If a linear map is represented by a symbol $R$ and two column quantities are $u$ and $v$, then the equality
\[
(Ru)^\top (Rv) = u^\top v
\]
holds for every choice of $u$ and $v$ exactly when $R$ satisfies
\[
R^\top R = I.
\]
This identity captures two central geometric consequences. First, lengths are unchanged: $\|Ru\| = \|u\|$ for every $u$. Second, angles are unchanged: the cosine of the angle between two columns is the same before and after application of $R$. These facts make orthogonal maps natural when the goal is to adjust a layer while keeping its internal relational geometry intact.

From a singular-value perspective, an orthogonal map has unit singular values only. If an arbitrary linear map $W$ admits a singular-value decomposition $W = U\Sigma V^\top$, then pre-multiplication by an orthogonal $R$ yields
\[
RW = (RU)\,\Sigma\,V^\top,
\]
so the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$, which record stretch factors along principal directions, do not change. In particular, the largest singular value of $W$ is identical to the largest singular value of $RW$. This implies that the operator norm that controls worst-case amplification of an incoming signal is unchanged by left multiplication with $R$, and that energy scaling properties of the original map remain intact.

A further spectral remark concerns eigenvalues when an orthogonal map acts on a space with inner product. Eigenvalues of an orthogonal operator lie on the unit circle of the complex plane; when real-valued, they correspond to rotations or reflections. These properties collectively explain why orthogonal adjustments are often chosen when one wants modification that is geometrically conservative rather than distortive.

# Cayley mapping: ensuring orthogonality via skew-symmetric entries

To introduce a learnable orthogonal operator while keeping it on the orthogonality manifold at every update, one may re-express orthogonality through an auxiliary skew-symmetric quantity. A skew-symmetric object $Q$ satisfies $Q^\top = -Q$. The Cayley mapping sends such a $Q$ to an orthogonal $R$ by the formula
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
provided the inverse exists. This construction guarantees $R^\top R = I$ because algebraic manipulation yields
\[
R^\top R = \big((I - Q)^{-1}\big)^\top (I + Q)^\top (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
and the skew property of $Q$ makes the middle product collapse to the identity. Thus, any skew-symmetric $Q$ generates a valid orthogonal $R$.

There are two practical consequences of representing orthogonal adjustments via this mapping. First, initializing $Q$ at the zero object gives $R = I$, which provides a neutral start that leaves the original linear map untouched. Second, the free degrees of freedom can be tuned in the smaller space of skew-symmetric objects while algebraically ensuring $R$ remains orthogonal; this avoids numerical projection steps that would otherwise be needed to restore orthogonality after each update.

A further structural point: a skew-symmetric $Q$ carries redundant signs across its off-diagonal entries, so the number of independent scalars that control $R$ is reduced relative to an unconstrained full table of entries. This reduction matches the geometric intuition that the orthogonality constraint is tight: one cannot freely change every entry of $R$ independently and still remain orthogonal.

# Columns as model units: preserving pairwise angles during task-specific update

In many linear layers, the columns of the operator correspond to interpretable units whose mutual relations matter for function. Consider a collection of such columns $\{w_i\}$ gathered in a map $W$. Left-multiplication by an orthogonal $R$ acts on each column by $w_i \mapsto R w_i$. Because inner products are invariant under $R$, the pairwise quantities
\[
(R w_i)^\top (R w_j) = w_i^\top w_j
\]
remain unchanged. Geometrically, the collection of columns experiences a rigid motion: they are rotated and perhaps reflected as a whole, but relative angles and lengths do not change.

This rigid-motion viewpoint offers intuition for why left-multiplication is a conservative form of modification. If the semantics encoded by columns depend on their mutual angles — for example, when cosine similarities guide downstream decisions — then an orthogonal left-action preserves those semantics while still permitting global reorientation. Such a property can help avoid inadvertent destruction of structures learned earlier, which is why orthogonal left-actions are appealing in scenarios where one wishes to alter global alignment without disturbing pairwise relations.

Because columns move coherently, this design encourages stability of inter-unit geometry across adaptation steps. That stability helps maintain features and roles learned in earlier phases while allowing the whole collection to be steered toward a new task by a compact global operator.

# Largest singular value and optimization stability: why preserving $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ matters

The largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ of a linear map $W$ controls how much an incoming perturbation can be amplified in norm. When optimization proceeds by gradient-based updates, gradients back-propagated through a linear map are scaled by factors tied to singular values; thus, very large singular values can magnify gradient magnitudes, leading to instability in step sizes and sensitivity to hyper-choices. Conversely, maintaining a stable bound on $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ helps keep updates well conditioned.

Because a left-orthogonal action leaves singular values unchanged, it preserves this crucial bound. If one starts from a pretrained map whose $\sigma_{\max}$ is already reasonable, then applying an orthogonal left-action will not increase the worst-case amplification. This property contributes to smoother optimization traces and mitigates risks of exploding activations or gradients that can occur when the operator norm grows uncontrolled.

Formally, with $W = U\Sigma V^\top$ and an orthogonal $R$, the composition $RW$ has singular values given by the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ as before. Thus, Lipschitz constants of layers and, indirectly, step scale for gradient updates that involve these layers, remain governed by the original singular-value profile. Preserving this profile is therefore a principled way to favor stable optimization dynamics during task-specific adjustment.

# Complexity notions: counts of free scalars, scaling like $n^{\text{two}}$ and $n\log n$, memory versus runtime trade-offs

A full unconstrained adjustment of an $n$-by-$n$ linear map would allow each of the $n^{\text{two}}$ entries to change independently. Orthogonality substantially reduces the degrees of freedom: the manifold of orthogonal maps in $n$ dimensions has about $\tfrac{n(n-\text{one})}{\text{two}}$ independent directions, which is roughly half of $n^{\text{two}}$ for large $n$. That reduction is significant, yet it still grows quadratically in $n$.

There are strategies to represent orthogonal adjustments with far fewer stored scalars while keeping the final action expressive. One route factors the final orthogonal operator into a product of many sparse orthogonal components. If each component has only a linear number of nonzero rows per factor and the number of factors grows logarithmically with $n$, then the total count of stored, tunable scalars can scale like $n\log n$ rather than like $n^{\text{two}}$. The classic structured pattern that achieves this is the butterfly-style factorization: through a small number of structured sparse factors, signals from any input index can influence every output index after the full product, and the stored scalars are linear in $n$ per factor.

The trade-off is clear. Storing fewer scalars reduces memory footprint and the cost to save checkpoints or ship an adapter bundle. However, each application of the full adjusted map now requires applying a sequence of sparse factors at runtime, which raises per-step arithmetic work during optimization. In other words, there is a memory versus runtime compromise: compressing storage into a factorized representation increases repeated computation while a dense, single-table representation reduces per-application work but costs more memory.

A practical middle ground is to keep the factorized representation throughout the optimization phase, where repeated application is inevitable, and then, at deployment, multiply all factors into a single dense operator to avoid runtime overhead. This final multiplication collapses the factor chain into one map that can be applied with a single dense operation. The collapse step costs time once but delivers lower per-evaluation runtime after deployment.

Beyond pure counts, structure matters for inductive preference. Compact structured factorizations impose a prior that favors transforms with certain global regularities — for example, those that resemble fast harmonic transforms. When the new task aligns with those regularities, the compact representation can yield better generalization than an unconstrained dense adjustment that might overfit.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has laid out the linchpin facts that make orthogonal left-actions attractive for cautious, geometry-preserving adjustment of linear maps. Orthogonality preserves inner products and singular-value profiles; the Cayley mapping offers a stable way to represent orthogonal maps from skew-symmetric antecedents; left-action by an orthogonal map rotates column collections while preserving pairwise relations; and factorized structured representations trade storage for repeated computation, enabling compact yet expressive adjustments that run efficiently in practice after an optional collapse step. These mathematical foundations explain why such conservative transformations can be effective tools in practice when one wishes to steer large preexisting maps toward new tasks without wholesale disruption of established internal structure.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles, Variants, and Limitations

# OFT forward pass and identity start

Orthogonal finetuning works by left-multiplying an existing linear mapping with a learned orthonormal operator before applying that mapping to an input. If the original, frozen linear mapping is written as $W_{\mathrm{init}}$ and an input is $x$, the modified forward computation produces an output $z$ via the compact expression
\[
z = \big(R\,W_{\mathrm{init}}\big)^\top x,
\]
where $R$ denotes the learned orthonormal operator and the superscript $\top$ denotes transpose. In words: the columns of the frozen mapping are first rotated by $R$, and then the rotated columns interact with the input in the usual way.

A key practical choice is how to start the finetuning process. OFT sets $R$ to the identity operator at the start, so that the initial model behavior is exactly the same as the frozen mapping. This identity start keeps the initial output unchanged and gives the learning procedure a conservative beginning: the system only departs from the original mapping as the finetuning signal accumulates.

This multiplicative approach contrasts with additive alternatives that modify the frozen mapping by adding a corrective term. The multiplicative left action has a geometric flavor: it changes how the frozen columns relate to one another while keeping their relative magnitudes intact at the outset.

# Cayley form and skew-symmetric operator

To maintain the orthonormal constraint on $R$ during optimization, one convenient analytic representation is the Cayley form. In this representation $R$ is expressed from an auxiliary skew-symmetric linear object $Q$ through the Cayley map:
\[
R = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
and $Q$ satisfies the skew condition
\[
Q^\top = -Q.
\]
Because of this skew condition, the Cayley map yields an operator $R$ that is orthonormal whenever the inverse exists. In practical finetuning, one works with $Q$ as the quantity that is adjusted by the optimizer; the Cayley formula then produces an $R$ that automatically satisfies the orthonormal constraint at every step.

The identity start is achieved simply by setting the initial auxiliary object to the null operator, so that the Cayley map returns the identity. During finetuning the adjustable entries of $Q$ evolve and induce a smooth path of orthonormal operators $R$. This approach has two attractive consequences. First, the orthonormal structure is enforced analytically rather than via a projection step, which simplifies constrained updates. Second, because the Cayley map is continuous around the identity, small changes in $Q$ lead to small, controlled changes in $R$, giving a stable trajectory early in finetuning.

# Partitioned-group OFT: structure, adjustable-values, and representational capacity

The full orthonormal operator acting on a space of dimension $D$ is highly expressive but also has a large count of adjustable scalar entries. To reduce that count, a common variant partitions the coordinate axes of the mapped space into a collection of groups and applies an independent orthonormal operator to each group. Concretely, imagine splitting the $D$ coordinates into several contiguous segments and letting a separate orthonormal operator act within each segment while leaving cross-segment coupling absent. This yields a structured operator that is the direct sum of small orthonormal operators, so the overall transformation can be written informally as a composition of independent transformations that act on disjoint coordinate subsets.

That structure sharply reduces the number of adjustable scalars: instead of having to specify on the order of $D^{\text{two}}$ values for an unconstrained orthonormal operator, the grouped variant requires only the sum of the squared sizes of the groups. If each group has size $S$ and the number of groups is $G$ so that $S\cdot G = D$, the total adjustable count scales like $G\cdot S^{\text{two}}$, which is much smaller when $S$ is modest. This trade produces a practical lever: by changing group size one can trade representational capacity against the total number of adjustable scalars to be optimized.

However, that economy comes at a representational cost. The grouped structure forbids any mixing of information across different groups: a column whose coordinates are split across groups cannot influence coordinates that lie in a different group under the learned orthonormal action. As a result, some global linear transforms that rely on coordinated mixing across many coordinates cannot be represented unless one of the groups spans the entire space.

# Structural prior and limits of partitioning

Viewing the grouped structure as a structural prior helps to see both its rationale and its limitations. On the positive side, the prior encourages conservative changes that alter only local coordinate relationships; this can reduce overfitting in settings where the downstream adjustments should be local refinements of the original mapping. Starting from the identity and allowing only small independent rotations inside each group makes the learning problem easier and often yields stable behavior.

On the negative side, fixing the partition by coordinate index is arbitrary with respect to the semantic structure of features. Important cross-coordinate interactions often do not respect simple contiguous partitions, so the grouped restriction may cut useful connections and thus limit final performance. Moreover, choosing a good partitioning is nontrivial in practice: there is no universal rule that tells which coordinates should be grouped together, and hand-crafted choices must be validated empirically. In tasks that require dense mixing across many coordinates, the grouped variant will be unable to reach the best transforms unless the grouping is relaxed to include larger groups, which reintroduces a higher adjustable count.

# Empirical trade-offs: OFT versus narrow-factor additive updates

Two families of light-touch finetuning stand out in practice. One family, represented by narrow-factor additive updates, modifies the frozen mapping by adding a small correction formed as the composition of two narrow linear maps. Conceptually, this is an additive, low-complexity adjustment: it augments the original mapping with a thin corrective term. The other family, orthonormal left-multiplicative finetuning, transforms the columns of the frozen mapping via an orthonormal operator and thereby preserves certain geometric properties of the original mapping.

Empirical studies show complementary strengths. The multiplicative orthonormal action has the appealing property that it preserves the singular values of the frozen mapping when applied on the left. In practical terms, the magnitude relationships among the original columns are retained under left multiplication by an orthonormal operator; this often yields more stable optimization trajectories and can help generalization because the overall scaling behavior of the mapping is unchanged. The grouped orthonormal variant retains some of that stability while reducing the number of adjustable scalars, which is helpful when compute or optimizer budget is tight.

Narrow-factor additive approaches typically require far fewer adjustable scalars for the same nominal memory budget and can be highly effective when the main adaptation needed is a low-complexity correction. They have shown strong empirical success across many tasks, especially when the downstream change can be captured by a thin correction. However, additive corrections can change the singular structure of the original mapping, and this change can sometimes lead to less stable learning unless care is taken.

Which approach is preferable depends on the task and available budget. If preserving the original mapping’s geometric structure and ensuring stable finetuning are primary concerns, the orthonormal multiplicative route is attractive. If extreme economy of adjustable scalars is required and the task can be solved with a thin additive correction, the narrow-factor additive route is often competitive. The partitioned orthonormal variant provides a middle ground: it keeps many of the orthonormal benefits while letting practitioners control the number of adjustable scalars through the choice of group granularity.

Closing remark: OFT’s core idea — conservative, geometry-preserving left-multiplicative adaptation — gives a clear conceptual alternative to additive low-complexity updates. The grouped variant makes that idea practical at constrained budgets, but the practitioner must weigh the representational sacrifices of fixed grouping against the stability and inductive advantages that orthonormal transformations bring.

An Information Transmission View of Array Factorization

# Grid model

Imagine a layered grid that makes the process of composing a wide linear transform tangible. The grid has $d$ nodes along the source side and the same count along the receiving side, and there are $m+\text{one}$ vertical layers that sit between those two margins. Each pair of adjacent layers is connected by a sparse linear mapping; think of each mapping as a simple wiring pattern that allows some signals to jump from specific nodes in one layer to specific nodes in the next. When these local mappings are composed, the full left-to-right transform emerges as their serial composition.

Formally, label the local mappings by $B^{(t)}$, one for each adjacent pair of layers. The overall transform is the composition of those parts, written compactly as $\displaystyle R \;=\; \prod_{t} B^{(t)}$, where the product indicates sequential application from leftmost wiring to rightmost wiring. Each $B^{(t)}$ is sparse in the sense that most pairings of sender and receiver nodes at that stage are inactive; the pattern of active linkages in each part defines which node-to-node paths are possible when you look across the whole stack.

This view replaces the usual algebraic picture of a single large table of coefficients by a networked picture of nodes and local connections. A nontrivial entry in the final transform corresponds to at least one active path in this layered network that carries a signal from a particular source node to a particular sink node. Conversely, the absence of any path results in an absent entry in the composed transform.

# Dense connectivity criterion

From the layered perspective, the classical desideratum of a fully connecting left side to right side becomes a reachability condition: every source node must have at least one path to every receiving node through the sequence of local link patterns. Put differently, the composite transform is fully populated precisely when, for every ordered pair of a source and a sink, there exists a chain of active linkages that threads through the intermediate layers and connects the two.

This reachability condition admits a clear operational test. Pick a source node and follow every active outgoing connection in the first local mapping; for each resulting node in the next layer, follow that node's active outgoing connections forward, and so on. If after traversing all layers the set of reachable nodes on the receiving side equals the whole receiving set, then that source node meets the requirement. The composite is fully populated only when every source passes this test.

Why is this useful? It reframes a global algebraic requirement — every entry present in the final table — as a local graph traversal requirement across the stack. That in turn guides the design of sparsity patterns for the parts: patterns that create efficient mixing of paths at early stages tend to help every source reach every sink later, while patterns that isolate groups tend to create unreachable pairs and hence absent entries.

# Minimum free links under orthogonal restrictions

When the local mappings must also satisfy an orthogonal requirement — that each part preserve inner geometry at its own stage — the design problem acquires extra structure. Orthogonal parts cannot be arbitrary sparse patterns with arbitrary active values; they must form full-rank wiring patterns with structured numerical relationships among active entries. In the layered graph picture this means that each level-to-level mapping needs to create a bijection in the active skeleton so that no entire sender or receiver dimension is lost at that step.

A useful way to think about economy of design is to separate two kinds of links. Some links are necessary for the orthogonal parts to be well formed; they are structural and, in a sense, fixed in role. Other links are free degrees of freedom where numeric tuning occurs during adaptation. The goal of an economical design is to minimize those free links while preserving full reachability across the stack.

Graphically, each layer must supply enough active one-to-one pairings so that rank is preserved through the stack. Once that baseline is provided, carefully placed extra active connections can be added to merge information streams across pathways so that, after composing all parts, each source can influence every sink. Designs that achieve this with only a mild growth in active link count per layer attain a favorable trade-off: they retain the geometric integrity offered by orthogonality at each stage while enabling the global mixing needed for expressive transforms.

From an asymptotic viewpoint, wiring patterns that reuse structure across layers can lower the total number of free links needed for dense reachability. A well known family of topologies achieves mixing with a number of free links that grows like $d$ times the logarithm of $d$, rather than like the square of $d$. The layered picture makes this behavior intuitive: progressive halving and recombination of streams, or other recursive interleaving patterns, let a modest number of local active links generate many distinct end-to-end paths.

# Interpreting absent entries: unreachable pairs and induced sparsity

An absent entry in the composed transform has a direct combinatorial meaning: it signals that no chain of active local links connects the corresponding source and sink. That combinatorial absence is often more revealing than the raw algebraic value would be. When parts are orthogonal, absences can propagate: an absence at one stage may force absences later, and conversely some absences may be forbidden because they would violate the rank or orthogonality requirement.

There are two typical ways an absent entry arises. The first is purely structural: the pattern of active links fails to provide any path. The second is structural but indirect: the orthogonal requirement ties together several link values so that eliminating one link would imply elimination of another. Thus a seemingly small local sparsity choice can ripple through the composition and create a pattern of absent entries in the final transform that is larger than the original choice.

This perspective helps explain surprising phenomena. For instance, if a local mapping isolates a pair of nodes into an independent subgroup, then even with rich connectivity later, sources inside that subgroup cannot influence sinks outside it. Conversely, carefully interleaving groups at intermediate layers prevents such isolation and ensures that local absences need not create global absences. In design terms, one should therefore avoid persistent grouping that prevents cross-group paths unless such grouping is intended.

# Worked example: when product of sparse parts fails or succeeds to be fully populated

To make these ideas concrete, consider a small layered stack described purely in words. Take a system with a few source nodes on the left and the same count on the right. Suppose the first local mapping connects each left node to only its immediate neighbor on the next level, producing narrow, nonoverlapping streams. If the next local mapping preserves those streams without interleaving them, then each left node can reach only a small subset of right nodes; the composite will therefore contain many absent entries. No amount of freedom in the numerical values of active links can overcome this structural isolation.

Contrast that with a different choice: design the first local mapping so that each left node fans out to two nearby nodes on the next level, and design the second mapping so that those two streams are recombined and interleaved with streams from other sources. Iterating this pattern of fan-out followed by interleaving across successive layers multiplies the number of distinct end-to-end paths available to each source. After enough repetitions the reachability condition becomes satisfied and the composite operator is fully populated, even though each local mapping remains sparse.

A second illustrative situation highlights orthogonal-induced link dependencies. Imagine a local mapping that pairs nodes into two-by-two units and performs a rotation within each pair. If a designer chooses to remove one link inside a pair, the orthogonal requirement forces a compensating change elsewhere in that pair; in certain removal choices, that compensating change amounts to also removing the other link in that pair. Thus a single local removal can create a pair of absences that then propagate. The layered view shows how such local choices can either break global reachability or, if chosen carefully, leave other recombination stages capable of rescuing full reachability.

Taken together, these examples underline the central lesson: sparsity at the local level does not imply sparsity at the global level. What matters is the pattern of connections and how those patterns allow streams to mix as they progress from left to right. A well designed stack achieves rich global mixing while keeping each local mapping economical and respecting orthogonal form at every step.

# Concluding remarks

Recasting the assembly of a large transform as an information transmission problem on a layered grid gives immediate design principles. Reachability across layers is the practical proxy for a fully populated composite. Orthogonal form imposes structural baselines of active links and injects dependencies that can magnify or suppress the effect of local sparsity. Finally, recursive interleaving topologies deliver a sweet spot: they produce global mixing with only modest per-layer complexity. For anyone designing compact yet expressive linear transforms, thinking in terms of nodes, local link patterns, and path reachability yields both intuition and concrete prescriptions.

Butterfly Structures: From Cooley–Tukey to Efficient Orthogonal Representation

# Cooley–Tukey and butterfly graphs: efficient wide-range mixing

The Cooley–Tukey idea is a way to reorganize a transform so that information that would otherwise require a single, large dense link can be exchanged through a small number of simple, local interchanges. The core insight is recursive: take a large collection of entries, split it into two equal groups, perform smaller transforms inside each group, then perform a small pattern of pairwise exchanges that weave the two halves together. Repeating this process at successive scales produces a pattern of connectivity whose depth grows only with the logarithm of the overall size, while each stage involves only local, sparse exchanges.

This connectivity pattern is often drawn as a layered graph in which each layer holds a copy of the entries and edges between adjacent layers indicate where information is shared during one local step. The total effect of traversing all layers can produce a dense mapping from the first layer to the last, even though each intermediate step is sparse. Because the depth is logarithmic in the number of entries and each step touches only a linear number of links, this layout achieves a striking trade: near-global mixing while keeping the total number of active links much smaller than what a single dense link would need.

In practical use, such graphs are prized for how they blend local operations into global influence with predictable regularity. Each source position can reach every destination position by following a path through the layers, so the layered graph is a compact way to realize a full interchange without storing or manipulating a full two-dimensional table of coefficients at once. This makes the pattern attractive when one needs a dense transform but wants to limit the number of free numeric values used to represent that transform.

# Butterfly factor $B^{F}(K)$: a paired partition with diagonal scaling

A single, small building piece in the butterfly family is a paired partition that combines two equal-sized groups into one local mixing step. Using a capital letter $K$ to denote the size of the local group, a common form is

\[
B^{F}(K)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2}) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Here each vector $\mathbf{d}_{i}$ supplies the scaling applied to matched pairs inside the two subgroups. Written this way, the piece is a $K$-by-$K$ two‑by‑two partition where the four quadrants are themselves simple diagonal scalings. The role of each quadrant is to route, scale, and combine the paired entries coming into the small patch. Because each quadrant is diagonal, the internal pattern of nonzero positions inside this small patch is extremely sparse: only the paired entries interact.

What matters for later assembly is that this small patch mixes information between a pair of subgroups in a structured and invertible fashion when its scalings are chosen appropriately. By chaining many such patches at different scales and interleaving suitable reordering of indices, one can achieve complex global mixing with a small set of local scalings per patch.

# Butterfly stage $\tilde{B}(N,K)$: forming a layered assembly and recursive build

To build a size-$N$ transform from those small pieces, one arranges many copies of the paired patch in parallel across the full index set. Let $\tilde{B}(N,K)$ denote the linear operator that places disjoint copies of $B^{F}(K)$ along the diagonal of a larger two‑dimensional array so that each copy acts on a separate contiguous group of size $K$. In words, $\tilde{B}(N,K)$ is the operator that performs the same local paired mixing in many separate positions simultaneously.

The full butterfly operator for the whole index set is obtained by applying a sequence of such stage operators at decreasing local sizes. Conceptually, one first performs paired patches that act on small adjacent pairs, then rearranges indices, then performs paired patches that act on larger pairs formed from the previous step, and so on. By composing a logarithmic number of stages that progressively double the size of the mixed group (in the usual recursive instantiation), the composition yields an operator that, overall, can map any source entry to any destination entry through some path of local interchanges.

Viewed on the layered graph introduced earlier, each $\tilde{B}(N,K)$ corresponds to the set of links between two adjacent layers: within a layer, groups of $K$ positions are handled in parallel, and the links cross between the two halves inside each of those groups. Repeating these layers at the right pattern of sizes produces the characteristic crisscross wiring of the butterfly graph.

# From pairwise scalars to grouped subarrays: generalizing to grouped building blocks

The paired patch described above uses scalar scalings for matched entries. A straightforward generalization replaces each scalar with a small dense subarray of size $B\times B$, so that each quadrant becomes a diagonal arrangement of those small subarrays. Using grouped subarrays allows the same sparse structural pattern while giving richer local transforms inside each paired unit.

Concretely, when the basic scalings are themselves small square operators of side length $B$, the local patch mixes $2B$ entries at a time using a $2B$-by-$2B$ local unit. The global assembly then places many such local units in parallel inside each stage operator $\tilde{B}(N,K)$ so the large operator remains sparse across stages but carries richer expressivity at each local exchange. This grouped view is useful in practice when the dimension being mixed divides naturally into channels or multi-dimensional features that should be handled as chunks rather than as single scalars.

Two practical benefits arise from this generalization. First, treating chunks as atomic allows alignment with internal tiling or width choices in a larger system, which can reduce bookkeeping and improve memory locality. Second, the grouped seeds permit a smooth interpolation between extremely sparse scalar patches (which are very compact) and larger local units (which are more expressive locally). By tuning the chunk size, one can trade compactness for richer local behavior while preserving the same logarithmic stage depth.

# Ensuring orthogonality via a skew‑symmetric rational map

When the global intent is to produce an operator that preserves length and angles (an orthogonal transform), each stage operator must be chosen so that their product remains orthogonal. A convenient way to force a stage to be orthogonal while keeping its internal free values represented compactly is to generate it from a skew‑symmetric seed. Let $\mathbf{Q}$ denote a skew‑symmetric square seed (so that $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$). The rational map

\[
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
\]

produces an orthogonal operator $\mathbf{R}$ whenever the inverse exists. With this construct one parametrizes orthogonal stage operators by filling only the entries of the skew seed $\mathbf{Q}$; the skew property induces the necessary symmetry relations so that the number of independent scalar degrees of freedom is reduced.

This choice has several practical consequences. First, initialization is straightforward: setting the seed to the zero array yields the identity operator, which is often desired when one wants the overall system to start from an initial state that exactly preserves the original representation. Second, enforcing the skew constraint removes redundant freedom and simplifies the guarantee that each stage does not introduce scaling artifacts. Finally, because each stage is created from a small skew seed (possibly grouped in the chunked generalization), the number of free entries per stage is linear in the large size, not quadratic.

# Complexity: sparse stage count versus a full dense operator

One of the most attractive numerical consequences of the layered butterfly design is the reduction in the total number of nonzero entries that must be stored or optimized. A single full dense operator on $N$ entries carries on the order of $N^{\text{two}}$ entries. In contrast, a butterfly built from a logarithmic number of stages each holding only linear many active entries requires on the order of $N\log N$ total nonzero entries. Put differently, the butterfly realizes the reach of a dense operator while controlling the growth of free numeric values to near‑linear rather than quadratic.

This economy comes with a computational trade: evaluating the composed operator requires passing through the sequence of sparse stages, so the run‑time cost is proportional to the number of stages times the per‑stage cost. Because the stage count scales with the logarithm of the overall size, the total computation for a single application scales like $N\log N$ arithmetic operations, which is the same asymptotic count that makes the recursive Cooley–Tukey layout attractive in classical fast transforms.

From a representation perspective, this means that one can represent a rich, near‑dense linear mapping with far fewer free numeric values, and yet still apply that mapping in times that are modest multiples of the cost of a single sparse pass. When the goal is to restrict the number of free entries—either to save storage, to reduce the degrees of freedom for better stability, or to impose a structured inductive bias—this trade is highly appealing.

# Practical notes: identity start, randomized stage masking, and expressivity trade-offs

A practical recipe that follows naturally from this family of designs is to initialize each stage so that it is the identity operator; this is accomplished by initializing the skew seed to the zero array. Starting from identity ensures that the composed operator initially leaves the underlying representation unchanged, and then the system can progressively introduce structured change through the limited set of free entries.

To discourage overreliance on a small number of stages and to inject robustness, one may randomly replace some stages or some of their grouped subunits by identity during iterative optimization. This multiplicative masking method, applied stochastically across updates, reduces the effective variance seen by any single free entry and can act as a regularizer that encourages the use of the whole sparse pattern rather than a fragile subset.

Finally, it is important to understand the expressivity trade-offs. A butterfly constructed with minimal grouped units (scalar patches) and logarithmic depth forms a structured subset of all orthogonal operators; it can exactly realize many classical transforms and approximate a wide family of targets, but it cannot represent every possible orthogonal operator unless the depth and grouping choices are expanded significantly. Allowing larger grouped seeds or composing more stage sequences expands expressivity—potentially to the point of recovering the whole orthogonal group—while incurring more free entries or more computation. Choosing a point on this continuum is an engineering decision: the butterfly pattern supplies a flexible scaffold with clear performance and storage advantages that can be tuned to the needs of a given adaptation or resource budget.

BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly

# BOFT definition: $\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$

BOFT constructs a left-side orthogonal transform for a linear mapping by composing a sequence of structured orthogonal stages. Concretely, an orthogonal operator of dimension $d$ is written as
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m)}\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m-1)}\cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,1)}.
\]
Each factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is a partitioned orthogonal operator formed by tiling many small butterfly units along the main axis, so that every such factor is orthonormal in the sense that
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top=\mathbf{I}_d.
\]
The small building block used inside each partition is a compact two-by-two style orthonormal rotation (or its blockwise extension of size $2b\times 2b$ when a block width $b$ is used). These tiny rotations are arranged in the classic butterfly wiring pattern, and each stage permutes and mixes entries in a structured way. The full product therefore represents an orthonormal transform that can move information from any input coordinate to any output coordinate after all stages are applied.

Describing the construction at a conceptual level: each stage performs local pairwise orthonormal mixes of groups of coordinates and then a fixed wiring permutation shuffles which coordinates meet at the next stage. Repeating this local mix-and-shuffle pattern over $m$ stages yields a global orthonormal transform that is both structured and dense as a whole, while each stage itself only contains many sparse local mixes.

# Special cases

The BOFT family interpolates smoothly between the very local, groupwise transforms and an unconstrained full orthonormal update. When only a single stage is used, $\mathbf{R}(1,b)$ applies an orthonormal transform independently to each contiguous group of width $b$; in other words, the full $d$ coordinates are partitioned into groups of size $b$ and each group receives its own orthonormal rotation. This recovers the familiar groupwise orthonormal update strategy that treats disjoint coordinate groups independently.

At the other extreme, if the group width equals the full dimension, $\mathbf{R}(1,d)$ is simply a single unconstrained orthonormal operator on all $d$ coordinates. That setting is the unrestricted orthonormal left-multiplicative update: it offers maximal flexibility but comes with the largest set of free scalars to specify the transform.

Between these extremes, BOFT uses more than one stage with modest group widths. As the number of stages increases and the group width decreases, the composed operator becomes capable of representing dense orthonormal transforms while keeping the number of free scalars much smaller than the unrestricted case.

# Dense transforms with far fewer free scalars

A key design point is that a dense orthonormal operator can be realized by composing several sparse orthonormal stages. A useful choice that yields a dense result with a logarithmic count of stages is
\[
m=\log\!\bigg(\frac{2d}{b}\bigg),
\]
with group width $b\ge 2$. When $b=2$ this recovers the classic radix pattern from fast transforms: each stage mixes small pairs and the composition over $\log d$ stages produces a dense operator whose wiring mirrors the butterfly network. With larger $b$ the same principle holds, but the local mixes operate on small blocks of width $b$ rather than simple pairs; choosing $b$ trades local expressivity versus the number of stages needed to reach full connectivity across coordinates.

This construction is attractive because the global operator becomes dense after a modest number of stages: information that starts in any coordinate can be routed, stage by stage, to any other coordinate at the final output. The wiring that accomplishes that is the butterfly pattern, which is known for efficient all-to-all mixing while using only sparse local connections at each stage.

# Count of free scalars (effective degrees of freedom)

Because each tiled partition in a stage uses small orthonormal rotations, the total count of free scalar degrees for $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ grows linearly in $d$ and linearly in the number of stages $m$. A compact expression for the effective degrees of freedom is
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
One can see this by noting that each local orthonormal block of width $b$ contributes roughly $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)b$ free scalars, the tiling across the $d$ coordinates yields a factor proportional to $d/b$, and multiplication gives the formula above after simplifying.

To put this in perspective, an unrestricted orthonormal operator on $d$ coordinates requires about $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free scalars, which scales quadratically in $d$. The single-stage groupwise scheme that independently rotates groups of width $b$ requires on the order of $b\,d$ free scalars. BOFT bridges these two regimes: with $b$ small and $m$ on the order of $\log d$, the total grows like $d\log d$, offering a dramatic reduction from the quadratic count while retaining the ability to realize dense transforms.

In asymptotic notation:
\[
\text{unrestricted orthonormal: }\mathcal{O}(d^2),\qquad
\text{groupwise single-stage: }\mathcal{O}(b\,d),\qquad
\text{BOFT with }b=2: \mathcal{O}(d\log d).
\]
Thus BOFT attains much lower complexity in terms of free scalars than a fully unconstrained choice, while giving far greater mixing power than a single-stage groupwise scheme of equal local width.

# Identity initialization

A practical desideratum for gentle adaptation is that the initial composed operator should not alter the original linear mapping. BOFT satisfies this naturally: each stage is constructed so that it can be set to the identity operator by choosing the local rotations to be trivial (for example, by initializing the skew parts that generate rotations to zero). With each stage initialized to identity, the product $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is the identity, and the original linear mapping is unchanged at the start of any subsequent adjustment procedure. This identity-start property enables controlled updates that do not abruptly disturb the initial mapping.

# Zero extra cost at run time

Although BOFT composes its orthonormal operator as a product of several sparse stages during the adaptation process, there is no persistent run-time overhead after the adaptation concludes. Once the composed orthonormal operator $\mathbf{R}$ has been fixed by the optimization used for the task, it can be multiplied into the original left-side linear operator to form a single, updated linear mapping. That folded mapping replaces the original pair of operators for all subsequent forward passes, so execution at deployment uses exactly the same cost as the original mapping. In short, BOFT incurs its modest extra cost only during the adaptation procedure; at deployment the transform is merged and no per-call extra work remains.

# Intuition summary

BOFT attains a practical middle ground: it keeps the adaptation space structured and compact by using many sparse, orthonormal stages wired in the butterfly pattern, yet the overall product can reach dense orthonormal transforms. The number of free scalars grows with $d$ and with the number of stages $m$, and by choosing small local width $b$ together with $m$ on the order of a logarithm in $d$ one gets a dense final operator with only about $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$ degrees of freedom. Identity initialization guarantees a gentle start, and final folding of the learned operator into the base linear mapping means there is no long-term run-time penalty. These features explain why BOFT subsumes the groupwise and the unconstrained orthonormal update schemes as special settings and why it is attractive for compact, stable adaptations of large linear mappings.

Training BOFT in Practice

# Optimization loop: representing small orthogonal subunits via a skew generator

A stable adaptation loop for BOFT treats each small orthogonal subunit as the exponential-like outcome of a compact antisymmetric generator. Concretely, one works with a skew-symmetric tensor $\bm{Q}$ that lives on the small two-by-two subspace of interest and updates the entries of $\bm{Q}$ during adaptation steps. The orthogonal transform that is applied to the larger linear operator is expressed through a Cayley-style map written as
$\displaystyle \bm{R}=(\mathbb{I}+\bm{Q})(\mathbb{I}-\bm{Q})^{\text{inv}}$,
where the superscript “inv” denotes classical inversion of the indicated operator. This representation has three practical virtues: it starts at exact identity when the generator is set to neutral, it enforces the orthogonality constraint by construction, and it links additive updates on $\bm{Q}$ to multiplicative changes in the final transform, which empirically yields smoother behavior for downstream adaptation.

For the smallest subunit, the skew generator is zero except for an antisymmetric two-by-two pattern inside a small diagonal tile. Writing the two-by-two action in familiar trigonometric form helps intuition:
$\displaystyle
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\[6pt]
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}$,
which one recovers by choosing an appropriate $\bm{Q}$ and applying the Cayley-style map. In practice, rather than storing dense small arrays for all such tiles, it is common to store only the independent scalars that define each local skew entry; these are the tunable quantities that the optimizer updates.

The outer adaptation loop uses any mainstream solver for stochastic objectives. Two practical tips improve robustness. First, perform updates on the skew generators at a finer numerical precision than used elsewhere: apply the solver step in a higher-precision buffer, then cast the resulting generators back to the working precision. Second, for stability of the inversion involved in the Cayley-style map, compute the inverse by solving a linear system (for instance, via a triangular solver when the structure permits) rather than forming an explicit inverse; this avoids amplifying numerical noise when the generator entries are small.

# Multiplicative stochastic masking: random identity substitution for subunits

To regularize the multiplicative update and avoid over-specialization of a small set of subunits, apply multiplicative stochastic masking that occasionally replaces some subunits with exact identity before a forward pass. Implement this in two hierarchical steps. First, randomly pick a small fraction of the top-level components across the whole transform and replace each chosen component by the identity operator for that forward computation. Second, within each remaining top-level component, randomly pick a small fraction of the local tiles and set those tiles to identity. The dual probabilities control coarse and fine sparsification: the first probability reduces reliance on any single big component, while the second dithers the internal structure to encourage redundancy.

When sampling these masks, use a fresh draw per example batch rather than a single global draw per epoch; this encourages the representation to be robust in expectation and prevents deterministic patterning. For deterministic evaluation or deployment, switch all masks off so the full orthogonal composition is active. Finally, implement the masking as in-place substitution of precomputed identity templates rather than conditionally skipping computation; this avoids code-path divergence and keeps throughput predictable.

# Step-size choices and schedule: stability considerations compared to OFT and LoRA

Orthogonal multiplicative adaptation inherits a natural spectral stability because the left multiplication by a true orthogonal transform preserves singular values of the base linear operator. This often permits slightly more aggressive step choices than purely additive updates, but caution is still warranted because the multiplicative nature amplifies certain directions in early iterations.

A practical schedule that balances stability and speed is a short linear warmup of the solver step size followed by a gradual decay toward a small final step. Use a solver with adaptive moments and bias correction for robust, per-tunable scaling; this reduces the need for hand-tuned per-stage gain factors. When comparing to purely additive techniques, practitioners often find the following behavioral guidance useful: begin with a warmup that brings the skew generators out of exact neutrality slowly, then let the adaptive solver handle curvature; reduce the base step-size if you observe erratic loss spikes in the first adaptation epoch.

Regularization that penalizes large entries of the skew generators is useful: rather than applying a direct penalty on the final composed transform, apply a modest quadratic penalty on the generator entries themselves. This keeps the transform close to identity at initialization while still permitting controlled deviation. When using burned-in solvers that maintain per-parameter moment statistics, reset those statistics if the adaptation is resumed from a checkpoint produced under a different step-size regime.

# Module selection: where BOFT helps in large language, vision transformer, and diffusion U-shaped systems

Not all linear operators in a backbone system benefit equally from multiplicative orthogonal adaptation. Practical evidence and intuition point to two categories that typically yield strong returns.

First, projection operators that distribute or gather information across feature coordinates are high-leverage targets. In attention mechanisms, the output projection and the value projection often carry substantial representational responsibility; adapting the left-side transform there can realign how stored features are presented to subsequent computation. Similarly, in the feed-forward pathway, the first projection that expands feature dimensionality and the final projection that collapses it back are natural places to place BOFT.

Second, operators that sit at bottlenecks or at the interface between hierarchical stages are effective locations for BOFT. In vision transformers, mid-stack projections where spatial and channel information get mixed are productive; in U-shaped denoising architectures, transforms in the bottleneck and at skip-connection interfaces allow global adjustments with a small footprint.

As a rule of thumb, prioritize operators that have high input dimensionality or that mediate major routing of information. Reserve adaptation for those modules first, and only expand to others if budget permits, watching for diminishing returns.

# Budgeting tunables across modules: allocating depth and tile size per stage

Two knobs control the expressiveness versus resource usage of BOFT at each target operator: the number of multiplicative components composed in sequence (call this the depth of factorization) and the local tile size used for the small orthogonal subunits. A deeper composition with smaller tiles tends to produce a dense, highly expressive transform while keeping the count of tunable scalars modest. Conversely, a shallow composition with larger tiles gives more local expressivity per tile but raises the tunable-count.

How to allocate a fixed global budget across modules? Start by allocating most of the budget to a handful of high-impact operators (attention output, feed-forward projections, bottleneck transforms). For these, favor moderate depth with small tiles: that configuration usually captures global permutation-like and mixing operations with low unit cost. For peripheral operators (early-stage projections, small-dimension heads), use shallow depth and larger tiles to avoid unnecessary computational overhead.

When adapting very large systems, it can help to use a tapered scheme: early modules get conservative allocations, middle modules get the bulk, and very late modules get a modest allocation to fine-tune final alignment. Monitor held-out performance as you reassign budget: the marginal gain per added tunable typically diminishes, and the optimal split is task-dependent.

# Implementation notes: permutations, batching, reduced precision, and caching structure

Efficient implementation hinges on making the sparse pattern cheap to compute and cheap to apply. The butterfly-style composition relies on fixed index permutations interleaved with small local transforms. Precompute and store the index maps for those permutations once per instantiation; during forward passes, perform an index-based gather/scatter rather than materializing intermediate dense operators. This keeps memory traffic predictable and exposes opportunities for fused kernels.

Group the application of many small local transforms into vectorized kernels so that hardware can process multiple tiles in parallel. When devices support fused gather-plus-local-transform operations, exploit them to reduce intermediate memory movement. For the Cayley-style map, avoid explicit inversion; instead solve the implied linear system with a batched solver on the packed skew generator blocks.

For reduced-precision arithmetic, apply mixed-precision carefully. Keep the skew generators and the small-linear-system solves in a higher format (for example, use a format with extended mantissa during the Cayley solve), and cast the final local orthogonal tiles back to the accelerator’s working format for bulk arithmetic. This provides numerical stability while retaining the throughput benefit of lower formats elsewhere.

Finally, cache the composed permutation pattern and any identity templates used for stochastic masking. When sweeping across many small operators, reuse the same batched kernel and preallocated buffers to avoid allocation overhead.

# Deployment fusion: merging transforms for runtime, serialization, and compatibility

One practical advantage of BOFT is that, once adaptation converges, the learned left-hand orthogonal composition can be absorbed into the stored linear operator so runtime inference runs with a single dense transform. To do this, multiply the base operator on the left by the final orthogonal composition and store the resulting operator as the deployed linear transform. Perform this fusion once, using a numerically stable routine that applies the small permute-and-local-transform sequence directly to the base operator; avoid reconstructing intermediate dense forms that exceed memory capacity.

For serialization, save the fused operator in the format your runtime expects. Also store the original representation of the BOFT composition (index maps, final skew generators) if you plan to resume further adaptation or wish to inspect the adaptation trajectory. When exporting to other runtimes, ensure that the fused operator is compatible with the target numeric format and that any quantization step is applied after fusion to preserve the orthogonal-induced spectral properties.

Compatibility notes: because BOFT preserves singular value magnitudes when applied as a left multiplication, fusing it into the stored transform preserves spectral behavior for downstream components. This is reassuring for deployment: the fused transform will not unexpectedly amplify activation norms relative to the original base operator.

# Practical checklist for reliable runs

- Represent small orthogonal actions via skew generators and apply the Cayley-style map; solve the inversion step by linear solves rather than explicit inversion.
- Use multiplicative stochastic masking at two hierarchical scales; sample per batch and disable for deployment.
- Warm up the solver step-size, then decay; prefer adaptive solvers with moment estimates and modest generator-level regularization.
- Prioritize adaptation targets: high-dimension projection operators, bottleneck and interface transforms.
- Allocate resource budget by trading factorization depth against local tile size; favor depth-plus-small-tiles in high-impact modules.
- Precompute permutation index maps, vectorize small transforms, use mixed precision sensibly with higher precision for the Cayley solve.
- After adaptation, fuse the composition into the stored linear operator for efficient runtime, and serialize both fused and decomposed representations if you may resume adaptation.

Following these principles will make BOFT practical and robust when adapting very large systems: it keeps numerical behavior stable, encourages generalizable adjustments, and permits efficient runtime deployment once adaptation is complete.

Theory and Insights: Expressivity, Regularization, and Structure

# Coverage versus OFT: BOFT’s broader reach with the same block size

When one constrains a left-multiplicative operator to act as a collection of independent small blocks, each block reshapes only a subset of coordinates and leaves cross-subset relations untouched. That original blockwise specialization (OFT in the source work) therefore trades freedom for simplicity: it controls how much the pre-existing mapping can be altered, but at the cost of being unable to mix entries that lie in different index groups. The method introduced in the paper replaces this rigid partitioning with a structured product of shallow exchange layers. Each shallow layer only mixes coordinates in small, localized pairs or small groups, yet when many such layers are composed the overall operator can reach patterns that a single blockwise actor of the same per-block size cannot.

Intuitively, the reason for this larger reachable set is that composition allows gradual propagation of influence across indices. A blockwise operator either connects two coordinates (inside the same block) or not (if they lie in different blocks). By contrast, a composition of pairwise exchange steps can route influence across many indices in a small number of steps, so interactions that would require moving out of a block in the original partition become feasible. In geometric terms, the set of angle-preserving transforms obtainable with a product of many shallow, structured layers spans a strictly larger subset of the full angle-preserving group than the set obtained when those same shallow layers are constrained to act only in parallel independent groups.

The paper formalizes this claim: with the same per-block budget (the same size of the local mixing unit), arranging those local units across several staggered layers yields strictly greater representational reach than keeping them isolated in a single stage. Practically, this means one can keep the same per-unit footprint (which controls how many free coordinates are allotted per local unit) yet obtain richer global rearrangements by increasing the depth of composition. This provides a principled way to trade counting of free degrees of freedom for expressive global mixing without increasing the per-unit footprint.

# Approximating random angle-preserving operators: error versus tunables

A direct way to probe representational reach is to attempt to approximate a randomly sampled angle-preserving operator (a random rotation-like operator) and measure how well a given structured family can match it as the number of free coordinates grows. The experiments in the source study carried out exactly this stress test at moderate dimensionalities. Each structured family is specified by two knobs: the local mixing size and the number of sequential stages. For a fixed allotment of free coordinates, structures that spread their degrees of freedom across log-depth, staggered stages tend to match random targets with smaller residual error than structures that concentrate the same allotment into independent blocks.

Two qualitative trends emerge from those empirical curves. First, increasing the total number of free coordinates always reduces approximation error, but the slope of error reduction depends on how those degrees of freedom are organized: staged, staggered architectures obtain a steeper improvement per added free coordinate. Second, for a fixed per-local-unit footprint, increasing the number of sequential stages yields stronger returns than simply enlarging each local unit. Put differently, depth with narrow units tends to be a more efficient use of degrees of freedom than shallow but wide units, insofar as approaching a generic rotation-like target is the goal.

These findings illuminate the design choice behind the structured composition: a logarithmic number of shallow stages can produce dense, global mixing while keeping the number of free coordinates near-linear in the ambient dimension. This yields practical savings in the budget for adaptation while retaining good ability to match complex angle-preserving transforms.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchies: alternating products of exchange-pattern operators and their transposes

A striking constructive route to approach the full set of angle-preserving operators is to interleave structured exchange-pattern operators with their transposes (or inverses), repeating such alternation multiple times. The paper refers to such alternating chains as a hierarchy that mirrors the classical idea of composing different sparse mixing steps in sequence, and shows that as the number of alternations grows, the family of reachable transforms expands and can in the limit approximate the entire group of angle-preserving operators.

Formally, let each shallow exchange operator be denoted by \(B_{i}\), where each \(B_{i}\) itself is highly structured and easy to parametrize. Consider compositions of the form
\[
R = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\bigl(B_{\ell}\,B_{\ell}'{}^{\!\top}\bigr),
\]
where \(B_{\ell}'\) denotes a second structured operator at level \(\ell\) (often a shuffled version of \(B_{\ell}\)). The alternating product of such pairs increases the variety of column permutations and sign/rotation mixes that can be realized. The theoretical message is that, with sufficiently many alternations and appropriately chosen structured blocks, this family becomes dense in the full set of angle-preserving operators: any rotation-like operator can be approximated arbitrarily well by some finite composition of these patterned factors.

This construction is sometimes called a kaleidoscope-style hierarchy because repeated alternating reflections and small rotations produce very rich global effects, reminiscent of how repeated reflections and symmetries in a kaleidoscope create complex patterns from simple local mirrors. From a practical standpoint, the hierarchy offers a knob to trade added stage count for greater coverage of the target space, while retaining efficient per-stage representation and low per-stage storage.

# Preservation of largest singular value: role in stability and generalization

One of the central technical virtues of constraining the left-multiplier to be angle-preserving is the preservation of all singular values of the original mapping. If the frozen preexisting linear map is written in its singular value decomposition form as
\[
W^{(0)} = U\,\Sigma\,V^{\top},
\]
where \(U\) and \(V\) have orthonormal columns and \(\Sigma\) is diagonal with nonnegative entries, then left-multiplying by an angle-preserving operator \(R\) yields
\[
R\,W^{(0)} = (R\,U)\,\Sigma\,V^{\top}.
\]
Because \(R\,U\) remains a matrix with orthonormal columns, the diagonal entries of \(\Sigma\) (the singular values of \(W^{(0)}\)) are unchanged. In particular, the largest singular value, which controls Lipschitz continuity and worst-case amplification of perturbations, is preserved exactly.

Why does this matter for stability and generalization during adaptation? Preserving the largest singular value prevents the adapted linear mapping from becoming more amplifying than the original in any direction. This constrains the operator norm and thereby guards gradient magnitudes and signal propagation through downstream processing. Empirical and theoretical work in related areas has repeatedly shown that keeping spectral magnitudes in check reduces instability and the tendency to over-specialize to a small training set. The structured compositions discussed in this chapter inherit this preservation: each shallow stage is constrained to be angle-preserving, so their product likewise preserves singular values of the frozen factor that multiplies them from the right. This property is a principled route to ensure that the specialized mapping remains well-conditioned with respect to the original pre-adaptation geometry.

# Two-sided linear form view: estimating the rotation-like operator in \(w_i^{(0)} R x\)

It is instructive to rewrite the per-output computation in a way that isolates the learned operator. If the \(i\)-th column of the frozen map is written as \(w_i^{(0)}\), and the input is \(x\), the pre- and post-adaptation inner product becomes
\[
\text{output}_i = \bigl(w_i^{(0)}\,R\bigr)\cdot x = w_i^{(0)}\,\bigl(R\,x\bigr).
\]
Viewed this way, adaptation reduces to learning how to transform inputs before they are matched against the original column \(w_i^{(0)}\), or equivalently how to rotate the original column before taking its inner product with the input. This is a two-sided linear form: the scalar output is linear in both the stored column and the input, with the learned operator \(R\) sitting in the middle.

Constraining \(R\) to be angle-preserving means that the learned similarity preserves pairwise angles among transformed inputs or among transformed columns, depending on perspective. From the metric viewpoint, such a constraint amounts to learning a similarity that re-orients directions without stretching or squashing distances. In applications where semantic relationships are encoded by angles and relative directions in activation space, this re-orientation-only restriction can be a desirable regularizer: it enables flexible matching through rotation while avoiding distortions that would change scale relations and could corrupt previously learned geometry.

Thinking in terms of similarity learning clarifies why modest, structured families for \(R\) can be powerful. The adaptation task often needs to rotate the representation to better align with new labels or prompts; having access to a rich but constrained set of rotations is sufficient in many cases, and it keeps the learned change focused on relabeling directions rather than altering the entire scale structure of the original representation.

# Built-in structural prior: alignment with global mixing transforms

The staged exchange-pattern construction carries an implicit structural prior. Each shallow stage mixes only small groups of coordinates, and the pattern of those groups changes across stages in a carefully designed schedule. This design mirrors the structure of many classical fast orthonormal transforms: they achieve global reordering and mixing by composing many localized pairwise or small-group operations in a recursive fashion. Thus the staged composition is predisposed to realize transforms that spread and recombine local signals into global patterns.

Why is this prior useful? Many signals of interest—images, text-derived embeddings, or feature activations—have both local and global structure. A transform that can readily rearrange and combine local pieces into coherent global modes aligns well with the kinds of adjustments typically needed when a large frozen mapping is specialized to a new task. In plain language, the staged exchange pattern favors global rearrangements that are built from many small, interpretable steps, which tends to match the sorts of geometric corrections that downstream tasks require.

The source work emphasizes that certain classical orthonormal transforms (for example, the Hadamard transform and other fast, structured orthonormal maps) are special cases of this staged architecture. Therefore, adopting this staged architecture imports a beneficial prior: it is well suited to represent structured global mixes while keeping each stage compact and cheap to store.

# Implicit regularization from products of mostly-zero operators

Composing many shallow operators that each have only a small set of active entries induces an implicit regularizing effect. Even if the overall product can reach complex global rearrangements, the path by which coordinates influence each other must be routed through a series of localized mixes. This makes extreme, highly irregular reshaping of geometry less likely unless many degrees of freedom are expended. In optimization, such constraints frequently lead to smoother solutions: gradient-based adjustments tend to propagate gradually through the chain of small stages, which discourages sudden, large distortions that would require synchronized, coordinated changes across many entries.

The literature has documented several mechanisms through which structured reparametrizations produce implicit regularization. First, by reducing the effective number of independent free coordinates for a fixed storage budget, the hypothesis set is constrained, which typically reduces overfitting risk. Second, the optimization landscape can become friendlier: structured compositions often break a large global adjustment into many smaller, weakly coupled subadjustments, which can help iterative solvers find moderate solutions more reliably. Third, initializing the composition at the identity (or an identity-like operator) and allowing only gradual deviations produces an implicit trust region that keeps the adapted mapping near the pretrained geometry until evidence from the adaptation objective warrants larger moves.

Together, these effects explain why the staged composition can yield strong practical generalization despite using fewer degrees of freedom than a completely free left-multiplier. The structured product manages to capture the kinds of global reorientations that downstream tasks need, while imposing a form of smoothness and parsimony that helps avoid over-specialization.

Concluding remarks

The construction studied in the source work offers a principled compromise between the extremes of (a) leaving the left-multiplier entirely free (which grants universal coverage but risks instability and overfitting) and (b) restricting it to disjoint local blocks (which is stable but too rigid to represent many useful transforms). By composing many shallow, structured exchange stages, one attains a family that is both expressive—able to reach rich global reorientations—and regularized—preserving singular values and encouraging smooth, interpretable adjustments. The connections to classical fast structured transforms, the preservation of singular-value geometry, the two-sided similarity interpretation, and the empirical approximation trends together provide a coherent theoretical picture for why this design works well in practice.

Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Benchmarks and tasks: large ViTs, LLMs, and text-to-image diffusion
The experiments in the paper span three broad categories of large pretrained systems: vision transformers for visual recognition, large autoregressive networks for language modeling, and diffusion-based generators for text-conditional image synthesis. For vision, the target tasks include standard classification and transfer recognition tasks where the pretrained vision transformer is adapted to new labels or domains and final classification accuracy is reported. For language, the targets include next-token prediction and handfuls of downstream language tasks where perplexity and task scores measure quality of the adapted network. For generative image work, the targets evaluate how well a base text-conditional generator aligns with new textual concepts or with held-out prompts; generative quality is measured with fidelity/diversity statistics and alignment-type scores specific to text-to-image benchmarks.

Across these settings the paper studies the same class of left-multiplicative transforms applied to frozen base weight matrices. That is, the original linear layer output $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ is replaced by $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal and has a compact factorized form. The experiments test how this approach behaves when the transform uses the butterfly-style factorization (BOFT) with various choices of stage count $m$ and chunk width $b$, and how it compares when the full left transform is updated directly or when alternative compact update strategies are used.

# Baselines: full-weight update, LoRA and variants, OFT partitioned-diagonal, inserted modules
The evaluations compare the proposed factorized orthogonal transform to several widely used adaptation strategies. One reference point is a full-weight update that lets all entries of the linear layers change; this serves as an upper bound on representational flexibility. A popular compact additive scheme that is also included is the low-rank additive update (LoRA), which augments a frozen weight by a product of two low-rank maps that are added to the base. The previously proposed orthogonal left-multiplicative approach with partitioned diagonal form (the original OFT) is evaluated as the orthogonal prior with a grouped diagonal pattern on $\bm{R}$. Finally, approaches that inject small extra modules into the network architecture (often called adapter inserts in the literature) are included for comparison as another way to reduce the count of adjustable scalars while still altering network behavior.

These reference strategies test complementary points along the trade-off: full updates maximize representational richness but carry a high cost in adjustable scalars; additive low-rank updates reduce that cost by restricting updates to a narrow subspace; partitioned-diagonal orthogonal transforms enforce angular preservation with grouped structure; and module inserts change architecture locally. BOFT is evaluated against all of these to show where it lands in the accuracy-versus-compactness trade-off.

# Metrics: accuracy / perplexity / FID and task-alignment measures
To compare methods across modalities the paper reports standard, interpretable metrics suited to each task family. For classification tasks the primary number is top-line classification accuracy on held-out sets. For language experiments the main metric is perplexity on validation or held-out text, complemented by task-specific scores when relevant. For image synthesis the paper reports common generative quality measures such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) along with alignment-type measures that quantify how well generated images match the guiding text prompts (these may include task-specific scoring protocols used in the text-to-image literature).

Beyond these task-level metrics, the authors also track optimization traces (loss curves and validation metric trajectories), and measure the match between target dense orthogonal matrices and their approximations in a controlled simulation. Where comparisons involve compact transforms, the plots use the count of adjustable scalars as the horizontal axis so that accuracy (or FID or perplexity) can be read against compactness. The formula that links design choices to adjustable-scalar count for BOFT is reported in the paper: the number of adjustable scalars for BOFT($m$, $b$) grows linearly in the ambient dimension $d$ and in the number of stages $m$, specifically as
\[ \text{adjustables} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m, \]
which makes the compactness comparison transparent when $d$ and $m$ vary.

# Results overview: BOFT’s accuracy–compactness trade-off improvements
The empirical picture is consistent across the three modality groups. BOFT occupies a favorable region of the trade-off curve: with far fewer adjustable scalars than a full left update it often matches or exceeds the task accuracy (or matches perplexity / improves FID) of other compact schemes. Against low-rank additive updates, BOFT typically yields better generalization when the same budget of adjustable scalars is used; this is attributed to the multiplicative orthogonal prior that preserves inter-neuron angular relations and spectral properties of the frozen base. Compared with the partitioned-diagonal orthogonal transform, BOFT attains dense, global left transformations while keeping a compact factor form; empirically this produces higher final scores for the same compactness budget, showing that the structured inter-stage mixing of info in the butterfly factorization pays off.

In generative image adaptation, BOFT achieves stronger alignment with textual guidance and often yields improved fidelity metrics relative to additive low-rank updates and to group-diagonal orthogonal updates, while using substantially fewer adjustable scalars than a full update. In vision transfer tasks BOFT reaches near-full-update accuracies at a small fraction of the adjustable-scalar count. For language perplexity, BOFT reduces validation perplexity under the same scalar budget compared to low-rank additive baselines. Across modalities, the paper emphasizes that BOFT tends to offer a smoother generalization profile as the compactness budget is varied: its curves interpolate gracefully from highly regular (close to identity) transforms to more expressive dense transforms as $m$ and $b$ are tuned.

# Ablations: effects of $m$, $b$, and multiplicative dropout
The paper studies the role of the two key design knobs in BOFT: the number of stages $m$ (how many sparse orthogonal stages are multiplied) and the chunk width $b$ (the inner grouping width used in the block-wise butterfly building blocks). Increasing $m$ deepens the factorization and allows more cross-dimension mixing; for fixed $b$ and dimension $d$, larger $m$ increases the number of adjustable scalars and hence representational richness. Increasing $b$ widens the per-stage groups, which also increases the per-stage degrees of freedom. In practical terms a small $b$ with moderate $m$ often gives the best compactness: the staged sparse mixing can approximate dense transforms while keeping the count of adjustables modest.

The multiplicative dropout scheme introduced for BOFT is also examined in ablation. Because BOFT applies a multiplicative, orthogonal left transform, conventional additive dropout is not directly applicable. The multiplicative dropout randomly replaces entire stages or a fraction of the per-stage groups with identity at each optimization step. The empirical effect is analogous to conventional dropout: it reduces overfitting in low-data or narrow-budget regimes and produces better validation performance. The paper reports that judicious choices of the stage-level and group-level replacement probabilities yield measurable improvements in generalization without harming final expressiveness when the compactness budget is increased.

# Stability analysis: optimization smoothness and convergence behavior
An important experimental finding is that BOFT inherits the favorable spectral properties of orthogonal left transformations. Multiplying the frozen layer on the left with an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values of the original weight block, so the adapted linear map keeps its spectral norm. This preservation stabilizes the optimization dynamics: loss and validation metric traces are smoother, gradients exhibit less extreme scaling, and optimization tends to avoid abrupt jumps that can occur when all entries are updated freely. Compared to low-rank additive updates, BOFT typically shows less variance in validation trajectories across random seeds and hyperparameter jitter, indicating more stable convergence.

The paper also notes a small overhead at optimization time for BOFT because the factorized orthogonal transform requires multiple sparse multiplies at each forward/backward pass. However, this cost is confined to the adaptation phase: after adaptation the left factors can be consolidated into the frozen base, producing no inference overhead. Thus, the optimization-time overhead is a one-time expense that buys compactness and stability during adaptation.

# Approximation study: 512\times512 orthogonal matrix fitting curves
To isolate representational questions the authors run a controlled approximation experiment: a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ with $d=512$ is sampled, and various compact forms attempt to approximate it. The objective is the Frobenius-norm error between the target dense orthogonal and the compactly represented orthogonal. Curves plot approximation error versus adjustable-scalar count. BOFT variants trace markedly better error-versus-budget curves than partitioned-diagonal orthogonal factorizations with the same per-stage group width. For example, a BOFT configuration with many stages and small $b$ attains the same approximation error as a partitioned-diagonal configuration with much larger per-group width while using far fewer adjustables.

This controlled fit study reproduces the intuitive trade-offs: increasing $m$ (more stages) reduces approximation error at fixed $b$; increasing $b$ reduces error at fixed $m$. Importantly, BOFT’s staged sparse mixing yields a pareto front that lies below the partitioned-diagonal front: for the same adjustable-scalar budget BOFT attains lower approximation error, which helps explain the downstream empirical gains seen in vision, language, and generative adaptation tasks.

# Synthesis and practical guidance
The multi-modal experiments support a consistent story. Enforcing an orthogonal left transform and representing it with a butterfly-style factorization yields a compact, stable, and expressive family of transforms that adapt large pretrained networks across different task families. Practitioners should view the two controls $m$ and $b$ as a compactness–richness dial: increase $m$ or $b$ if the task requires more expressive adaptation and computational budget during optimization allows it; keep $b$ small and raise $m$ for strong compactness with high mixing effectiveness. Multiplicative dropout is a practical regularizer in low-data regimes. Finally, although optimization has a modest extra cost during adaptation, the orthogonal factors can be merged with the base weights after adaptation to avoid any inference cost.

Practical Guide and Recipes

# Choosing m and s under storage cap and layer width

When you bring the butterfly orthonormal routine into a pretrained linear array, two design knobs dominate the tradeoffs: the count of composed stages, which we call $m$, and the tile width inside each stage, which we call $s$. These two choices set how many tunable entries are introduced, how much arithmetic the update requires while tuning, and how flexible the final orthonormal operator can be.

A compact bookkeeping expression that is helpful to keep in mind is that the total tunable entry count grows linearly with the layer width and linearly with $m$, and it also scales with $(s-\text{one})$. A convenient symbolic form you can use while planning is
$\displaystyle \frac{\text{one}}{\text{two}}\,(s-\text{one})\,m\,n$,
where $n$ denotes the input dimension of the linear array you are adapting. Reading that expression intuitively: when $s$ is small the extra degrees of freedom per tile are small, and increasing $m$ multiplies how many of those tiles appear in sequence.

Rule of thumb guidance from the experiments and simulations in the source study:

- If storage for tunable entries is very tight, pick a small tile width (prefer $\text{two}$) and a moderate stage count. With tile width set to $\text{two}$ and stage count chosen near the logarithm (base two) of the layer width, the tunable entry budget follows the familiar near-$n\log n$ scaling that the butterfly arrangement can deliver.
- If you want fewer arithmetic steps while tuning (less runtime during adaptation), pick a smaller $m$ and a larger tile width. Larger tiles give more expressiveness per stage but increase the per-stage tunable footprint.
- If you want the densest possible final operator for a fixed tunable storage cap, prefer smaller $s$ and larger $m$. This tends to yield better approximation of a fully general orthonormal operator for the same tunable-entry budget.

When planning, convert your storage allowance into a tunable-entry cap, then solve the symbolic relation above for feasible $(m,s)$ pairs. If you prefer, start with $s=\text{two}$, increase $m$ until the tunable-entry count reaches your cap, and only then experiment with larger $s$ if runtime during tuning becomes the limiting factor.

# Where to insert BOFT in transformer blocks and in diffusion U‑Nets

The orthonormal butterfly routine multiplies a frozen pretrained linear array on the left with a learned orthonormal operator that is shared across the neurons of that layer. In practice this makes the routine naturally fit at any place where a linear mapping is used and where preserving the angular relations among neuron vectors is desirable.

In transformer stacks common insertion sites are the projection arrays inside the attention model and the two linear sublayers inside the feedforward subnetwork (the part often called the positionwise feedforward or MLP). In attention modules, applying the routine to the query and key projection arrays tends to be especially impactful when the new task primarily requires reorienting how the model computes similarity. In the feedforward subnetwork, applying the routine to the first projection (the expansion from model width to inner width) gives a strong lever to manipulate intermediate features while retaining the original spectral scale of the pretrained array.

For encoder–decoder and decoder‑only setups you can treat each linear mapping as a candidate; when in doubt, prefer the first linear in a two‑stage MLP or the attention projections that are closest to the output used in the target task.

For diffusion U‑Nets the same insertion idea applies: apply the orthonormal routine to the linear arrays in the residual blocks and to the cross‑attention projections that fuse conditioning signals. In the source experiments these positions yielded significant gains while preserving stable dynamics during sampling.

A practical prioritization when you want to limit tunable entries is to start with a small set of locations that are most task‑proximal (the projections right before task heads, cross‑attention injectors, or the first dense mapping in the MLP) and expand the set only if adaptation performance is insufficient.

# Default choices by modality and model scale

While every use case benefits from a short tuning sweep, the following defaults are a compact starting point that reflect the empirical findings in the source study.

Vision models (image encoders, image classification backbones)
- Small to moderate model widths (hundreds to low thousands): start with $s=\text{two}$ and $m$ around $\lceil \log_{\text{two}}(n/s)\rceil$; this yields very compact tunable footprint and often preserves the pretrained spectral behavior.
- Very wide models (several thousands): still prefer $s=\text{two}$ for storage parsimoniousness; increase $m$ so the composed operator can cover the necessary mixing.

Language models (decoder stacks and encoder‑decoder)
- For medium language stacks, put the routine on key linear projections in attention and on the expansion linear in the MLP. Default $s=\text{two}$, and set $m$ so the tunable-entry count is roughly on par with light additive updates that the model team might already be comfortable with.
- For very large language stacks, the small tile width plus more stages is the common strategy: it keeps per‑layer tunable entries modest while allowing sufficient flexibility overall.

Diffusion generative models (U‑Nets for image synthesis)
- Cross‑attention heads and early residual blocks are prime targets. Again, $s=\text{two}$ with moderate $m$ gives a compact, high‑quality adaptation.

These defaults are intentionally conservative: small tile width and moderate stage count yield a strong regularizing prior, often improving generalization while keeping tuning stable.

# Combining BOFT with low‑precision storage and other compact adapters

The orthonormal butterfly routine composes multiplicatively with the frozen pretrained array. This makes it naturally compatible with several other compact adaptation tricks.

Low‑precision storage. If you keep the pretrained arrays in low precision for storage economy, the multiplicative routine can be trained normally because the learned orthonormal operator is a separate set of tunable entries. Two practical approaches work well:
- Keep the pretrained arrays in the same low‑precision format during tuning, and train the tunable entries in higher precision when you perform the small inversions needed for the orthonormal mapping. Use higher numeric precision for the local linear algebra that enforces orthonormality while keeping the bulk storage compact.
- Alternatively, fuse the learned orthonormal operator into the pretrained array after tuning and then re-quantize the fused array for deployment. Fusion eliminates any extra cost at serving time.

Combining with additive compact updates. The multiplicative orthonormal routine does not conflict with additive two‑factor updates (the family that updates a pretrained array by adding a product of two small arrays). If your workflow already uses an additive compact adapter, you can:
- Train the multiplicative routine while keeping the additive adapter frozen, or
- Train both simultaneously, allowing the multiplicative routine to reorient the pretrained space and the additive adapter to provide small residual corrections.
Empirically, using both can sometimes improve final performance, since the multiplicative part preserves spectrum and coarse geometry while the additive part supplies fine localized change.

When combining methods keep an eye on total tunable‑entry budget and on numerical precision during orthonormal enforcement steps.

# Runtime and memory: tuning overhead versus deployment parity

Two practical performance notes come from the source study.

Tuning time. Because the orthonormal operator is composed as a small sequence of staged arrays, each update requires applying that sequence during the forward and backward passes. This composition yields a modest runtime overhead during adaptation compared with an approach that only updates a single small additive term. The overhead grows with $m$ because each composed stage incurs arithmetic and gradient work.

Memory in tuning. The tunable entries described earlier are typically a small extra memory footprint compared with keeping the pretrained arrays in memory. However, automatic differentiation needs to keep intermediate activations for the composed stages during the backward pass; for very large $m$ this can increase peak memory use. Two practical mitigations are checkpointing of intermediate stage activations and using the smaller tile width ($s=\text{two}$) which reduces per‑stage activation size.

Deployment (serving). A useful property is deployment parity: after tuning finishes you can multiply the learned orthonormal operator into the frozen pretrained array and store only the fused array for serving. That means the adaptation introduces no extra cost at run time if you perform fusion and then reformat the fused array into your deployment precision. In short: modest extra cost while tuning, zero overhead after fusion for serving.

# Debugging tips: loss of orthonormality, NaN issues in inversion, numeric fixes

Because the routine keeps each stage orthonormal via a rational mapping that inverts an array like $(I - Q)$ where $Q$ is skew‑symmetric, a few numerical pathologies can arise during tuning. Here are practical checks and remedies.

Monitor orthonormality. Periodically compute the deviation metric $\|R^\top R - I\|_F$ for a few representative adapted operators during tuning. If this deviation grows, consider the mitigations below. Avoid the banned practice of trusting final loss alone to detect drift.

Small step sizes for the skew update. Use conservative learning gains for the variables that parameterize the skew‑symmetric $Q$ arrays. When those gains are too aggressive the mapping inversion can approach singularity and cause instabilities.

Add a tiny diagonal jitter. If you see near‑singular inversion when forming $(I-Q)^{-1}$, add a tiny multiple of the identity to the term being inverted: invert $(I-Q+\varepsilon I)$ with $\varepsilon$ set to a tiny scalar like $10^{-6}$ in double precision. This stabilizes the solver and prevents dramatic condition number blowups.

Use higher precision for inversion. If your training stack supports mixed precision, allocate the inversion and any orthonormal checks to higher precision (for example running those few linear solves in FP64 or FP32 while the bulk model stays in a lower format). This keeps the core cost small but helps numerical robustness.

Clip skew entries. If NaN appears, as an emergency measure clip the entries of the skew variable $Q$ to a bounded range before computing the rational mapping. Clipping bounds the condition number growth and can recover training.

Periodic reinitialization of stages. If a stage has drifted badly, replace that stage with the identity operator $I$ for a short warmup and then resume tuning. Replacing with $I$ restores the original pretrained orientation for that stage while leaving other stages intact.

Watch learning rate interaction with other adapters. If you combine the multiplicative routine with additive adapters, ensure their learning settings are balanced. The rational mapping is more sensitive to large, rapid moves in its tunable entries than many additive updates.

When you see NaN in gradients, trace whether it originates in the inversion step, in exceptionally large activations after composition, or in an unrelated part of the model. Isolating the routine by running a small model or a tiny batch often reveals the root cause quickly.

# Worked example: an eight‑wide linear array with $m=\text{three}$ and $s=\text{two}$ — shapes and tunable counts

This minimal worked example illustrates shapes and the tunable‑entry tally in concrete terms. Suppose the linear array you are adapting maps an input vector of width $n=\text{eight}$ to some output. You choose stage count $m=\text{three}$ and tile width $s=\text{two}$.

Structure of stages. Each stage is a tiled operator that is identity‑like except for per‑tile small orthonormal tiles of size $s\times s$. With $n=\text{eight}$ and tile width $s=\text{two}$ there are $\text{four}$ tiles per stage (since eight divided by two yields four tiles). Each tile contributes tunable degrees of freedom that, for an $s=\text{two}$ orthonormal tile, equals one free scalar per tile when using a skew‑symmetric two‑by‑two parametrization and the Cayley‑style rational mapping.

Tunable‑entry count. Using the bookkeeping formula in words, the total tunable entries are one half times $(s-\text{one})$ times $m$ times $n$. Substituting $s=\text{two}$, $m=\text{three}$ and $n=\text{eight}$ gives the final tunable count of twelve. In plain terms, this tiny setup introduces twelve learnable scalars that implement a composed orthonormal reorientation for the eight‑wide layer.

Shapes during forward pass. Conceptually, the composed operator is the composition of three staged tiled operators. During a forward apply you perform three small tiled applies of shapes that each read an $n$‑length vector and write an $n$‑length vector. Internally each tile handles a pair of coordinates at a time when $s=\text{two}$, so the arithmetic per stage is small while the composition across stages yields a surprising mixing effect across the full eight coordinates.

Practical takeaways from this toy count. Twelve tunable scalars for an eight‑wide array is extremely compact and illustrates why the butterfly composition can provide rich reorientation for little storage cost. Scaling this pattern to realistic widths yields the familiar near‑$n\log n$ economy when $s=\text{two}$ and $m$ is chosen near the logarithm of the width.

- Convert your allowable tunable‑entry budget into a target and select $(m,s)$ that satisfy $\frac{\text{one}}{\text{two}}\,(s-\text{one})\,m\,n \le$ budget.
- Start by applying the routine to the most task‑proximal linear arrays (attention projections and the first linear in MLPs). Expand only if needed.
- Default start: $s=\text{two}$; set $m$ so tunable budget is respected. Tune conservatively at first.
- During tuning use higher precision for the inversion steps and include a tiny jitter $\varepsilon$ on the inverted term if instability appears.
- After tuning, fuse the learned operator into the pretrained array and reformat for deployment to avoid any serving cost.

Extensions and Connections

# R^G: alternating products and coverage of the full orthogonal set

A key general idea expands the orthogonal-butterfly construction by allowing alternating products of two families of structured orthogonal transforms. Concretely, denote a left-type orthogonal operator as $\bm{R}_{i,1}(m_1,b_1)$ and a right-type orthogonal operator as $\bm{R}_{i,2}(m_2,b_2)$; the generalized form is written as
$$
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
=\prod_{t=1}^{l}\big(\bm{R}_{t,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{t,2}(m_2,b_2)^\top\big),
$$
where the product runs in an alternating fashion and each factor is an orthonormal linear transform. The paper shows that, when the small block size in each structured factor is minimal and the count $l$ of alternating pairs is large enough, this kind of composition can span the entire set of orthonormal transforms of a given dimension. Intuitively, each pair of factors acts like a set of local rotations and permutations that, when chained many times, can move signal content from any input coordinate to any output coordinate while preserving inner-product geometry.

Why does alternating help? Each single structured transform has limited wiring: it moves information in a restricted pattern across stages. By alternating with a complementary structured transform and repeating, one obtains a richer global wiring while keeping each per-stage transform compact. This creates a trade: a single long, fully flexible orthonormal operator has many adjustable entries and thus high capacity, but it is costly to represent directly. The alternating-product family attains near-universal representational reach while keeping each factor small, so the total count of adjustable entries grows much more slowly than in an unconstrained full operator. From a practical stance, this generalized form provides knobs to tune expressiveness via the number of alternations $l$ and the internal block sizes $b_1,b_2$ while preserving the desirable spectral properties that come with orthonormal left multiplication.

# Hybrid designs: combining butterfly transforms with compact add-on updates

Pure multiplicative orthonormal transforms (as in the butterfly chain) emphasize preserving geometric relationships in the original coefficient arrays. An orthogonal-butterfly chain changes the coordinate frame without altering singular-value magnitudes. In contrast, compact additive updates — the family of techniques that add a small structured correction to an existing linear mapping — permit direct modification of amplitude directions with a controlled footprint. Combining the two families yields a hybrid where a compact add-on corrects amplitudes while an orthonormal chain reorders and rotates coordinates to expose the most useful directions for that correction.

There are two natural ways to combine them. One places a compact additive correction after an orthonormal chain so that the correction operates in a rotated coordinate frame; this often reduces the needed size of the add-on because the orthonormal chain can concentrate task-relevant content into a few coordinates. The other inserts the add-on before the orthonormal chain, letting the subsequent rotations distribute the corrections more broadly if desired. In equations, if $\bm{R}$ is a structured orthonormal product and $\Delta$ denotes a compact additive operator with few degrees of freedom, one may use either
$$
\text{updated linear map} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 + \Delta
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\text{updated linear map} = (\bm{R} + \Delta)\,\bm{W}^0,
$$
where $\bm{W}^0$ stands for the original core linear operator. The first choice keeps the orthonormal action pure and places corrections after rotation; the second slightly relaxes the strict orthonormal action while still imposing strong structure.

From an intuition viewpoint, think of the orthonormal chain as a set of mechanical gears that re-route motion without amplification or damping, and the compact add-on as a small actuator that injects a targeted force. The gear train alone preserves energy norms, while the actuator allows controlled departures. Practitioners can exploit this complementarity: use a very compact add-on if the orthonormal chain already aligns important directions, or increase add-on size if amplitude changes are crucial.

# Alternative structured arrays: Monarch, Pixelated Butterfly, and permutations

The butterfly topology is one among several structured wiring schemes that give dense global connectivity through many sparse stages. Variants alter local wiring motifs, grouping pattern, or the kinds of small orthonormal blocks employed at each stage. Two notable variants mentioned in the work are the Monarch family and the pixelated form.

The Monarch family arranges stages so that local mixing occurs within patterned tiles and those tiles shift and interleave across stages. This creates a kind of hierarchical interleave that can reproduce classic fast transforms and yet offers a different inductive bias than the canonical butterfly. The pixelated form treats each local entry as itself a small block (a tile), enabling blockwise permutations and local rotations that resemble image processing pipelines where local neighborhoods interact more strongly at early stages and long-range mixing appears later.

Another simple but powerful ingredient is the insertion of fixed coordinate permutations between structured stages. Permutations themselves are orthonormal and cheap to encode; they change where local mixing acts next and can dramatically expand the set of global transforms reachable with a given collection of small rotations. The combination of small orthonormal blocks, inter-stage permutations, and tiled grouping yields a palette of structured arrays that trade off compactness and flexibility in varied ways. Choosing among these forms is an important design decision that affects behavior on specific adaptation tasks.

# Beyond butterfly: other information-transmission topologies from communication layouts

The information-transmission viewpoint treats the composition of sparse stages as routing channels on a layered grid. The butterfly is a celebrated topology because it achieves full routing with a logarithmic number of stages and only linear wiring per stage. Yet the literature on communication layouts offers many alternate topologies with interesting trade-offs that may inspire new constructions for compact orthonormal transforms.

Examples include fat-tree layouts, folded-Clos designs, toroidal meshes, and hypercube interconnects. Each has its own pattern of short hops and long-range shortcuts. For instance, a folded-Clos topology achieves high bisection throughput by arranging multiple intermediate concentrators; translated into staged linear transforms, such a pattern would mix groups of coordinates through concentrator stages, then redistribute, and so on. A hypercube arrangement pairs coordinates that differ in a single binary digit at each stage; chained stages can therefore disseminate local information across all coordinates in logarithmic depth, similar to butterfly, but the wiring regularity differs. Designs inspired by toroidal meshes favor locality and might be attractive when signals have intrinsic spatial order.

Adopting a communication-inspired layout is essentially choosing a wiring policy for how information can flow across stages. The best choice depends on where task-relevant interactions live: if those interactions align with frequency-like decompositions, butterfly-style mixes shine; if locality dominates, mesh-inspired layouts may work well; if throughput and redundancy are priorities, folded-Clos patterns may help. Exploring these alternatives can yield compact orthonormal constructions that better match the structure of the target adaptation task.

# Automatic structure selection: discovering thin connectivity and stage depth

Manual selection of wiring patterns and stage count is practical for small experiments but impractical at scale. The paper sketches paths toward automating this process so that the wiring layout and the number of stages adapt to task signals. Two broad approaches exist.

One approach treats the presence or strength of links across stages as variables to be adjusted during a fitting run. Starting from an over-complete set of possible local links, one may impose a penalty that favors few active links or few stages, then optimize both link strengths and the usual fit objective. In practice, direct combinatorial sparsification is hard, so continuous relaxation techniques are used: give each candidate local link a gating scalar and encourage those scalars to shrink to trivial values, then prune near-zero gates to obtain a compact layout. This yields an emergent wiring pattern that reflects which local mixes the task actually needed.

A second approach searches in the space of architectures using a controller that proposes layouts and a performance signal that scores them. This meta-search can be guided by heuristics drawn from the information-transmission viewpoint: prefer layouts that quickly reach full connectivity while keeping per-stage wiring small. One may also allow variable stage depth, letting the search discover whether short or deep chains suit the task.

Both approaches face a common balance: heavy search or elaborate gate tuning increases the fitting overhead, whereas lightweight rules may under-explore. Hybrid strategies combine an initial coarse search with local refinement via gating. Practical implementations also exploit warm-starts: initialize candidate transforms close to identity so that early optimization steps do not destabilize the core system.

# Connections to distance-based forms and kernel methods

Orthogonal multiplicative transforms have a direct interpretation in the language of distance measures and bilinear similarity. Multiplying an input by an orthonormal operator before taking inner products with frozen columns preserves pairwise angles while reorienting those columns in ambient space. Thus, adjusting an orthonormal operator corresponds to re-shaping which directions are emphasized by simple inner-product similarity tests.

This viewpoint links the orthonormal-butterfly family to classical metric shaping tasks: in a distance-based paradigm one seeks a transform $T$ that, when applied, makes Euclidean distances align with desired criteria. When $T$ is restricted to be orthonormal, distances remain unchanged in magnitude but relative coordinate allocation shifts; if $T$ is allowed to be a short-additive correction on top of an orthonormal chain, actual stretching or compressing of directions becomes possible, enabling richer metric reweighting.

There is also a kernel perspective. Many kernel approximation techniques proceed by applying a structured linear transform to the input followed by a nonlinear map; fast transforms such as Hadamard or Fourier structures have been used to quickly compute randomized features that approximate shift-invariant kernels. The butterfly family and its variants are natural candidates for such structured random-projection stages because they deliver fast, dense mixing with few adjustable numbers. In an adapted system, structured orthonormal transforms can be tuned to concentrate kernel-relevant energy into a small set of coordinates that follow with nonlinearities, improving sample efficiency of the approximation.

From a practical viewpoint, these connections suggest hybrid recipes: use a compact orthonormal chain to preprocess inputs into a coordinate frame where a small additive kernel sketch suffices, or learn an orthonormal chain that directly matches pairwise similarity targets on held-out examples. In both cases, the orthonormal structure brings numeric stability and spectral preservation, while compact add-ons and kernel sketches bring amplitude flexibility.

# Final remarks

The landscape around structured orthonormal transforms is rich. Alternating products can approach full orthonormal expressiveness while keeping per-stage complexity small; hybrids with compact additive corrections offer complementary strengths; many tile, permutation, and communication-inspired topologies broaden the design space; and automatic selection mechanisms can tailor wiring and depth to task needs. Finally, the geometric links to metric shaping and to kernel approximation provide principled motivations for why structured, orthonormal constructions often yield strong general-purpose behavior in practical adaptation scenarios.

Limitations, Risks, and Open Problems

# Run-time overhead from many thin stages
The method constructs a global transform as a product of several stage-wise, thin linear transforms. While that product yields a compact description in terms of tunable entries, it requires applying multiple transform stages in sequence during the adaptation pass. For a layer that maps a $d$-dimensional input to an $n$-dimensional output, the cost of applying the composed transform scales roughly with the number of stages times the cost of a single stage. In practice this means the per-batch compute and the memory needed for backpropagation during the adaptation step can be noticeably larger than for schemes that only add a single small additive update.

There are mitigation patterns, each with trade-offs. One option is to fold the learned composition into the frozen weight array after adaptation, which removes any inference overhead but does not reduce the extra work during the adaptation process itself. Another is to fuse adjacent stage operations into custom kernels so that the sequence of light-weight multiplies becomes one wider kernel launch, lowering runtime overhead. Gradient checkpointing and mixed precision update can reduce peak memory use at the price of some extra compute. Finally, a mixed strategy that applies only a subset of stages per mini-batch or alternates which stages receive updates can trade per-step cost against convergence speed; this can be useful when wall-clock budget during adaptation is tight. Understanding the sweet spot among these choices for different hardware is an important systems question.

# Scaling to arbitrary $d$ and padding choices
The structured factor design is most naturally framed when the internal dimensionality $d$ conforms to a regular decomposition strategy. When $d$ does not align with that pattern, practitioners typically embed the vectors into a slightly larger dimension $d'$ that does fit, apply the structured chain, and then slice back to the original size. This padding trick is practical but not neutral: it can introduce wrap-around effects, impose artificial grouping of coordinates, and change how signals are mixed across coordinates.

Alternatives to naive padding include grouping channels into irregular blocks with custom-permutation maps, or designing stage primitives that operate on mixed-size blocks so that the pipeline accepts a broad range of $d$ without large expansion. Each choice affects runtime and representational trade-offs. For instance, padding to a larger $d'$ increases the work per stage; irregular grouping complicates kernel design and can reduce throughput. Research directions include principled ways to choose $d'$, to design stage primitives that gracefully handle residual sizes, and to analyze how the padding choice affects adaptation quality for different classes of tasks.

# Choosing stage count $m$ and block width $b$ per layer: principles and rules of thumb
Two structural knobs control the trade-off between representational richness and compactness: the number of sequential stages, which we denote by $m$, and the internal block width that each small stage operates over, denoted by $b$. Increasing $m$ tends to increase the transform depth and the set of reachable maps; increasing $b$ tends to increase local mixing within a stage. Together they govern the expressible family of transforms and the number of tunable entries.

There is no universal recipe that suits all layers. A practical heuristic is to allocate greater effective capacity (larger $b$ or $m$) to layers that are early or late in the pipeline where the pretrained weights are most task-sensitive, and to use leaner settings in mid-depth layers where wholesale change is less necessary. Cross-validation over a small held-out split remains a robust way to select $(m,b)$ per layer, but this can be costly. Automating this choice is an open direction: one could imagine a sparsity-inducing regularizer that drives unnecessary stage blocks to identity, or a budget-aware controller that assigns per-layer budgets subject to a global cost cap. Theoretical guidance that connects per-layer signal statistics to optimal $(m,b)$ is also lacking and would be particularly valuable.

# Provable approximation guarantees and sample needs
On the theoretical side, several foundational questions remain open. One concerns approximation: given constraints on $m$ and $b$, how well can the product of the stage-wise transforms approximate an arbitrary norm-preserving linear map of size $d$ up to a target distortion $\varepsilon$? The paper gives constructive paths showing that with enough stages and suitable block widths the family is dense in the set of norm-preserving maps, but quantitative bounds that link $m$, $b$, $d$, and an approximation error are not tight. Closing that gap requires tools from approximation theory for structured factorizations and from geometric functional analysis.

A related question is sample complexity: for a constrained family of transforms determined by $(m,b)$, how many labeled examples are needed during adaptation to reach a target generalization error on a downstream distribution? Intuitively, smaller effective degrees of freedom should decrease the number of labeled examples required, but the precise dependence on $m$, $b$, and properties of the base frozen weights is unknown. Progress will likely involve combining covering-number or Rademacher-complexity bounds for the structured family with data-dependent measures that capture alignment between the pretrained representation and the target task. Tight, non-vacuous bounds that practitioners can use to select $(m,b)$ for a targeted sample budget are an important open problem.

# Potential failure modes: over-constraint or underfit on niche tasks
The structured product imposes a strong prior on how the pretrained weights can be altered. When this prior matches the needs of the downstream task, the method promotes stable adaptation with limited labeled examples. When it does not, two distinct failure modes can appear.

In one mode the family is too restrictive and the adapted solution underfits: the adaptation cannot realize the needed transform without violating the structural pattern. This tends to occur on tasks that require large, irregular rewiring of internal channels or unusual coordinate permutations. Simple remedies are to increase $m$ or $b$, or to allow a small additive correction pathway in parallel to the multiplicative chain so that large deviations can be expressed more easily.

In the other mode the structured chain can, perhaps unexpectedly, produce degenerate optimization landscapes for certain pretrained weight geometries: gradients may be vanishingly small early in adaptation because the identity initialization combined with the structure makes useful directions hard to discover. Careful initialization strategies, modest scaling of initial update magnitudes, occasional randomized perturbations of stage blocks, or hybrid updates that temporarily permit small unconstrained updates can alleviate this. Characterizing when each failure mode is likely and developing diagnostic signals that predict them during early adaptation are practical research targets.

# Hardware-aware designs: exploiting thin connectivity for speed
The structured pattern offers an opportunity for runtime acceleration, but realizing that opportunity requires aligning the pattern with what actual accelerators support. Off-the-shelf dense linear algebra libraries are not optimized for a sequence of many narrow multiplies interleaved with fixed permutations; naively implementing the stages with generic kernels yields poor throughput. Better performance comes from fusing stage multiplies, laying out weights to favor contiguous memory access for the common stage shapes, and exploiting structured permutations that can be implemented as index remapping rather than costly gathers.

Designing kernels that accept a small repertoire of stage widths and that can batch many stage applications in one fused primitive is promising. On hardware that supports block-sparse or structured-sparse primitives, one can map the stage pattern to those primitives to reduce memory movement. Finally, compiler-level support that recognizes the recurring stage pipeline and emits a single fused instruction stream can unlock large speedups. Co-design across the transform pattern and available hardware primitives is necessary to make the approach attractive in production settings.

# Benchmark gaps and reproducibility considerations
Empirical evaluations to date cover a useful but limited set of tasks and settings. There is a need for broader benchmarks that probe low-resource regimes, long-sequence behavior, multimodal transfer, robustness to domain shift, and tasks that demand drastic reconfiguration of internal representations. Important metrics beyond final task score include wall-clock cost of adaptation, peak memory use during the adaptation step, number of effective tunable entries, and sensitivity to random seed.

For reproducibility, authors and practitioners should publish full configuration artifacts: exact frozen model revisions, per-layer $(m,b)$ choices, optimization schedules, initialization seeds, and terse but complete notes on any fused kernels or custom primitives used. Reference implementations that offer both a straightforward, portable version and a hardware-optimized variant will make comparisons fairer. Benchmarks should report not only mean scores but also variance across runs so that claims of stability are supported.

Concluding remark
The staged, thin-transform approach to compact adaptation offers a compelling mix of regularity and reach. Practical adoption hinges on resolving several systems and theory gaps: lowering adaptation-time cost, handling arbitrary internal sizes cleanly, principled per-layer budgeting, sharpening approximation and sample guarantees, identifying and mitigating failure modes, and delivering hardware-friendly implementations. Progress on these fronts will determine how broadly this style of adaptation can be used in real-world settings.

Summary and Key Takeaways

# BOFT’s central insight: factorize full orthogonal updates via butterfly stages
The core idea behind the method is to represent a large, all-to-all orthogonal transform as a product of many small, structured transforms arranged in a layered pattern. Rather than trying to directly tune every entry of a huge orthogonal operator, BOFT composes that operator from a sequence of lightweight stages that exchange information across the coordinate axes in a carefully organized fashion. Each stage is an orthogonal local rearrangement that touches only a small group of coordinates at a time; when the stages are composed in the butterfly pattern, the composition can effect an all-to-all orthogonal mapping even though each stage touches only a few coordinates.

Thinking in these terms highlights two useful intuitions. First, the difficult global change is achieved by many local updates that route information step by step; this is analogous to how a fast transform moves local frequency pieces into global patterns by repeating a small kernel. Second, each local stage imposes strong internal structure, so the space of reachable orthogonal transforms is constrained in a way that prefers smooth, structured adjustments rather than arbitrary chaotic rotations. That constraint is what lets BOFT be compact yet effective: the method only tunes the small degrees of freedom inside each stage, yet the composition still moves signals across all dimensions.

# Compact economy of tunable scalars: scaling with dimension and stage count
A key practical win of BOFT is that the total count of adjustable scalar values grows much more slowly with ambient dimension than it would if one tried to adjust a dense orthogonal operator directly. Conceptually, an unconstrained orthogonal operator on a large coordinate set carries a very large number of degrees of freedom. BOFT replaces that single large object with a product of many structured stages; each stage contributes a modest handful of scalars, and the product of a logarithmic number of such stages suffices to route information from every input coordinate to every output coordinate.

Put succinctly, when the number of stages grows slowly with the ambient dimension, the total tunable-scalar budget behaves like an order of D times the logarithm of D, rather than order of D times D. Because each stage is itself enforced to be orthogonal, the tunable scalars do not freely fill a full unconstrained space; rather they parameterize a compact, structured family whose effective size scales gently with dimension. This economy is what enables reuse of very large foundation systems: a small, structured set of scalars suffices to customize a large linear mapping that multiplies the frozen base transform.

# Why constrained orthogonal transforms help for transfer and stability
There are two complementary reasons why imposing an orthogonal structure and the butterfly pattern helps when specializing a preexisting foundation.

First, multiplication on the left by an orthogonal operator preserves singular values of the base linear map. Intuitively, left-multiplying by an orthogonal operator performs a rotation of the row space without stretching or shrinking. That preservation limits how much the largest amplification factor of the base mapping can change, which often improves numerical behavior and keeps the overall system in a familiar operating regime during specialization. Practitioners frequently see that such constrained updates are less prone to destabilizing gradients or producing pathological outputs during the early phases of adaptation.

Second, the staged butterfly layout injects an inductive preference for structured transformations that appear in many useful classical transforms (for example, transforms that mix across scales or reorder coordinates in systematic ways). This bias reduces the effective hypothesis set the adaptation procedure searches over. In typical transfer problems, searching over a narrower, structured family can improve out-of-sample performance because the search avoids exotic rotations that destroy previously learned geometric relations. In short, orthogonal regularity helps stability, and the butterfly pattern helps pick structured, useful rotations among the many possible ones.

# Practical viability: performance across visual, textual, and image-creation settings
Empirical evaluations reported alongside this idea show that the approach is not just theoretically neat but also practically effective. When applied to large foundation systems in visual recognition, textual understanding, and image-creation pipelines, the compact butterfly-based orthogonal update often matches or improves on alternatives that either tune many more scalars or add separate small modules. Two factors contribute to this practical success. One is the preservation of spectral norms discussed above, which reduces the risk of exploding or vanishing transformations during specialization. The other is the inductive match between the staged mixing pattern and the kinds of global interactions that many downstream tasks require.

Importantly, after the specialization phase is finished, the staged orthogonal operators can be fused into the base linear maps. This fusion yields a single modified linear map for each layer and introduces no extra run-time cost at deployment. Thus, there is no inference-time penalty for having used the structured multiplicative approach during specialization.

# Actionable guidance: recommended settings and deployment practice
If you want to apply this approach in practice, here are concise recommendations grounded in the reported study.

- Initialization: start each staged orthogonal stage as the identity transform so that initial outputs match the base behavior exactly. A zero-like offset in the internal skew representation yields this identity initialization without manual construction of fused operators.

- Stage granularity and count: choose stage subgroups that are small (so each stage has only a few tunable scalars) and use a modest number of stages that grows with the log of the ambient coordinate count. In practice, a design with many short stages produces a smooth trade-off that tends to be more compact while retaining expressiveness. If extreme compactness is required, increase the number of stages and shrink subgroup size; if maximal expressiveness is required, use fewer, larger subgroups and more total scalars.

- Regularization and dropout: for multiplicative updates, implement dropout by occasionally replacing full local stages with identity stages during specialization. This multiplicative form of dropout keeps the orthogonal constraint intact while discouraging overreliance on any single stage.

- Fusion for deployment: when specialization is complete, multiply the learned staged orthogonal operators into the base linear map and store the fused map. This removes any run-time overhead introduced by the staged representation and yields zero extra run-time cost for deployment.

These heuristic choices balance compactness and expressiveness; they are a good starting point and were effective across the reported use cases.

# Future outlook: better routing patterns and automated structure search
The information-routing perspective that underlies BOFT naturally suggests possible directions for further improvement. The butterfly pattern is a highly efficient routing graph, but it is not the only one; graph topologies studied in network design or combinatorial routing may offer alternative stage arrangements that provide even tighter trade-offs between tunable budget and reachable transforms. Exploring those topologies—whether hand-designed or found by automated search—could yield even more compact specialization recipes that are tailored to particular families of foundation systems.

Another promising direction is automated discovery of stage granularity and stage count for each layer. Rather than fixing a single schematic across a whole system, one could search for per-layer routing patterns that allocate tuner budget where it matters most. Because the staged representation composes orthogonal pieces, such per-layer search remains computationally feasible and yields fused results with no deployment overhead.

Finally, studying other structured constrained transforms that share the preservation and routing properties of BOFT but inject different inductive biases is a fertile area. The broader message of the paper is a simple one: by thinking of a large orthogonal update as an information-routing task, we can achieve compact, stable, and effective specialization strategies that scale to very large foundation systems.

# Bottom line
BOFT trades raw freedom for structured, staged freedom. That trade yields a compact set of tunable scalars that still enables powerful all-to-all orthogonal adjustments, preserves useful spectral properties of the base mapping, and can be fused away for deployment so there is no run-time penalty. For practitioners needing compact and stable specialization of very large foundation systems, BOFT offers a principled and practical option.
