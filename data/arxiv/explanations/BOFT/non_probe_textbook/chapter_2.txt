Mathematical Foundations for Orthogonal Tuning

# Orthogonal operators: angle and norm preservation; singular-value properties

Orthogonal operators are linear maps whose action keeps inner products intact. If a linear map is represented by a symbol $R$ and two column quantities are $u$ and $v$, then the equality
\[
(Ru)^\top (Rv) = u^\top v
\]
holds for every choice of $u$ and $v$ exactly when $R$ satisfies
\[
R^\top R = I.
\]
This identity captures two central geometric consequences. First, lengths are unchanged: $\|Ru\| = \|u\|$ for every $u$. Second, angles are unchanged: the cosine of the angle between two columns is the same before and after application of $R$. These facts make orthogonal maps natural when the goal is to adjust a layer while keeping its internal relational geometry intact.

From a singular-value perspective, an orthogonal map has unit singular values only. If an arbitrary linear map $W$ admits a singular-value decomposition $W = U\Sigma V^\top$, then pre-multiplication by an orthogonal $R$ yields
\[
RW = (RU)\,\Sigma\,V^\top,
\]
so the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$, which record stretch factors along principal directions, do not change. In particular, the largest singular value of $W$ is identical to the largest singular value of $RW$. This implies that the operator norm that controls worst-case amplification of an incoming signal is unchanged by left multiplication with $R$, and that energy scaling properties of the original map remain intact.

A further spectral remark concerns eigenvalues when an orthogonal map acts on a space with inner product. Eigenvalues of an orthogonal operator lie on the unit circle of the complex plane; when real-valued, they correspond to rotations or reflections. These properties collectively explain why orthogonal adjustments are often chosen when one wants modification that is geometrically conservative rather than distortive.

# Cayley mapping: ensuring orthogonality via skew-symmetric entries

To introduce a learnable orthogonal operator while keeping it on the orthogonality manifold at every update, one may re-express orthogonality through an auxiliary skew-symmetric quantity. A skew-symmetric object $Q$ satisfies $Q^\top = -Q$. The Cayley mapping sends such a $Q$ to an orthogonal $R$ by the formula
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
provided the inverse exists. This construction guarantees $R^\top R = I$ because algebraic manipulation yields
\[
R^\top R = \big((I - Q)^{-1}\big)^\top (I + Q)^\top (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
and the skew property of $Q$ makes the middle product collapse to the identity. Thus, any skew-symmetric $Q$ generates a valid orthogonal $R$.

There are two practical consequences of representing orthogonal adjustments via this mapping. First, initializing $Q$ at the zero object gives $R = I$, which provides a neutral start that leaves the original linear map untouched. Second, the free degrees of freedom can be tuned in the smaller space of skew-symmetric objects while algebraically ensuring $R$ remains orthogonal; this avoids numerical projection steps that would otherwise be needed to restore orthogonality after each update.

A further structural point: a skew-symmetric $Q$ carries redundant signs across its off-diagonal entries, so the number of independent scalars that control $R$ is reduced relative to an unconstrained full table of entries. This reduction matches the geometric intuition that the orthogonality constraint is tight: one cannot freely change every entry of $R$ independently and still remain orthogonal.

# Columns as model units: preserving pairwise angles during task-specific update

In many linear layers, the columns of the operator correspond to interpretable units whose mutual relations matter for function. Consider a collection of such columns $\{w_i\}$ gathered in a map $W$. Left-multiplication by an orthogonal $R$ acts on each column by $w_i \mapsto R w_i$. Because inner products are invariant under $R$, the pairwise quantities
\[
(R w_i)^\top (R w_j) = w_i^\top w_j
\]
remain unchanged. Geometrically, the collection of columns experiences a rigid motion: they are rotated and perhaps reflected as a whole, but relative angles and lengths do not change.

This rigid-motion viewpoint offers intuition for why left-multiplication is a conservative form of modification. If the semantics encoded by columns depend on their mutual angles — for example, when cosine similarities guide downstream decisions — then an orthogonal left-action preserves those semantics while still permitting global reorientation. Such a property can help avoid inadvertent destruction of structures learned earlier, which is why orthogonal left-actions are appealing in scenarios where one wishes to alter global alignment without disturbing pairwise relations.

Because columns move coherently, this design encourages stability of inter-unit geometry across adaptation steps. That stability helps maintain features and roles learned in earlier phases while allowing the whole collection to be steered toward a new task by a compact global operator.

# Largest singular value and optimization stability: why preserving $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ matters

The largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ of a linear map $W$ controls how much an incoming perturbation can be amplified in norm. When optimization proceeds by gradient-based updates, gradients back-propagated through a linear map are scaled by factors tied to singular values; thus, very large singular values can magnify gradient magnitudes, leading to instability in step sizes and sensitivity to hyper-choices. Conversely, maintaining a stable bound on $\sigma_{\max}(W)$ helps keep updates well conditioned.

Because a left-orthogonal action leaves singular values unchanged, it preserves this crucial bound. If one starts from a pretrained map whose $\sigma_{\max}$ is already reasonable, then applying an orthogonal left-action will not increase the worst-case amplification. This property contributes to smoother optimization traces and mitigates risks of exploding activations or gradients that can occur when the operator norm grows uncontrolled.

Formally, with $W = U\Sigma V^\top$ and an orthogonal $R$, the composition $RW$ has singular values given by the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ as before. Thus, Lipschitz constants of layers and, indirectly, step scale for gradient updates that involve these layers, remain governed by the original singular-value profile. Preserving this profile is therefore a principled way to favor stable optimization dynamics during task-specific adjustment.

# Complexity notions: counts of free scalars, scaling like $n^{\text{two}}$ and $n\log n$, memory versus runtime trade-offs

A full unconstrained adjustment of an $n$-by-$n$ linear map would allow each of the $n^{\text{two}}$ entries to change independently. Orthogonality substantially reduces the degrees of freedom: the manifold of orthogonal maps in $n$ dimensions has about $\tfrac{n(n-\text{one})}{\text{two}}$ independent directions, which is roughly half of $n^{\text{two}}$ for large $n$. That reduction is significant, yet it still grows quadratically in $n$.

There are strategies to represent orthogonal adjustments with far fewer stored scalars while keeping the final action expressive. One route factors the final orthogonal operator into a product of many sparse orthogonal components. If each component has only a linear number of nonzero rows per factor and the number of factors grows logarithmically with $n$, then the total count of stored, tunable scalars can scale like $n\log n$ rather than like $n^{\text{two}}$. The classic structured pattern that achieves this is the butterfly-style factorization: through a small number of structured sparse factors, signals from any input index can influence every output index after the full product, and the stored scalars are linear in $n$ per factor.

The trade-off is clear. Storing fewer scalars reduces memory footprint and the cost to save checkpoints or ship an adapter bundle. However, each application of the full adjusted map now requires applying a sequence of sparse factors at runtime, which raises per-step arithmetic work during optimization. In other words, there is a memory versus runtime compromise: compressing storage into a factorized representation increases repeated computation while a dense, single-table representation reduces per-application work but costs more memory.

A practical middle ground is to keep the factorized representation throughout the optimization phase, where repeated application is inevitable, and then, at deployment, multiply all factors into a single dense operator to avoid runtime overhead. This final multiplication collapses the factor chain into one map that can be applied with a single dense operation. The collapse step costs time once but delivers lower per-evaluation runtime after deployment.

Beyond pure counts, structure matters for inductive preference. Compact structured factorizations impose a prior that favors transforms with certain global regularities — for example, those that resemble fast harmonic transforms. When the new task aligns with those regularities, the compact representation can yield better generalization than an unconstrained dense adjustment that might overfit.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has laid out the linchpin facts that make orthogonal left-actions attractive for cautious, geometry-preserving adjustment of linear maps. Orthogonality preserves inner products and singular-value profiles; the Cayley mapping offers a stable way to represent orthogonal maps from skew-symmetric antecedents; left-action by an orthogonal map rotates column collections while preserving pairwise relations; and factorized structured representations trade storage for repeated computation, enabling compact yet expressive adjustments that run efficiently in practice after an optional collapse step. These mathematical foundations explain why such conservative transformations can be effective tools in practice when one wishes to steer large preexisting maps toward new tasks without wholesale disruption of established internal structure.
