# Chapter 1: Introduction and Motivation

## Large-Scale Foundation Models and Fine-Tuning Challenges

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable surge in the development and deployment of large-scale foundation models. These models, such as GPT-3, ChatGPT, and Stable Diffusion, have demonstrated extraordinary capabilities across a wide range of tasks in natural language processing, computer vision, and generative modeling. For instance, GPT-3, a language model with approximately 175 billion parameters, can generate coherent text, answer questions, and perform translation with impressive fluency. Similarly, Stable Diffusion has revolutionized text-to-image generation, enabling the creation of high-quality images from textual descriptions.

The success of these foundation models is largely attributed to their massive scale and the extensive pretraining on vast datasets. However, training such models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, requiring enormous computational resources, time, and energy. This reality poses a significant barrier to researchers and practitioners who wish to leverage these models for specific downstream tasks. Instead of retraining, the practical approach is to adapt pretrained foundation models to new tasks through finetuning.

Finetuning involves adjusting the parameters of a pretrained model to improve its performance on a target task. While conceptually straightforward, finetuning large models presents its own challenges. The sheer number of parameters makes full finetuning computationally intensive and memory-demanding. Moreover, naively finetuning all parameters risks overfitting, catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge, and increased inference latency if architectural changes are introduced.

Therefore, the community has focused on developing parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods. These methods aim to adapt large models to downstream tasks by updating only a small subset of parameters or introducing a small number of additional parameters, thereby reducing computational cost, memory footprint, and preserving pretrained knowledge.

## Three Families of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

Parameter-efficient finetuning methods broadly fall into three categories: full finetuning with parameter selection, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each approach offers a different trade-off between parameter efficiency, ease of implementation, and inference cost.

1. **Full Finetuning with Parameter Selection:**  
   This approach involves finetuning only a subset of the model’s parameters while keeping the rest frozen. Techniques such as BitFit finetune only the bias terms, while other methods select specific layers or modules to update. This strategy maintains the original model architecture and does not introduce inference overhead. However, the choice of which parameters to finetune can be ad hoc and may require careful tuning.

2. **Adapter Tuning:**  
   Adapter methods insert small trainable modules (adapters) into the pretrained model’s architecture. These adapters are lightweight neural networks that transform intermediate representations. During finetuning, only the adapter parameters are updated, while the original model remains frozen. Adapters have been successful in NLP and vision tasks, offering a good balance between parameter efficiency and expressivity. However, they increase inference latency due to the additional computations.

3. **Prompt Tuning:**  
   Prompt tuning methods prepend trainable tokens or embeddings to the input sequence, effectively steering the pretrained model’s behavior without modifying its weights. This approach is particularly popular in language models, where prompt tokens can encode task-specific instructions. Prompt tuning is highly parameter-efficient and does not affect inference speed but may be less effective for tasks requiring deeper model adaptation.

Among these, full finetuning with parameter selection stands out for its simplicity and zero inference latency, making it attractive for many applications. However, achieving parameter efficiency while preserving pretrained knowledge remains a challenge.

## Reparameterization Approaches: LoRA and Related Methods

A prominent family of PEFT methods employs reparameterization of weight updates. Instead of directly updating the original weight matrices, these methods represent the finetuning update as a structured transformation, reducing the number of trainable parameters.

One influential example is **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**. LoRA models the weight update as a low-rank additive matrix: the pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \) is updated to \( \mathbf{W}^0 + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top \), where \( \mathbf{A} \) and \( \mathbf{B} \) are low-rank matrices with much fewer parameters than \( \mathbf{W}^0 \). This low-rank structure exploits the observation that many finetuning updates lie in a low-dimensional subspace, enabling efficient adaptation with fewer parameters.

LoRA and its variants have gained popularity due to their simplicity, effectiveness, and compatibility with existing architectures. They have been applied successfully to large language models and vision transformers. However, additive low-rank updates may not always preserve the geometric structure of the pretrained weights, potentially leading to suboptimal generalization.

Other reparameterization methods explore different structured updates, such as sparse or block-sparse matrices, but balancing expressivity and parameter efficiency remains an open problem.

## Motivation for Orthogonality: Preserving Pretraining Structure

A key insight motivating orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is the desire to preserve the relational structure of the pretrained model’s parameters during adaptation. Specifically, the neurons in a layer can be viewed as vectors in a high-dimensional space, and their pairwise angles encode important semantic relationships learned during pretraining.

Orthogonal transformations preserve angles and norms, meaning that applying an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) to the pretrained weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \) maintains the geometric relationships between neurons. Formally, if \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal, then for any two neurons \( \mathbf{w}_i^0 \) and \( \mathbf{w}_j^0 \), the angle between \( \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0 \) and \( \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0 \) is the same as between \( \mathbf{w}_i^0 \) and \( \mathbf{w}_j^0 \).

This property suggests that finetuning via orthogonal transformations can adapt the model while preserving the semantic knowledge encoded in the pretrained weights. Empirically, OFT has demonstrated improved generalization and training stability, especially in text-to-image diffusion models.

Mathematically, OFT reparameterizes the finetuned weight matrix as
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0,
\]
where \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) is an orthogonal matrix learned during finetuning. The orthogonality constraint ensures that \( \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \), preserving the spectral norm and pairwise angles.

## Limitations of Existing OFT and the Parameter-Efficiency Problem

Despite its appealing theoretical properties and empirical success, the original OFT method faces a significant limitation: the number of trainable parameters in the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) scales quadratically with the dimension \( d \), i.e., \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \). For large models with high-dimensional layers, this parameter count can be prohibitively large.

To mitigate this, prior work introduced a block-diagonal structure for \( \mathbf{R} \), decomposing it into smaller orthogonal blocks along the diagonal. This reduces the number of parameters to \( \mathcal{O}(bd) \), where \( b \) is the block size. However, this block-diagonal constraint imposes a fixed sparsity pattern and applies orthogonal transformations independently within each block.

While parameter-efficient, the block-diagonal structure has drawbacks:

- **Reduced Expressivity:** The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate arbitrary dense orthogonal matrices, limiting the flexibility of finetuning.

- **Inductive Bias:** The fixed sparsity pattern may introduce undesirable biases, as it assumes that neuron dimensions can be partitioned into independent groups, which is often not semantically meaningful.

- **Sparsity Pattern Selection:** Choosing an effective sparsity pattern is nontrivial and remains an open question.

These limitations motivate the search for a parameter-efficient method that can represent dense orthogonal matrices without incurring the quadratic parameter cost.

## High-Level Idea of BOFT and a Roadmap of the Textbook

The key innovation introduced in the paper is **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**, a novel parameter-efficient finetuning method that leverages the butterfly structure from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices efficiently.

The main idea is to factorize a dense orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, each with a carefully designed sparsity pattern inspired by butterfly graphs. This factorization enables:

- **Parameter Efficiency:** The number of trainable parameters scales as \( \mathcal{O}(d \log d) \), a significant reduction from \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \).

- **Dense Connectivity:** Unlike block-diagonal OFT, BOFT’s factorization allows information to flow across all neuron dimensions, enabling dense orthogonal transformations.

- **Expressivity and Regularity Trade-off:** BOFT provides a smooth interpolation between identity and full orthogonal matrices, balancing expressivity and regularization to improve generalization.

- **Theoretical Foundations:** The butterfly factorization is grounded in an information transmission framework, viewing matrix factorization as transmitting information across a layered graph, which guides the design of efficient sparse factors.

- **Practical Effectiveness:** BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case and demonstrates superior performance across diverse tasks, including vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models.

## Vectors, Matrices, Inner Products, and Norms

At the heart of machine learning and deep learning lie vectors and matrices, which represent data, parameters, and transformations.

- **Vectors** are ordered lists of numbers, often denoted as $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, where $d$ is the dimension. For example, $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_d)^\top$.

- **Matrices** are two-dimensional arrays of numbers, denoted as $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, representing linear transformations from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$. The element in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is $A_{ij}$.

### Inner Product

The inner product (or dot product) between two vectors $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is defined as

\[
\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \sum_{i=1}^d x_i y_i.
\]

This operation measures the similarity or alignment between two vectors.

### Norms

A norm is a function that assigns a non-negative length or size to vectors or matrices.

- The **Euclidean norm** (or $\ell_2$ norm) of a vector $\mathbf{x}$ is

\[
\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x} \rangle} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2}.
\]

- For matrices, several norms exist. The **Frobenius norm** of a matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is

\[
\|\mathbf{A}\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}^2}.
\]

It can be viewed as the Euclidean norm of the matrix when flattened into a vector.

- The **spectral norm** (or operator norm) of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is

\[
\|\mathbf{A}\|_2 = \sup_{\mathbf{x} \neq 0} \frac{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_2}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2},
\]

which equals the largest singular value of $\mathbf{A}$ (discussed later).

---

## Orthogonal Matrices and Orthonormal Bases

An **orthogonal matrix** is a square matrix $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ whose columns (and rows) form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$. Formally,

\[
\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{Q}^\top = \mathbf{I}_d,
\]

where $\mathbf{I}_d$ is the $d \times d$ identity matrix.

### Properties of Orthogonal Matrices

- **Isometry:** Orthogonal matrices preserve the Euclidean norm of vectors:

\[
\|\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2, \quad \forall \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d.
\]

- **Preservation of angles:** Since inner products are preserved,

\[
\langle \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle,
\]

orthogonal transformations preserve angles between vectors.

- **Determinant:** The determinant of an orthogonal matrix is either $+1$ or $-1$. Those with determinant $+1$ form the special orthogonal group $SO(d)$, representing rotations.

### Orthonormal Bases

The columns of an orthogonal matrix form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$, meaning they are mutually orthogonal and each has unit length.

---

## Skew-Symmetric Matrices and the Cayley Transform

### Skew-Symmetric Matrices

A matrix $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is **skew-symmetric** if

\[
\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}.
\]

This means $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$ for all $i,j$. The diagonal entries of a skew-symmetric matrix are always zero.

### Cayley Transform

The Cayley transform provides a way to parameterize orthogonal matrices (except those with eigenvalue $-1$) using skew-symmetric matrices. Given a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$, the Cayley transform defines an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\]

This parameterization is smooth and differentiable, making it suitable for optimization in machine learning. It ensures that $\mathbf{R}$ remains orthogonal during training if $\mathbf{Q}$ is unconstrained except for skew-symmetry.

### Intuition

- The space of skew-symmetric matrices forms the Lie algebra corresponding to the Lie group of orthogonal matrices.

- The Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices, providing a convenient way to enforce orthogonality constraints.

---

## Singular Value Decomposition and Spectral Norm

### Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Any matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ can be decomposed as

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top,
\]

where

- $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal matrices,

- $\mathbf{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix with non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$ called singular values.

### Spectral Norm

The spectral norm of $\mathbf{W}$ is the largest singular value:

\[
\|\mathbf{W}\|_2 = \sigma_1.
\]

This norm measures the maximum stretching effect of $\mathbf{W}$ on any unit vector.

### Importance in Finetuning

- Orthogonal transformations preserve singular values, which helps maintain the spectral properties of pretrained weight matrices.

- Preserving spectral norms is linked to training stability and generalization.

---

## Matrix Sparsity Patterns, Block Matrices, and Low-Rank Factorizations

### Matrix Sparsity Patterns

A matrix is **sparse** if many of its entries are zero. The pattern of zeros and non-zeros is called the sparsity pattern.

- Sparsity can be exploited to reduce storage and computation.

- In finetuning, imposing sparsity on parameter updates reduces the number of trainable parameters.

### Block Matrices

A **block matrix** partitions a matrix into smaller submatrices (blocks). For example, a block-diagonal matrix has non-zero blocks only along its diagonal:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R}_1 & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{R}_r
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each $\mathbf{R}_i$ is a smaller matrix.

- Block-diagonal structures reduce parameters by restricting interactions between blocks.

- Used in OFT to reduce the parameter count of orthogonal matrices.

### Low-Rank Factorizations

A matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is **low-rank** if its rank $r$ is much smaller than $m$ and $n$. It can be factorized as

\[
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{V}^\top,
\]

where $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$.

- Low-rank updates are used in methods like LoRA to efficiently finetune large models.

- They reduce the number of trainable parameters from $mn$ to $r(m + n)$.

---

## Matrix Factorization vs Additive Updates (Multiplicative vs Additive Reparameterization)

### Additive Updates

Many finetuning methods update pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$ by adding a small perturbation:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta \mathbf{W},
\]

where $\Delta \mathbf{W}$ is a trainable update, often low-rank or sparse.

- Example: LoRA adds a low-rank matrix to $\mathbf{W}^0$.

- Intuitive and simple, but may not preserve certain structural properties of $\mathbf{W}^0$.

### Multiplicative Reparameterization

Alternatively, one can update weights by multiplying with a transformation matrix:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0,
\]

where $\mathbf{R}$ is a trainable matrix, often constrained to be orthogonal.

- This approach preserves geometric properties such as angles and norms.

- Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) uses multiplicative updates with orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$.

- Multiplicative updates can be more stable and preserve pretrained knowledge better.

---

## Graph Representations of Matrices: Adjacency and Information Flow

Matrices can be interpreted as adjacency matrices of graphs, providing an intuitive way to understand sparsity and matrix multiplication.

- Consider a matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as the adjacency matrix of a directed graph with $d$ nodes.

- The entry $A_{ij}$ indicates the weight of the edge from node $j$ to node $i$.

- A zero entry means no direct edge; a non-zero entry means information can flow along that edge.

### Matrix Multiplication as Information Transmission

- Multiplying matrices corresponds to composing graphs or transmitting information through multiple layers.

- For example, if $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1$, then the information flow from input nodes to output nodes can be viewed as passing through $m+1$ layers of nodes connected by edges defined by the $\mathbf{B}_i$ matrices.

- This layered graph perspective helps design sparse factorizations that still allow dense connectivity overall.

---

## Brief Overview of FFT and the Cooley–Tukey Algorithm (Intuition)

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a fundamental algorithm in signal processing that efficiently computes the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of a sequence.

### Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

Given a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^d$, the DFT computes

\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{d-1} x_n e^{-2\pi i k n / d}, \quad k=0, \ldots, d-1.
\]

Naively, this requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ operations.

### Cooley–Tukey Algorithm

The Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm reduces the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ by recursively dividing the DFT into smaller DFTs.

- It exploits symmetries and periodicities in the complex exponentials.

- The algorithm's computation can be represented as a **butterfly graph**, where data flows through layers of sparse linear transformations.

- Each butterfly stage mixes pairs of elements, enabling efficient global information mixing with sparse local operations.

### Relevance to BOFT

- The butterfly structure inspires the factorization of dense orthogonal matrices into products of sparse orthogonal matrices.

- This factorization achieves parameter efficiency by trading off the number of parameters with computational depth.

- The butterfly graph ensures dense connectivity (information from every input node can reach every output node) with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ non-zero parameters.

# Chapter 3: Survey of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Methods

In this chapter, we delve into the landscape of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods, which have become essential tools for adapting large pretrained foundation models to downstream tasks without incurring the prohibitive costs of full retraining. We begin by reviewing the traditional full finetuning approach, highlighting its benefits and limitations. Then, we explore three major families of PEFT methods: adapter modules, prompt tuning, and reparameterization-based methods, with a particular focus on LoRA and orthogonal finetuning (OFT). Throughout, we emphasize the mathematical formulations, practical considerations, and empirical strengths and weaknesses of these methods. This survey sets the stage for understanding the motivation and contributions of the Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) method introduced later in this book.

---

## Full Finetuning: Benefits and Pitfalls

The most straightforward approach to adapting a pretrained model to a new task is **full finetuning**, where all the parameters of the model are updated using task-specific data. Formally, if the pretrained model has parameters $\bm{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^P$, full finetuning optimizes

\[
\bm{\theta}^* = \arg\min_{\bm{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}; \mathcal{D}),
\]

where $\mathcal{L}$ is the task loss and $\mathcal{D}$ is the downstream dataset.

### Benefits

- **Expressivity:** Full finetuning allows the model to adapt all parameters, providing maximal flexibility to fit the new task.
- **Simplicity:** It requires no architectural modifications or additional components.
- **Strong performance:** When sufficient data and compute are available, full finetuning often achieves the best possible performance.

### Pitfalls

- **Computational cost:** Large foundation models can have billions of parameters (e.g., GPT-3 with 175B parameters). Updating all parameters requires substantial GPU memory and compute resources.
- **Storage inefficiency:** For each downstream task, a full copy of the model parameters must be stored, which is impractical for many tasks.
- **Overfitting risk:** With limited downstream data, full finetuning can lead to overfitting and catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge.
- **Inference latency:** Although inference cost remains the same, the need to maintain multiple full models for different tasks increases deployment complexity.

These challenges motivate the development of **parameter-efficient finetuning** methods that update only a small subset of parameters or introduce a small number of additional parameters, while preserving most of the pretrained model.

---

## Adapter Modules: Architecture, Parameter Count, and Use Cases

**Adapter modules** are a popular PEFT approach that inserts small trainable modules into the pretrained model, leaving the original parameters frozen. The key idea is to augment the model with lightweight components that can learn task-specific transformations.

### Architecture

Typically, adapters are small bottleneck feedforward networks inserted after certain layers. For example, an adapter module for a layer with input $\bm{h} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ can be formulated as

\[
\text{Adapter}(\bm{h}) = \bm{h} + \bm{W}_\text{up} \sigma(\bm{W}_\text{down} \bm{h}),
\]

where $\bm{W}_\text{down} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ and $\bm{W}_\text{up} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ are trainable weight matrices with $r \ll d$, and $\sigma$ is a nonlinearity (e.g., ReLU). The residual connection ensures the adapter learns a residual update.

### Parameter Count

The number of trainable parameters per adapter is $2rd$, which is typically much smaller than the original layer parameters ($d^2$ for a fully connected layer). By inserting adapters only in selected layers, the total trainable parameter count remains low.

### Use Cases and Strengths

- Adapters have been successfully applied in NLP and vision tasks.
- They allow modular finetuning: different adapters can be swapped in for different tasks without changing the base model.
- They introduce minimal inference latency since adapters are lightweight.

### Limitations

- The added modules increase inference time slightly.
- The choice of adapter size $r$ and placement requires tuning.
- Adapters may not fully capture complex task-specific transformations if the bottleneck is too narrow.

---

## Prompt Tuning and Prefix Tuning: Mechanism and Limitations

**Prompt tuning** and **prefix tuning** are PEFT methods that modify the input to the pretrained model rather than its internal weights.

### Prompt Tuning

In prompt tuning, a set of trainable tokens (embeddings) $\bm{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ is prepended to the input sequence. The model parameters remain frozen, and only $\bm{P}$ is optimized:

\[
\bm{x}' = [\bm{P}; \bm{x}],
\]

where $\bm{x}$ is the original input token embeddings.

### Prefix Tuning

Prefix tuning generalizes prompt tuning by prepending trainable continuous vectors to the key and value inputs of the transformer’s attention layers, effectively modifying the model’s internal context.

### Strengths

- Extremely parameter-efficient: only a small number of prompt vectors are trained.
- No changes to model architecture or weights.
- Fast to train and deploy.

### Limitations

- Often requires large prompt sizes to achieve competitive performance.
- May underperform compared to weight-based finetuning methods on complex tasks.
- Limited to transformer architectures and may not generalize well to other model types.

---

## LoRA: Low-Rank Updates, Mathematical Formulation, and Practicalities

**LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)** is a reparameterization-based PEFT method that updates pretrained weight matrices by adding a low-rank correction.

### Mathematical Formulation

Consider a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. LoRA models the finetuned weight as

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A} \bm{B},
\]

where $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ are trainable low-rank matrices with $r \ll \min(d,n)$. The rank $r$ controls the parameter budget.

### Intuition

- The low-rank update $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ captures task-specific modifications in a subspace of the full parameter space.
- By keeping $r$ small, LoRA drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters.

### Practicalities

- LoRA can be applied to multiple layers independently.
- The pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ remain frozen, enabling efficient storage and deployment.
- LoRA is compatible with existing training pipelines and does not increase inference latency.

### Strengths

- Strong empirical performance on NLP tasks.
- Simple to implement and tune.
- Good trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity.

### Limitations

- The low-rank assumption may limit expressivity for some tasks.
- Choosing the rank $r$ requires careful tuning.
- LoRA updates weights additively, which may not preserve certain geometric properties of the pretrained weights.

---

## Reparameterization-Based PEFT Methods Beyond LoRA

Beyond LoRA, several reparameterization methods have been proposed to improve parameter efficiency or adaptivity.

### Examples

- **KronA**: Uses Kronecker product-based low-rank approximations to reduce parameters further.
- **BitFit**: Finetunes only the bias terms of the model, drastically reducing trainable parameters.
- **Intrinsic Dimension Methods**: Optimize in a low-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space.
- **Dynamic Rank LoRA Variants**: Adjust the rank $r$ per layer or during training to allocate parameters adaptively.

### Common Themes

- These methods seek to balance parameter efficiency with sufficient expressivity.
- Many rely on additive updates to pretrained weights.
- They often require careful hyperparameter tuning and may introduce additional complexity.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Original Formulation and Empirical Findings

**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** is a novel PEFT approach that updates pretrained weights multiplicatively using orthogonal matrices, preserving geometric properties of the pretrained model.

### Formulation

Given a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT models the finetuned weight as

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0,
\]

where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$) learned during finetuning.

### Motivation

- Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$), maintaining relational information learned during pretraining.
- This geometric preservation is hypothesized to improve generalization and training stability.

### Parameter Efficiency

- A full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often too large.
- To reduce parameters, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure:

\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]

where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is orthogonal and $b r = d$.

- This reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(b d)$ but restricts expressivity.

### Orthogonality Enforcement

- OFT uses the **Cayley transform** to parameterize orthogonal matrices via skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}, \quad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}.
\]

- This ensures $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal during training.

### Empirical Findings

- OFT demonstrates improved generalization and training stability compared to additive methods like LoRA, especially in text-to-image diffusion models.
- The block-diagonal structure reduces parameters but limits the ability to approximate dense orthogonal transformations.
- OFT generally requires more parameters than LoRA for similar tasks.

### Limitations

- The block-diagonal assumption imposes a fixed sparsity pattern that may introduce undesirable inductive biases.
- Finding optimal block sizes and sparsity patterns is nontrivial.
- Dense orthogonal matrices are desirable but challenging to parameterize efficiently.

---

| Method               | Parameter Budget           | Update Type       | Orthogonality Preserved? | Inference Overhead | Generalization & Stability          |
|----------------------|---------------------------|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------------|------------------------------------|

- **Inference cost:** All PEFT methods except adapters and prompt tuning introduce no or minimal inference latency since the pretrained weights are either frozen or the modifications can be fused.
- **Parameter efficiency:** LoRA and OFT with block-diagonal structure reduce parameters significantly compared to full finetuning.
- **Generalization:** OFT’s orthogonality constraint preserves pretrained geometric structure, often leading to better generalization and training stability.
- **Expressivity vs efficiency trade-off:** LoRA’s low-rank additive updates and OFT’s block-diagonal multiplicative updates represent different points on this spectrum.

# Chapter 4: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) — Theory and Practice

In this chapter, we delve into the theory and practical aspects of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled approach to adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks. OFT distinguishes itself by reparameterizing the pretrained weight matrices multiplicatively using orthogonal transformations, which preserves important geometric properties of the original model. We will formalize the OFT framework, explain the motivation behind using orthogonal matrices, describe how orthogonality is enforced via the Cayley transform, and discuss initialization strategies. We then explore the block-diagonal variant of OFT designed for parameter efficiency, its benefits and limitations, and conclude with practical considerations regarding optimization, stability, and empirical performance.

---

## OFT Formal Definition: Multiplicative Reparameterization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a reparameterization-based finetuning method that updates pretrained weight matrices by applying a learnable orthogonal transformation multiplicatively. Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension (number of neurons). The forward pass of this layer for an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n.
\]

In OFT, instead of directly updating $\bm{W}^0$, we introduce a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and define the finetuned weight matrix as:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0.
\]

The forward pass becomes:

\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]

Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, $\bm{R}^\top = \bm{R}^{-1}$, so the transformation is invertible and preserves certain geometric properties of the input space.

The key idea is that the finetuning is performed by learning the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, which acts as a rotation or reflection in the input space before applying the pretrained weights. This multiplicative reparameterization contrasts with additive updates such as LoRA, which add a low-rank matrix to $\bm{W}^0$.

---

## Preserving Pairwise Neuron Angles: Why Orthogonality?

One of the main motivations for using orthogonal matrices in finetuning is to preserve the relational structure of the pretrained model's neurons. Specifically, the columns of $\bm{W}^0$ correspond to neurons, and their pairwise angles encode important semantic and geometric information learned during pretraining.

Recall that the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between two neurons (columns) $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$ is given by:

\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \frac{\langle \bm{w}^0_i, \bm{w}^0_j \rangle}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]

When we apply an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to the input space, the new neurons become:

\[
\bm{w}_i = \bm{R} \bm{w}^0_i.
\]

Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and norms, we have:

\[
\langle \bm{w}_i, \bm{w}_j \rangle = \langle \bm{R} \bm{w}^0_i, \bm{R} \bm{w}^0_j \rangle = \langle \bm{w}^0_i, \bm{w}^0_j \rangle,
\]

and

\[
\|\bm{w}_i\| = \|\bm{w}^0_i\|.
\]

Therefore, the pairwise angles between neurons remain unchanged:

\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \frac{\langle \bm{w}_i, \bm{w}_j \rangle}{\|\bm{w}_i\| \|\bm{w}_j\|} = \frac{\langle \bm{w}^0_i, \bm{w}^0_j \rangle}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|}.
\]

This property is crucial because it preserves the relational geometry of the pretrained neurons, which encodes semantic knowledge and helps maintain generalization. By finetuning via orthogonal transformations, OFT aims to adapt the model to new tasks while retaining the pretrained structure, leading to more stable and generalizable finetuning.

---

## Cayley Transform for Orthogonal Parameterization: Derivation and Properties

A central challenge in OFT is to parameterize and optimize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ efficiently while ensuring it remains orthogonal throughout training. Directly optimizing over the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices is nontrivial. The Cayley transform provides a convenient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices using skew-symmetric matrices.

### Skew-Symmetric Matrices

A matrix $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is skew-symmetric if:

\[
\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}.
\]

Skew-symmetric matrices form the Lie algebra associated with the orthogonal group $O(d)$.

### Cayley Transform

The Cayley transform maps a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (without eigenvalues equal to $-1$) as:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]

This formula guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal:

\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]

The inverse $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ is guaranteed to be invertible if $\bm{Q}$ has no eigenvalues equal to $1$.

### Properties and Benefits

- **Parameterization:** Instead of directly optimizing $\bm{R}$, OFT optimizes the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free parameters (since the diagonal of $\bm{Q}$ is zero and the upper triangle determines the lower triangle).

- **Orthogonality Guarantee:** The Cayley transform ensures that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal at every optimization step, avoiding the need for expensive projection or re-orthogonalization.

- **Smoothness:** The mapping from $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{R}$ is smooth, enabling gradient-based optimization.

- **Initialization:** Setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which is a natural identity initialization for finetuning.

In practice, the Cayley transform is used to parameterize $\bm{R}$ as a function of $\bm{Q}$, and gradients are computed with respect to $\bm{Q}$ during training.

---

## Zero/Identity Initialization and Its Role in Finetuning

Initialization plays a critical role in finetuning large pretrained models. The goal is to start from a model close to the pretrained one to avoid catastrophic forgetting and ensure stable convergence.

In OFT, the natural initialization is to set the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This corresponds to no change in the pretrained weights initially:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{I} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0.
\]

Using the Cayley transform parameterization, this is achieved by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to zero:

\[
\bm{Q} = \bm{0} \implies \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}.
\]

This zero initialization ensures that the finetuning process starts from the pretrained model exactly, and the learned orthogonal transformation gradually adapts the model to the downstream task. This approach contrasts with additive methods like LoRA, which initialize the additive low-rank matrices to zero.

Identity initialization helps maintain the pretrained knowledge and leads to more stable training dynamics, as the model parameters do not deviate abruptly at the start of finetuning.

---

## Block-Diagonal OFT: Motivation, Parameter Savings, and Limitations

While OFT with a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is conceptually elegant, it suffers from a major practical limitation: the number of trainable parameters scales quadratically with the input dimension $d$.

Specifically, a full skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ parameters, which becomes prohibitively large for high-dimensional layers in modern foundation models.

### Block-Diagonal Structure

To reduce the parameter count, the original OFT paper proposes a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$:

\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]

where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is an orthogonal matrix, and $b$ is the block size such that $b r = d$.

Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is parameterized independently using the Cayley transform with a skew-symmetric matrix of size $b \times b$.

### Parameter Savings

- The total number of parameters reduces to $r \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{b(b-1)}{2} \times \frac{d}{b} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2}$, which scales linearly with $d$ for fixed $b$.

- This is a significant reduction compared to the quadratic scaling of the full matrix.

### Intuition and Benefits

- The block-diagonal structure assumes that the input dimensions can be partitioned into $r$ groups, and each group is transformed independently.

- This reduces the complexity of the orthogonal transformation and makes training more efficient.

- It also reduces the risk of overfitting by limiting the expressiveness of the transformation.

### Limitations

- **Reduced Expressiveness:** The block-diagonal matrix cannot represent all orthogonal transformations. It only applies independent rotations within each block, ignoring interactions across blocks.

- **Fixed Sparsity Pattern:** The block-diagonal sparsity pattern is fixed and may introduce undesirable inductive biases. For example, it cannot approximate classic dense linear transforms like the discrete Fourier transform.

- **Arbitrary Grouping:** Dividing the input dimensions into blocks based on their indices is arbitrary and may not align with meaningful feature groupings.

- **No Dense Connectivity:** The resulting orthogonal matrix is sparse, limiting the ability to capture complex dependencies across all input dimensions.

These limitations motivate the search for alternative parameterizations that can represent dense orthogonal matrices efficiently, which leads to the development of BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) discussed in later chapters.

---

## Practical Considerations: Optimization, Stability, and Empirical Behavior

### Optimization

- OFT optimizes the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ using standard gradient-based optimizers such as Adam or SGD.

- The Cayley transform is differentiable, allowing gradients to flow through $\bm{Q}$ to update the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$.

- Learning rates for $\bm{Q}$ are typically set small to ensure smooth updates and prevent large deviations from the identity initialization.

### Stability

- Orthogonal transformations preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, which helps maintain stable gradients during training.

- This spectral norm preservation contrasts with additive updates (e.g., LoRA), which can increase the spectral norm and potentially destabilize training.

- The identity initialization further contributes to stable training by starting from a known good solution.

### Empirical Behavior

- OFT has been shown to improve generalization and convergence stability compared to additive low-rank methods in certain settings, such as finetuning text-to-image diffusion models.

- The preservation of neuron angles helps retain pretrained semantic knowledge, leading to better transfer performance.

- However, the full OFT with dense orthogonal matrices is often impractical due to parameter count.

- The block-diagonal OFT variant reduces parameters but may sacrifice expressiveness and performance on some tasks.

- OFT generally introduces no inference latency since the orthogonal transformation can be fused into the pretrained weights after finetuning.

---

## Where OFT Works Well and Where It Struggles

### Strengths

- **Preservation of Pretrained Structure:** By preserving pairwise neuron angles, OFT maintains the semantic relationships learned during pretraining.

- **Stable Training:** Orthogonality constraints and identity initialization lead to stable and smooth finetuning dynamics.

- **No Inference Overhead:** The multiplicative update can be fused into the pretrained weights, incurring no additional inference cost.

- **Improved Generalization:** Empirical results show OFT can outperform additive methods in some domains, especially where preserving pretrained geometry is critical.

### Challenges

- **Parameter Inefficiency:** Full OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitive for large models.

- **Limited Parameter Efficiency of Block-Diagonal OFT:** While block-diagonal OFT reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d)$, it restricts expressiveness and cannot represent dense orthogonal transformations.

- **Arbitrary Sparsity Patterns:** The block-diagonal assumption may not align with the true structure of the data or model, potentially limiting adaptation.

- **Scalability:** Applying OFT to very large models or layers with huge input dimensions remains challenging.

These limitations motivate the development of more parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning methods, such as Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT), which factorizes dense orthogonal matrices into products of sparse orthogonal matrices with structured sparsity patterns inspired by butterfly graphs. This approach aims to balance expressiveness and parameter efficiency, as we will explore in subsequent chapters.

---

# Summary

In this chapter, we have formalized Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as a multiplicative reparameterization of pretrained weights using orthogonal matrices. We explained the motivation for orthogonality in preserving neuron angles and semantic knowledge, and described how the Cayley transform parameterizes orthogonal matrices via skew-symmetric matrices. Identity initialization ensures stable finetuning starting from the pretrained model. To address parameter inefficiency, block-diagonal OFT restricts the orthogonal matrix to block-diagonal form, reducing parameters but limiting expressiveness. We discussed practical optimization and stability considerations, and highlighted the strengths and limitations of OFT. These insights set the stage for exploring more efficient and expressive orthogonal finetuning methods in the following chapters.

# Chapter 5: An Information Transmission View of Matrix Factorization

In this chapter, we introduce a novel and insightful perspective on the problem of factorizing dense matrices, particularly orthogonal matrices, which is central to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its parameter-efficient variant Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT). The key idea is to interpret the factorization of a dense matrix into a product of sparse matrices as an information transmission problem on a layered graph. This viewpoint not only provides intuition about the structure and sparsity patterns of the factorization but also guides the design of parameter-efficient factorizations that maintain expressivity.

We will begin by motivating why a graphical interpretation of matrix multiplication is natural and useful. Then, we will formalize the unrolling of matrix products into a grid-structured graph, where nodes and edges correspond to matrix rows/columns and nonzero entries, respectively. We will define the desiderata for such factorizations—dense connectivity and minimal free edges—under the orthogonality constraint. We will discuss how orthogonality imposes structural constraints on the graph edges. To ground these ideas, we will analyze the block-diagonal factorization used in the original OFT and its corresponding graph. Finally, we will discuss how this information transmission framework can be used to search for more efficient and expressive topologies, setting the stage for the butterfly structure introduced in the next chapter.

---

## Motivation for Graphical Interpretation of Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is a fundamental operation in linear algebra and machine learning. When we multiply two matrices, say $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the resulting matrix $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ can be viewed as a transformation that combines the linear transformations represented by $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.

In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning, we want to represent a dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]

where each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is sparse and orthogonal. The motivation is to reduce the number of trainable parameters by exploiting sparsity while still being able to represent a dense transformation.

To understand how the sparsity patterns of the factors $\mathbf{B}_i$ combine to produce a dense matrix $\mathbf{R}$, it is helpful to think of the multiplication process as a flow of information through layers. Each matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ can be seen as a connectivity pattern that transmits information from one layer of nodes to the next. This naturally leads to a graphical interpretation where nodes represent vector components and edges represent nonzero entries that allow information flow.

This perspective is powerful because it translates the algebraic problem of matrix factorization into a combinatorial problem of designing graphs with certain connectivity properties and minimal complexity. It also provides a framework to reason about parameter efficiency and expressivity in terms of graph topology.

---

## Unrolling Matrix Products into a $d \times (m+1)$ Grid Graph

Consider the factorization of a $d \times d$ matrix $\mathbf{R}$ into $m$ factors:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]

where each $\mathbf{B}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$.

We can represent this factorization as a layered graph with $m+1$ layers (or levels), each containing $d$ nodes. The layers are indexed from $1$ to $m+1$, and each node corresponds to one dimension/component of the vector space.

- The first layer (layer 1) corresponds to the input nodes.
- The last layer (layer $m+1$) corresponds to the output nodes.
- Each intermediate layer corresponds to the intermediate vector after multiplication by some subset of the factors.

The edges between layer $i$ and layer $i+1$ are determined by the nonzero entries of the matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$. Specifically, the $(j_1, j_2)$-th entry of $\mathbf{B}_i$ corresponds to a directed edge from node $j_2$ in layer $i$ to node $j_1$ in layer $i+1$.

This construction "unrolls" the matrix multiplication into a graph where information flows from the input layer through the intermediate layers to the output layer. The product $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \cdots \mathbf{B}_1$ corresponds to the composition of these information transmissions.

Formally, the graph $G$ has:

- Nodes: $d \times (m+1)$ nodes arranged in $m+1$ layers.
- Edges: For each $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, edges from layer $i$ to $i+1$ given by the nonzero pattern of $\mathbf{B}_i$.

---

## Edges, Nodes, and Interpretation of Nonzero Entries

In this graph representation:

- Each node represents a coordinate/dimension of the vector at a particular stage of the multiplication.
- An edge from node $j_2$ in layer $i$ to node $j_1$ in layer $i+1$ exists if and only if the matrix entry $(\mathbf{B}_i)_{j_1, j_2} \neq 0$.
- The weight of the edge corresponds to the value of the matrix entry.

The presence of an edge indicates that information from the $j_2$-th component at layer $i$ can influence the $j_1$-th component at layer $i+1$.

The overall matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is dense if and only if, for every output node in layer $m+1$ and every input node in layer 1, there exists at least one directed path connecting them. In other words, information from every input dimension can reach every output dimension through the sequence of sparse transformations.

If some entries of $\mathbf{R}$ are zero, it means that there is no path from the corresponding input node to the output node, indicating a lack of connectivity in the graph.

---

## Desiderata: Dense Connectivity and Minimum Free Edges Under Orthogonality

Given the above graph interpretation, we can formalize two key desiderata for the factorization matrices $\mathbf{B}_i$:

1. **Dense Connectivity:** The composed matrix $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \cdots \mathbf{B}_1$ should be dense. Equivalently, the graph should be such that for every input node in layer 1 and every output node in layer $m+1$, there exists at least one directed path connecting them. This ensures that the factorization can represent a dense transformation, allowing full expressivity.

2. **Minimum Free Edges:** The total number of trainable parameters corresponds to the number of free (non-fixed) edges in the graph, i.e., the number of nonzero entries in the $\mathbf{B}_i$ matrices that are not fixed by constraints. To achieve parameter efficiency, we want to minimize the total number of such edges while maintaining dense connectivity.

These desiderata are in tension: dense connectivity tends to require many edges, while parameter efficiency demands sparsity. The challenge is to find a factorization pattern that balances these goals.

---

## Orthogonality Constraints as Structural Constraints on Edges

In Orthogonal Finetuning, each factor matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ is not just sparse but also orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{B}_i^\top \mathbf{B}_i = \mathbf{B}_i \mathbf{B}_i^\top = \mathbf{I}_d.
\]

Orthogonality imposes strong structural constraints on the sparsity pattern of $\mathbf{B}_i$:

- Each $\mathbf{B}_i$ must be full-rank and invertible.
- The nonzero pattern must allow for a bijection between input and output nodes at adjacent layers.
- The number of nonzero entries per row and column is constrained to maintain orthogonality.

For example, a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix must have at least $d$ nonzero entries to be full-rank (one per row and column). These $d$ edges correspond to a perfect matching between nodes in adjacent layers.

Moreover, orthogonality can induce dependencies among edges. For instance, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, if one entry is zero, the orthogonality condition forces another entry to be zero as well. This means that missing edges in the graph can propagate constraints on other edges.

Therefore, the orthogonality constraint restricts the possible sparsity patterns and the corresponding graph topologies. Not all sparse patterns are feasible for orthogonal matrices.

---

## Example: Block-Diagonal Factorization and Its Graph

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method uses a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ to reduce parameters:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \text{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{R}_r),
\]

where each $\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is an orthogonal matrix and $b r = d$.

In the graph interpretation, this corresponds to a factorization with $m=1$ (a single factor) whose adjacency matrix is block-diagonal. The graph consists of $r$ disconnected subgraphs, each of size $b \times b$, with no edges connecting nodes across different blocks.

This structure has the following implications:

- **Parameter Efficiency:** The number of trainable parameters is reduced because each block is smaller.
- **Sparse Connectivity:** The graph is disconnected between blocks, so information cannot flow between different blocks.
- **Limited Expressivity:** Since the overall matrix is block-diagonal, it cannot represent dense transformations that mix all dimensions. The composed matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is sparse, with zero entries outside the blocks.

Thus, while block-diagonal factorization reduces parameters, it sacrifices the dense connectivity desideratum and limits the expressivity of the finetuning transformation.

---

## Using the Information Transmission View to Search for Efficient Topologies

The information transmission framework provides a systematic way to explore and design sparse orthogonal factorizations:

- By representing factorization as a layered graph, we can analyze connectivity and sparsity patterns visually and combinatorially.
- We can formalize the trade-off between parameter count (number of free edges) and expressivity (dense connectivity).
- We can identify minimal sparse topologies that achieve dense connectivity, satisfying orthogonality constraints.

This perspective motivates looking beyond block-diagonal structures to more sophisticated sparse factorizations that enable dense information flow with fewer parameters.

One such topology is the **butterfly graph**, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, which achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. The butterfly structure efficiently routes information from every input node to every output node through a logarithmic number of sparse layers.

In the next chapter, we will explore how the butterfly structure can be used to parameterize orthogonal matrices efficiently, leading to the Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) method that balances parameter efficiency and expressivity.

---

This framework formalizes the key desiderata of dense connectivity and minimal free edges under orthogonality constraints and provides intuition about the limitations of block-diagonal factorizations. It sets the stage for exploring more efficient sparse topologies, such as butterfly structures, that enable parameter-efficient and expressive orthogonal finetuning.

# Chapter 6: Butterfly Structures and the Cooley–Tukey Algorithm

In this chapter, we delve into the butterfly structures that underpin the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, a cornerstone of efficient signal processing. Butterfly structures provide a powerful and elegant way to represent certain linear transformations as products of sparse matrices, enabling fast computation and efficient information mixing. Understanding these structures is crucial for grasping the Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) method introduced in this book, which leverages butterfly factorizations to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices efficiently.

We begin by exploring the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm itself, focusing on its recursive divide-and-conquer strategy and the resulting butterfly diagram. Then, we formalize the notion of butterfly factors and butterfly components, and explain how these build up to a full butterfly matrix. We analyze the key properties of butterfly matrices, including their sparsity and logarithmic depth, which lead to their computational efficiency. Next, we introduce block butterfly matrices, a generalization that handles vector-valued blocks or channels, which is essential for practical applications like BOFT. We also discuss classical transforms expressible via butterfly factorizations, such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and Hadamard transform, highlighting the expressive power of butterfly structures. Finally, we interpret butterfly graphs as efficient network topologies for information transmission, connecting back to the information transmission perspective introduced earlier.

---

## The Cooley–Tukey FFT: Recursive Divide-and-Conquer and the Butterfly Diagram

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is an algorithm to compute the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of a sequence efficiently. The DFT transforms a vector of complex numbers from the time domain into the frequency domain, and is fundamental in signal processing, image analysis, and many other fields.

The Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm, introduced in 1965, revolutionized the computation of the DFT by reducing the naive complexity from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d \log d)\), where \(d\) is the length of the input vector (assumed to be a power of two for simplicity). The key insight is a recursive divide-and-conquer approach that breaks down a DFT of size \(d\) into smaller DFTs of size \(d/2\), and then combines their results efficiently.

### Recursive Decomposition

Suppose we have an input vector \(\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{d-1})\). The DFT is defined as

\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{d-1} x_n e^{-2\pi i k n / d}, \quad k = 0, \ldots, d-1.
\]

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm splits the sum into even and odd indices:

\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{d/2 - 1} x_{2n} e^{-2\pi i k (2n) / d} + \sum_{n=0}^{d/2 - 1} x_{2n+1} e^{-2\pi i k (2n+1) / d}.
\]

This can be rewritten as

\[
X_k = E_k + e^{-2\pi i k / d} O_k,
\]

where

\[
E_k = \sum_{n=0}^{d/2 - 1} x_{2n} e^{-2\pi i k n / (d/2)}, \quad O_k = \sum_{n=0}^{d/2 - 1} x_{2n+1} e^{-2\pi i k n / (d/2)}.
\]

Notice that \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) are DFTs of size \(d/2\). Thus, the DFT of size \(d\) is computed by two DFTs of size \(d/2\), plus a combination step involving multiplication by complex roots of unity (called twiddle factors).

### Butterfly Diagram

The recursive decomposition can be visualized as a butterfly diagram, where data flows through stages of computation, mixing pairs of elements at each stage. Each stage corresponds to a sparse linear transformation that combines pairs of inputs with twiddle factors.

The butterfly diagram has \(\log_2 d\) stages, each stage performing \(d/2\) pairwise operations. The pattern of connections resembles a butterfly’s wings, hence the name.

This recursive structure is the foundation for the butterfly factorization of matrices, which we will define next.

---

## Definition of a Butterfly Factor and Butterfly Component

To formalize the butterfly structure in matrix form, we start by defining a **butterfly factor**, which is a sparse matrix that performs local mixing of pairs of elements.

### Butterfly Factor

Let \(k\) be a power of two, \(k \geq 2\). A butterfly factor \(\mathbf{B}^F(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}\) is defined as a block matrix composed of four diagonal blocks:

\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each \(\mathbf{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) is a vector representing the diagonal entries of the corresponding block.

This structure means that \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) only mixes pairs of elements: the top half of the output depends on the top and bottom halves of the input via diagonal scaling, and similarly for the bottom half.

### Butterfly Component

A **butterfly component** \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, k)\) is a block-diagonal matrix of size \(d \times d\) composed of \(\frac{d}{k}\) butterfly factors \(\mathbf{B}^F_i(k)\):

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, k) = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{B}^F_1(k), \mathbf{B}^F_2(k), \ldots, \mathbf{B}^F_{d/k}(k)).
\]

Each block acts independently on a disjoint subset of the input vector.

---

## Recursive Construction of a Full Butterfly Matrix

The full butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is constructed recursively by composing butterfly components with decreasing block sizes:

\[
\mathbf{B}(d) = \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, d) \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, d/2) \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, d/4) \cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, 2).
\]

Here, each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, k)\) is a block-diagonal matrix with blocks of size \(k\), and the product proceeds from the largest block size \(d\) down to the smallest \(2\).

At the base case, \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, 2)\) consists of \(d/2\) independent \(2 \times 2\) butterfly factors, each mixing pairs of elements.

This recursive factorization mirrors the stages of the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm, where each stage mixes pairs of elements at different scales.

---

## Properties: Sparsity, Depth (\(O(\log d)\)), and \(O(d \log d)\) Nonzeros

The butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) has several important properties that make it computationally efficient and suitable for parameter-efficient finetuning:

1. **Sparsity:** Each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, k)\) is block-diagonal with blocks of size \(k\), and each butterfly factor \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) has only \(4 \times (k/2) = 2k\) nonzero entries (since each diagonal block is diagonal). Therefore, each component has \(d \times 2\) nonzero entries, i.e., \(O(d)\).

2. **Depth:** The full butterfly matrix is a product of \(\log_2 d\) such components (since block sizes halve each time), so the depth of the factorization is \(O(\log d)\).

3. **Total Nonzeros:** Multiplying the number of components by the nonzeros per component yields \(O(d \log d)\) nonzero entries in total.

This is a dramatic reduction compared to a dense \(d \times d\) matrix, which has \(d^2\) entries. The butterfly factorization thus achieves a sparse factorization with logarithmic depth and near-linear parameter count.

---

## Block Butterfly Matrices: Definition and Motivation (Handling Channels/Blocks)

In practical applications, especially in neural networks, the input vectors often have multiple channels or blocks of features. To handle this, the butterfly factorization is generalized to **block butterfly matrices**.

### Block Butterfly Component

A block butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d, k)\) is defined similarly to \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d, k)\), but each scalar entry in the butterfly factors \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) is replaced by a \(b \times b\) matrix block.

Formally, each diagonal vector \(\mathbf{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) becomes a sequence of \(b \times b\) matrices, and the butterfly factor becomes a block matrix with \(2 \times 2\) blocks of size \(b \times b\).

### Motivation

- **Expressivity:** Block butterfly matrices can represent more complex transformations by mixing feature blocks rather than individual scalars.

- **Parameter Efficiency:** By choosing block size \(b\), one can trade off between parameter count and expressivity.

- **Compatibility:** This generalization aligns with the structure of weight matrices in neural networks, where neurons or channels are naturally grouped.

---

## Examples of Transforms Expressible via Butterfly Factorization (DFT, Hadamard)

Butterfly factorizations are not just computational tricks; they capture the structure of many classical linear transforms:

- **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT):** The Cooley–Tukey FFT itself is a butterfly factorization of the DFT matrix.

- **Hadamard Transform:** The Hadamard matrix, used in signal processing and coding theory, can be factorized into butterfly components with simple \(\pm 1\) entries.

- **Discrete Cosine and Sine Transforms:** Variants of the butterfly structure can represent these transforms efficiently.

This shows that butterfly matrices form a rich family of structured matrices capable of representing important linear operators with sparse factorizations.

---

## Butterfly Graphs as an Efficient Network Topology for Information Transmission

From the information transmission perspective introduced earlier, butterfly matrices correspond to a particular network topology known as the **butterfly graph**.

- **Nodes and Levels:** The butterfly graph has \(d\) nodes at each of \(\log_2 d + 1\) levels, corresponding to the input and output nodes and intermediate stages.

- **Edges:** Each butterfly component corresponds to edges connecting nodes between adjacent levels, with sparse connections that enable efficient mixing.

- **Dense Connectivity:** Despite sparsity at each stage, the overall product of butterfly components ensures that information from every input node can reach every output node.

- **Parameter Efficiency:** The butterfly graph achieves dense connectivity with only \(O(d \log d)\) edges, minimizing the number of trainable parameters needed to represent dense transformations.

This efficient topology is the key insight behind BOFT: by parameterizing orthogonal matrices as products of butterfly components, one can achieve dense, expressive transformations with far fewer parameters than naive dense matrices.

In this chapter, we have explored the butterfly structures that form the backbone of the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm and their generalizations. We defined butterfly factors and components, showed how they recursively build up to full butterfly matrices, and highlighted their sparsity and logarithmic depth properties. We introduced block butterfly matrices to handle vector-valued blocks, and discussed classical transforms expressible via butterfly factorizations. Finally, we connected butterfly matrices to butterfly graphs, efficient network topologies for information transmission, which provide the theoretical foundation for the parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method BOFT.

Understanding these concepts equips us to appreciate how BOFT leverages butterfly factorizations to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices efficiently, balancing expressivity and parameter efficiency in finetuning large foundation models.

# Chapter 7: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT): Method

In this chapter, we delve into the core methodology of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT), a novel parameter-efficient finetuning approach that builds upon the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework. BOFT addresses the key limitation of OFT—its relatively large number of trainable parameters when using dense orthogonal matrices—by leveraging a structured factorization inspired by butterfly matrices from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. This factorization composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, dramatically reducing the parameter count while maintaining expressivity and stability.

We will first formalize the BOFT reparameterization, explain how orthogonality is preserved in each factor, analyze the role of block size and the number of stages in the factorization, and show how BOFT generalizes and subsumes OFT as special cases. We then discuss how BOFT achieves dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, describe the initialization strategy, and conclude with practical considerations for implementing BOFT.

---

## BOFT Reparameterization: Product of Orthogonal Butterfly Components

The fundamental idea behind BOFT is to represent the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which is used to finetune the pretrained weight matrix, as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices structured according to the butterfly pattern. Formally, BOFT parameterizes $\bm{R}$ as

\[
\bm{R}(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component matrix of size $d \times d$, parameterized with block size $b$, and $m$ is the number of such components (or stages) in the product.

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of smaller orthogonal blocks arranged in a butterfly pattern. The product of these sparse matrices yields a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ that transforms the pretrained weights multiplicatively:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m, b) \bm{W}^0,
\]

where $\bm{W}^0$ is the frozen pretrained weight matrix, and $\bm{W}$ is the finetuned weight matrix used in the forward pass.

This factorization enables BOFT to control the trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity by adjusting $m$ and $b$, as we will see in the following sections.

---

## Ensuring Orthogonality of Each Component via Cayley Parameterization or Block Rotations

A critical requirement in BOFT is that each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ must be orthogonal to ensure that their product $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is also orthogonal. To guarantee this, BOFT parameterizes each block within the butterfly components as an orthogonal matrix.

### Orthogonality via Cayley Transform

One effective way to parameterize orthogonal matrices is through the Cayley transform. For a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$), the Cayley transform defines an orthogonal matrix as

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]

In BOFT, each $2b \times 2b$ block within a butterfly component is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of the same size, and the Cayley transform is applied to obtain an orthogonal block. This ensures that each block is orthogonal, and since the butterfly component is block-diagonal with these orthogonal blocks, the entire component is orthogonal.

### Block Rotations

Alternatively, for the smallest block size $b=1$, each block reduces to a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, which can be parameterized as a rotation matrix:

\[
\bm{R}(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}
\cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\
\sin \theta & \cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

This parameterization is simple and efficient, and it naturally fits the butterfly structure where each butterfly factor is composed of many such $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks.

By ensuring orthogonality at the block level, BOFT maintains the orthogonality of the entire matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ as the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal.

---

## Block Size $b$ and Number of Stages $m$: Trade-offs and Parameter Count Formula

The two key hyperparameters controlling BOFT’s parameter efficiency and expressivity are:

- **Block size $b$:** The size of each block in the butterfly components. Each block is a $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal matrix.
- **Number of stages $m$:** The number of butterfly components multiplied together to form $\bm{R}(m, b)$.

### Parameter Count

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ consists of $\frac{d}{2b}$ blocks of size $2b \times 2b$. Each such block is orthogonal and parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix, which has $\frac{(2b)(2b - 1)}{2} = b(2b - 1)$ free parameters. However, since the Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices, the number of effective trainable parameters per block is $b(2b - 1)$.

For simplicity, the paper approximates the number of effective trainable parameters per butterfly component as

\[
\frac{1}{2} (b - 1) d,
\]

and since there are $m$ such components, the total number of trainable parameters in BOFT is approximately

\[
\text{Parameters} = \frac{1}{2} (b - 1) d m.
\]

This formula shows that the parameter count scales linearly with the dimension $d$, the block size $b$, and the number of stages $m$.

### Trade-offs

- Increasing **block size $b$** increases the number of parameters per stage but allows each block to represent more complex orthogonal transformations.
- Increasing **number of stages $m$** increases the depth of the factorization, allowing more expressive compositions of sparse orthogonal matrices, but also increases parameters and computational cost.

By tuning $b$ and $m$, BOFT can smoothly interpolate between highly parameter-efficient but less expressive models and more expressive but parameter-heavy models.

---

## Recovering OFT as Special Cases

BOFT generalizes the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework and its block-diagonal variant by appropriate choices of $m$ and $b$.

- **Original OFT (full dense orthogonal matrix):** This corresponds to BOFT with a single stage ($m=1$) and block size equal to the full dimension ($b=d$). In this case, $\bm{R}(1, d)$ is a single dense orthogonal matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

- **Block-diagonal OFT:** This corresponds to BOFT with a single stage ($m=1$) and block size $b < d$. Here, $\bm{R}(1, b)$ is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with blocks of size $b \times b$, exactly matching the block-diagonal structure used in OFT for parameter efficiency.

Thus, BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case:

\[
\text{OFT} \equiv \text{BOFT}(m=1, b).
\]

By increasing the number of stages $m$ beyond 1, BOFT introduces a richer factorization that can represent dense orthogonal matrices with fewer parameters than the full OFT.

---

## Dense Orthogonal Matrices with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ Parameters When $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$

One of the most important theoretical and practical results of BOFT is that it can represent dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters by choosing:

- Number of stages: $m = \log d$,
- Block size: $b = 2$.

This corresponds to the classical butterfly factorization inspired by the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm, where the orthogonal matrix is decomposed into $\log d$ sparse butterfly components, each composed of $2 \times 2$ orthogonal blocks (rotations).

Since each stage has approximately $\frac{d}{2}$ blocks of size $2 \times 2$, and each block has one parameter (the rotation angle), the total number of parameters is roughly

\[
m \times \frac{d}{2} = \frac{d}{2} \log d = \mathcal{O}(d \log d).
\]

This is a significant reduction compared to the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required for a full dense orthogonal matrix in OFT.

Despite this parameter reduction, the product of these butterfly components yields a dense orthogonal matrix, enabling BOFT to maintain high expressivity and preserve the geometric properties of the pretrained weights.

---

## Identity Initialization for BOFT and Rationale

A crucial practical consideration in finetuning is to start from a model close to the pretrained one to avoid large deviations that can harm generalization and training stability.

In BOFT, this is achieved by initializing each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ as an identity matrix. Since each component is parameterized via the Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix, this corresponds to initializing the skew-symmetric matrices as zero matrices.

This initialization ensures that the product $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is initially the identity matrix:

\[
\bm{R}(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \bm{I} = \bm{I}.
\]

Consequently, the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m, b) \bm{W}^0$ starts exactly as the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, and the model begins finetuning from the pretrained parameters without any initial perturbation.

This zero-deviation initialization is analogous to zero initialization in additive PEFT methods like LoRA and is important for stable and effective finetuning.

---

## Practical Instantiation: Choices of Permutations, Block Types, and Implementation Notes

### Permutations in Butterfly Components

The butterfly factorization involves permutations of the input and output dimensions to achieve the characteristic butterfly connectivity pattern. In BOFT, these permutations are fixed and follow the classical Cooley-Tukey FFT pattern, which recursively splits the input dimension into halves and interleaves them.

These permutations ensure that information from every input dimension can propagate to every output dimension through the product of sparse butterfly components, enabling dense connectivity with sparse factors.

### Block Types and Parameterization

- For block size $b=1$, each block is a $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix parameterized by a single angle.
- For larger block sizes $b > 1$, each block is a $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal matrix parameterized via the Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix of the same size.

Choosing larger $b$ increases expressivity but also parameter count and computational cost.

### Implementation Notes

- **Matrix Multiplication:** The forward pass requires multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by the product of butterfly components $\bm{R}(m, b)$. Since each component is sparse and block-diagonal, these multiplications can be implemented efficiently using batched small matrix multiplications and permutations.

- **Training Overhead:** BOFT introduces some runtime overhead during training due to multiple matrix multiplications per forward pass. However, after finetuning, the product $\bm{R}(m, b)$ can be fused into a single dense matrix and multiplied into the pretrained weights, incurring no additional inference latency.

- **Multiplicative Dropout:** To prevent overfitting, BOFT employs a multiplicative dropout scheme that randomly replaces some butterfly components or blocks with identity matrices during training, effectively dropping parts of the orthogonal transformation.

- **Compatibility:** BOFT can be applied to any linear layer in a pretrained model, including large language models, vision transformers, and diffusion models, making it a versatile PEFT method.

---

# Summary

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) innovatively parameterizes the orthogonal transformation used in finetuning as a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. This factorization enables dense orthogonal matrices to be represented with dramatically fewer parameters—$\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$—while preserving the geometric properties that underpin OFT’s strong generalization and stability.

By adjusting the block size $b$ and the number of stages $m$, BOFT smoothly interpolates between the original OFT and highly parameter-efficient factorizations. Identity initialization and multiplicative dropout further enhance training stability and generalization. Practical implementation leverages the structured sparsity and permutations inherent in butterfly matrices for efficient computation.

BOFT thus provides a powerful, flexible, and efficient framework for parameter-efficient finetuning of large foundation models across diverse domains.

# Chapter 8: Training BOFT: Regularization, Dropout and Optimization

In this chapter, we delve into the practical aspects of training Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) models. While the previous chapters have introduced the theoretical foundations and the parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization via butterfly factorization, here we focus on how to effectively optimize these models in practice. We discuss the optimization setup, introduce a novel multiplicative dropout technique tailored for BOFT’s multiplicative updates, explain initialization strategies, analyze computational and memory costs, and provide practical heuristics to guide hyperparameter choices.

---

## Optimization Setup: Learning Rates, Optimizers, and Stability Considerations

Training BOFT involves optimizing the parameters of multiple sparse orthogonal butterfly components that compose the overall orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. Each butterfly component is parameterized to be orthogonal, typically via the Cayley transform applied to skew-symmetric matrices or block rotations. This multiplicative reparameterization introduces unique optimization challenges compared to standard additive finetuning methods.

### Learning Rate Selection

A key consideration is the choice of learning rate. Since BOFT parameters control orthogonal transformations that multiply the pretrained weights, large updates can cause significant changes in the effective weight matrix, potentially destabilizing training. To mitigate this, practitioners often use smaller learning rates for BOFT parameters compared to standard finetuning. This conservative step size helps maintain the pretrained model’s knowledge while allowing gradual adaptation.

Empirically, learning rates in the range of $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ are common starting points, but tuning is necessary depending on the model size, dataset, and task. Layer-wise learning rate schedules or adaptive optimizers like Adam or AdamW are also effective, as they can adjust step sizes per parameter.

### Choice of Optimizer

BOFT training benefits from optimizers that handle sparse and structured parameters well. Adam and AdamW are popular choices due to their adaptive moment estimation, which can stabilize updates in the presence of complex parameter interactions induced by the butterfly factorization.

However, since the parameters are constrained to produce orthogonal matrices, the optimizer must work in conjunction with the orthogonal parameterization (e.g., Cayley transform) to ensure the orthogonality constraint is preserved after each update. This means the optimizer updates the underlying skew-symmetric parameters, and the orthogonal matrix is recomputed accordingly.

### Stability Considerations

Orthogonal finetuning methods, including BOFT, generally exhibit better training stability than additive low-rank methods like LoRA because the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix is preserved. This spectral norm preservation prevents exploding or vanishing gradients and helps maintain smooth optimization landscapes.

Nevertheless, the multiplicative nature of BOFT updates means that the product of multiple butterfly components must be carefully managed. Numerical instabilities can arise if the parameters deviate too far from identity early in training. To address this, identity initialization (discussed later) and careful learning rate scheduling are crucial.

Gradient clipping can also be employed to prevent large parameter updates that might break orthogonality or cause training divergence.

---

## Multiplicative Dropout: Concept, Implementation, and Hyperparameters

Dropout is a widely used regularization technique that randomly zeroes out activations or weights during training to prevent overfitting. However, conventional dropout is designed for additive or feedforward parameter updates and does not directly apply to BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal updates.

### Why Conventional Dropout Does Not Apply

In BOFT, the finetuned weight matrix is obtained by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ composed of multiple butterfly components:

\[
\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. Since the update is multiplicative, zeroing out elements or blocks arbitrarily would break orthogonality and disrupt the carefully structured parameterization.

### Multiplicative Dropout: The Proposed Solution

To regularize BOFT without violating orthogonality, the authors propose a *multiplicative dropout* scheme that operates at the level of butterfly components and their internal blocks.

The key idea is to randomly replace some butterfly components or blocks within components with identity matrices during training. This effectively "drops" the corresponding transformation stage or block, forcing the model to rely on other components and preventing over-reliance on any single part of the factorization.

Formally, multiplicative dropout proceeds as follows:

1. **Component-level dropout:** Randomly select a fraction $p_1$ of the $m$ butterfly components to be dropped. For each dropped component, replace the entire $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ with the identity matrix $\bm{I}_d$ for that training iteration.

2. **Block-level dropout:** Within each butterfly component, which is block-diagonal with blocks of size $2b \times 2b$, randomly select a fraction $p_2$ of these blocks to be replaced by identity blocks.

This dropout scheme preserves the orthogonality of the overall matrix $\bm{R}$ because replacing orthogonal components or blocks with identity matrices results in a product of orthogonal matrices (some of which are identity), which remains orthogonal.

### Hyperparameter Choices

Typical values for the dropout rates $p_1$ and $p_2$ depend on the task and model size but often range from 0.1 to 0.3. These values balance regularization strength and training stability.

Multiplicative dropout can be combined with other regularization techniques such as weight decay or early stopping to further improve generalization.

---

## Initialization Strategies and Zero/Identity Start for Safe Finetuning

Initialization plays a critical role in finetuning, especially for multiplicative reparameterizations like BOFT.

### Identity Initialization

BOFT initializes each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ as an identity matrix. Since each component is parameterized via a Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, this corresponds to initializing $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix.

This initialization ensures that the overall orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initially the identity matrix:

\[
\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \bm{I}_d = \bm{I}_d,
\]

meaning the finetuned weight matrix starts exactly as the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This zero-deviation start is crucial for stable finetuning, as it prevents sudden large changes in the model’s behavior at the beginning of training.

### Benefits of Identity Initialization

- **Preserves pretrained knowledge:** The model begins with the pretrained weights unchanged, ensuring that the finetuning process only gradually adapts the model.

- **Improves convergence:** Starting from identity reduces the risk of divergence or unstable gradients.

- **Facilitates interpretability:** The learned orthogonal transformations can be viewed as incremental rotations or perturbations from the identity.

### Alternative Initialization Strategies

While identity initialization is standard, one could consider initializing with small random perturbations around identity to encourage exploration. However, this risks destabilizing training and is generally not recommended without careful tuning.

---

## Computational Complexity: Per-Step Cost and Trade-Offs vs OFT and LoRA

BOFT’s parameterization introduces computational overhead compared to simpler finetuning methods due to the multiplicative composition of multiple butterfly components.

### Forward and Backward Pass Costs

- **Matrix multiplication:** Each forward pass requires multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by the product of $m$ butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Since each component is sparse and structured, the multiplication cost is $\mathcal{O}(d b)$ per component, leading to a total cost of $\mathcal{O}(m d b)$.

- **Gradient computation:** Backpropagation through the product of $m$ components requires computing gradients with respect to each component’s parameters, which involves additional matrix multiplications and chain rule applications.

- **Comparison to OFT:** Original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters and matrix multiplications, which can be more expensive for large $d$. Block-diagonal OFT reduces this cost but at the expense of expressivity.

- **Comparison to LoRA:** LoRA uses additive low-rank updates with parameter count $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ for rank $r$, and its forward pass involves a low-rank matrix multiplication, which is generally cheaper than BOFT’s multiplicative updates.

### Trade-Offs

- **Parameter efficiency vs runtime:** BOFT achieves a significant reduction in trainable parameters compared to full OFT (from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$), but this comes with increased per-step computation due to multiple sparse matrix multiplications.

- **Inference cost:** Importantly, BOFT’s orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be fused into the pretrained weights after finetuning, incurring no additional inference latency.

- **Training overhead:** The multiplicative structure means training is slower than block-diagonal OFT or LoRA, but the improved parameter efficiency and generalization often justify this cost.

---

## Memory Considerations and Ways to Fuse Matrices Post-Training

### Memory Footprint During Training

- **Parameter storage:** BOFT stores parameters for each butterfly component, which is $\mathcal{O}(m d b)$, significantly less than full OFT but more than LoRA for small ranks.

- **Intermediate activations:** Backpropagation through the product of $m$ components requires storing intermediate results for gradient computation, increasing memory usage.

- **Sparse structure advantage:** The sparsity and block structure of butterfly components help reduce memory compared to dense matrices.

### Post-Training Fusion for Inference

After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to produce a single updated weight matrix:

\[
\bm{W}^\text{finetuned} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0.
\]

This fusion eliminates the need to store or compute the product of butterfly components during inference, resulting in:

- **No additional inference latency:** The model runs as if it were fully finetuned.

- **Simplified deployment:** The finetuned weights can be saved and loaded as a standard weight matrix.

- **Reduced memory footprint:** Only the fused weight matrix needs to be stored.

---

## Practical Heuristics: When to Use Larger Block Size $b$ vs Larger Number of Stages $m$

BOFT’s parameterization depends on two key hyperparameters:

- **Block size $b$:** The size of blocks within butterfly components.

- **Number of stages $m$:** The number of butterfly components multiplied together.

The total number of trainable parameters scales roughly as $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$.

### Trade-Offs Between $b$ and $m$

- **Larger $b$ (block size):**

  - Increases the number of parameters per butterfly component.

  - Each block is a larger orthogonal matrix, potentially increasing expressivity within each component.

  - Fewer stages $m$ may be needed to achieve a given expressivity.

  - May increase per-step computation due to larger block multiplications.

- **Larger $m$ (number of stages):**

  - More butterfly components multiplied together.

  - Each component is sparser (smaller block size), reducing per-component parameter count.

  - Increases the depth of the factorization, potentially improving expressivity via composition.

  - Increases the number of sequential multiplications, potentially increasing runtime.

### Practical Recommendations

- For **small to medium-sized models or layers**, using a moderate block size (e.g., $b=4$ or $8$) with fewer stages ($m=2$ or $3$) can balance expressivity and efficiency.

- For **very large dimensions $d$**, using smaller block sizes (e.g., $b=2$) with more stages ($m \approx \log d$) leverages the full power of butterfly factorization and achieves $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameter scaling.

- When **runtime is a bottleneck**, prefer larger $b$ and smaller $m$ to reduce the number of sequential multiplications.

- When **parameter efficiency is paramount**, prefer smaller $b$ and larger $m$.

- Empirical tuning on validation sets is essential, as the optimal $(m,b)$ depends on the task, model architecture, and dataset.

---

# Summary

Training BOFT models requires careful consideration of optimization strategies, regularization, initialization, and computational trade-offs. The multiplicative nature of BOFT updates necessitates novel techniques such as multiplicative dropout to regularize without breaking orthogonality. Identity initialization ensures stable and safe finetuning starting from the pretrained model. While BOFT introduces some training overhead compared to simpler methods, its parameter efficiency and strong generalization benefits make it a compelling approach for adapting large foundation models. Practical heuristics for choosing block size and number of stages help tailor BOFT to diverse scenarios, balancing expressivity, efficiency, and runtime.

# Chapter 9: Theoretical Analysis: Expressivity, Spectra and Bias

In this chapter, we delve into the theoretical foundations underlying Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT). We explore why BOFT is a powerful and parameter-efficient method for finetuning large pretrained models, focusing on its expressivity, spectral properties, and the inductive biases it introduces. These theoretical insights help us understand the empirical successes of BOFT and guide its practical use.

---

## Expressivity of BOFT: Statement and Intuition of the Main Theorem

Expressivity refers to the ability of a parameterized model class to approximate a wide range of target functions or transformations. In the context of BOFT, expressivity concerns how well the class of orthogonal matrices parameterized by butterfly factorizations can approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices.

Recall that BOFT parameterizes the finetuning transformation matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components:

\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block butterfly component with block size $b$, and $m$ controls the number of stages (depth) of the factorization.

The main theoretical claim, formalized in Theorem 1 of the paper, states that BOFT is strictly more expressive than the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with the same block size. More precisely:

> **Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT):**  
> BOFT can represent a strictly larger subset of the orthogonal group than OFT with the same block size. Moreover, by composing multiple butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy, BOFT can approximate any orthogonal matrix arbitrarily well.

Intuitively, this means that while OFT with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices restricts transformations to independent rotations on disjoint subspaces (blocks), BOFT’s butterfly factorization allows for a richer set of transformations by enabling information mixing across all dimensions through a structured sequence of sparse orthogonal factors.

This expressivity is crucial because it allows BOFT to capture complex finetuning transformations with fewer parameters than a full dense orthogonal matrix, while avoiding the overly restrictive assumptions of block-diagonal OFT.

---

## Kaleidoscope Hierarchy and Approaching the Full Orthogonal Group

The kaleidoscope hierarchy is a concept from prior work on fast linear transforms and matrix factorization, which shows how products of butterfly matrices and their transposes can densely cover the entire orthogonal group.

Formally, the kaleidoscope hierarchy constructs an orthogonal matrix as a product of alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes:

\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1) \bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2) \cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1) \bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]

where each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(m,b)$ is a BOFT orthogonal matrix parameterized by butterfly components.

When the number of stages $m_1, m_2$ and block sizes $b_1, b_2$ are chosen appropriately (e.g., $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d-1$), this product can represent any orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ exactly.

This result implies that BOFT, when generalized to such a kaleidoscope hierarchy, is a universal parameterization of the orthogonal group. In practice, however, using fewer stages and moderate block sizes strikes a balance between expressivity and parameter efficiency.

---

## Proof Sketch and Key Lemmas (High-Level, Accessible)

While the full proof of the kaleidoscope hierarchy’s universality is beyond this chapter’s scope, we provide an intuitive sketch of why products of butterfly matrices can approximate any orthogonal matrix.

1. **Butterfly matrices as building blocks:**  
   Each butterfly matrix is a product of sparse orthogonal factors that perform local rotations and permutations. These local operations mix information between pairs or small groups of coordinates.

2. **Recursive structure and mixing:**  
   The recursive construction of butterfly matrices ensures that after $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages, information from any input coordinate can influence any output coordinate. This dense connectivity is essential for approximating arbitrary orthogonal transformations.

3. **Alternating products and transposes:**  
   By composing butterfly matrices and their transposes in an alternating fashion (the kaleidoscope hierarchy), the parameterization gains the ability to represent more complex rotations that cannot be captured by a single butterfly matrix alone.

4. **Density and coverage:**  
   The set of matrices generated by these products is dense in the orthogonal group, meaning any orthogonal matrix can be approximated arbitrarily closely by some product of butterfly matrices.

5. **Parameter count and efficiency:**  
   Despite this expressivity, the number of parameters grows only as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, which is significantly more efficient than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required for a full dense orthogonal matrix.

This proof sketch highlights the power of structured sparse factorizations in capturing complex transformations efficiently.

## Spectral Properties: Why BOFT Preserves Singular Values and Implications

A key advantage of orthogonal finetuning methods like OFT and BOFT is their preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. This property has important implications for training stability and generalization.

Consider a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ with singular value decomposition (SVD):

\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top,
\]

where $\bm{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\bm{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values.

In BOFT, the finetuned weight matrix is:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0,
\]

where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix parameterized by BOFT.

Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves singular values:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top = (\bm{R} \bm{U}) \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top,
\]

and since $\bm{R} \bm{U}$ is also orthogonal, the singular values of $\bm{W}$ are the same as those of $\bm{W}^0$.

**Implications:**

- **Training stability:** Preserving the spectral norm prevents exploding or vanishing gradients during backpropagation, which is crucial for stable finetuning of large models.

- **Generalization:** Maintaining the spectral properties of pretrained weights helps retain the learned representations and prevents overfitting to downstream tasks.

- **Contrast with additive methods:** Methods like LoRA add low-rank updates, which can alter the spectral norm and potentially destabilize training.

Thus, BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal updates provide a principled way to adapt pretrained weights while preserving their essential spectral characteristics.

---

## BOFT as Learning Bilinear Similarity / Metric Learning Interpretation

BOFT’s finetuning transformation can be interpreted as learning a bilinear similarity function with strong regularization.

Recall that the output of a linear layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and input $\bm{x}$ is:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]

BOFT modifies this to:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]

Focusing on a single neuron (column) $\bm{w}^0_i$ of $\bm{W}^0$, the output is:

\[
z_i = (\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x} = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]

This can be viewed as a bilinear form:

\[
z_i = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x} = \langle \bm{w}^0_i, \bm{R}^\top \bm{x} \rangle,
\]

where $\bm{R}$ acts as a learned bilinear similarity matrix between the neuron and the input.

Because $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal, this bilinear similarity respects a metric structure, preserving angles and distances in the transformed space. This connects BOFT to metric learning and bilinear form theory, where learning a similarity or distance metric under orthogonality constraints is a well-studied problem.

This interpretation provides intuition for why BOFT preserves pretrained knowledge (angles between neurons) while allowing flexible adaptation.

---

## Inductive Bias Induced by Structured Factorization: Benefits and Risks

Inductive bias refers to the assumptions a learning algorithm makes to generalize beyond the training data. BOFT’s structured parameterization introduces a specific inductive bias through its butterfly factorization.

**Benefits:**

- **Structured hypothesis class:** By restricting $\bm{R}$ to be a product of butterfly components, BOFT limits the space of possible finetuning transformations to those that can be efficiently represented by sparse, recursive mixing patterns.

- **Implicit regularization:** This constraint acts as a form of regularization, preventing overfitting by disallowing arbitrary dense orthogonal transformations.

- **Connection to classical transforms:** Butterfly structures underlie many classical fast transforms (e.g., discrete Fourier transform, Hadamard transform). This connection suggests that BOFT’s parameterization aligns well with natural data structures and signal processing principles, potentially improving generalization.

- **Efficient information mixing:** The butterfly topology ensures that information from all input dimensions can influence all output dimensions through a logarithmic number of sparse steps, balancing expressivity and efficiency.

**Risks:**

- **Limited expressivity for some tasks:** Although BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, it still represents a subset of the full orthogonal group. For some downstream tasks, this restriction might limit adaptation performance.

- **Potential bias towards certain transformations:** The butterfly structure may favor transformations similar to classical fast transforms, which might not always align with the optimal finetuning transformation for a given task.

Overall, the inductive bias introduced by BOFT is a deliberate design choice to improve parameter efficiency and generalization, trading off some expressivity for better practical performance.

---

## When More Expressivity Can Harm Generalization: Trade-Offs Explained

A natural question arises: if BOFT can approximate any orthogonal matrix by increasing the number of stages and block sizes, why not always use the most expressive parameterization?

The answer lies in the classic trade-off between expressivity and generalization:

- **High expressivity (e.g., full dense orthogonal matrices):**  
  Allows the model to fit a wide range of finetuning transformations, including those that perfectly adapt to the downstream task. However, this flexibility can lead to overfitting, especially when the downstream dataset is small or noisy.

- **Low expressivity (e.g., block-diagonal OFT):**  
  Strongly restricts the finetuning transformations, which may prevent the model from adapting sufficiently to the task, leading to underfitting.

- **Moderate expressivity (e.g., BOFT with moderate $m$ and $b$):**  
  Provides a structured, parameter-efficient hypothesis class that balances adaptation capacity and regularization, often yielding better generalization.

Empirically, full finetuning or unconstrained orthogonal finetuning often performs worse than structured methods like BOFT, due to overfitting or optimization difficulties.

Thus, BOFT’s design enables practitioners to tune the expressivity via $m$ (number of butterfly stages) and $b$ (block size), finding a sweet spot that maximizes downstream performance while maintaining parameter efficiency and training stability.

# Pseudo-code for BOFT Forward and Backward Passes

At the core of BOFT is the reparameterization of a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ by multiplying it on the left with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which is factorized as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components:

\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)} \cdot \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m-1)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block butterfly component parameterized to be orthogonal.

The forward pass computes the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b) \bm{W}^0$ and then applies it to input vectors $\bm{x}$. The backward pass computes gradients with respect to the parameters of each butterfly component.

Below is high-level pseudo-code illustrating the forward and backward computations:

```python
# Pseudo-code for BOFT forward pass
def BOFT_forward(x, W0, butterfly_components):
    """
    x: input vector or batch, shape (batch_size, d)
    W0: pretrained weight matrix, shape (d, n)
    butterfly_components: list of m orthogonal butterfly components,
                          each of shape (d, d), sparse block structure
    
    Returns:
        z: output vector or batch, shape (batch_size, n)
    """
    # Compute R = product of butterfly components
    R = identity_matrix(d)
    for B in butterfly_components:
        R = B @ R  # Matrix multiplication
    
    # Compute finetuned weights
    W = R @ W0  # shape (d, n)
    
    # Forward pass: z = W^T x
    z = x @ W  # shape (batch_size, n)
    return z
```

The backward pass is handled automatically by modern autodiff frameworks, but care must be taken to implement the butterfly components and their parameterizations correctly to enable gradient flow.

# Implementing Cayley Parameterization for Block Components

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks of size $2b \times 2b$ (or $b \times b$ depending on notation). To ensure orthogonality, each block is parameterized via the Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix.

Recall the Cayley transform for an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric: $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$.

**Implementation details:**

- For each block, parameterize the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ by storing only the upper-triangular entries (excluding diagonal, which are zero).
- Compute $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley transform.
- Initialize $\bm{Q}$ to zero to start from the identity matrix.

**Code snippet (PyTorch-like):**

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class CayleyOrthogonalBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, block_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.block_size = block_size
        # Number of parameters for skew-symmetric matrix: block_size * (block_size - 1) / 2
        self.num_params = block_size * (block_size - 1) // 2
        # Parameters stored as a vector
        self.params = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(self.num_params))
    
    def skew_symmetric_matrix(self):
        Q = torch.zeros(self.block_size, self.block_size, device=self.params.device)
        # Fill upper triangle with params
        idx = 0
        for i in range(self.block_size):
            for j in range(i+1, self.block_size):
                Q[i, j] = self.params[idx]
                Q[j, i] = -self.params[idx]
                idx += 1
        return Q
    
    def forward(self):
        Q = self.skew_symmetric_matrix()
        I = torch.eye(self.block_size, device=Q.device)
        # Compute Cayley transform
        R = torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)  # (I - Q)^{-1} (I + Q)
        return R
```

In BOFT, many such blocks are arranged in a block-diagonal pattern with permutations to form the butterfly component.

# Integrating BOFT into PyTorch / TensorFlow Model Layers

To integrate BOFT into an existing pretrained model, the key idea is to replace or augment the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ of a linear or convolutional layer with the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b) \bm{W}^0$.

**Modular design:**

- Implement a `BOFTLayer` module that wraps a pretrained linear layer.
- Store the pretrained weights as frozen parameters.
- Maintain trainable parameters for the butterfly components.
- During forward pass, compute $\bm{R}(m,b)$ from butterfly components, multiply with $\bm{W}^0$, and apply to input.

**Example PyTorch module skeleton:**

```python
class BOFTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, pretrained_weight, m, b):
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = nn.Parameter(pretrained_weight, requires_grad=False)
        self.m = m
        self.b = b
        self.d = pretrained_weight.shape[0]
        
        # Initialize butterfly components as a list of modules
        self.butterfly_components = nn.ModuleList([
            ButterflyComponent(self.d, self.b) for _ in range(m)
        ])
    
    def forward(self, x):
        R = torch.eye(self.d, device=x.device)
        for B in self.butterfly_components:
            R = B() @ R  # Each B() returns an orthogonal matrix
        W = R @ self.W0
        return x @ W
```

Here, `ButterflyComponent` is a module that constructs the block butterfly matrix from Cayley-parameterized blocks and applies the necessary permutations.

# Efficient Batched Multiplication of Sparse Factors and Permutation Handling

Naively multiplying $m$ dense $d \times d$ matrices in each forward pass is computationally expensive. However, butterfly components are highly sparse and structured, enabling efficient computation.

**Key points:**

- Each butterfly component is block-diagonal with small blocks (e.g., $2 \times 2$ or $4 \times 4$).
- Multiplication by a butterfly component can be implemented as a sequence of small block multiplications and permutations.
- Permutations correspond to fixed index shuffles and can be implemented efficiently without materializing permutation matrices.
- The product $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ can be computed by sequentially applying these sparse block operations and permutations to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ directly, avoiding explicit construction of full $d \times d$ matrices.

**Implementation strategy:**

- Represent each butterfly component as a function that takes a matrix and applies block-wise orthogonal transformations and permutations.
- Compose these functions sequentially to compute $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ efficiently.
- Use batched matrix multiplications for blocks to leverage GPU parallelism.

**Example outline:**

```python
def apply_butterfly_component(W, blocks_params, permutation):
    """
    W: input matrix of shape (d, n)
    blocks_params: parameters for each block (e.g., Cayley params)
    permutation: permutation indices to shuffle rows
    
    Returns:
        W_new: transformed matrix after applying butterfly component
    """
    # 1. Permute rows of W according to permutation
    W_perm = W[permutation, :]
    
    # 2. For each block, apply orthogonal block transformation
    W_blocks = W_perm.view(num_blocks, block_size, -1)  # reshape for batched matmul
    R_blocks = compute_orthogonal_blocks(blocks_params)  # shape (num_blocks, block_size, block_size)
    W_transformed = torch.bmm(R_blocks, W_blocks)
    
    # 3. Reshape back to (d, n)
    W_new = W_transformed.view(d, -1)
    return W_new
```

By chaining such functions for all $m$ butterfly components, we avoid constructing large dense matrices and reduce memory and computation.

# Optimizations: Grouping Blocks, Custom CUDA Kernels, and Lazy Fusion

To further improve runtime efficiency and reduce overhead during training, several optimizations can be applied:

1. **Grouping Blocks:**

   - Group multiple small blocks into larger batches to maximize GPU utilization.
   - Use batched matrix multiplications (`torch.bmm`) to process all blocks in parallel.

2. **Custom CUDA Kernels:**

   - Implement custom CUDA kernels for butterfly operations and permutations to minimize memory movement and kernel launch overhead.
   - Such kernels can fuse permutation and block multiplication steps.

3. **Lazy Fusion:**

   - Instead of computing the full product $\bm{R}$ at every forward pass, delay multiplication by caching intermediate results.
   - For example, precompute partial products of butterfly components and update only when parameters change.
   - This reduces redundant computation during multiple forward passes in the same training iteration.

4. **Mixed Precision and Quantization:**

   - Use mixed precision (FP16) to speed up computation and reduce memory.
   - Quantize butterfly parameters if applicable, trading off precision for speed.

5. **Memory Sharing:**

   - Reuse memory buffers for intermediate computations to reduce peak memory usage.

# Post-Training Fusion: Multiplying $\bm{R}$ into Pretrained Weights for Inference

One major advantage of BOFT is that after finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be fused into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to produce a single finetuned weight matrix:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0.
\]

This fusion eliminates any additional inference latency or complexity introduced by BOFT.

**Fusion procedure:**

1. Compute the full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying all butterfly components.

2. Multiply $\bm{R}$ with the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain the finetuned weights $\bm{W}$.

3. Replace the original weight matrix in the model with $\bm{W}$.

4. Remove BOFT modules and parameters from the model.

This fusion can be done once after training and before deployment.

# Suggested Hyperparameter Defaults for Common Model Families

Choosing appropriate hyperparameters $(m, b)$ for BOFT depends on the model size, layer dimensions, and parameter budget.

- **Small to medium models (e.g., ViT-base, GPT-2 small):**

  - Block size $b = 4$ or $8$.
  - Number of stages $m = \log_2(d / b)$ (e.g., for $d=512$, $m=6$ if $b=8$).
  - This balances parameter efficiency and expressivity.

- **Large models (e.g., GPT-3, large ViTs):**

  - Smaller block size $b=2$ to maximize expressivity.
  - Larger $m$ to maintain dense connectivity.
  - Use multiplicative dropout to regularize.

- **Text-to-image diffusion models:**

  - Use $b=4$ or $8$ with moderate $m$.
  - Identity initialization and multiplicative dropout are important for stable training.

- **General recommendations:**

  - Initialize all butterfly components to identity (zero skew-symmetric parameters).
  - Use learning rates similar to OFT or LoRA finetuning.
  - Apply multiplicative dropout with dropout rates $p_1=0.1$ (component dropout) and $p_2=0.1$ (block dropout) as starting points.

# Common Pitfalls and Debugging Checklist

Implementing BOFT can be challenging due to the complexity of butterfly structures and orthogonal parameterization. Below are common pitfalls and tips to debug:

1. **Orthogonality Violations:**

   - Ensure Cayley parameterization is correctly implemented.
   - Check that skew-symmetric matrices are constructed properly.
   - Verify that each butterfly component is orthogonal by testing $B^\top B = I$ numerically.

2. **Incorrect Permutations:**

   - Butterfly components rely on precise permutations.
   - Validate permutation indices carefully.
   - Use unit tests to confirm that permutations are invertible and consistent.

3. **Initialization Issues:**

   - Initialize skew-symmetric parameters to zero to start from identity.
   - Non-zero initialization can cause unstable training.

4. **Gradient Flow Problems:**

   - Confirm that all parameters are registered as trainable.
   - Check for detached tensors or in-place operations that break gradients.

5. **Runtime Overhead:**

   - Profile code to identify bottlenecks.
   - Use batched operations and avoid explicit loops over blocks.

6. **Numerical Stability:**

   - Cayley transform involves matrix inversion; add small damping if needed.
   - Monitor condition numbers and singular values during training.

7. **Mismatch in Dimensions:**

   - Ensure $d$ is divisible by $b$.
   - Handle non-power-of-two dimensions by padding or truncation.

8. **Overfitting or Underfitting:**

   - Tune multiplicative dropout rates.
   - Adjust $m$ and $b$ to control model capacity.

# Chapter 13: Extensions, Limitations and Future Research Directions

In this chapter, we critically examine the Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) method, focusing on its current limitations and potential avenues for future research. While BOFT represents a significant advance in parameter-efficient finetuning by leveraging butterfly factorization to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters, it is not without challenges. We will discuss practical and theoretical limitations, explore how to handle real-world constraints such as non-power-of-two dimensions, and consider whether the butterfly structure is the best possible choice. We also explore promising directions for hybrid methods, automated architecture search, and broader applications beyond supervised finetuning. Finally, we outline experimental directions that can further validate and extend BOFT’s utility.

---

## Limitations Highlighted in the Paper: Training Runtime and Overhead

One of the primary limitations of BOFT, as identified in the original paper, is the increased training runtime overhead compared to the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). This overhead arises because BOFT represents the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal butterfly components:

\[
\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. During training, each forward and backward pass requires sequential multiplication through these $m$ components, which is computationally more expensive than applying a single orthogonal matrix or a block-diagonal matrix as in OFT.

While this multiplicative factorization reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, the trade-off is an increase in per-iteration computation. This overhead can be significant for very large models or when computational resources are limited. However, it is important to note that this overhead only affects training; after finetuning, the product of butterfly components can be fused into a single dense orthogonal matrix and multiplied into the pretrained weights, incurring no additional inference latency.

Addressing this training overhead is an open problem. Potential solutions include optimized implementations of butterfly matrix multiplications, parallelization strategies, or approximations that reduce the number of butterfly components without sacrificing expressivity. Research into efficient sparse matrix multiplication kernels tailored for butterfly structures could also alleviate this bottleneck.

---

## Handling Non-Power-of-Two Dimensions and Irregular Block Sizes

The butterfly factorization and the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, which inspire BOFT, are naturally defined for dimensions $d$ that are powers of two. This is because the recursive divide-and-conquer structure splits the dimension into halves repeatedly. However, in practice, model layers often have dimensions that are not powers of two, and block sizes $b$ may not neatly divide $d$.

This mismatch poses a practical challenge: how to apply BOFT to arbitrary layer dimensions without excessive padding or dimension adjustments that may waste parameters or introduce artifacts.

Several strategies can be considered:

1. **Padding to the Next Power of Two:** The simplest approach is to pad the weight matrices to the next power of two dimension. While straightforward, this increases parameter count and computation unnecessarily.

2. **Generalized Butterfly Structures:** Extending butterfly factorization to handle arbitrary dimensions is an active area of research. Variants of the Cooley-Tukey algorithm exist for composite dimensions (products of small primes), and similar ideas could be adapted to BOFT. This would involve defining butterfly components that split dimensions unevenly or according to factorization of $d$.

3. **Hybrid Block Sizes:** Instead of uniform block sizes, one could allow variable block sizes that better fit the layer dimension. This would require more complex bookkeeping but could improve parameter efficiency.

4. **Approximate Factorizations:** For dimensions that do not fit the butterfly pattern exactly, approximate factorizations that relax strict orthogonality or sparsity constraints could be used.

Developing flexible BOFT variants that handle arbitrary dimensions efficiently is an important practical extension to make the method widely applicable.

---

## Is Butterfly Optimal? Other Network Topologies from Communication Theory

BOFT’s key insight is to view the factorization of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem on a layered graph, where each factor corresponds to edges connecting nodes between adjacent layers. The butterfly graph topology, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm, achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, which translates to parameter efficiency.

However, the question remains: is the butterfly structure the most efficient or best topology for this purpose?

From the perspective of communication and network theory, many other network topologies have been studied for efficient information transmission, including:

- **Hypercube Networks:** These have logarithmic diameter and high connectivity, potentially enabling efficient mixing with different sparsity patterns.

- **Expander Graphs:** Known for strong connectivity and spectral properties, expanders could provide robust information mixing with sparse connections.

- **De Bruijn and Kautz Graphs:** These directed graphs have small diameters and high connectivity, which might translate to efficient matrix factorizations.

- **Shuffle-Exchange Networks:** Used in parallel computing, these have structured sparse connections that could be adapted.

Each topology offers different trade-offs in terms of connectivity, sparsity, and ease of parameterization under orthogonality constraints. The butterfly structure is attractive because it is well-studied, has a recursive definition, and is closely tied to classical fast transforms, which may induce beneficial inductive biases.

Future research could explore alternative topologies within the information transmission framework to discover factorizations that further reduce parameters, improve expressivity, or accelerate training. Automated search over network topologies or hybrid topologies combining features of multiple graphs is another promising direction.

---

## Combining BOFT with LoRA or Adapters: Hybrid PEFT Strategies

BOFT and LoRA represent two distinct approaches to parameter-efficient finetuning: BOFT uses multiplicative orthogonal transformations parameterized by sparse butterfly factorizations, while LoRA uses additive low-rank updates.

Each approach has strengths and weaknesses. LoRA is simple, effective, and widely adopted, but may not preserve spectral properties or relational structure of pretrained weights. BOFT preserves orthogonality and spectral norms, potentially improving stability and generalization, but at the cost of more complex training.

A natural extension is to combine these approaches into hybrid PEFT methods that leverage the complementary benefits of both:

- **Additive + Multiplicative Updates:** One could finetune weights as

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 + \bm{L},
\]

where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix parameterized by BOFT and $\bm{L}$ is a low-rank additive update as in LoRA. This allows the model to capture both multiplicative transformations preserving angles and additive corrections.

- **Layer-wise or Module-wise Hybridization:** Some layers could be finetuned with BOFT, others with LoRA or adapters, depending on their sensitivity or parameter budgets.

- **Regularization and Constraints:** The additive and multiplicative components could be jointly regularized to balance expressivity and stability.

Such hybrid methods could improve performance and parameter efficiency, especially in large models or complex tasks. They also open questions about optimization dynamics and interactions between additive and multiplicative updates.

## Automatic Search for Factorization Parameters (m, b) per Layer

BOFT introduces two key hyperparameters controlling the factorization:

- $m$: the number of butterfly stages (depth of factorization)
- $b$: the block size within butterfly components

These parameters govern the trade-off between parameter count, expressivity, and computational cost. For example, increasing $m$ increases expressivity and parameter count, while increasing $b$ increases block size and parameter count but may improve approximation quality.

Currently, these parameters are typically set uniformly across layers or chosen heuristically. However, different layers in large models may have different sensitivity to finetuning and different optimal parameter budgets.

Automated methods to select $(m,b)$ per layer could improve efficiency and performance:

- **Hyperparameter Optimization:** Use Bayesian optimization or evolutionary strategies to tune $(m,b)$ per layer.

- **Neural Architecture Search (NAS):** Incorporate BOFT parameterization choices into NAS frameworks.

- **Gradient-based Meta-learning:** Learn layer-wise $(m,b)$ during training via differentiable proxies.

- **Adaptive Sparsity:** Dynamically prune or grow butterfly components during training.

Such automated search would tailor the finetuning parameterization to the model and task, potentially yielding better trade-offs and reducing manual tuning.

## Theoretical Open Problems: Expressivity vs Generalization Trade-offs

BOFT’s theoretical foundation includes the expressivity of butterfly factorizations to approximate dense orthogonal matrices with fewer parameters. The kaleidoscope hierarchy shows that products of butterfly matrices can represent the entire orthogonal group.

However, more expressivity does not always translate to better finetuning performance. Full finetuning, which is maximally expressive, often overfits or converges poorly. Conversely, overly restrictive parameterizations may underfit.

Key theoretical questions remain open:

- **Characterizing the Hypothesis Class:** What is the effective function class induced by BOFT with given $(m,b)$? How does it compare to full orthogonal matrices or block-diagonal OFT?

- **Generalization Bounds:** Can we derive generalization guarantees or bounds that relate the structure of BOFT to sample complexity or robustness?

- **Optimization Landscape:** How does the butterfly factorization affect the geometry of the loss surface and convergence properties?

- **Implicit Bias:** What implicit regularization does the butterfly structure induce during gradient-based optimization?

- **Trade-offs:** How to balance expressivity and regularity optimally for different tasks?

Addressing these questions would deepen our understanding of BOFT and guide principled design of parameter-efficient finetuning methods.

While BOFT is primarily proposed for parameter-efficient finetuning of large pretrained models, its core idea—parameterizing dense orthogonal matrices via sparse butterfly factorizations—has broader applicability.

Some promising directions include:

- **Model Compression:** Orthogonal butterfly factorizations could be used to compress large weight matrices by approximating them with structured sparse factors, reducing storage and computation.

- **Pruning and Sparsification:** The information transmission view and butterfly structure could guide structured pruning strategies that maintain connectivity and expressivity.

- **Efficient Inference:** After finetuning, the fused orthogonal matrix could enable fast inference with structured linear transforms.

- **Continual Learning:** The structured parameterization may facilitate incremental updates and reduce catastrophic forgetting.

- **Robustness and Regularization:** Orthogonal constraints and structured factorizations may improve robustness to adversarial attacks or noisy inputs.

- **Other Domains:** Applications in signal processing, graph neural networks, or kernel methods where orthogonal transforms are useful.

Exploring these applications requires adapting BOFT’s principles and developing new algorithms tailored to these contexts.

To fully realize BOFT’s potential and understand its practical impact, several experimental directions are promising:

- **Scaling to Larger Models:** Applying BOFT to state-of-the-art foundation models with hundreds of billions of parameters to validate scalability and efficiency.

- **Multi-task and Transfer Learning:** Evaluating BOFT’s ability to finetune a single model on multiple tasks or transfer knowledge across domains.

# Proofs and Complete Derivations

## Theorem on Expressivity of BOFT

Recall the key theoretical result from Chapter 9:

**Theorem 9.1 (Expressivity of BOFT).**  
*BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Specifically, the entire orthogonal group \( O(d) \) can be approximated arbitrarily well by products of butterfly matrices of the form*  
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d) \bm{B}_{d-1,2}^\top(d) \cdots \bm{B}_{1,1}(d) \bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d),
\]  
*where each \(\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)\) is a butterfly matrix.*

### Intuition and Sketch of Proof

The theorem states that by composing a finite sequence of butterfly-structured orthogonal matrices and their transposes, one can approximate any orthogonal matrix arbitrarily closely. This is a powerful statement about the density of the subgroup generated by butterfly matrices within the full orthogonal group.

The proof relies on the following key ideas:

1. **Butterfly matrices as building blocks:** Butterfly matrices correspond to sparse orthogonal matrices with a recursive block structure. Each butterfly matrix can be viewed as a product of simple orthogonal transformations acting on pairs of coordinates (e.g., Givens rotations).

2. **Kaleidoscope hierarchy:** The product of butterfly matrices and their transposes forms a "kaleidoscope" factorization, which has been shown in prior work (e.g., Dao et al., 2019) to be dense in \( O(d) \).

3. **Approximation via Givens rotations:** Since any orthogonal matrix can be decomposed into a product of Givens rotations, and butterfly matrices can represent collections of such rotations arranged in a structured manner, their products can approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices.

4. **Density argument:** By increasing the number of factors (i.e., the depth of the product), the approximation error can be made arbitrarily small.

### Formal Proof Sketch

- **Step 1:** Recall that any orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R} \in O(d) \) can be decomposed into a product of \( \frac{d(d-1)}{2} \) Givens rotations, each acting on a 2D subspace.

- **Step 2:** Butterfly matrices are constructed from block-diagonal matrices of size 2 (or block size \(b\)) that are orthogonal. Each such block corresponds to a Givens rotation or a small orthogonal transform.

- **Step 3:** By carefully arranging these butterfly factors and their transposes, one can cover all pairs of coordinates required for the Givens rotations in the decomposition.

- **Step 4:** The product  
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d) \bm{B}_{d-1,2}^\top(d) \cdots \bm{B}_{1,1}(d) \bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d)
\]  
thus can represent any orthogonal matrix exactly or approximate it arbitrarily well.

- **Step 5:** Since BOFT parameterizes orthogonal matrices as products of butterfly components, it follows that BOFT can represent a dense subset of \( O(d) \), and is strictly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size.

For a complete and rigorous proof, readers are referred to the kaleidoscope hierarchy construction in Dao et al. (2019) and related literature on structured orthogonal matrix factorizations.

# Details of Cayley Transform Parameterization and Numerical Stability

## Cayley Transform for Orthogonal Matrices

To maintain orthogonality of the finetuning matrix \(\bm{R}\) during training, BOFT and OFT use the Cayley transform parameterization:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]

where \(\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is a skew-symmetric matrix, i.e., \(\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}\).

### Properties

- The Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices without eigenvalue \(-1\).
- It provides a smooth and differentiable parameterization suitable for gradient-based optimization.
- Initialization with \(\bm{Q} = \bm{0}\) yields \(\bm{R} = \bm{I}\), ensuring zero deviation from pretrained weights at the start.

### Implementation Details

- In BOFT, \(\bm{Q}\) is block-sparse, corresponding to the butterfly factorization structure.
- Each block (e.g., \(2 \times 2\) or \(b \times b\)) is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix.
- The inverse \((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\) is computed efficiently due to the block-sparse structure.

### Numerical Stability Considerations

- The Cayley transform is undefined if \(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}\) is singular, which can occur if \(\bm{Q}\) has eigenvalue 1.
- To avoid instability, the magnitude of \(\bm{Q}\) is kept small during training, often by using small learning rates or gradient clipping.
- Initialization at zero ensures starting in a stable region.
- In practice, the block sizes and number of butterfly stages are chosen to balance expressivity and numerical stability.

# Derivation of Parameter Counts and Computational Complexity

## Parameter Counts

Let:

- \(d\) be the dimension of the orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\).
- \(b\) be the block size in the butterfly components.
- \(m\) be the number of butterfly stages (layers).

### Original OFT

- Full orthogonal matrix: \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters.
- Block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\): number of blocks \(r = \frac{d}{b}\).
- Each block is an orthogonal matrix of size \(b \times b\), parameterized by \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) parameters (dimension of skew-symmetric matrices).
- Total parameters:  
\[
r \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d}{b} \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2}.
\]

### BOFT

- BOFT parameterizes \(\bm{R}\) as a product of \(m\) butterfly components.
- Each butterfly component \(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)\) is block-diagonal with blocks of size \(2b \times 2b\).
- Each block is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix with \(\frac{2b(2b-1)}{2} = 2b(2b-1)/2\) parameters.
- Number of blocks per butterfly component: \(\frac{d}{2b}\).
- Parameters per butterfly component:  
\[
\frac{d}{2b} \times \frac{2b(2b-1)}{2} = \frac{d}{2b} \times b(2b-1) = \frac{d b (2b - 1)}{2b} = \frac{d(2b - 1)}{2}.
\]
- For simplicity, approximate as \(\frac{d (2b - 1)}{2} \approx d b\) for moderate \(b\).
- Total parameters for \(m\) stages:  
\[
m \times \frac{d (2b - 1)}{2} \approx m \times d b.
\]

In the paper, the effective trainable parameters are given as:

\[
\frac{1}{2} (b - 1) d m,
\]

which aligns with the above derivation when considering the skew-symmetric parameter count per block.

### Summary

| Method               | Parameter Count                 |
|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| Full OFT             | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\)            |
| Block-diagonal OFT    | \(\mathcal{O}(d b)\)             |
| BOFT                 | \(\mathcal{O}(d b m)\), typically \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) when \(m = \log d\) and \(b=2\) |

---

## Computational Complexity

- **Forward pass:**  
  BOFT requires multiplying the pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) by the product of \(m\) butterfly components \(\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\). Each butterfly component is sparse with \(\mathcal{O}(d b)\) non-zero entries.

- **Cost per forward pass:**  
  \[
  \mathcal{O}(m \times d \times b \times n),
  \]
  where \(n\) is the output dimension of the linear layer.

- **Comparison:**  
  - Full OFT: \(\mathcal{O}(d^2 n)\)  
  - Block-diagonal OFT: \(\mathcal{O}(d b n)\)  
  - BOFT: \(\mathcal{O}(m d b n)\), with \(m = \log d\) typically.

- **Training overhead:**  
  BOFT has higher training cost than block-diagonal OFT due to multiple matrix multiplications but remains more efficient than full OFT.
