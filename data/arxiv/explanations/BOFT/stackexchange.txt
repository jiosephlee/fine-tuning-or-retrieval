\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What exactly is being multiplied by the orthogonal matrix in OFT/BOFT for non-square layers?
Question:
In the paper, a pretrained linear layer has weights $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and the finetuned weights are $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ orthogonal. I'm confused about how $d$ is chosen in practice for different modules:
- For attention, do you apply one $\mathbf{R}$ to each of $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$ (each with potentially different input/output dims), or share one $\mathbf{R}$ across them?
- For MLPs with $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 4d}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4d \times d}$, is $\mathbf{R}$ applied only to the $d$-input side (i.e., left-multiply $W_1$ and $W_2$), or something else?
- For convolutional or embedding layers, how do you define $d$ and apply $\mathbf{R}$ consistently?
Answer:
Short answer: $R$ multiplies the neuron vectors (the column vectors of the pretrained weight matrix) on the left. For every parameter matrix you treat "d" as the length of a neuron (the input dimension the layer expects) and you left-multiply that weight matrix by an orthogonal $R$ of size $d\times d$. If several parameter matrices share the same input/neuron dimension you may apply the same $R$ (i.e. share $R$ across them) — BOFT/OFT operate per-layer (or per group of layers that have the same input dimensionality) unless you choose to tie or split them.

Explanation and how this maps to the modules you asked about

The paper defines OFT/BOFT by starting from a pretrained linear layer $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and replacing the forward pass $z = (W^0)^T x$ with $z = (R W^0)^T x$, where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal. That formulation makes two facts concrete and simple to remember: (1) the columns of $W^0$ (the "neurons") live in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and (2) OFT/BOFT left-multiply $W^0$ by an orthogonal transform acting on those neuron vectors. Thus "d" is the neuron length / the input dimension to that linear map.

Attention blocks. In standard attention each projection ($W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$, $W_O$) is a linear map whose input dimension is typically the model hidden size (call it $d_{\text{model}}$). In the paper’s formulation you would use an $R$ of size $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$ and left-multiply each such weight matrix: $W_Q \leftarrow R W_Q^0$, $W_K \leftarrow R W_K^0$, etc. You can either give each projection its own $R$ or reuse (share) one $R$ across them if they all share the same input dimensionality — sharing is an option to reduce parameter cost and the paper already discusses "layer-shared weight matrices" and parameter-efficiency tradeoffs. The important point is $R$'s size is dictated by the input/neuron dimension (here $d_{\text{model}}$).

MLP feed‑forward (W1 and W2). The paper’s convention again says use $R$ sized to the input-neuron length of the linear layer you are transforming, and left-multiply. For the FFN where $W1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times 4d}$ (maps $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to a $4d$ hidden vector) you apply $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as $R W1^0$. For $W2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4d\times d}$ (maps the $4d$ hidden back to $\mathbb{R}^d$) the input neuron length is $4d$, so a separate $R$ of size $4d\times 4d$ would be the natural orthogonal to apply: $W2 \leftarrow R_{4d} W2^0$ with $R_{4d} \in \mathbb{R}^{4d\times 4d}$. You could instead decide to only finetune the input side of the entire MLP (for example, apply $R$ to $W1$ but not to $W2$), or to tie $R$ across layers if dimensions match — those are design choices but the paper’s basic mathematical prescription is always "left-multiply by an orthogonal $R$ whose dimension equals the input/neuron length of that linear layer."

Convolutions and embeddings. Apply the same rule: treat each convolutional or embedding weight matrix as a linear map whose columns are neurons of length equal to the layer’s input dimension (i.e., the flattened input-channel $\times$ kernel-size for a conv, or the embedding dimension for an embedding table). For a convolution with weight shape $(\text{out\_ch},\ \text{in\_ch},\ kH,\ kW)$ you can view each column (neuron) as living in $\mathbb{R}^{\text{in\_ch}\times kH \times kW}$, so you would use $R \in \mathbb{R}^{(\text{in\_ch}\times kH \times kW)\times(\text{in\_ch}\times kH \times kW)}$ and left-multiply the flattened weight matrix by that $R$. For an embedding matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{emb}}\times V}$, the natural $R$ is $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{emb}}\times d_{\text{emb}}}$ and you do $E \leftarrow R E^0$. In other words "d" is the length of a neuron in that layer: embedding dimension for embedding tables, flattened input-channel$\times$kernel for convs.

Practical notes and trade-offs from the paper
- BOFT parameterizes $R$ efficiently (butterfly/block-butterfly) rather than as dense $d\times d$ when $d$ is large; the algorithmic form remains $R W^0$ (left multiplication), but $R$ is built as a product of sparse orthogonal factors to reduce trainable parameters and enforce structure. Identity initialization and Cayley/skew-symmetric parameterization keep $R$ close to the pretrained model at start, as discussed in the paper.
- Sharing an $R$ across multiple matrices that have the same neuron length is a valid implementation choice and reduces parameter count; the paper even discusses layer-shared orthogonal matrices. Conversely, if two matrices have different input dimensions you cannot directly reuse the same $R$ without projection or reshaping — the natural approach is separate $R$'s sized to each layer’s input.
- You can also choose block sizes $b$ in BOFT (block-butterfly) to trade parameter-efficiency and expressivity; that choice affects how $R$ is implemented but not the basic left-multiply semantics.

In short: the orthogonal transform multiplies the neurons (columns) of a weight matrix on the left. "d" is the neuron/input dimension of the layer (hidden size, embedding dim, flattened conv input channels$\times$kernel area, etc.). Use an $R$ of size $d\times d$ for that layer; share across matrices only when their input dimensions match and you want to save parameters.

### How does Cayley parameterization translate into the stated parameter count for BOFT?
Question:
The paper uses the Cayley transform $\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ with $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$ to parameterize each orthogonal block. For BOFT($m,b$), it claims an "effective trainable parameter" count of $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ for finetuning a $d\times n$ layer.
- Could someone derive this formula step by step? For a single $2b\times 2b$ Cayley-parameterized block, how many independent parameters are there (should it be $b(2b-1)$ or $2b(2b-1)/2$?), and how many such blocks appear per butterfly component and per depth $m$?
- What exactly does "effective" mean here relative to the full number of nonzeros in the product?
Answer:
Short answer: the counting comes from (a) each orthogonal $2b$-by-$2b$ block is Cayley-parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix, which has $s(s-1)/2$ independent entries for $s=2b$, (b) there are $d/(2b)$ such independent blocks in each butterfly component, and (c) there are $m$ independent butterfly components. Plugging these together and doing the simple algebra (and noting the paper’s notational shift of $b$) yields the stated effective parameter count $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$. Below I show the steps and clarify what “effective” means.

Step‑by‑step derivation
1. Cayley parameterization of an $s\times s$ orthogonal block. If $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^T = -Q$, then $Q$ is skew‑symmetric and the independent degrees of freedom in $Q$ are exactly the independent entries above (or below) the diagonal. For an $s\times s$ skew matrix the number of independent parameters is
$$
\frac{s(s-1)}{2}.
$$

2. The block used in the paper. BOFT constructs orthogonal butterfly components from blockwise $2\times 2$ (or blockwise $2b\times 2b$) orthogonals. If a single orthogonal block has size $2b\times 2b$, set $s = 2b$. The number of independent parameters per such block is therefore
$$
\frac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2} = b(2b-1).
$$

3. How many blocks per butterfly component? Each butterfly component is block-diagonal, covering the whole $d\times d$ matrix by repeating that $2b\times 2b$ block along the diagonal. The number of independent $2b\times 2b$ blocks in one component is $d/(2b)$. So parameters per butterfly component equals
$$
\frac{d}{2b}\cdot b(2b-1)=\frac{d(2b-1)}{2}.
$$

4. Multiply by the number of components $m$. The full BOFT orthogonal $R(m,\cdot)$ is the product of $m$ such butterfly components, each with independent parameters, so total trainable parameters is
$$
m\cdot\frac{d(2b-1)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}dm(2b-1).
$$

5. Reconciling notation in the paper. The paper defines BOFT$(m,b)$ in a slightly confusing way (they earlier use a $2b\times 2b$ block and then state BOFT$(m,b/2)$ is denoted BOFT$(m,b)$). If you replace $b\to b/2$ in the last formula (i.e. interpret the paper’s final “$b$” as twice the block half-size used in the intermediate derivation) you obtain
$$
\frac{1}{2}dm\big(2\cdot\frac{b}{2}-1\big)=\frac{1}{2}dm(b-1),
$$
which is the formula reported in the paper:
$$
\text{effective trainable parameters}=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
$$
So the numerical factor and the linear dependence on $d$ and $m$ come directly from the skew-symmetric counting and the number of repeated blocks per component.

Answering your subquestions directly
- Number of independent parameters in one $2b\times 2b$ Cayley block: exactly $(2b)(2b-1)/2=b(2b-1)$. If you interpreted the block size as $s$, in general it is $s(s-1)/2$.
- Number of such blocks per butterfly component: $d/(2b)$. Number of butterfly components (depth) = $m$. Multiply per-block parameters by blocks-per-component and by $m$ to get the total.
- What “effective” means: it counts trainable degrees of freedom (i.e., independent scalar parameters in the skew-symmetric $Q$ blocks that you optimize). It is not the same as the number of nonzero entries in the final dense product $R = B_m\cdots B_1$. The product $R$ can be much denser (many nonzero entries) even though the number of independent trainable parameters is small because those dense entries are deterministic nonlinear functions of the (far fewer) $Q$ entries in the blocks. In short, “effective” = number of free/trainable parameters (independent entries in the $Q$s), not the number of non-zeros in the composed matrix or its final memory footprint.

Intuition and broader view
The Cayley map converts a skew matrix (with $O(s^2)$ entries reduced by skew symmetry) to an orthogonal matrix; the butterfly factorization repeats small orthogonal blocks across the layer and composes $m$ such sparse factors so that after multiplication you can achieve dense $R$ while having only $O(d m b)$ free parameters. This is exactly the space/time tradeoff the paper exploits: fewer parameters (space) at the cost of extra factor multiplications (time), with the orthogonality constraint drastically reducing the number of independent parameters relative to an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix (which would need $O(d^2)$ degrees of freedom).

### Why does dense connectivity in the information-transmission graph imply a dense product matrix?
Question:
The paper reframes factorizing $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$ as information transmission on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid and states that if every source node has a path to every sink node, then the product can be dense. But a path existing only guarantees it is possible for an entry to be nonzero, not that it must be. Couldn't different paths cancel out and produce zeros? Under what assumptions does "dense connectivity" imply a dense nonzero pattern of $\mathbf{R}$? Is the claim that, for generic continuous parameters, the set of cancellations yielding extra zeros has measure zero?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper is relying on the usual “generic parameter” fact: an entry of the product $R = B_m \cdots B_1$ is a polynomial in the free parameters of the factors; if the graph (i.e. the set of paths) allows a nonzero polynomial term, then that polynomial is not identically zero, so random continuous choices of the parameters will almost surely make it nonzero. Thus “dense connectivity” (a path from every source to every sink) permits a dense $R$ for generic parameters; the set of parameter choices that produce extra cancellations (making some allowed entry exactly zero) is an algebraic variety of strictly lower dimension and therefore has Lebesgue measure zero.

Longer, intuitive + technical explanation grounded in the paper

Think of the unrolled multiplication as the paper does: each element $R_{ij}$ of the final matrix is the sum of contributions from all paths that go from source $j$ (first level) to sink $i$ (last level). Concretely, if the factors $B_1,\dots,B_m$ have entries which are treated as symbolic parameters, $R_{ij}$ is a finite polynomial (indeed a multivariate polynomial) in those parameters: each path contributes a monomial (the product of the edge-entries along that path), and $R_{ij}$ is the sum of those monomials.

Two distinct phenomena can make $R_{ij}=0$.

1) Structural zeros: there is no path from $j$ to $i$. In that case there are literally no monomials in the polynomial for $R_{ij}$, so the polynomial is identically zero and $R_{ij}$ is always zero regardless of parameter values. This is the only case where a zero is forced by topology.

2) Cancellation zeros: there are paths (so the polynomial is nontrivial), but the particular numerical choices of the parameters happen to make that polynomial evaluate to zero (the monomials cancel). These are possible, but they require satisfying a nontrivial polynomial equation among the parameters.

The crucial algebraic-geometric fact used implicitly in the paper is: a nonzero polynomial in continuous parameters cannot vanish on an open set; its zero set is an algebraic variety of strictly lower dimension and therefore has measure zero in the ambient parameter space. Equivalently, a random draw of continuous parameters (e.g. from any absolutely continuous distribution) will almost surely not land on that cancellation set. So when the authors say “dense connectivity implies a dense product can be obtained,” they mean “there exist parameter settings giving nonzero values on all allowed entries, and for generic continuous parameters those entries will indeed be nonzero.” In simple terms: the existence of a path guarantees a nonzero monomial in the polynomial for that entry; unless the polynomial is identically zero (i.e. no path), the set of cancellations is non-generic (measure zero).

A few small caveats relevant to OFT/BOFT and orthogonality

Orthogonality of each factor imposes polynomial constraints among the factor entries (for example, orthogonality conditions are quadratic equations). Those constraints reduce the feasible parameter space from $\mathbb{R}^N$ to an algebraic manifold, but the same genericity argument still applies inside that manifold: as long as the polynomial expression for $R_{ij}$ is not identically zero on the orthogonality-constrained manifold, the subset of orthogonal-factor parameters that make $R_{ij}=0$ is again a lower-dimensional algebraic subset and thus has measure zero (with respect to the natural volume on that manifold). Practically, this means cancellations are still nongeneric even when you require each $B_k$ to be orthogonal, unless the orthogonality relations force the polynomial to vanish identically. That forced case can happen for small examples (symmetries, parity constraints, or very sparse structured blocks) and is precisely what the “no-path” condition is intended to catch: only if the topology + orthogonality constraints make every monomial cancel symbolically will an entry be always zero.

How you can check in practice

If you want to know whether an entry is forced zero by structure/orthogonality, either (a) inspect the transmission graph: if there is no path, it is forced zero; or (b) symbolically expand $R_{ij}$ as a polynomial in the free parameters (or reason about symmetries) and check if that polynomial is identically zero under the orthogonality relations. A simple practical heuristic is numeric: draw one or a few random orthogonal parameter choices (respecting the Cayley/rotation parameterization) and evaluate $R$; if an entry is generically nonzero it will almost always be nonzero numerically.

Bottom line

Yes — the intended meaning in the paper is the generic one: dense connectivity (a path from every source to every sink) makes it possible to obtain a dense $R$, and for generic continuous parameter choices the accidental cancellations that would produce extra zeros form a measure-zero set. The only guaranteed zeros are those forced by the absence of any path (or by algebraic identities that make the path-contributions sum to zero for all feasible parameters).

### Clarifying "minimum free edges" and the claim about $\pm 1$ entries for sparse orthogonal matrices
Question:
In the graph view, the paper distinguishes edges required by orthogonality from trainable ones, and says a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with exactly $d$ nonzeros must have entries in $\{\pm 1\}$. Could someone formalize this notion of "free edges"? Why are those $d$ edges considered non-trainable? Also, how exactly does one prove that an orthogonal matrix with exactly one nonzero per row and column must be a signed permutation (hence $\pm 1$ values)?
Answer:
Short answer: “Minimum free edges” refers to the unavoidable one-nonzero-per-row-and-column pattern that any full-rank (hence orthogonal) $d$-by-$d$ factor must provide between two adjacent levels in the information-transmission graph. Those $d$ edges are not “free” (i.e. not useful trainable degrees of freedom) because with exactly one nonzero per row and column orthogonality forces each nonzero’s magnitude to be $1$ and its sign to be the only remaining degree of freedom. In particular, any real orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix whose support has exactly one nonzero in each row and in each column is a signed permutation matrix, so all nonzero entries lie in $\{+1,-1\}$.

Formalization and proof (concise). Let $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be orthogonal ($R^T R = I$) and suppose its support has exactly one nonzero per row and exactly one nonzero per column. Equivalently, there is a permutation $\sigma$ on $\{1,\dots,d\}$ and nonzero scalars $c_j$ such that column $j$ of $R$ has its single nonzero at row $i = \sigma(j)$ with value $R_{\sigma(j),j} = c_j$, and all other entries are zero. Write $R = P D$ where $P$ is the permutation matrix with $P_{\sigma(j),j} = 1$ and zeros elsewhere, and $D = \mathrm{diag}(c_1,\dots,c_d)$ is diagonal.

Orthogonality gives
$$
R^T R = D^T P^T P D = D^T I D = D^T D = I .
$$
Since $D$ is diagonal and real, $D^T D = \mathrm{diag}(c_1^2, \dots, c_d^2) = I$. Hence for each $j$ we must have $c_j^2 = 1$, so $c_j \in \{+1,-1\}$. Therefore $R = P D$ is a permutation times a sign diagonal, i.e. a signed permutation matrix; every nonzero entry is $\pm 1$.

Why these $d$ edges aren't counted as trainable in the paper’s graph viewpoint. In the information-transmission graph, an orthogonal adjacency between two adjacent levels must be a bijection (every node on the left must map to a unique node on the right) if that factor is to be full-rank. That bijection requires at least $d$ nonzero positions (one per row and column). If you force the factor to have exactly those $d$ nonzeros, orthogonality then forces their magnitudes to be $1$ and leaves only discrete sign choices. In the continuous optimization the authors care about (learning real-valued parameters), those positions therefore do not provide meaningful continuous degrees of freedom beyond signs (which are non-differentiable and discrete), so they are treated as “not counting” toward the number of continuous trainable parameters. Put differently, the support pattern that guarantees full rank contributes a baseline of $d$ nonzeros that are structural necessities rather than tunable, and with the one-per-row-and-column assumption those entries have no continuous freedom (only $\pm 1$).

Broader context. Signed permutation matrices are exactly the (extreme) orthogonal matrices with minimal sparsity (one nonzero per row/column). This explains why to get continuous, expressive, parameter-efficient orthogonal factorizations one must allow more than $d$ nonzeros per factor (or use multiple sparse factors): otherwise orthogonality kills the continuous degrees of freedom. The paper’s “minimum free edges” language is a way to separate structural/bijective edges necessary for orthogonality from the extra nonzeros that actually carry trainable continuous parameters.

### How are higher-$k$ butterfly components guaranteed to be orthogonal via permutations?
Question:
The paper claims each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)$ has a nonzero pattern that is a permutation of $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$, hence can be parameterized to be orthogonal. Could someone write the explicit relation like $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)=\mathbf{P}_1\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\,\mathbf{P}_2$ for some permutation matrices $\mathbf{P}_1,\mathbf{P}_2$? How does this extend to the block case $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)$ where entries are $b\times b$ matrices?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — each higher-$k$ butterfly component is a row/column permutation (conjugation) of the basic $2$-sized component, so you can write it as permutation matrices surrounding a block-diagonal $2\times 2$ structure. Concretely, the permutations just reindex which pairs are coupled at that stage, and conjugating an orthogonal $2\times 2$-block matrix by those permutations preserves orthogonality. The same statement carries to the block case by replacing scalar permutations with block (Kronecker) permutations.

Explanation and an explicit template. Let $d = 2^N$ and consider the smallest-stage butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,2)$. Its nonzero pattern is a block-diagonal matrix made of $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ blocks (these are the pairwise rotators). For a larger block size $k = 2^t$ ($t \ge 1$) the construction of $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ pairs different indices into $2\times 2$ interactions, but those pairings are exactly a permutation of the pairings used in $\tilde{B}(d,2)$. Therefore there exist permutation matrices $P_{\text{row}}$ and $P_{\text{col}}$ (orthogonal, consisting of exactly one $1$ per row/column) such that

$\tilde{B}(d,k) = P_{\text{row}} \cdot \tilde{B}_{\text{diag}}(d,2) \cdot P_{\text{col}},$

where $\tilde{B}_{\text{diag}}(d,2)$ denotes the block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are the same $2\times 2$ rotators used in $\tilde{B}(d,2)$ (possibly arranged in some canonical order). Intuitively $P_{\text{col}}$ reorders the input coordinates so that the $k$-sized component’s pairings become aligned with adjacent $2$-sized blocks, then we apply the $2\times 2$ block-diagonal rotations, then $P_{\text{row}}$ permutes the outputs back to the original index order.

A more structured way to write this uses perfect-shuffle / stride permutations that are standard in radix-2 FFT derivations. Let $S(k)$ be the $(d\times d)$ perfect-shuffle permutation that groups indices into the $k$-sized contiguous blocks required at that stage. Then you can take

$\tilde{B}(d,k) = S(k)^{-1} \cdot \big(I_{d/k} \otimes \tilde{B}^F(k)\big) \cdot S(k),$

and since each $\tilde{B}^F(k)$ itself is a $(k\times k)$ matrix built from diagonal stacks of length-$k/2$ vectors (see Eq. (1) in the paper), when $k = 2$ the middle factor reduces to the simple block-diagonal of $2\times 2$ rotators. Equivalently, by further decomposing $\tilde{B}^F(k)$ into $2\times 2$ blocks you get the previous $P_{\text{row}}$ / $P_{\text{col}}$ form. The important property is that $S(k)$ and the simple reorderings used are permutation matrices, so they are orthogonal and do not affect the ability to parameterize the interior $2\times 2$ blocks as orthogonal rotators.

Block (matrix-entry) case. When entries are $b\times b$ blocks, everything lifts by Kronecker product with $I_b$. Concretely, let $\tilde{B}^b(d,2)$ be the component with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks along the diagonal (each block parameterized as an orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ matrix). The same permutations that reindex scalar coordinates become block-permutations: if $P$ is a $d\times d$ permutation, then the corresponding block permutation is $P \otimes I_b$ (a $db\times db$ permutation that permutes $b\times b$ blocks). Thus

$\tilde{B}^b(d,k) = (P_{\text{row}} \otimes I_b) \cdot \tilde{B}^b_{\text{diag}}(d,2) \cdot (P_{\text{col}} \otimes I_b),$

and because each $2b\times 2b$ diagonal block is constrained to be orthogonal, conjugating by the block-permutation matrices yields an orthogonal $\tilde{B}^b(d,k)$. So in practice you only need to parameterize the small $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) orthogonal blocks; all other components are obtained by permuting where those blocks act.

Why this guarantees orthogonality. If $Q$ is orthogonal and $P_1,P_2$ are permutation (orthogonal) matrices then $P_1 Q P_2$ is orthogonal iff $P_2 = P_1^{\top}$ (for conjugation) or more generally the product of orthogonals is orthogonal. In the constructions above we either conjugate ($P^{-1} Q P$) or left/right multiply by permutations that correspond exactly to the required index reorderings; because permutations and the small blocks are orthogonal, the resulting matrix is orthogonal. In the block case the same holds because $P \otimes I_b$ is orthogonal and the $2b\times 2b$ blocks are orthogonal.

Summary. Every $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ is just a reindexing (permutation) of the basic $2\times 2$-block pattern, so you can write it as permutation $\cdot$ ( $2\times 2$-block-diagonal ) $\cdot$ permutation. For block entries replace permutations with their block versions $P \otimes I_b$. This is exactly why parameterizing the $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) rotators suffices to make all butterfly components orthogonal.

### Why does BOFT($m,b$) become dense at $m=\log(2d/b)$? Deriving the depth needed for full connectivity
Question:
The paper states BOFT($\log \tfrac{2d}{b}, b$) yields a dense orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$. How is this depth computed from the butterfly structure? Can someone derive the relationship that at depth $m$, each output mixes information from $\min(d, b2^{m-1})$ inputs (or similar), leading to $m=\log(2d/b)$ as the point where every output can receive information from all $d$ inputs?
Answer:
Short answer: because each butterfly component doubles the number of input positions that can reach a given output (starting from a local block of size $b$), after $m$ stages the receptive field is $b\cdot 2^{m-1}$ (capped at $d$). Requiring this to cover all $d$ inputs gives
\[
b\cdot 2^{m-1}\ge d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m \ge 1+\log_2\!\left(\frac{d}{b}\right)=\log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right),
\]
hence BOFT($\log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right),b$) yields a dense orthogonal $\mathbf R$.

Explanation and derivation linked to the butterfly structure in the paper
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Think of the factorization $\mathbf R=\tilde B_{(d,m)}\cdots\tilde B_{(d,1)}$ as an unrolled layered graph (the paper's information-transmission view). Each $\tilde B_{(d,i)}$ is a block-wise butterfly component built from $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) mixing blocks. At initialization (one component, $m=1$) each output node only receives direct information from entries inside its local block of size $b$ — so the receptive field size is $b$.

The key property of the radix-2 butterfly (the same doubling mechanism behind Cooley–Tukey FFT) is that each subsequent component mixes pairs of previously disjoint groups, doubling the number of distinct input positions that can reach a given output. Concretely:
- After 1 component the receptive field size = $b$.
- After 2 components it becomes $2b$.
- After 3 components it becomes $4b$.
- After $m$ components it becomes $b\cdot 2^{m-1}$.

Because there are only $d$ input positions, the effective number of inputs that can influence any given output after $m$ stages is
\[
\min\big(d,\; b\cdot 2^{m-1}\big).
\]
To make $\mathbf R$ dense we need every output to be able to receive from all $d$ inputs, so set $b\cdot 2^{m-1}\ge d$. Solving gives
\[
m \ge 1+\log_2\!\left(\frac{d}{b}\right)=\log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right).
\]
Thus the stated depth $m=\log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$ is exactly the stage at which the butterfly factorization first has global connectivity (after rounding up $m$ to an integer).

Intuition connection
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the same intuition as the FFT: local pairwise mixes at each stage cause the "influence radius" to double each layer, so logarithmically many layers suffice to connect all positions. In BOFT the block size $b$ controls the base local neighborhood; smaller $b$ means you need more stages (larger $m$) to reach full density, while larger $b$ reduces the number of stages needed.

### How are non-power-of-two dimensions handled in BOFT?
Question:
Butterfly factorizations are typically defined for $d=2^N$. What does BOFT do when $d$ is not a power of two or when $b$ does not divide $d$? Do you pad dimensions, use mixed-radix butterflies, or introduce irregular blocks? How does this affect orthogonality, parameter count, and the guarantee of achieving a dense $\mathbf{R}$ at a given depth $m$?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper presents BOFT mainly in the radix‑2 (power‑of‑two) setup and states the dense guarantee and the neat parameter count for that case. It does not give a worked‑out, formal treatment for arbitrary $d$ or arbitrary $b$ that does not divide $d$, so in practice one must adopt one of the standard engineering workarounds (padding to a power of two, mixed‑radix butterflies, or small irregular blocks). Each option has clear consequences for orthogonality, parameter count and the depth $m$ needed to obtain a dense $R$; I summarize those consequences and give practical recommendations below.

What the paper assumes and what it proves
The BOFT construction and the density/parameter bounds in the paper are written for the usual radix‑2 butterfly recursion (they write $d = 2^N$ and define butterfly components at block sizes $2,4,\ldots$). The paper explicitly states that BOFT($m,b$) with $m = \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$ and $b = 2$ yields the classic $O(d\log d)$ parameter count and yields a dense orthogonal $R$. The theorems and counts in the text use that radix‑2 structure.

If $d$ is not a power of two or $b$ does not divide $d$
Because the paper does not prescribe a single canonical treatment for non‑power‑of‑two $d$, one must adopt a compatible extension. There are three commonly used, consistent approaches; I describe how each affects the key properties:

1) Zero / identity padding to the next convenient size
A simple and robust practical choice is to pad the vector dimension to the next size that fits the chosen butterfly decomposition (for example next power of two, or next multiple of $b$), implement BOFT in the larger space, and either (a) ignore the padded coordinates at inference or (b) multiply the resulting orthogonal update into the original weights and then crop back. If you pad with extra coordinates initialized so the extra rows/cols act like identity (e.g., skew parameters zero in Cayley), then the composed larger matrix is orthogonal; restricting back to the original coordinates gives a valid orthogonal transform on the original subspace.

Effects: orthogonality is preserved in the larger space and the effective transform on the original $d$ coordinates remains a valid partial orthogonal embedding. Parameter count increases because you are parameterizing a larger $d_{\text{padded}}$: the number of trainable parameters scales with $d_{\text{padded}}$ (roughly $\frac{(b-1)d_{\text{padded}} m}{2}$ in the paper’s formula). The guaranteed depth $m$ for achieving a dense $R$ follows the padded size: use $m$ large enough for the padded problem (e.g. $m=\log_2\!\left(\frac{2 d_{\text{padded}}}{b}\right)$ in radix‑2). Practically this is simple and safe; tradeoff is slightly more parameters and computation during finetuning.

2) Mixed‑radix or non‑radix‑2 butterfly constructions
Butterfly factorizations generalize beyond radix‑2. Mixed‑radix FFT and kaleidoscope/butterfly literature define factorizations for sizes that factor into other small integers (e.g., radix‑3, radix‑5 stages, or combinations). One can design block butterfly components whose local blocks are $k\times k$ factors with $k$ not equal to $2$, and compose stages to cover the prime factorization of $d$.

Effects: with a correct mixed‑radix construction you can keep the parameter scaling similarly subquadratic (roughly $O(d\log d)$ up to constants that depend on the radix choices). Orthogonality can still be enforced stagewise (each small block is parameterized as an orthogonal block via Cayley or small rotations), so the global product is orthogonal. The depth $m$ required to get dense connectivity is determined by the number of stages in the mixed‑radix decomposition (roughly sum of logarithms in each radix), so the same qualitative guarantee (dense $R$ with $O(\log d)$ stages) still holds but you must design the factorization to cover the entire permutation/connectivity needed for density. Implementationally this is more complex than padding but avoids parameter waste.

3) Irregular (nonuniform) blocks / leftover handling
If $b$ does not divide $d$, you can use mostly uniform blocks and handle leftovers with a final smaller block (a slightly different block size) or with one or two irregular stages. This is common: e.g., use blocks of size $b$ where possible and have one remainder block of size $r < b$. You then design the last‑stage factor to include orthogonal rotations that handle the smaller block. Another variant is to use permutations that move leftovers into positions where small $2\times2$ rotations suffice.

Effects: orthogonality can be preserved if you parameterize the irregular blocks as proper orthogonal submatrices. Parameter count formula in the paper $\left(\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}\right)$ no longer exactly holds; you must account explicitly for smaller block(s) so the actual parameter count is slightly lower (because remainder blocks have fewer internal parameters) or slightly different in bookkeeping. The density guarantee becomes case specific: you still can achieve dense $R$ with enough stages, but you must ensure the connectivity pattern introduced by irregular blocks reaches all source\to sink pairs. In short, guarantees become manual but achievable.

Summary of how each choice affects the three things you asked about
- Orthogonality: All three options can preserve orthogonality. Padding embeds the problem in a larger orthogonal matrix; mixed‑radix designs enforce orthogonality stagewise; irregular blocks must be parameterized themselves as orthogonal to keep the global product orthogonal. Use Cayley or small block rotations in every stage to guarantee orthogonality.
- Parameter count: Padding increases $d$ $\to$ larger parameter count proportional to $d_{\text{padded}}$. Mixed‑radix keeps the $O(d\log d)$ flavor but changes constants depending on radix factors. Irregular block handling slightly changes the exact count; you must explicitly add parameters for leftover blocks.
- Guarantee of dense $R$ at depth $m$: The paper’s closed‑form guarantee ($m = \log\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right)$) assumes radix‑2 recursion and evenly partitioned blocks. For non‑power‑of‑two you either (a) pad and use the same formula with $d_{\text{padded}}$, (b) design a mixed‑radix stage count whose total number of stages is the sum of the appropriate logarithmic factors so you still need $O(\log d)$ stages, or (c) choose enough irregular stages so the connectivity graph becomes dense. If you adopt an ad‑hoc irregular scheme without verifying the connectivity graph, you may lose the clean theoretical density guarantee.

Practical recommendation
If you want a simple, robust implementation with correct orthogonality and minimal engineering, pad to the nearest multiple that fits your chosen stage decomposition (often the next power of two). Initialize the padded parameters so the padding behaves like identity (Cayley skew = 0) and finetune. If you care about minimizing extra parameters and are willing to implement more complex routing, implement mixed‑radix butterflies or small irregular orthogonal blocks tailored to the factorization of $d$; that will reduce parameter waste while preserving the conceptual BOFT guarantees.

Closing remark
The paper deliberately presents BOFT in the clean radix‑2/block‑aligned setting and notes that block sizes can be any $b$, but does not give the full cookbook for every nonconforming $d$. The three approaches above are the standard, consistent ways to extend BOFT in practice; which to choose depends on your tolerance for extra parameters (padding) versus implementation complexity (mixed‑radix / irregular blocks).

### Does a product of sparse orthogonal matrices necessarily stay sparse? If not, how does BOFT become dense?
Question:
I'm mistakenly thinking that multiplying sparse matrices should keep things sparse, but the paper claims BOFT can produce a dense $\mathbf{R}$ from a product of sparse orthogonal factors. Could someone explain concretely (perhaps with a small $d=8$ example) how the particular butterfly sparsity patterns compose to yield a dense matrix, even though each factor is sparse?
Answer:
Short answer: No — multiplying sparse matrices need not preserve sparsity. BOFT uses a sequence of sparse orthogonal factors whose sparsity patterns differ (they “route” coordinates differently at each stage). Each factor is sparse, but their product can be dense because nonzero entries in later factors mix linear combinations produced by earlier factors, filling previously-zero positions. The butterfly pattern is exactly a small number of sparse stages that, when composed, create full connectivity (and thus generally nonzero entries) between inputs and outputs.

Concrete $d = 8$ example (intuitive, minimal algebra): BOFT (radix-2 butterfly) for $d=8$ uses $m=\log_2 8=3$ stages. Each stage is block-diagonal with four $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks, but the pairings change between stages.

Label coordinates $1,\dots,8$. Stage 1 pairs: $(1,2),(3,4),(5,6),(7,8)$. Stage 2 pairs: $(1,3),(2,4),(5,7),(6,8)$. Stage 3 pairs: $(1,5),(2,6),(3,7),(4,8)$. Denote the orthogonal $2\times2$ block acting on a pair $(i,j)$ by
$$
R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix},
$$
so each stage is a block-diagonal matrix of four such blocks (one angle per block).

Why the product is dense: consider a single input basis vector $e_1$ (value 1 at index 1, zeros elsewhere). After stage 1 (pair $(1,2)$) it becomes a vector supported only on indices 1 and 2 (a 2D rotation of the original). Stage 2 re-pairs those indices with indices 3 and 4, so the mass originally in indices 1/2 is spread into indices 1,2,3,4. Stage 3 pairs index-sets $\{1,2,3,4\}$ with $\{5,6,7,8\}$, so the mass spreads across all 8 coordinates. Algebraically, each stage applies a linear map that mixes only within its $2\times2$ blocks, but because the blocks are permuted between stages, the image of $e_1$ after three stages is a linear combination with generally nonzero coefficients in every coordinate. The same happens starting from any $e_j$, so the product matrix has nonzero entries in essentially every position — i.e., it is dense.

To see this more explicitly, write the product $R=R_3R_2R_1$. Each entry $R_{ij}$ is a sum of products of sines/cosines coming from paths through the three stages. Graphically, unroll the multiplication into a 4-level layered directed graph (input layer, stage1 outputs, stage2 outputs, output layer). There is a path from any input node to any output node because the butterfly pairings connect the layers so that information can flow from any source to any sink. Each entry of $R$ equals a sum over all length-3 paths from input $j$ to output $i$; unless the chosen angles make cancellations, those sums are generically nonzero. Thus the product is typically fully dense.

Connection to broader concepts: this is the same reason the FFT (a product of sparse butterfly matrices) computes a dense Fourier matrix with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters/operations. Sparsity patterns that are constant across factors can preserve sparsity under multiplication (for example, two matrices with identical block-diagonal pattern multiply to a matrix with the same block structure), but varying the pairing/permutation between stages breaks that invariance and allows sparse factors to generate a dense overall transform. Orthogonality of each factor just constrains entries (e.g., $2\times2$ blocks are rotations), it does not prevent filling of zeros in the product.

Takeaway: sparsity is not closed under multiplication unless the sparsity patterns are aligned in a way that the product cannot create new nonzeros. BOFT deliberately uses different sparse connectivity at each stage (butterfly pairings) so that the composited orthogonal matrix becomes dense while keeping only $O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.

### How exactly is the multiplicative Dropout implemented while preserving orthogonality?
Question:
The multiplicative Dropout randomly picks a fraction of butterfly components and a fraction of their diagonal blocks and replaces them with identities. Since the identity is orthogonal and the product of orthogonals is orthogonal, this seems fine structurally. But:
- Are different identity masks sampled per batch, and does this cause biased gradients for the retained blocks?
- Do the dropped blocks receive zero gradients (like standard Dropout), or do you scale the kept blocks by a factor to keep expectations consistent?
- Is orthogonality preserved numerically when mixing dropped and non-dropped blocks during training?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper’s multiplicative Dropout replaces randomly chosen butterfly components (and some of their diagonal blocks) with identity blocks during training. That replacement makes those blocks constant (orthogonal) for the forward pass, so their parameters do not receive gradient signal; the paper does not introduce an explicit expectation-preserving rescaling. Orthogonality is preserved by construction at the block level (identities and Cayley‑parameterized blocks are orthogonal), though floating‑point multiplication of many factors can introduce small numerical drift in practice.

Explanation and implications (intuitive + technical)

1) Mask sampling and gradient bias
The paper states that it “randomly picks $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks … and replaces these blocks as identity matrices.” It does not say this mask is fixed for an epoch or minibatch, but the natural and common implementation is to sample a fresh mask each training step (i.e. per minibatch / per forward). Doing so yields a stochastic regularizer analogous in spirit to standard dropout. However, unlike additive Dropout or LoRA-style additive updates, this is multiplicative: you replace multiplicative factors by the identity rather than zeroing an additive term. Because the replacement is not followed by any compensating scaling in the paper, the gradient estimator is biased with respect to the deterministic, unmasked objective. That is, the expectation of the product of masked orthogonal factors is not equal to the unmasked product unless special scaling/corrections are applied. The paper appears to accept this as a form of structured stochastic regularization rather than an unbiased estimator.

2) Gradients to dropped blocks and possible scaling
When a block is replaced by the identity for the forward pass, that block’s parameters no longer affect the forward outputs in that pass; consequently their gradients are zero for that example / batch (exactly the same effect as standard dropout sets the dropped units’ parameters to receive no gradient while they are dropped). The paper describes replacement-by-identity and does not mention any extra multiplicative scaling of the remaining (kept) blocks to make the expectation match the unmasked case, so you should assume no scaling is performed. Practically, one could design an alternative “inverted” variant that rescales kept blocks to approximate the unmasked expectation, but for multiplicative factorization this is nontrivial: because the overall transform is a product of factors, a simple scalar rescaling of kept blocks does not, in general, restore the expected product. That is likely why the authors chose the simpler replacement-with-identity strategy (a structured stochastic regularizer) rather than attempt an unbiased correction.

3) Orthogonality when mixing dropped and kept blocks (numerical considerations)
By construction orthogonality is preserved at the block level: the identity is orthogonal, and each kept block is parameterized (e.g. via Cayley transform or $2\times2$ rotations) to be orthogonal during training. The product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, so in exact arithmetic the composed $R$ is orthogonal even with a mixture of identity and learned blocks. In floating point arithmetic, repeated matrix multiplications can introduce small rounding errors, so you may observe tiny deviations from exact orthogonality after composing many factors. In practice these numerical deviations are small; common mitigations are to maintain orthogonality via the parameterization (Cayley / Givens / explicit $2\times2$ rotations) and, if needed, periodically reproject or renormalize blocks (or use higher precision accumulation) during training. The paper relies on Cayley parameterization for each block, which keeps each block orthogonal to machine precision up to accumulation error in the product.

Practical takeaways and suggestions
- Implement the mask per training step (per minibatch) if you want standard stochastic regularization; you can also experiment with per-example masks or masks that persist for several steps, but the paper gives no recommendation beyond “randomly pick … and replace.”
- Expect dropped blocks to get zero gradients while dropped. This is a design choice that trades bias in gradient expectation for a structural regularizer that maintains orthogonality.
- If you worry about numerical drift in composed $R$, rely on orthogonal parameterizations for blocks (Cayley/Givens) and optionally reproject or clamp (rarely needed). The identity-replacement itself does not break orthogonality in exact arithmetic.

In short: multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is a structured stochastic regularizer implemented by replacing selected orthogonal blocks with identity; it zeroes gradients for those blocks when dropped, introduces bias relative to the unmasked product (no expectation-preserving scaling is proposed), and preserves orthogonality by construction up to usual floating‑point accumulation errors.

### Proving spectral norm preservation under left orthogonal multiplication in BOFT
Question:
The paper claims $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2=\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2$ because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal. For $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, could someone provide a clean proof that $\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 x\|_2=\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|\mathbf{W}^0 x\|_2$? Also, in finite precision, if $\mathbf{R}$ is only approximately orthogonal, how sensitive is the spectral norm to small orthogonality errors accumulated across $m$ factors?
Answer:
Short answer: multiplying on the left by an exact orthogonal matrix does not change the spectral norm because left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix is an isometry on Euclidean space; in finite precision a small departure from orthogonality perturbs the norm by at most the operator norm of that departure, and for a product of m approximately-orthogonal factors the relative distortion accumulates roughly like the product of their operator norms (equivalently $\approx$ additive in the small-error regime).

Proof (clean, SVD-based). Write the SVD of $\mathbf{W}^0$ as $\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ where $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal and $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ is the rectangular diagonal of singular values. If $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$), then
$$
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top.
$$
But $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is orthogonal (product of orthogonals). The singular values of a matrix are invariant under left- or right-multiplication by orthogonal matrices, hence the singular values of $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ are the same as those of $\mathbf{W}^0$. In particular the largest singular value (the spectral norm) is preserved:
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2=\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2.
$$
Equivalently, by the variational definition,
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2=\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 x\|_2
=\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|\mathbf{W}^0 x\|_2,
$$
because $\|\mathbf{R}y\|_2=\|y\|_2$ for all $y$ when $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal.

Finite-precision / approximate-orthogonality sensitivity. Suppose $\mathbf{R}$ is only approximately orthogonal so that
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E},
$$
with symmetric error $\mathbf{E}$ and $\|\mathbf{E}\|_2=\varepsilon$ small. Then
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\|_2^2=\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\|_2=\|\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E}\|_2=1+\delta
$$
for some $\delta$ with $|\delta|\le\varepsilon$. Thus
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\|_2=\sqrt{1+\delta}\le\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx 1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\quad(\text{for small }\varepsilon).
$$
Using the submultiplicativity of the operator norm,
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 \le \|\mathbf{R}\|_2\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 \le \sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\,\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2,
$$
and also
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 \ge \|\mathbf{R}^{-1}\|_2^{-1}\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2,
$$
if $\mathbf{R}$ is invertible. So the relative change in spectral norm is bounded by about $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-1\approx \varepsilon/2$.

For m factors $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}_m\cdots\mathbf{R}_1$ with per-factor orthogonality errors $\varepsilon_i=\|\mathbf{R}_i^\top\mathbf{R}_i-\mathbf{I}\|_2$, we get the crude bound
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 \le \Big(\prod_{i=1}^m\sqrt{1+\varepsilon_i}\Big)\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2.
$$
If all $\varepsilon_i$ are small and similar, $\varepsilon_i\ll 1$, then using $\prod_i\sqrt{1+\varepsilon_i}\approx\exp\big(\tfrac12\sum_i\varepsilon_i\big)$, the relative distortion is approximately
$$
\frac{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2}{\|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2}\approx 1+\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^m\varepsilon_i.
$$
So errors accumulate roughly additively in the small-error regime (or multiplicatively exactly). In floating point, typical orthogonality drift per factor often scales with machine epsilon $u$ and the factor dimension (e.g. $\varepsilon_i=O(d\,u)$ for some unstable routines), so the total distortion after m factors can be on the order of $O(m d u)$ (or more precisely $O(\sum_i\varepsilon_i)$).

Practical recommendations. If you need spectral-norm preservation to high accuracy in finite precision, enforce exact orthogonality up to numerical solve precision or periodically re-orthogonalize: use numerically stable parameterizations (products of 2x2 Givens rotations or Cayley/Givens parameterizations managed carefully), periodically reproject $\mathbf{R}$ to the nearest orthogonal matrix (via a thin QR or SVD), or keep per-factor orthogonality tight (use stable updates for the 2x2 blocks in BOFT). These controls keep $\varepsilon_i$ tiny so the accumulated effect across m factors remains negligible.

In short: exact orthogonality gives exact spectral-norm preservation; approximate orthogonality perturbs the norm by a factor at most $\prod_i\sqrt{1+\varepsilon_i}$, which for small per-factor errors is approximately $1+\tfrac12\sum_i\varepsilon_i$.

### Is BOFT essentially a structured sequence of Givens rotations? Relation to Householder/Givens products
Question:
Any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of Householder reflectors or Givens rotations. BOFT builds $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of block-sparse orthogonal factors with $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal sub-blocks. Is BOFT equivalent to using a specific sequence of Givens rotations constrained by the butterfly topology? How does the required number of factors to approximate a generic orthogonal compare to the classical $O(d^2)$ Givens rotations versus the $O(d\log d)$ butterfly depth?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — BOFT is essentially a structured sequence of $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks, so at the low level it is built from the same building blocks as Givens rotations (or $2\times 2$ Householder/rotation blocks). The crucial difference is that BOFT fixes which pairs of coordinates are rotated at each layer according to the butterfly topology, trading full universality for a much smaller parameter budget and fast “information mixing.” Consequently BOFT with shallow butterfly depth (e.g. depth $m=\log d$, block size $b=2$) uses $O(d\log d)$ parameters but only spans a restricted structured subset of the orthogonal group; to recover full universality you must stack many more butterfly factors and the parameter count grows toward the classical $O(d^2)$ budget.

Why BOFT is a constrained Givens-product in plain language. A Givens rotation is a $2\times 2$ orthogonal acting on a selected pair of coordinates. BOFT’s primitive factors are block-diagonal matrices whose blocks are $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) orthogonal blocks (the paper uses Cayley / $2$D rotations for these). Multiplying those factors is therefore exactly multiplying many local $2\times 2$ orthogonals — the same algebraic act as composing Givens rotations. What BOFT adds is a fixed pattern (the butterfly graph) that specifies which coordinate pairs are coupled at each layer. So BOFT = product of many Givens-like blocks, but with the pairings constrained by the butterfly connectivity.

Expressivity vs. factor count (how many factors are needed). The classical facts: an arbitrary orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, and a generic Givens decomposition uses on the order of $d^2$ rotations (or parameters) to represent every orthogonal. BOFT parametrizes $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ butterfly components with block-size $b$. The paper gives the effective number of trainable parameters as approximately
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
$$
so for the canonical butterfly ($b=2$, $m=\log d$) we get $O(d\log d)$ parameters. This is much smaller than $O(d^2)$, but it also means the parameterized set is a strict (structured) subset of all orthogonals.

Universality requires more stacking. The paper’s Theorem and discussion show that by composing more butterfly-type matrices (their kaleidoscope/generalized composition) one can reach the entire orthogonal group: for example the construction $\bm{R}^G$ with many alternating butterfly factors can represent every orthogonal if you take enough factors (they mention needing something like $l=d-1$ in their generalized form when $m_1=m_2=\log d, b_1=b_2=2$). Translating that into parameter counting: to match the full $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom you need $m\approx O(d)$ (for $b=2$), which makes the total parameter count scale like $O(d^2)$ again. So BOFT can interpolate between the two regimes: shallow butterfly depth $\Rightarrow$ $O(d\log d)$ params and restricted expressivity; deep stacking $\Rightarrow$ approach $O(d^2)$ params and universality.

Intuition and practical implication. Think of Givens decompositions as “you may rotate any arbitrary pair when you like,” which gives maximal flexibility but costs many parameters. BOFT fixes a fast-mixing schedule (butterfly) so that each layer mixes many coordinates in a constrained, highly efficient way; after $O(\log d)$ layers information from any input coordinate can reach any output coordinate (hence the dense final matrix), but the space of matrices you can realize with only those degrees of freedom is structured. Empirically and theoretically (as the paper notes), that structured subset contains many useful transforms (FFT, Hadamard, etc.) and often gives a favorable bias for finetuning: better parameter-efficiency and generalization than naive block-diagonal OFT, and much smaller than full OFT when you accept the structured restriction.

Summary. BOFT is a sequence of local $2\times 2$ orthogonals (so algebraically a constrained Givens product) arranged in a butterfly topology. With depth $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ it uses $O(d\log d)$ parameters but is not universal; to represent every orthogonal you must stack many more butterfly factors (roughly $O(d)$ layers for $b=2$), at which point the parameter count approaches the classical $O(d^2)$ needed by arbitrary Givens decompositions. The practical trade-off is exactly what the paper emphasizes: you trade expressivity for parameter-efficiency via the butterfly inductive bias.

### Clarifying the expressivity theorem: how do alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes span $O(d)$?
Question:
The theorem states that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT and that multiplying many butterfly matrices $\mathbf{B}_{i,j}(d)$ and their transposes can represent the full orthogonal group when depth $l=d-1$ (a kaleidoscope hierarchy). Could someone unpack this construction? What exactly are $\mathbf{B}_{i,j}(d)$, why is the transpose used alternately, and how does $l=d-1$ suffice to generate $O(d)$? A proof sketch or a precise reference would help.
Answer:
Short answer: the $B_{i,j}(d)$ are structured “butterfly” orthogonal factors (block-diagonal permutations of $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks). Alternating such butterfly factors with their transposes implements a controlled set of Givens (planar) rotations that, when you stack enough layers (depth $l = d-1$ in the kaleidoscope construction), can realize every possible Givens rotation needed to build any orthogonal matrix. Thus the product
$B_{d-1,1}(d)\; B_{d-1,2}(d)^\top \cdots B_{1,1}(d)\; B_{1,2}(d)^\top$
is universal over $O(d)$. The formal statement and a constructive proof appear in the kaleidoscope / butterfly literature (see Dao et al. “Kaleidoscope” / “Learning Fast Algorithms” papers cited in the paper).

Longer, intuitive + technical sketch grounded in the paper

What a butterfly factor $B_{i,j}(d)$ is. In this paper a “butterfly factor” is a sparse $d\times d$ matrix whose nonzero pattern is a blockwise permutation of small $2\times 2$ blocks (or $b\times b$ blocks in the block version). At the smallest granularity ($b = 1$ or $b = 2$ with Cayley parameterization), each active block is a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix (a planar rotation/reflection). A full butterfly matrix $B(d)$ is built as a product of a small number of such block-permutation layers (the classic radix-2 Cooley–Tukey decomposition). Each layer mixes disjoint pairs of coordinates (so one layer contains $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks); different layers pair different index sets according to the butterfly permutation pattern.

Why alternate with the transpose. A single butterfly component (or a single layer of disjoint $2\times 2$ blocks) can only apply rotations within disjoint pairs of coordinates; by itself it cannot move arbitrary mass between arbitrary pairs of indices. Multiplying by another butterfly component whose pairings are a different permutation extends expressive power. Using transposes (i.e., putting $B$ and $B^\top$ alternately) is a convenient structured way to realize both a permutation of pairings and its inverse pairing pattern while keeping each factor orthogonal. Concretely, if a layer $L$ pairs coordinates according to some matching, then $L^\top$ is the same layer reversed; interleaving layers and their transposes gives controlled interactions between coordinates across multiple stages while preserving orthogonality of every factor. Dao et al. call such alternating products a “kaleidoscope” hierarchy: alternating factors and their transposes yields richer sets of pairwise interactions than repeating only forward factors.

How $l = d-1$ suffices (sketch). A standard universality route for orthogonal matrices is via Givens rotations: any orthogonal matrix can be decomposed into a product of planar rotations that zero out entries in a triangularization process (this is essentially the Givens-based QR decomposition), and at most $O(d^2)$ such planar rotations suffice in the generic algorithm. The key observation in the kaleidoscope/butterfly construction is that a butterfly layer implements many Givens rotations in parallel ($d/2$ independent ones), but on fixed disjoint pairs; different layers implement Givens on different matchings of index pairs. If you arrange a sequence of cleverly chosen butterfly matchings (the kaleidoscope schedule), the ensemble of applied $2\times 2$ rotations can emulate the sequence of Givens rotations needed to reduce or build any orthogonal matrix, with far fewer distinct layers than naïve pairwise scheduling.

Dao et al. show that with the kaleidoscope schedule you need only $l = d-1$ alternating “levels” to ensure that every unordered index pair $(i,j)$ is covered by at least one of the $2\times 2$ blocks across the whole sequence, in a way that allows composing rotations to reproduce arbitrary planar rotations between any pair. Intuitively, each new level increases the connectivity of the “mixing graph” (you can see the paper’s information-transmission picture): after $d-1$ alternations you have a family of matchings whose span (under multiplication of the corresponding orthogonal $2\times 2$ blocks) is rich enough to express any Givens rotation sequence required to synthesize any orthogonal matrix. Thus the product of $2(d-1)$ butterfly-type factors (the paper writes it as $B_{d-1,1}\; B_{d-1,2}^\top \cdots B_{1,1}\; B_{1,2}^\top$) can generate all of $O(d)$.

Where to find the precise proof. The paper you quoted cites the kaleidoscope/butterfly universality results of Dao et al. (for example, “Kaleidoscope: learning fast algorithms for linear transforms” and related “learning fast orthogonal transforms” works). Those works give the constructive schedules and formal universality arguments (they convert the problem into covering all index pairs by matchings in a logarithmic or linear number of stages and then show how to reorder planar rotations to simulate arbitrary Givens sequences). The statement in your paper is the same high-level result: when $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$ and you take $l = d-1$ alternating products, the resulting class equals the full orthogonal group. The paper does not reprove the full algebraic universality but references that kaleidoscope/butterfly constructions give this guarantee.

A compact intuition to remember. Butterfly layers = many disjoint $2\times 2$ orthogonal operations in parallel. Alternating different pairings (and their transposes) lets those local $2\times 2$ operations cascade and interact so that over enough layers every coordinate pair can be related by composed planar rotations. With the kaleidoscope schedule you need depth proportional to $d$ (specifically $d-1$ in the stated construction) to be able to emulate the arbitrary sequence of Givens rotations that generates any orthogonal matrix.

References to follow up: read Dao et al.’s kaleidoscope/learn-fast-linear-transform papers for the formal constructive proof and schedule; the paper you cited uses that result to assert Theorem 1 (they name the same $B_{i,j}(d)$ kaleidoscope factorization).

### Matching BOFT and LoRA under a fixed parameter budget: a concrete example
Question:
Suppose we finetune a layer with $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{4096\times 4096}$. LoRA with rank $r$ adds $4096\cdot r + r\cdot 4096 \approx 8192r$ parameters. For BOFT($m,b$), the paper says the budget is $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$. If I want to match a LoRA budget of, say, $r=8$ (about $65{,}536$ params), what $(m,b)$ choices would be comparable, and how does one choose $m$ to ensure $\mathbf{R}$ is dense or near-dense?
Answer:
Short answer: with $d = 4096$ the BOFT parameter budget
formula in the paper gives
$\text{params} = \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m = 2048\cdot(b-1)\cdot m$.
Equating this to LoRA with $r = 8$ ($\approx 65{,}536$ params) gives
$(b-1)\cdot m = 32$. So any integer pair $(m,b)$ satisfying $(b-1)\cdot m = 32$ matches the LoRA budget. Examples are $(m,b) = (32,2), (16,3), (8,5), (4,9), (2,17), (1,33)$. In practice you should prefer small $b$ and larger $m$ (for near-dense / dense $R$) and pick $b$ that fits the BOFT block requirements (usually even / powers-of-two blocks are used).

Why pick $m$ to ensure $R$ is dense? The paper states that BOFT produces a dense orthogonal matrix when you take enough butterfly layers: specifically the “dense” regime is reached when
$m \approx \log_2(2d/b)$.
With $d = 4096$ this becomes
$m_{\text{dense}} = \log_2(8192/b) = 13 - \log_2(b)$.
So for common block sizes:
- $b = 2 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 12$,
- $b = 4 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 11$,
- $b = 8 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 10$,
- $b = 16 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 9$.

Interpretation and practical guidance: density of the final $R$ is controlled by $m$ (number of butterfly components): larger $m$ lets information flow across more levels and so the product of sparse factors becomes dense. If you want a truly dense $R$ you should choose $m \ge m_{\text{dense}}$. Given the budget constraint $(b-1)\cdot m = 32$, the only choice that both matches the budget and meets typical $m_{\text{dense}}$ values is to pick a small block size ($b = 2$ is the simplest and commonly used) and a relatively large $m$. Concretely $(m,b) = (32,2)$ matches the LoRA budget and is well above $m_{\text{dense}} = 12$, so $R$ will be dense (but with higher runtime cost since you apply many factors). If you reduce $m$ to save compute (e.g. $(m,b) = (4,9)$ or $(8,5)$), you still match the parameter budget but may not reach the dense regime unless the chosen $m \ge 13 - \log_2(b)$.

Trade-offs to keep in mind: smaller $b$ and larger $m$ give better expressivity (but more forward/backward multiply steps), larger $b$ and smaller $m$ give fewer factor multiplications but induce more block structure (less global mixing). Choose according to whether you prioritize matching LoRA’s parameter count while getting a near-dense $R$ (pick $b$ small, $m$ large; e.g. $b = 2$, $m = 32$) or want fewer factor layers (pick larger $b$ but accept more sparsity / structured inductive bias).

### Do BOFT and OFT actually preserve pairwise neuron angles in the weight matrix?
Question:
The motivation for OFT is to preserve pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors (columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$). If $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ and $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, then for two columns $\mathbf{w}^0_i,\mathbf{w}^0_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we have $\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_j \rangle = \langle \mathbf{w}^0_i, \mathbf{w}^0_j \rangle$. Could someone confirm this holds exactly for BOFT (since it is a product of orthogonals) and discuss whether numerical drift from exact orthogonality could meaningfully affect this angle preservation in practice?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes in theory; both OFT and BOFT preserve pairwise angles exactly because they multiply the pretrained weight matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix, and BOFT is built as a product of orthogonal factors so the product is orthogonal. In practice, floating‑point errors can introduce tiny non‑orthogonality, but the induced change in inner products (and thus angles) is provably bounded and typically negligible; if needed one can re‑orthogonalize or use higher precision.

Explanation grounded in the paper
OFT represents the finetuned weights as $W = R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal. The paper uses Cayley parameterization (or small $2\times2$ rotations) so that each learned factor is orthogonal during training. BOFT parameterizes $R$ as a product of orthogonal butterfly components $R = B_m \dots B_1$; since each $B_i$ is orthogonal, $R$ is orthogonal (because a product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal). Therefore, for two neuron columns $w^0_i, w^0_j$, we have exactly
$\langle R w^0_i, R w^0_j\rangle = (R w^0_i)^T (R w^0_j) = (w^0_i)^T (R^T R) w^0_j = (w^0_i)^T w^0_j$,
because $R^T R = I$. This is the paper’s stated motivation: orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise angles and the spectral properties of $W^0$.

Finite‑precision / numerical drift
In floating point arithmetic the computed $R$ may not satisfy $R^T R = I$ exactly. Let $E := R^T R - I$ denote the deviation from orthogonality. The change in the inner product of two columns equals
$(w^0_i)^T E w^0_j$,
so an absolute bound is
$\left|(w^0_i)^T E w^0_j\right| \le \|E\|_{\mathrm{op}} \cdot \|w^0_i\|_2 \cdot \|w^0_j\|_2$,
where $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the operator (spectral) norm of $E$. Thus the angle error is controlled by the size of the orthogonality violation and the norms of the neuron vectors.

How large is $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ in practice? For typical training with Cayley parameterization or parameterizing each butterfly block as exact rotations, the algebraic parameterization enforces orthogonality up to numerical rounding. Rounding errors in FP32 are on the order of machine epsilon (approximately $10^{-7}$) per operation; after many ops the accumulated $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ remains very small (typically many orders of magnitude below signal norms), so the induced inner‑product perturbation is negligible for usual neural network scales. If you use very deep compositions, very large dimensions, or low precision (FP16) arithmetic, accumulation can grow and make $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ larger, but it still tends to be small relative to typical neuron norms.

Practical implications and mitigations
Because the paper already uses Cayley transforms and builds BOFT from block orthogonals ($2\times2$ rotations or their block analogs), orthogonality is enforced algebraically, so numerical drift is minimal. If you are concerned in a particular application (e.g., extreme low precision or very long training), you can monitor $\|R^T R - I\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ or periodically re‑orthogonalize (e.g., QR or polar decomposition) or use mixed precision where the orthogonal factors are stored/updated in higher precision. In summary, angle preservation is exact in the math of OFT/BOFT and in practice only suffers tiny, controllable numerical drift that can be bounded and mitigated if necessary.

### Cayley transform invertibility and stability during training
Question:
The Cayley transform uses $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ with $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$. I understand skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues, so $1-\lambda \neq 0$ for such $\lambda$, suggesting $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is always invertible. Is this a rigorous guarantee that holds for all real skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$? Are there practical numerical stability issues when $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$ grows during training, and how does Cayley compare to the exponential map $\exp(\mathbf{Q})$ in terms of stability and compute?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — for every real skew‑symmetric $Q$ the Cayley map $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ is mathematically well‑defined ($I-Q$ is always invertible). In practice the Cayley parameterization is also numerically benign in the usual senses and is much cheaper to compute than a full matrix exponential; the main practical concerns are how you implement the inverse (don’t form it explicitly) and the cost of solving linear systems / backprop through them. Below I give the precise reasoning and practical recommendations.

Why $I-Q$ is always invertible
For a real skew‑symmetric matrix $Q$ ($Q^T=-Q$) all eigenvalues of $Q$ are purely imaginary and appear in conjugate pairs (and in odd dimension there may be a zero eigenvalue). Concretely any eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $Q$ is $\lambda=i t$ for some real $t$ (possibly $t=0$). The eigenvalues of $I-Q$ are $1-\lambda=1-it$, which are never zero for any real $t$. Hence $\det(I-Q)=\prod_j(1-\lambda_j)\neq 0$ and $I-Q$ is invertible. This is a rigorous algebraic fact that holds for all real skew‑symmetric $Q$.

Numerical conditioning and stability
Two useful facts follow from the spectral form above. The magnitude of each eigenvalue of $I-Q$ is $|1-it|=\sqrt{1+t^2}\ge 1$, so eigenvalues of $I-Q$ are bounded away from zero; there is no risk that eigenvalues approach zero as $\|Q\|$ grows. In other words, $I-Q$ is not singular and does not become ill‑conditioned in the sense of having tiny eigenvalues as the imaginary parts grow.

That said, “numerical stability” has several practical dimensions:

1) Conditioning: Because $|1-it|$ grows with $|t|$, the minimum magnitude of eigenvalues of $I-Q$ is at least $1$, so you should not expect catastrophic conditioning coming from small denominators. In fact, in this eigenvalue sense, larger magnitude imaginary parts only increase the moduli of $I-Q$ eigenvalues.

2) Forward rounding / arithmetic: Computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ explicitly with floating point arithmetic can still introduce rounding error, but these are ordinary linear algebra rounding effects. They are not amplified by small singular values of $(I-Q)$ (there aren’t any). Use stable linear algebra routines rather than naive looped inversion.

3) Computing $R$ and gradients: $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ is orthogonal and has spectral norm $1$, so $R$ itself is not “exploding”. However, backprop requires differentiating through the inverse (or solving a linear system); forming explicit inverses is both slower and can be less stable than solving linear systems. In practice you should compute $R$ by solving $(I-Q)X=(I+Q)$ for $X$ (i.e., call a linear solver) instead of computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ directly, and similarly propagate gradients by solving the appropriate adjoint linear systems (linear solves are numerically stable when using LAPACK/BLAS).

4) Param magnitudes during training: There is no mathematical blow‑up in $R$ as $\|Q\|$ grows, but very large entries in $Q$ can increase round‑off error in computing products and solves. This is a normal floating‑point consideration; it’s mitigated by using double precision in diagnostics or sticking to structured parameterizations (the paper uses $2\times 2$ block rotations via Cayley or small block orthogonals within the butterfly factors, which keeps numeric sizes small). BOFT initializes $Q=0$ (identity $R$) which avoids any transient large $Q$ at start.

Comparison to the matrix exponential $\exp(Q)$
Both Cayley and $\exp$ produce orthogonal matrices from skew‑symmetric $Q$, but they differ in cost and numerical tradeoffs.

- Existence and theory: $\exp(Q)$ is always defined and yields an orthogonal matrix. Cayley is also globally defined for skew‑symmetric $Q$ (as argued above). So both are theoretically safe.

- Numerical behavior: $\operatorname{expm}(Q)$ computes a matrix exponential which may require scaling and squaring, Padé approximants or other techniques; for large norms these algorithms are stable but costlier. The Cayley map avoids series expansions and is algebraically cheaper. Gradients through $\operatorname{expm}$ require differentiating the exponential (often implemented with additional linear algebra work), and can be more expensive or complicated than differentiating through one or two linear solves for Cayley.

- Cost: For a dense $d\times d$ matrix, computing $\operatorname{expm}(Q)$ is typically $O(d^3)$ with a relatively large constant due to multiple matrix multiplications, whereas forming $R$ via Cayley also requires solving a $d\times d$ linear system (or performing an LU factorization once and solves each step), which is $O(d^3)$ but with a smaller constant in many implementations. In the BOFT approach you avoid a full $d\times d$ solve by parameterizing $Q$ as many small blocks ($2\times 2$ or small $b\times b$ blocks in butterfly components), so Cayley becomes extremely cheap: each small block has a closed form $2\times 2$ rotation or trivial linear solve. That is a key practical win emphasized in the paper.

- Backprop complexity: Differentiating through $\operatorname{expm}$ is heavier. Differentiating Cayley mostly reduces to differentiating a linear solve; adjoint solves are well understood and efficient. Again, when you use small‑block Cayley (as BOFT does), differentiation is very cheap.

Practical recommendations (based on the paper’s approach)
Because BOFT composes $R$ from many small orthogonal factors ($2\times 2$ or small $b\times b$ Cayley parameterized blocks), you get the theoretical invertibility, good numerical behavior, and cheap compute. Implement $R$ by solving linear systems (or using closed forms for $2\times 2$ blocks) rather than forming inverses; initialize $Q=0$ (identity $R$) as the paper does; and use the block/butterfly factorization to keep per‑block operations inexpensive and numerically robust. If you were to parameterize one large dense $Q$, Cayley remains mathematically safe but you must be careful to use stable linear algebra (LU factorization + forward/back substitution) and avoid explicit matrix inversion in the code.

Summary
(I) Rigorous: yes — $I-Q$ is always invertible for real skew‑symmetric $Q$. (II) Numerical stability: there is no eigenvalue‑driven singularity; the main practical issues are ordinary floating‑point rounding and the implementation choice. Use linear solves rather than explicit inverses and prefer small‑block Cayley (as BOFT does) to keep compute and numerical error modest. (III) Compared to $\exp(Q)$, Cayley is usually cheaper and simpler to differentiate; $\operatorname{expm}$ is robust but more expensive. BOFT leverages these facts by using many small Cayley blocks inside a butterfly factorization to get parameter efficiency, stability, and low per‑step cost.

### What is the actual runtime complexity of applying BOFT at training time?
Question:
The paper argues BOFT trades space for time. For input vector multiplication $y=\mathbf{R}(m,b) x$, what is the precise flop count as a function of $d$, $m$, and $b$? Is it $O(dm)$ for $b=2$ and $O(dbm)$ for blocks, or something else? How does this compare to a dense $d\times d$ multiplication ($O(d^2)$) and to LoRA's extra low-rank term addition ($O(dr)$)?
Answer:
Short answer: Applying BOFT’s orthogonal transform $R(m,b)$ to a vector $x$ costs $O(d m)$ arithmetic operations (linear in $d$ and linear in $m$), not $O(d b m)$. More concretely, one butterfly component (the block-butterfly block used in BOFT) has $O(d)$ nonzeros, so multiplying a length-$d$ vector by one component takes $O(d)$ multiplies and $O(d)$ adds; doing that for $m$ components yields about $m\cdot(2d)$ multiplies and a similar number of adds. In common flop conventions (counting one multiply and one add as 2 flops) this is $\approx 4 d m$ flops. By contrast a dense $d\times d$ matrix–vector multiply costs $O(d^2)$ ($\approx 2 d^2$ flops) and LoRA’s extra low-rank term $BAx$ costs $O(d r)$ ($\approx 2 d r$ flops).

Why this is true (intuitive + technical): BOFT represents the full orthogonal matrix $R$ as a product of $m$ sparse “butterfly” components. Each component is a blockwise arrangement of many small $2\times2$ (or $2b\times2b$ in the block variant) mixing blocks; the pattern is extremely sparse so that each component has only $O(d)$ nonzero entries (you can verify from the paper’s construction that one component contributes about $2d$ nonzeros). Multiplying a vector by a sparse matrix with $N_{\text{nz}}$ nonzeros takes $O(N_{\text{nz}})$ scalar multiplications and $O(N_{\text{nz}})$ additions; therefore each component costs $O(d)$ work and the whole product costs $m$ times that, i.e. $O(d m)$.

Notes tying to the paper’s parameter counts and tradeoffs: BOFT’s effective number of trainable parameters scales as $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ (the paper’s formula). The runtime cost of applying $R(m,b)$ to a vector depends on $m$ (the number of components) and $d$ (the dimension) but not multiplicatively on $b$ in the sparse-matrix sense — increasing $b$ increases the number of trainable parameters per component (hence can improve expressivity) but does not change the fact that each component is $O(d)$ sparse, so the asymptotic matvec cost stays $O(d)$ per component. That’s why the paper says BOFT trades space for time: you reduce parameter count (compared to a dense orthogonal with $O(d^2)$ params) by using many sparse multiplications, incurring an $O(m)$ runtime overhead during training.

Practical flop comparisons (using multiply+add = 2 flops):
- BOFT: $\approx 2d$ multiplies $+\,2d$ adds per component $\to\approx 4 d m$ flops total ($O(d m)$).
- Dense $d\times d$ matvec: $\approx d^2$ multiplies $+\,d^2$ adds $\to\approx 2 d^2$ flops ($O(d^2)$).
- LoRA extra term (rank $r$): computing $(B A) x$ or $A (B x)$ is $\approx 2 d r$ flops ($O(d r)$).

Also remember the operational context: BOFT’s extra runtime applies during training (or when you explicitly apply $R$ repeatedly); after finetuning you can absorb $R$ into the pretrained weights and have no extra inference cost.

### Could block-diagonal OFT approximate classic transforms if we allowed permutations between blocks?
Question:
The paper claims block-diagonal OFT cannot recover classic transforms like DFT or DCT, while butterfly structures can. If we were to interleave fixed or learned permutation matrices between block-diagonal orthogonals (still within a single layer's parameterization), would that fundamentally break the block-diagonal constraint and move us toward a butterfly-like expressivity? In other words, is it the absence of inter-block mixing, rather than block-diagonality per se, that prevents recovering such transforms?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — it’s not block-diagonality per se that forbids classic dense transforms, it’s the lack of any inter‑block mixing. If you allow permutations between block‑diagonal orthogonals (and especially if you allow several alternating stages of small orthogonal blocks and permutations), you restore the ability to route information between different index groups and can approach the connectivity and expressivity of butterfly factorizations. But a few important caveats follow from the paper’s analysis.

Why permutations help (intuitively and technically). The paper frames factorizing a dense orthogonal $R$ as “information transmission” on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid: each factor connects adjacent levels, and to get a dense $R$ every source node must have a path to every target node. A single block‑diagonal orthogonal has no edges between blocks, so whole groups of source nodes can never reach some target nodes — that is exactly the missing inter‑block mixing. A permutation between factors reorders nodes and therefore changes which block pairs get connected next; composing block‑diagonal rotations with permutations can create paths that transmit information across blocks. In fact the paper explicitly notes that butterfly components are permutations of the $2\times 2$ block structure: the non‑zero pattern of higher‑level butterfly components is “a permutation of the non‑zero pattern of $\tilde{B}(d,2)$”. Thus alternating small orthogonal blocks (e.g. $2\times 2$ rotations) with permutations is precisely the mechanism the butterfly uses to achieve global mixing with $O(d \log d)$ sparse factors.

How many permutations / stages you need. A single permutation interleaved with one block‑diagonal rotation is usually not enough to yield a dense $R$. To get full (or high) expressivity you need multiple stages: the butterfly factorization uses $O(\log d)$ stages of small orthogonal blocks interleaved with fixed index reordering (which are permutations). Theorem 1 in the paper and the kaleidoscope construction show that composing many butterfly (or permuted block) factors can represent large subsets of the orthogonal group and, with a suitable number of factors, can approximate (or even span) the whole orthogonal group. So permitting permutations moves you toward butterfly expressivity, but you need enough alternating stages (and appropriate block rotations) to reach the same capability.

What permutations alone cannot do. Permutations are parameter‑free rewirings: they only change which coordinates get combined next, they do not themselves implement rotations or mixing of values. To recover transforms like DFT/DCT/HT you still need non‑trivial orthogonal $2\times 2$ (or small) rotation blocks in the stages. In other words, permutations enable the routing pattern that lets small rotations propagate influence globally, but the rotations provide the actual amplitude/phase mixing. The butterfly is exactly the combination of these two ingredients: small orthogonal atoms (often $2\times 2$ rotations / complex twiddle factors) plus index permutations.

Practical implication for a single‑layer OFT parameterization. If your single‑layer parameterization allows $R$ to be a product of factors like (block‑diag orthogonal) · (permutation) · (block‑diag orthogonal) · (permutation) · …, then you have removed the “no inter‑block mixing” restriction and can obtain the dense connectivity patterns used by butterfly factorizations. That parameterization is effectively BOFT when you choose small block size (e.g. $b=2$) and the appropriate sequence of permutations: BOFT$(m,b)$ reduces to block‑diagonal OFT when $m=1$, and to dense/butterfly-like matrices when $m \approx \log d$ with the right permutations and rotations. The tradeoffs remain the same as the paper stresses: you gain expressivity (and the ability to recover classic fast transforms) but you need multiple factors (more computation per forward/backward step) and you must choose the number of stages/block size to control parameter efficiency and inductive bias.

Summary. The fundamental limitation of plain block‑diagonal OFT is the absence of inter‑block mixing. Introducing permutations between block‑diagonal orthogonals restores inter‑block paths and, when combined with enough alternating small orthogonal blocks (as in butterfly factorizations), yields the same kind of global expressivity that lets you recover DFT/DCT/Hadamard and approximate arbitrary orthogonals with sufficient depth. Permutations alone are necessary but not sufficient — you still need the small orthogonal rotations in multiple stages to perform the actual mixing.

### What exactly is meant by "layer-shared" matrices in BOFT versus OFT?
Question:
The paper says OFT/BOFT transform neurons in the same layer by the same orthogonal matrix and also mentions "layer-shared weight matrices." Does BOFT learn one $\mathbf{R}$ per linear weight matrix, one per layer shared across all projections in that layer, or a single $\mathbf{R}$ shared across multiple layers? For LLMs and ViTs, what sharing granularity was used in experiments?
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper “layer‑shared” means one orthogonal transform $R$ is shared across the linear projections that belong to the same neural network layer (e.g. the projection matrices inside a Transformer block). OFT as originally described learns one $R$ per individual linear weight matrix; BOFT uses the butterfly factorization for $R$ and in the experiments the authors use a single BOFT $R$ per layer (shared among that layer’s projections).  

Why that wording appears and what it means intuitively. The original OFT formulation treats each pretrained linear layer $W^0$ separately and learns an orthogonal matrix $R$ for that specific $W^0$, so the granularity there is “per linear weight matrix.” The paper then emphasizes a slightly different practical design: instead of having a distinct $R$ for every singly indexed matrix (which multiplies the number of trainable orthogonal factors), BOFT applies a single orthogonal transform to all neuron vectors in the same layer — i.e. the same $R$ is multiplied into multiple weight matrices that implement the layer’s projections (for example $Q/K/V$ or the feed‑forward in a Transformer block). That is what the authors refer to as “layer‑shared weight matrices.” The idea is to preserve the pairwise geometry of neurons in that layer while saving parameters by not instantiating separate $R$’s for each projection.

Where BOFT sits relative to the extremes. The framework is flexible: you can recover OFT’s per‑matrix behavior by giving each linear map its own $R$ (no sharing), and you could in principle share a single $R$ across many layers (more aggressive sharing), but the BOFT paper specifically picks the middle ground — sharing an orthogonal factor at the layer level — because it strikes a balance between parameter efficiency and expressivity. The butterfly factorization itself is orthogonal to this sharing choice: it reduces the number of parameters needed to represent each $R$ (from $O(d^2)$ down toward $O(d\log d)$ for the butterfly) whether $R$ is assigned per matrix or per layer.

What the experiments used. The paper reports BOFT applied to large language models and vision transformers and states that they “transform the weights with layer‑shared weight matrices.” That indicates the experimental setup used one BOFT $R$ per network layer (shared across that layer’s projections). This is the intended sharing granularity in the empirical results reported in the paper.

Implications. Sharing $R$ at the layer level preserves inter‑neuron angular relationships within the layer while reducing the number of separate orthogonal factors to learn. It saves parameters relative to per‑matrix OFT, while staying more expressive than forcing a single global $R$ for the whole network. The butterfly parameterization further improves parameter efficiency of each shared $R$, so the combined design yields practical finetuning with strong generalization in the authors’ LLM and ViT experiments.

### How does orthogonal left-multiplication interact with residual connections and LayerNorm?
Question:
If we replace $\mathbf{W}^0$ by $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, this changes the mapping from inputs to outputs. Given residual connections and LayerNorm, does left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ have any special effect on activation scales or gradient norms? Since $\mathbf{R}$ preserves the $\ell_2$ norm, is there a theoretical reason to expect better stability compared to additive updates like LoRA?
Answer:
Short answer: Left-multiplying a pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix $R$ mostly acts like a rotation of the layer’s input/neuron space and therefore preserves Euclidean lengths and singular values of the frozen weight; that gives a structural reason why orthogonal finetuning (OFT/BOFT) tends to be more stable than unconstrained additive updates (LoRA). However, residual connections and LayerNorm interact nontrivially with that rotation: LayerNorm removes (or rescales) much of any pure scale change, and residuals add the original signal back so the net effect seen by the network is a mixture of rotated and identity paths. Thus orthogonality helps with stability in an important, but not absolute, way.

Why $R$ “helps” (intuitive + a bit of math). Consider a linear layer with pretrained weights $W^0$ and an orthogonal update $R$, so the new forward is
$z = (R W^0)^T x = (W^0)^T (R^T x)$.
Because $R$ is orthogonal, $R^T R = I$, so $R$ preserves Euclidean norms: for any $v$, $\|R v\|_2 = \|v\|_2$. Equivalently, if $W^0 = U \Sigma V^T$ is the SVD, then
$R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^T$,
so the singular values $\Sigma$ (and hence the spectral norm $\|W^0\|_2$) are unchanged. Preserving the singular values is important: it prevents the layer from introducing larger operator norms that can amplify activations or gradients, which is a common source of instability. This is the paper’s key spectral-property argument: multiplicative orthogonal updates preserve spectral norm and pairwise angles between neurons, and that contributes to training stability and better generalization.

How LayerNorm affects this. LayerNorm (as commonly used in transformers) normalizes activations across features to zero mean and unit variance (then applies an affine scale+shift). An orthogonal left-multiplication preserves the variance (because orthogonal transforms preserve second moments up to permutation/rotation) but can change the mean of the pre-normalized vector (rotation mixes coordinates). If LayerNorm is applied after the linear map, the normalization will remove any pure scale change and mostly hide effects that are only scaling; it will not, however, remove changes in the directional geometry that affect relative coordinates after normalization. In short, orthogonality makes the layer’s contribution less likely to blow up variance; LayerNorm further reduces sensitivity to residual scaling differences, so the combination is doubly stabilizing for scale-related problems.

How residual connections interact. Residual connections add (often) the input or previous activations back to the layer output. If the layer produces $z$ and the residual adds $x_{\text{res}}$, the network sees $z + x_{\text{res}}$. There are two typical placements: Pre-LN (LayerNorm before the sublayer) and Post-LN (LayerNorm after the sublayer). The interactions differ:

- If the network uses pre-LN (LayerNorm before linear), the linear sees normalized inputs. $R$ acting on the weights is equivalent to rotating those normalized inputs; because the inputs are norm-controlled, the effect of $R$ is constrained and unlikely to produce exploding activations. The residual path still mixes the rotated output with the identity path, which tends to lessen extreme deviations.

- If the network uses post-LN (LayerNorm after addition), the LayerNorm will renormalize $z + x_{\text{res}}$, so again pure scale changes are suppressed. Orthogonality helps because it keeps $z$’s scale consistent with the pretrained model; the residual prevents the model from relying solely on the rotated sublayer output and thus acts as a regularizer.

In all cases, residuals + LayerNorm make the network robust to moderate changes; orthogonality reduces the chance of introducing large operator norms in the first place, so the three mechanisms act together to improve stability.

Gradients: preservation vs amplification. Because orthogonal left-multiplication preserves singular values of $W^0$, the linear mapping’s operator norm remains the same; therefore, at least for the linear part, orthogonal updates do not increase the worst-case amplification of gradients back through that layer. Put differently, for the linear map $y = (R W^0)^T x$ the Jacobian norm w.r.t. $x$ is bounded by the same spectral norm as the pretrained weight. Additive updates like LoRA change $W^0$ to $W^0 + \Delta$ where $\Delta$ is low-rank; $\Delta$ can change singular values (potentially increasing the largest singular value) and thus may increase gradient magnitudes or change the conditioning. That is the theoretical reason to expect improved gradient-scale stability from multiplicative orthogonal updates versus additive unconstrained updates.

Caveats and limits. Orthogonality helps but is not a panacea. First, orthogonal $R$ is a rotation/mixing only — it cannot change the singular values of $W^0$, so if the pretrained weights have problematic conditioning for the new task, OFT cannot fix that by scaling singular values (LoRA could). Second, nonlinearities, LayerNorm’s affine parameters, attention softmax, and downstream layers can still amplify or damp gradients; orthogonality only controls one piece of the whole computational graph. Third, orthogonal constraint reduces degrees of freedom and so may sacrifice expressivity compared to unrestricted additive updates; that trade-off can be good for generalization but may limit fit in some tasks. Finally, in practice BOFT uses Cayley parameterization with identity initialization (so the initial model is unchanged) and a structured parameterization (butterfly), which balances the stability benefits of orthogonality with parameter efficiency.

Summary. Left-multiplying by orthogonal $R$ is equivalent to a rotation that preserves activation norms and singular values of the pretrained layer; this gives a principled reason for improved stability of training and gradient norms compared to unconstrained additive methods like LoRA. Residual connections and LayerNorm further reduce sensitivity to scale and stabilize training, so the combination of orthogonality + residuals + LayerNorm is a natural and effective design for stable finetuning. Nonetheless, orthogonality cannot change singular-value spectrum to fix conditioning and reduces the parameter space, so practical trade-offs between stability, expressivity, and parameter-efficiency remain.
