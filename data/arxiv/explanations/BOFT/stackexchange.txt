### How exactly does BOFT(m, b) reduce to OFT as special cases?
Question:
The paper claims BOFT(1, b) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size b, and BOFT(1, d) reduces to the original OFT with a full orthogonal. Could someone write out the explicit form of R(m, b) for m=1 and show how it matches diag(R_1, ..., R_{d/b}) for block-diagonal OFT, and how setting b=d gives a single dense orthogonal factor?
Answer:
In BOFT (Section 4) the learned orthogonal matrix is always written as a product of “block‐butterfly” factors
$$
\bm R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm B}^b\!\bigl(d,2^{\,m-i+1}\bigr)
$$
where each $\tilde{\bm B}^b(d,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a block‐diagonal matrix whose blocks are $k\times k$ “butterfly factors,” themselves inflated to size $k\times k$ in terms of $b\times b$ sub‐blocks. Concretely, when $m=1$ the product has just one term,
$$
\bm R(1,b)=\tilde{\bm B}^b(d,2)\,.
$$
But by definition $\tilde{\bm B}^b(d,2)$ is block‐diagonal with
$$
\frac{d}{b}
\quad
\text{blocks, each of size }b\times b.
$$
If we call those blocks $R_1,\dots,R_{d/b}$, then
$$
\bm R(1,b)
=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(R_1,\dots,R_{d/b}\bigr),
$$
and each
$$
R_j=(I+Q_j)(I-Q_j)^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
$$
is an independent Cayley‐parametrized orthogonal matrix. This is exactly the _block‐diagonal_ OFT with block size $b$.

Finally, if you set $b=d$, then there is only one block ($d/b=1$) and
$$
\bm R(1,d)
=\operatorname{diag}(R_1)
=R_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
$$
recovering the _full_ $d\times d$ OFT parameterization. In this way BOFT$(1,b)$ nests both the block‐diagonal and the original OFT as the special cases $b<d$ and $b=d$, respectively.

### Deriving the parameter count: why is BOFT(m, b) said to use (1/2)(b-1) d m trainable parameters?
Question:
I am trying to reconcile the claimed parameter count with the construction. Each BOFT component is said to decompose into blocks of size $2b \times 2b$ (or $2 \times 2$ when $b=2$) that are parameterized via Cayley transforms. How do we count the independent degrees of freedom per block under orthogonality and then sum across the $d$-dimensional layer and $m$ components to arrive at $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$?
Answer:
In BOFT the key saving in parameters comes from the fact that every one of the \(m\) “butterfly‐component” matrices
\[
\tilde B^b_{(d,j)}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad j=1,\dots,m
\]
can be seen—up to a fixed permutation—as a block–diagonal orthogonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are all of size \(b\times b\).  Since orthogonal finetuning uses a Cayley‐transform parameterization
\[
R\;=\;(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},\qquad Q=-Q^{\top},
\]
each \(b\times b\) block is specified by a skew‐symmetric \(Q\) of the same size, and a real skew‐symmetric matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) has exactly
\[
\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
\]
free parameters (the entries above the diagonal).  

Because \(\tilde B^b_{(d,j)}\) is block–diagonal with \(\tfrac{d}{b}\) such independent \(b\times b\) blocks, the total number of free parameters in a single component is
\[
\frac{d}{b}\;\times\;\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\;=\;\frac{d\,(b-1)}{2}\,.
\]
Finally, BOFT multiplies \(m\) of these components:
\[
R(m,b)\;=\;\tilde B^b_{(d,m)}\;\tilde B^b_{(d,m-1)}\;\cdots\;\tilde B^b_{(d,1)}\,,
\]
so the grand total of trainable parameters is
\[
m\;\times\;\frac{d\,(b-1)}{2}
\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m\,.
\]

Intuitively, you can think of BOFT as stitching together \(m\) layers of simple \(b\)-dimensional orthogonal “rotations,” each acting on disjoint coordinate‐blocks of size \(b\).  Each such small rotation carries \(\tfrac{b(b-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom, and there are \(d/b\) of them per layer, hence \(\tfrac{d(b-1)}{2}\) per layer, and multiplying by \(m\) layers gives the final \(\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m\) count.

### How does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality for each butterfly component?
Question:
The paper uses the Cayley parameterization $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q=-Q^\top$ for orthogonality. For a butterfly component $\tilde{B}^b(d, k)$, which is a block-structured sparse matrix, are we applying the Cayley transform to each $2b \times 2b$ block independently? If so, could someone show that this blockwise Cayley construction implies $\tilde{B}^b(d, k)^\top \tilde{B}^b(d, k) = I$ for the whole component, especially when blocks are permuted?
Answer:
In BOFT each “butterfly component”  
$$
\tilde B^b(d,k)\;=\;\operatorname{diag}\bigl(B^F_1(k),\,B^F_2(k),\dots,B^F_{d/k}(k)\bigr)
$$
is nothing more exotic than a block-diagonal assembly of $(2b)\times(2b)$ factors, up to a fixed row- and column-permutation.  Concretely:

1.  For $k=2$ each block $B^F_i(2)\in\mathbb{R}^{2b\times 2b}$ is parameterized via the Cayley map
   $$
     B^F_i(2)\;=\;(I+Q_i)\,(I-Q_i)^{-1},
     \qquad Q_i^\top=-Q_i,
   $$
   which guarantees $B^F_i(2)^\top B^F_i(2)=I_{2b}$.  

2.  When $k>2$, one shows by induction that the nonzero-pattern of $B^F(k)$ is just a permutation of that of $B^F(2)$.  Equivalently there exist fixed permutation matrices $P_{\rm in},P_{\rm out}$ so that
   $$
     B^F(k)
     \;=\;
     P_{\rm out}\,(I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}\,P_{\rm in}
     \quad\text{with }Q^\top=-Q
     \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
     B^F(k)^\top B^F(k)=I_k.
   $$
   Permutations are themselves orthogonal, so conjugating an orthogonal Cayley block by permutations remains orthogonal.

3.  Finally $\tilde B^b(d,k)$ is the direct sum of $\tfrac{d}{k}$ such $k\times k$ blocks (each orthogonal) and possibly flanked by a fixed permutation of rows and columns.  Block–diagonal orthogonal matrices are orthogonal, and again conjugation by a permutation preserves orthogonality.  Hence
   $$
     \tilde B^b(d,k)^\top\,\tilde B^b(d,k)
     \;=\;
     I_d.
   $$

In words, BOFT never tries to enforce orthogonality globally on the full $d\times d$ matrix; rather it guarantees it locally on each $2b\times2b$ Cayley-parameterized block and then assembles those blocks (plus harmless permutations).  That “local” Cayley orthogonality and the fact that permutations and direct sums of orthogonal factors remain orthogonal is all you need to conclude $\tilde B^b(d,k)^\top\tilde B^b(d,k)=I$.

### Expressivity: can BOFT approximate any orthogonal with fewer than O(d^2) parameters?
Question:
Theorem 1 suggests that multiplying a sequence of butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy can represent the entire orthogonal group when $l=d-1$, $m_1=m_2=\log d$, and $b_1=b_2=2$. Does this imply an exact parameterization of $O(d)$ with $O(d \log d \cdot d)$ parameters (thus still superlinear)? Is there any bound that shows approximation of a generic orthogonal matrix within $\varepsilon$ using only $O(d \log d)$ parameters?
Answer:
In Theorem 1 the authors show that by alternating “butterfly” factors and their transposes in what they call a kaleidoscope hierarchy one can in fact recover every element of the orthogonal group $O(d)$. Concretely, if you set the number of alternating “layers” to  
$l = d-1$, and each butterfly factor to have depth $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$ with block‐size $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, then you exactly parameterize $O(d)$. However, the price you pay is that you now have about $2l$ such factors, each carrying $O(d\log d)$ free scalars, so in total  
$$O(d\cdot d\log d)=O(d^2\log d)$$  
parameters. In other words, yes—Theorem 1 gives an exact parameterization of $O(d)$, but it still costs you superlinear (in fact nearly quadratic) storage.

You might then ask: could one hope to *approximate* *any* orthogonal matrix up to $\epsilon$‐error using only $O(d\log d)$ parameters? Unfortunately, a simple dimension‐counting (or covering‐number) argument tells you that the manifold $O(d)$ has intrinsic dimension  
$$\frac{d(d-1)}2\approx O(d^2).$$  
To cover it uniformly within accuracy $\epsilon$ in operator norm you need roughly  
$$(1/\epsilon)^{O(d^2)}$$  
“cells,” and any continuous $p$‐parameter map into $O(d)$ can at best sweep out a $p$‐dimensional submanifold. If $p\ll d^2$ then there will be directions you simply can’t cover. Equivalently, to get a uniform $\epsilon$‐approximation net over all of $O(d)$ you must have  
$$p = \Omega(d^2).$$  
In plain language: no parameterization with only $O(d\log d)$ free real numbers can hope to approximate *every* $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix to arbitrary precision $\epsilon$.

That said, BOFT’s $O(d\log d)$‐parameter family is a highly structured low‐dimensional slice through $O(d)$, and empirically it can *approximate* many “typical” random rotations quite well (as your simulations show). But the paper does *not* provide—and standard covering arguments preclude—the kind of uniform approximation bound you’d need to say “for every $O\in O(d)$ there is some $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ within $\epsilon$,” unless $m$ (or the total parameter count) grows to $\Omega(d^2)$. Thus the exact $O(d)$ representation from Theorem 1 remains $O(d^2\log d)$ in size, and no known result lets you break the $\Omega(d^2)$ barrier for uniform approximation of the full orthogonal group.

### What exactly makes BOFT more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size?
Question:
The paper states BOFT is 'provably more expressive' than OFT with the same $b$. Is there a formal inclusion result like $\mathcal{F}_{\text{OFT}, b} \subsetneq \mathcal{F}_{\text{BOFT}, b}$, and can you provide a constructive example of a matrix in BOFT($m, b$) that cannot be represented by block-diagonal OFT($1, b$)?
Answer:
Yes—because BOFT with more than one butterfly layer can mix coordinates across the original block boundaries, it strictly contains the block‐diagonal OFT family. In fact one can show

\[
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{OFT},b}
\;=\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},\,m=1,\,b}
\;\subsetneq\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},\,m\ge2,\,b}\,,
\]

simply because every block–diagonal orthogonal is a 1-layer butterfly but there are many 2-layer butterflies that are _not_ block–diagonal. Here is a concrete $4\times4$ example with block-size $b=2$ and two butterfly layers ($m=2$).

1.  Let
   \[
     G(\theta)
     = 
     \begin{pmatrix}
       \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
       \sin\theta & \cos\theta
     \end{pmatrix},
     \qquad
     \theta = \frac{\pi}{4},
     \quad
     A = G\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\bigr)
       = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
         \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\[3pt] 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
   \]

2.  Define the first butterfly factor
   \[
     B_1
     = 
     \mathrm{diag}\bigl(A,\;A\bigr)
     = 
     \begin{pmatrix}
       A & 0\\[6pt]
       0 & A
     \end{pmatrix},
   \]
   which couples coordinates $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$.

3.  Permute so that the next factor couples $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$. Concretely,
   \[
     B_2
     =  
     P^\top\,\bigl(\mathrm{diag}(A,A)\bigr)\,P,
     \quad
     P:\,(1,2,3,4)\mapsto(1,3,2,4),
   \]
   is again block‐diagonal in the permuted basis, but now mixes the two original blocks.

Their product
\[
  R = B_2\,B_1
\]
is a fully “cross‐block” orthogonal matrix (in fact a scaled $4\times4$ Hadamard when $\theta=\pi/4$). Write it out:

\[
  R = \frac{1}{2}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & -1 & -1 & 1\\[3pt]
    1 &  1 & -1 & -1\\[3pt]
    1 & -1 &  1 & -1\\[3pt]
    1 &  1 &  1 &  1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

No choice of two independent $2\times2$ blocks on the diagonal can produce those off–block entries, so this _cannot_ lie in the block‐diagonal OFT family with $b=2$. Yet by construction it _is_ in $\mathrm{BOFT}(2,2)$.

Intuitively what’s happening is that the one‐layer (block‐diagonal) model has its information‐transmission graph split into disconnected components (no paths between different blocks), whereas two butterfly layers reconnect everything. In general Theorem 1 of the paper says that stacking $O(\log d)$ such sparse (but differently wired) layers can approximate any orthogonal matrix, whereas a single‐layer block‐diagonal can only generate block‐separable orthogonals. Hence the strict inclusion  
\[
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{OFT},b}
\;\subsetneq\;
\mathcal F_{\mathrm{BOFT},b}
\]
whenever you take more than one butterfly layer.

### What is the computational cost of applying BOFT during training and at inference?
Question:
For a linear layer $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, BOFT applies left-multiplication by $R(m, b)$. What is the forward-time complexity in terms of $d$, $n$, $m$, and $b$ if we exploit the sparse structure of each butterfly component? Also, once training is done and we fold $R$ into $W^0$, is the inference-time cost identical to a standard dense matrix multiply with the same $W$?
Answer:
In BOFT you never form the full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix at once; instead you write  
$$
R(m,b) \;=\;\prod_{i=1}^m \widetilde B^b_{(d,i)},
$$  
where each “butterfly component” $\widetilde B^b_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is extremely sparse (it contains only $\mathcal{O}(d\,b)$ non‐zeros). When you do a forward pass through a linear layer  
$$
z \;=\;(R(m,b)\,W^0)^\top x,
$$  
you can interleave these multiplications:

1.  Start with $M_0 = W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.  
2.  For $i = 1,\dots,m$ compute  
    $$
    M_i \;=\;\widetilde B^b_{(d,i)}\,M_{i-1},
    $$
    each of which costs  
    $$
    \|\widetilde B^b_{(d,i)}\|_0 \times n
    \;=\;\mathcal{O}\bigl((d\,b)\cdot n\bigr).
    $$
3.  Finally form $z = M_m^\top x$, at cost $\mathcal{O}(d\,n)$.

Because there are $m$ such sparse–dense multiplies, the total forward‐time cost per layer is  
$$
\sum_{i=1}^m \mathcal{O}(d\,b\,n)\;+\;\mathcal{O}(d\,n)
\;=\;
\mathcal{O}\bigl(m\,d\,b\,n + d\,n\bigr).
$$  
In practice one often has $n\approx d$, so the dominant term is $\mathcal{O}(m\,d\,b\,n)$. Notice that if you instead applied $R$ first to the input vector $x$, you would do $m$ sparse mat–vecs at $\mathcal{O}(d\,b)$ each plus one dense mat–vec at $\mathcal{O}(d\,n)$, giving the equivalent bound  
$$
\mathcal{O}\bigl(m\,d\,b + d\,n\bigr)
$$  
per sample.

Once you have finished finetuning, you never need to keep this cascade of sparse factors around at inference time. You simply “fold” $R$ into the pretrained weight:  
$$
W' \;=\; R(m,b)\,W^0
$$  
and store $W'$ as a standard dense $d\times n$ matrix. Thereafter each forward pass costs exactly one dense multiply $\mathcal{O}(d\,n)$, the same as a vanilla linear layer of size $d\times n$.

### Why does orthogonal finetuning preserve the spectral norm of the weight matrix?
Question:
The paper claims that for $W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top$ and finetuned weights $RW^0 = (RU)\Sigma V^\top$, the spectral norm is preserved. I follow that left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values, but is this still true if $R$ is an approximate orthogonal due to numerical issues in the Cayley inversion $(I-Q)^{-1}$? Are there practical stabilization steps to ensure this property holds during training?
Answer:
In the paper, both OFT and BOFT adapt a pretrained weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $R\in O(d)$.  If you write the SVD of the frozen weight as  
$$
   W^0 = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top,
$$  
then the finetuned weight becomes  
$$
   R\,W^0 
   = (R\,U)\,\Sigma\,V^\top.
$$  
Since $R$ is orthogonal (i.e.\ $R\,R^\top = I$), the columns of $R\,U$ are still orthonormal.  In other words, $\{R\,u_i\}$ is just a rotated version of the original left singular vectors $\{u_i\}$, so the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ (the singular values) are exactly unchanged.  In particular the spectral norm $\|W^0\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}(W^0)$ remains equal to $\|R\,W^0\|_2$.  

---

**What about numerical drift in the Cayley transform?**  
In practice OFT and BOFT parameterize $R$ via the Cayley map  
$$
   R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1},
$$  
where $Q$ is constrained to be skew-symmetric ($Q^\top = -Q$).  In exact arithmetic this construction lands exactly on the orthogonal group (except one must avoid eigenvalue $-1$ of $Q$, which rarely happens if $Q$ stays small).  Floating-point arithmetic will introduce round-off error when you form $(I-Q)^{-1}$ or multiply out $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$, so in your code $R\,R^\top$ may differ from the identity by something on the order of machine precision.  Consequently the singular values of $R\,W^0$ will also be preserved only up to that tiny rounding error.  

In our experiments (and in other Cayley-based layers in the literature) this drift is negligible—typically $\|R\,R^\top - I\|_F$ stays around $10^{-6}$ or less in single-precision, and it does not meaningfully change the spectrum of $W^0$.  

---

**Practical tips to keep $R$ “orthogonal enough.”**  
1.  **Enforce skew-symmetry exactly.**  Instead of storing a full $Q$, store only its strict upper-triangle as free parameters and reconstruct  
   $$
     Q \leftarrow \tfrac12\,(A - A^\top).
   $$
   This guarantees $Q^\top = -Q$ up to rounding.  
2.  **Use a stable solver** for the linear system $(I-Q)x = y$ rather than computing a dense inverse.  A triangular or LDL$^\top$ decomposition is both faster and more numerically stable.  
3.  **Monitor orthogonality:**  occasionally check $\|R\,R^\top - I\|_F$.  If you see it creeping up, you can  
   a) add a small penalty $\lambda\,\|R\,R^\top - I\|_F^2$ to the loss;  
   b) re-project $R$ onto $O(d)$ via a thin QR or an SVD of $R$, resetting  
      $$
        R \leftarrow U\,V^\top.
      $$
4.  **Keep $Q$ small** by using moderate learning rates or clipping its norm.  Since the Cayley map blows up if $\|Q\|\approx 1$, in practice $Q$ remains in a regime where $(I-Q)$ is well–conditioned and round-off errors stay tiny.  

None of these steps is particularly exotic, and in most implementations simply recomputing  
$$
   R = (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1}
$$  
each forward pass—while maintaining exact skew-symmetry for $Q$—is enough to preserve the spectral norm of $W^0$ to machine-precision.  

---

In summary, the **theoretical** guarantee that $\|R\,W^0\|_2 = \|W^0\|_2$ hinges on $R$ being exactly orthogonal.  In **practice**, the Cayley parameterization enforces this to within floating-point noise, and standard tricks (exact skew-symmetrization, stable solves, occasional re-projection) keep that noise so small that the spectral norm of the finetuned weight truly remains the same as the pretrained one.

### How are the permutations between butterfly components chosen and are they learnable?
Question:
The construction refers to each $\tilde{B}(d, k)$ as a 'blockwise permutation' of $\tilde{B}(d, 2)$. Are these permutations fixed a priori by a standard butterfly schedule (like Cooley-Tukey radix-2), or is there any learnable choice over the permutation pattern? If fixed, could learnable permutations improve expressivity at the same parameter count?
Answer:
In the BOFT construction every \(\tilde B(d,k)\) is indeed “a block-wise permutation” of the \(2\times2\) building block \(\tilde B(d,2)\), but—and this is key—the pattern of those permutations is not something you learn during finetuning. Rather, it is hard-coded once and for all according to the standard radix-2 Cooley–Tukey butterfly schedule. Concretely, you fix a sequence of sparsity‐patterns (the familiar “butterfly network” connectivity) and then learn only the nonzero entries inside each little \(2\times2\) (or \(b\times b\)) orthogonal block via the Cayley transform.  

Why did the authors keep the permutations fixed, rather than trying to learn them? There are three practical reasons:

  1. **Discrete combinatorics**: A permutation matrix lies in the set of \(\{0,1\}\)-matrices with exactly one “1” per row and column. Learning those discrete choices directly would push you into a combinatorial optimization that is neither differentiable nor parameter‐efficient.  
  2. **Compute/parameter budget**: The magic of the butterfly factorization is that it connects every input to every output in just \(O(d\log d)\) nonzeros. If you allowed arbitrary permutations between stages, you would either destroy that \(O(d\log d)\) structure or have to pay for extra parameters (or both).  
  3. **Inductive bias**: The fixed Cooley–Tukey graph carries a strong bias—one that underlies FFT, Hadamard transforms, etc.—and that bias turns out to be beneficial for generalization.  

That said, nothing in principle prevents you from treating those stage‐wise shuffles as additional learnable parameters. In fact, in the “kaleidoscope” hierarchies (see Dao et al. 2019) one interleaves learned butterfly factors with fixed or learned transposes/permutions to asymptotically span the full orthogonal group. But to make that work you generally need more layers (or extra parameters) and you lose the simple \(O(d\log d)\) counts that make BOFT so attractive.  

So in BOFT as presented, the answer is: the inter-stage permutations are fixed once and for all by the classical radix-2 schedule, and only the small diagonal blocks within each butterfly component are learned. Exploring *learnable* shuffles is a perfectly reasonable research direction, but it was left outside the scope of this paper because it would sacrifice either clean differentiability, parameter/compute efficiency, or the very inductive bias that makes BOFT perform so well.

### Clarifying the notation: what does BOFT(m, b) mean when the text defines R(m, b) using b/2?
Question:
The paper writes: 'For convenience, we denote BOFT with $R(m, \tfrac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m, b$)'. This confuses me. Does 'b' in BOFT($m, b$) denote the $2b \times 2b$ block size, or the block dimension used in the block-diagonal baseline? Could someone restate the exact shapes of the elementary blocks and how that maps to the $b$ reported in experiments?
Answer:
In the heart of the BOFT construction there are two slightly different “block‐size” parameters floating around:

1.  **The internal sub-block size** $s$ used in the formal definition  
2.  **The more intuitive block-diagonal size** $b$ that you see reported in the experiments  

The authors first write everything in terms of an internal parameter $s$, defining

$$
\mathbf{R}(m,s) \;=\;\prod_{i=1}^m \;\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^s\bigl(d,\,2^{\,m-i+1}\bigr),
$$

where each factor $\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^s(d,k)$ is a block‐diagonal matrix whose scalar “butterfly factors” have been lifted to $s\times s$ blocks. Concretely, at the $i$th step you get blocks of size

$$
\bigl(2^{\,m-i+1}\cdot s\bigr)\;\times\;\bigl(2^{\,m-i+1}\cdot s\bigr).
$$

In particular, the smallest butterfly factor $\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^s(d,2)$ consists of $\tfrac{d}{2s}$ blocks of size $2s\times2s$.

---

To avoid carrying around this extra “/2” in the text, they then make the substitution

$$
s = \frac{b}{2}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)\;:=\;\mathbf{R}\!\bigl(m,\tfrac{b}{2}\bigr).
$$

Under this new name:

•  **BOFT(1, $b$)** is exactly the block‐diagonal OFT with blocks of size $b\times b$.  
•  **BOFT(1, $d$)** (i.e.\ $b=d$) recovers a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (the original OFT).  
•  **BOFT$\bigl(\log\frac{2d}{b},\,b\bigr)$** is the classic butterfly factorization that yields a *dense* $d\times d$ orthogonal.  

More generally, BOFT$(m,b)$ multiplies $m$ of these “block‐butterfly” factors whose block‐sizes sweep through

$$
2b,\;4b,\;8b,\;\dots,\;2^m b.
$$

So **when you see** BOFT$(m,b)$ **in the tables**, that $b$ is exactly the *block size* in the block‐diagonal baseline (and hence the size of the smallest $2\times2$ Cayley rotations once you unfold the internal half‐block notation).

### BOFT vs. Householder/CSR/Rotation factorizations for orthogonals: trade-offs?
Question:
One can parameterize any orthogonal as a product of $O(d)$ Householder reflections or Givens rotations, which leads to $O(d^2)$ parameters if we make them dense. How does BOFT compare in terms of parameter count, computational cost, and approximation quality to such classical factorizations when used for finetuning? Is there a principled reason BOFT's structured sparsity should regularize better than, say, a learned sequence of Givens rotations?
Answer:
In classical numerical linear algebra one can factor an arbitrary $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix $R$ either as the product of $d$ Householder reflections or as roughly $\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}$ Givens rotations.  Both of these factorizations are *exact* and have the attractive property that—once you have stored your reflectors or your sequence of rotations—you can represent *any* orthogonal matrix.  However they incur two costs:

 1.  **Parameter count**.  A single Householder reflector in $d$ dimensions has $d$ free parameters (it’s really a dense vector up to scale), so $d$ reflectors cost $\Theta(d^2)$.  A Givens rotation has 1 angle plus 2 indices, and you need on the order of $d^2$ of them to span the entire group, again $\Theta(d^2)$ real parameters.  
 2.  **Compute**.  Applying $d$ Householder reflections to a vector is $\Theta(d^2)$ work, and similarly a dense Givens‐rotation schedule costs $\Theta(d^2)$ arithmetic.

By contrast, BOFT trades *full* expressivity for a much smaller parameter footprint and cheaper application, by plumbing together $m$ *sparse* orthogonal factors in a *butterfly* topology.  Concretely:

 •  **BOFT parameter count**.  If you choose block size $b$ and depth $m$, BOFT uses on the order of $\tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m$ trainable parameters.  In particular, the common choice $b=2$, $m=\log_2 d$ gives only
$$
  \tfrac12(2-1)\,d\,\log_2 d \;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,d\log_2 d
$$
parameters, i.e.\ $\Theta(d\log d)$.  That is a $d/\log d$–fold reduction relative to the $\Theta(d^2)$ you’d pay with Householder or full Givens.

 •  **BOFT compute cost**.  Applying each of the $m$ butterfly layers to a $d$-vector costs $\Theta(d)$ work (each layer is a block-diagonal of $\tfrac d b$ small orthogonal blocks), so overall it’s $\Theta(d\,m)=\Theta(d\log d)$.  Again you save a factor of $d$ versus the $\Theta(d^2)$ cost of classical methods.

 •  **Approximation quality**.  BOFT does *not* live in the full orthogonal group unless you stack a *large* number (on the order of $d$) of butterfly blocks (and even then you need the so-called “kaleidoscope” trick of alternating forward and transpose butterflies).  But in practice one finds that with as few as $\Theta(\log d)$ butterfly factors you cover a very expressive, *multi-scale* submanifold of the orthogonal group.  In the paper’s simulations (Section 4) BOFT with $O(d\log d)$ parameters approximates random $512\times 512$ orthogonals far more accurately—per‐parameter—than simply using a block-diagonal structure, and it would similarly outperform any *unstructured* truncation of Householder or Givens that uses the same $O(d\log d)$ degrees of freedom.

Why should BOFT *regularize* better than “just” learning a handful of Householder vectors or Givens angles?  The key is that BOFT’s *sparsity pattern*—the butterfly graph—injects a *multi-scale mixing* inductive bias:

  •  **Structured connectivity**.  In a depth-$m$ butterfly you ensure that information from *every* input coordinate can reach *every* output coordinate in $\sim\log d$ hops, but you only pay for $O(d\log d)$ “edges.”  A naive truncation of reflectors doesn’t guarantee such uniform, scale-balanced connectivity.  
  •  **Classic transforms on the manifold**.  The butterfly topology is exactly the one that underlies the FFT, Hadamard, and many other fast transforms.  Hence BOFT can *easily* represent these highly structured rotations—offering a strong prior that data in vision and language often likes multi-scale, global mixing.  
  •  **Smoother interpolation**.  Starting from identity, each butterfly layer gently propagates information across blocks of size $b$, progressively weaving global correlations.  A handful of arbitrary reflectors or Givens rotations, by contrast, can swing the geometry around more chaotically and tend to overfit when you have only a tight parameter budget.

In short, if you have the *budget* to fit $\Theta(d^2)$ parameters (and the time to apply $\Theta(d^2)$ operations), nothing beats the *universal* expressivity of Householder or full‐Givens factorizations.  But once you insist on very *few* parameters—say $O(d\log d)$—BOFT delivers far cheaper application and, thanks to its fixed multi-scale sparsity pattern, a more *regularized* hypothesis class.  Empirically in the paper that translates into better approximation of random orthogonals per parameter and more stable, generalizable finetuning on large vision and language backbones.

### Application details: to which weight matrices is BOFT applied in transformers?
Question:
For LLMs and vision transformers, do you apply $R$ to all linear layers (attention $Q/K/V/O$, MLP projections), or only a subset? If applied to, say, $W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ per attention head, is $R$ shared across heads or per-head? How do you handle layers where $d \neq n$ (rectangular $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$)?
Answer:
In our experiments we treated BOFT exactly as a drop‐in replacement for the standard “LoRA‐style” adapters—you simply wrap each linear map in the Transformer with a left–multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $R$.  Concretely, suppose a transformer block has a linear layer  
$$
   z = (W^0)^\top x,\quad W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm out}},\; x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}.
$$  
In BOFT we replace that forward pass with  
$$
   z = \bigl(R\,W^0\bigr)^\top x,
$$  
where $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm in}}$ is our learned orthogonal “butterfly” factor.  A few clarifications follow:

1. Which layers get an $R$?  Exactly the same ones you would hook LoRA onto: every projection in the multi‐head attention (the Q, K, V and the output projection) and every fully‐connected (MLP) projection in the feed‐forward sub‐layers.  We do not touch LayerNorms or bias vectors.

2. Heads vs. a single $R$.  In practice most libraries implement Q (or K or V) as one big linear map of shape $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm model}\times(d_{\rm heads}\cdot d_{\rm head})}$.  We attach one $R$ of size $d_{\rm model}\times d_{\rm model}$ to that whole map, so the same orthogonal transform is shared across all heads.  We did not learn a separate $R$ for each head.

3. Rectangular weight matrices.  Nothing in BOFT forces $d_{\rm in}=d_{\rm out}$.  If $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm out}}$ with $d_{\rm in}\neq d_{\rm out}$, you still form  
$$
     R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm in}},\quad 
     W' = R\,W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm out}}.
$$  
In other words the butterfly‐parameterized $R$ always lives in the input dimension of that layer.  You learn a distinct $R$ for each linear layer, with its own input size $d_{\rm in}$, regardless of whether the map is square or rectangular.

Because BOFT is simply a left‐multiply by a structured orthogonal matrix, you can plug it into any linear projection that has an input dimension $d_{\rm in}$.  In our LLM and vision‐transformer experiments we did exactly this for all of the attention and MLP weight matrices (and nowhere else), always sharing one $R$ per projection and never needing the layer to be square.

### Training dynamics: does the non-commutativity of orthogonal factors matter for optimization?
Question:
Since $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)} \tilde{B}^b_{(d,j)} \neq \tilde{B}^b_{(d,j)} \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ in general, the order of components matters. Is the order fixed as in the canonical butterfly schedule? Have you observed sensitivity to permuting the order of the components, and could reordering during training help escape bad minima?
Answer:
In BOFT we in fact adopt the canonical Cooley–Tukey “butterfly” schedule and keep it fixed throughout training. Concretely, we always set
$$
\mathbf{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde B^b_{(d,i)}
$$
where $\tilde B^b_{(d,1)}$ is the block‐diagonal factor at scale $k = d$, then $\tilde B^b_{(d,2)}$ at $k = d/2$, and so on down to $k = 2$. Because these sparse orthogonal blocks do *not* commute in general,
$$
\tilde B^b_{(d,i)}\,\tilde B^b_{(d,j)} \neq \tilde B^b_{(d,j)}\,\tilde B^b_{(d,i)},
$$
any permutation of their order produces a *different* overall transform—and in effect a different hypothesis class.

We chose the standard descending‐scale schedule for two reasons. First, it is the one that guarantees the $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ connectivity and parameter counts that underlie our expressivity and efficiency theorems (see Theorem 1). Second, in our empirical studies we did not observe any training pathologies—no “bad” minima that would seem to require shuffling the factors to escape. In other words, with the fixed schedule BOFT already converges reliably and outperforms the block‐diagonal OFT baseline across vision, language, and diffusion‐model finetuning benchmarks.

That said, nothing in the mathematics forbids you from exploring alternative orders (or even learning a permutation schedule) as a way to inject additional flexibility. Permuting factors simply walks you through a different family of dense orthogonal matrices, so in principle one could imagine a meta‐algorithm that periodically reorders the $\tilde B^b_{(d,i)}$ or interleaves them in “kaleidoscope” fashion (as in the generalized hierarchy in Theorem 1). We have not experimented with such dynamic schedules, but it is indeed an intriguing direction for future work if one wants to further enlarge the hypothesis class or introduce novel escape‐mechanisms for optimization.

### Interpreting the 'weight interpolation' property in BOFT
Question:
The paper mentions an 'intriguing weight interpolation' due to factorization (Figure referenced). What exactly is being interpolated? Is it that intermediate products $R_t = \prod_{i=1}^t \tilde{B}_i$ provide a path on the orthogonal manifold from identity to the final $R$, akin to a homotopy? Does this have practical benefits such as smoother training or better early stopping?
Answer:
In BOFT the phrase “intriguing weight interpolation” refers to the fact that, since your final orthogonal update

$$
R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1
$$

is built as a product of $m$ orthogonal “butterfly” factors $B_i$, you automatically get $m+1$ orthogonal weight‐matrices

$$
R_0 = I,\quad
R_1 = B_1,\quad
R_2 = B_2 B_1,\;\dots,\;
R_m = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1 = R
$$

that smoothly march from the identity $I$ to the fully finetuned $R$.  In other words, by stopping the product after the first $t$ factors you sit on a discrete path

$$
R_t = \prod_{i=1}^t B_i
$$

inside the orthogonal group $O(d)$.  This is exactly a (discrete) homotopy from $I$ to your learned update $R$, and Figure 6 in the paper simply visualizes how the row‐patterns of the weight matrix evolve as $t$ increases.

As for practical payoffs, the paper presents this interpolation mainly as an elegant by‐product of the factorization rather than a module they explicitly exploit during training.  However, you can see two attractive corollaries: first, identity initialization plus Cayley‐based parameterization guarantees that you begin training exactly at $R_0 = I$, so your finetuning starts from the original pretrained weights; second, you *could* monitor or even truncate this chain—i.e.\ use only the first $t < m$ factors—to get a “light” intermediate model, which might serve as an early‐stopping or curriculum device.

That said, the authors do *not* report experiments where they actually employ partial‐factor inference or gradually unfreeze the $B_i$.  So while the interpolation *does* give you a built-in smooth path on the orthogonal manifold, its direct impact on training stability or early stopping remains to be explored in future work.

### How does Multiplicative Dropout interact with gradients through the Cayley transform?
Question:
When blocks are replaced with identity during dropout, their parameters are effectively masked. Do you stop gradients for those blocks for that step, or do you use a straight-through estimator? Also, does dropout-before-Cayley vs dropout-after-Cayley make a difference for maintaining orthogonality and gradient stability?
Answer:
In BOFT we introduce “multiplicative dropout” simply by randomly turning some of the sparse, Cayley‐parameterized blocks into the identity, but otherwise leave the Cayley machinery intact. Concretely, recall that in BOFT each butterfly component (or each $2b\times 2b$ block within it) is built as
$$
Q\;\mapsto\;R\;=\;(I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1}\qquad(Q^\top = -Q)\,,
$$
and that the overall orthogonal update is
$$
R_{\rm total}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^m R_i\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^m\;(I+Q_i)(I-Q_i)^{-1}\,.
$$

Multiplicative dropout then works as follows. At each step we sample a binary mask $D_i\in\{0,1\}$ for each block (or each butterfly component), and replace
$$
Q_i\;\longleftarrow\;D_i\,Q_i
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R_i\;=\;(I + D_i Q_i)\,(I - D_i Q_i)^{-1}\;=\;
\begin{cases}
(I+Q_i)(I-Q_i)^{-1},&D_i=1,\\
I,&D_i=0.
\end{cases}
$$
Equivalently we could have applied the mask after Cayley by
$$
R_i\;\longleftarrow\;D_i\,R_i\;+\;(1-D_i)\,I\,,
$$
but both views are algebraically identical and both preserve orthogonality (the product of orthogonal factors and identities is orthogonal).

As for gradients, no straight-through estimator is needed: because we simply zero‐out $Q_i$ (or $R_i$) when $D_i=0$, the back-propagated gradient into $Q_i$ is multiplied by $D_i$ and thus drops to zero on that step. In other words dropped blocks receive no gradient rather than having to invent a surrogate update.

Whether you mask *before* Cayley (i.e.\ $Q_i\leftarrow D_i\,Q_i$) or *after* Cayley (i.e.\ $R_i\leftarrow D_i\,R_i + (1-D_i)\,I$) is largely a matter of implementation taste—both keep $R_{\rm total}$ orthogonal, and in both cases the gradient for a dropped block is zeroed out. Masking on the $Q$ (skew) level is often a bit cleaner because you never compute a Cayley factor you then immediately discard, but neither choice hurts orthogonality or gradient stability.

### Can BOFT be composed with LoRA, and if so, in what order?
Question:
Since LoRA is additive $W = W^0 + BA$, and BOFT is multiplicative $W = RW^0$, is there a sensible combined parameterization, e.g., $W = R(W^0 + BA)$ or $W = RW^0 + BA$? Would this interfere with the spectral-norm preservation and angle-preservation properties that motivate BOFT?
Answer:
In principle there is nothing stopping you from “stacking’’ a BOFT‐style multiplicative update and a LoRA‐style additive update — e.g. you could write your finetuned weight in either of the two orders

1.  
   $$W = R\,(W^0 + BA)$$  

2.  
   $$W = R\,W^0 + BA$$  

where \(R\) is your BOFT orthogonal matrix, \(W^0\) is the frozen pretrained weight, and \(BA\) is the usual low-rank LoRA update. Which ordering you choose simply decides whether you (a) first add your low-rank delta to the pretrained weight and then rotate it, or (b) first rotate \(W^0\) and then add the low-rank delta in the “native’’ basis.  

However, one of the key theoretical virtues of OFT/BOFT is that by keeping  
$$
W = R\,W^0
$$
with \(R \in O(d)\), you exactly preserve all the singular values of \(W^0\) (so the spectral norm of the layer never drifts) and likewise preserve all pairwise angles between the columns of \(W^0\). Once you introduce an additive term \(BA \neq 0\), those guarantees no longer hold exactly.  In fact:

•  If you do  
   $$
   W = R\,(W^0 + BA),
   $$
   then you have  
   $$
   W = R\,\widetilde W
   $$  
   with  
   $$
   \widetilde W = W^0 + BA.
   $$
   You still preserve the spectrum of \(\widetilde W\), but \(\widetilde W\) itself is no longer the original pretrained weight, so the spectral norm of \(W^0\) can change once \(BA\) grows.  

•  If you do  
   $$
   W = R\,W^0 + BA,
   $$
   then the “pure’’ BOFT part \(R\,W^0\) still has the correct spectrum, but the final addition of \(BA\) spoils the spectrum of the total \(W\).  

In either case you lose the *exact* spectral‐norm and angle‐preservation that motivated OFT and BOFT. In practice, if the LoRA update \(BA\) is small in norm the distortion will be mild, so you might still reap some of BOFT’s stability benefits while getting the extra flexibility of a low‐rank additive term.  

If you really *must* have exact preservation of \(W^0\)’s singular values and continue to inject a low‐rank “LoRA‐style’’ change, you would need to fold that change into the orthogonal group itself. Concretely you could parametrize a small skew‐symmetric low‐rank \(Q\) (for instance by writing  
$$
Q = U\,V^T - V\,U^T
\quad\text{with}\quad
U, V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
$$
) and then let  
$$
\Delta R = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},
$$
so that \(\Delta R\in O(d)\) by the Cayley transform. You would then absorb that \(\Delta R\) into one of your BOFT butterfly factors (or treat it as an extra sparse factor) so that every update remains in \(O(d)\). In that way you keep *every* elementary update spectral‐norm preserving and angle‐preserving, at the cost of reinterpreting your “LoRA’’ step as a *multiplicative* low‐rank perturbation rather than an additive one.  

In summary, you *can* naively combine BOFT and LoRA in either order, but once you add \(BA\) the pure BOFT guarantees go away. To maintain the exact spectral/angle‐preservation you’d have to reparameterize the low-rank component *within* the orthogonal group (e.g.\ via a low-rank skew-symmetric \(Q\) and Cayley), which is a more involved but fully consistent way to get both low-rank efficiency and the \(O(d)\)-preservation properties.

### Does the butterfly inductive bias risk hurting tasks that need highly localized adaptations?
Question:
The paper argues butterfly structure provides a helpful inductive bias because it can express many classic transforms. But if a downstream task requires very localized changes (akin to a small subset of coordinates), could the global mixing enforced by butterfly cause over-smoothing? Is there empirical evidence on tasks where block-diagonal OFT outperforms BOFT due to locality?
Answer:
While it is true that the butterfly parameterization injects a more “global” mixing pattern than a purely block‐diagonal OFT (i.e. $OFT(1, b)$), we have two ways to see why it need not hurt—and in fact rarely seems to hurt in practice—tasks that only require very localized adaptation.

First, BOFT itself contains block‐diagonal OFT as an extreme special case. In the notation of the paper, BOFT $(m, b)$ with $m=1$ is exactly the block‐diagonal OFT with block size $b$. As you increase $m$ you introduce more cross‐block mixing—so if you really did want to restrict your finetuning to purely local subspaces you simply set $m=1$ and recover the exact locality‐only model.

Second, in all of the empirical studies reported in the paper (vision tasks like CIFAR‐100, ImageNet‐1k transfer, NLP tasks on GLUE, and text-to-image conditional generation), BOFT with moderate $m$ consistently outperforms its block‐diagonal cousin at the same or lower parameter count. In particular, the ablation in Figure 4 (the “parameter‐efficiency” plot) shows that for any fixed budget of trainable parameters, a small‐block, deeper butterfly (small $b$, larger $m$) fits even a random orthogonal target more closely than a single block‐diagonal layer ($m=1$). Concretely, $BOFT(9, 2)$ yields lower approximation error than $BOFT(1, 16)$, even though the former uses far fewer parameters. We do not observe a downstream task where $OFT(1, b)$ strictly beats a $BOFT(m, b')$ of comparable size.

Intuitively, the butterfly graph is a “multi‐scale” connectivity: at the first few factors you still have mostly local ($2\times 2$ or $b\times b$) rotations, and only as you compose $O(\log d)$ of them does information from all coordinates mix together. If your adaptation is truly confined to small coordinate‐blocks, then even BOFT with full depth only pays a small additional cost in those first few factors—later factors can simply remain near identity (and indeed they are initialized that way and often move very little). In the extreme case, you could even apply a “multiplicative dropout” on the deeper layers of the butterfly as suggested in §3.3 to force the model to stay local.

In short, there is no evidence in the paper of a task where block‐diagonal OFT outperforms BOFT of similar capacity. And because BOFT nests the block‐diagonal case, you retain a “pure locality” option exactly when you need it, but gain broad expressivity (and better parameter‐efficiency) when your task benefits from more global mixing.

### How does BOFT handle convolutional layers or weight tensors not easily flattened to d x n?
Question:
In CNNs or diffusion U-Nets, many weights are 4D tensors. Is BOFT applied after reshaping kernels to $d \times n$ matrices, and if so, along which dimension is the orthogonal $R$ applied? Does this reshaping choice affect the inductive bias or performance?
Answer:
In BOFT—as in its predecessor OFT—every “layer” that implements a linear map

$$
z = (W^0)^\top x
$$

is re-written as

$$
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
$$

where $R \in O(d)$ and $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Nothing in the derivation assumes $W^0$ was originally a fully-connected weight matrix; any linear operator (including a convolution) can be cast in this form by reshaping its kernel into a $d\times n$ matrix.

Concretely, suppose you have a convolutional kernel of shape

$$
(C_{\text{out}},\,C_{\text{in}},\,H,\,W).
$$

If you think of each sliding-window patch $x\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}}\cdot H\cdot W}$ being multiplied by the flattened kernel $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with $d=C_{\text{in}}\cdot H\cdot W$ and $n=C_{\text{out}}$, then BOFT simply inserts the orthogonal mixer $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ on the left:

$$
z = \bigl((R\,W^0)^\top x\bigr),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^d,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^n.
$$

In other words, you fold the spatial-$\times$-channel dimensions of the kernel into one “input” axis of length $d$ and let $R$ rotate that entire $d$-dimensional patch before it hits your original conv filters. Crucially, because the same $R$ is applied at every spatial location, translation-equivariance is preserved.

–––

**On alternative flattenings and inductive bias**  
Sometimes you may worry that mixing across $H\times W$ as well as $C_{\text{in}}$ breaks the usual locality inductive bias of a convolution. A simple workaround is to only mix the channel dimension and leave the spatial dimensions “untouched.” To do this you reshape

$$
W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}\times (C_{\text{in}}\cdot H\cdot W)}
$$

into

$$
W^{0\prime} \in \mathbb{R}^{(C_{\text{out}}\cdot H\cdot W)\times C_{\text{in}}},
$$

so that BOFT’s $R$ is of size $C_{\text{in}}\times C_{\text{in}}$ and acts only on channels, replicated across all spatial positions. Equivalently, this is just a block-diagonal orthogonal transform on the full flattened space, with one $C_{\text{in}}\times C_{\text{in}}$ block per $(H,W)$ location.

In practice the paper’s experiments on large CNNs and U-Nets (e.g.\ Stable Diffusion’s UNet) flatten the full patch ($d=C_{\text{in}}\cdot H\cdot W$) and apply BOFT exactly as written above. The multiscale mixing inherited from the butterfly factorization still respects convolutional equivariance (since $R$ is shared spatially) and yields strong generalization with no obvious degradation in performance. If your task really hinges on preserving strict locality you can dial back BOFT by choosing smaller “block-size” $b$ in BOFT$(m,b)$, which in the limit $b=C_{\text{in}}$ reverts to a purely channel-wise orthogonal transform.

–––

**Summary**  
1) Any 4D conv kernel can be seen as a $d\times n$ matrix by collapsing $(C_{\text{in}},H,W)$ to $d$. BOFT’s $R$ always multiplies on that “input” axis.  
2) Because $R$ is shared at every spatial location, you retain translation equivariance.  
3) If you worry about mixing spatial dims, you can instead collapse only $C_{\text{in}}$ into $d$ and leave $H\times W$ in the “output” axis; that forces $R$ to be channel-only and sharpens the convolutional bias.  
4) Empirically, the “full-patch” flattening has worked well in BOFT’s vision and diffusion experiments without any drop in accuracy.

### Why does the paper say block-diagonal OFT 'cannot approximate classic linear transforms'?
Question:
It is stated that block-diagonal orthogonals reduce expressiveness and cannot approximate classic transforms like DFT. Is there a formal argument that a block-diagonal orthogonal with block size $b \ll d$ cannot approximate a dense transform such as DFT in operator norm unless $b$ scales with $d$?
Answer:
In one sentence, the reason block‐diagonal OFT “cannot recover” something like the DFT is that a single block‐diagonal orthogonal only mixes coordinates within each block, whereas a full DFT has “mass’’ (i.e.\ nonzero entries) spread evenly across all $d$ coordinates. You can make this precise in operator norm as follows.

Let
$$
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d},\quad
F_{i,j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,e^{-2\pi\,\mathrm{i}\,(i-1)(j-1)/d}
$$
be the unitary DFT matrix. Its $j$th column $F e_j$ has unit norm and each of its $d$ entries has magnitude $1/\sqrt{d}$. Now let
$$
R = \mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)\;\in O(d)
$$
be any real orthogonal whose blocks $R_\ell\in O(b)$ have size $b\ll d$. Each column of $R$ is supported on exactly one of those blocks, so no column of $R$ can spread its mass over more than $b$ coordinates.

To lower‐bound the operator‐norm distance $\|R - F\|_2$, observe that
$$
\|R - F\|_2
\;\ge\;
\max_{1\le j\le d}\|(R - F)\,e_j\|_2
\;=\;
\max_j\bigl\|R e_j - F e_j\bigr\|_2.
$$
Fix any column index $j$. The vector $F e_j$ has squared norm
$$
\|F e_j\|_2^2 \;=\;\sum_{i=1}^d\bigl|F_{i,j}\bigr|^2
\;=\;
d\cdot\frac{1}{d}
\;=\;1,
$$
and its projection onto the coordinates of the single block that $R e_j$ can occupy has squared norm
$$
\Bigl\|P_{\mathrm{block}}\,F e_j\Bigr\|_2^2
\;=\;
\frac{b}{d},
$$
because exactly $b$ of the $1/\sqrt{d}$‐entries lie in that block. Since $R e_j$ is a unit vector supported inside that block, the best choice is to align $R e_j$ with that projection. In that case the only remaining error is the component of $F e_j$ outside the block, whose squared norm is
$$
\bigl\|F e_j - R e_j\bigr\|_2^2
\;=\;
1 \;-\;\frac{b}{d}.
$$
Hence for every block‐diagonal $R$ with block‐size $b$,
$$
\|R - F\|_2
\;\ge\;
\sqrt{\,1 - \tfrac{b}{d}\,}.
$$
Unless $b$ grows linearly with $d$, this gap stays bounded away from zero. In other words, a block‐diagonal orthogonal of size $(b\ll d)$ cannot approximate the DFT (or any similarly “fully mixing’’ unitary) to small operator‐norm error.

Viewed through the paper’s “information transmission’’ lens, a single block‐diagonal step only connects nodes within each block, so information cannot flow from one block to another. The DFT’s dense connectivity demands that every input coordinate influence every output coordinate, and you only get that once your block‐size—and hence cross‐block connectivity—scales up to the full dimension $d$. That is why the paper says “block‐diagonal orthogonals reduce expressiveness and cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as DFT.”

### What is meant by 'minimum free edges' in the information transmission criteria?
Question:
The desiderata include 'dense connectivity' and 'minimum free edges'. Could someone formalize the optimization problem implied here? Is there a precise graph-theoretic measure of free edges tied to the number of independent parameters under orthogonality constraints, and is the butterfly topology known to be near-optimal for this measure?
Answer:
In the paper’s “information‐transmission” picture, you think of factorizing a dense orthogonal matrix  
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
\]  
as routing “information’’ from $d$ source nodes (the columns of $R$) through $m$ intermediate layers to $d$ sink nodes.  Each sparse factor $B_i$ induces a bipartite graph between layer $i-1$ and layer $i$: a nonzero entry $(B_i)_{jk}\neq 0$ is a directed edge from node $k$ in layer $i-1$ to node $j$ in layer $i$.  

Dense connectivity then simply means that in the overall layered graph, every source node can reach every sink node by some path.  But orthogonality of each $B_i$ forces each layer‐to‐layer graph to have at least $d$ edges (indeed a full‐rank $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix must contain a permutation of $\pm 1$ in each row and column, which you cannot “train’’ away).  The paper calls those $d$ edges necessary—they aren’t “free’’ parameters—but any extra nonzero entries in $B_i$ above that minimum are free edges, i.e.\ trainable parameters.  

Formally, let  
\[
E_i = \bigl\{(k\to j)\colon (B_i)_{jk}\neq 0\bigr\}, 
\qquad
F_i = \lvert E_i\rvert - d
\]  
so that $\lvert E_i\rvert \ge d$ by orthogonality and $F_i$ counts the additional “free’’ edges in layer $i$.  Then the minimum‐free‐edges desideratum is simply  
\[
\min_{\,\{B_i\}\subset O(d)} \sum_{i=1}^m F_i
\quad\text{subject to dense connectivity from layer $0$ to layer $m$.}
\]  
Equivalently one can write
\[
\min_{\,E_1,\dots,E_m}\sum_i\bigl(\lvert E_i\rvert - d\bigr)
\quad\text{s.t.\ each $E_i$ admits an orthogonal $B_i$ and the overall graph is strongly complete.}
\]

Viewed this way, the problem is akin to designing a directed, layered network that multicasts $d$ symbols from inputs to outputs using as few extra links as possible.  In classical network‐coding and expander‐graph theory one shows that to achieve full $d\to d$ connectivity in $m$ hops requires at least on the order of $d\log d$ edges (beyond the $d$ per hop), and that the butterfly network realizes this bound up to constant factors.  Concretely, if you choose $m\approx\log d$ layers and connect them in the radix‐2 Cooley–Tukey “butterfly” pattern, then you only need $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ total nonzeros across all $B_i$, so $\sum_i F_i=\mathcal O(d\log d)$.  

No smaller asymptotic construction is known for layered orthogonal factorizations, and finding the absolute minimum pattern of free edges is a combinatorial problem (in fact NP‐hard in general if you treat zero‐pattern design as decision variables).  Thus the butterfly topology is effectively near‐optimal for the “minimum free edges” objective in the paper’s sense: it guarantees dense end‐to‐end connectivity while incurring only $\Theta(d\log d)$ trainable parameters, compared to the $\Theta(d^2)$ of a single dense orthogonal or the $\Theta(d)$ of a trivial block‐diagonal (which fails to be dense).
