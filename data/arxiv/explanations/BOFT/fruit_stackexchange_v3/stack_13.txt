### Matching BOFT and LoRA under a fixed parameter budget: a concrete example
Question:
Suppose we finetune a layer with $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{4096\times 4096}$. LoRA with rank $r$ adds $4096\cdot r + r\cdot 4096 \approx 8192r$ parameters. For BOFT($m,b$), the paper says the budget is $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$. If I want to match a LoRA budget of, say, $r=8$ (about $65{,}536$ params), what $(m,b)$ choices would be comparable, and how does one choose $m$ to ensure $\mathbf{R}$ is dense or near-dense?
Answer:
Short answer: with $d = 4096$ the <|endoftext|> parameter budget
formula in the paper gives
$\text{params} = \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m = 2048\cdot(b-1)\cdot m$.
Equating this to <|endoftext|> with $r = 8$ ($\approx 65{,}536$ params) gives
$(b-1)\cdot m = 32$. So any integer pair $(m,b)$ satisfying $(b-1)\cdot m = 32$ matches the <|endoftext|> budget. Examples are $(m,b) = (32,2), (16,3), (8,5), (4,9), (2,17), (1,33)$. In practice you should prefer small $b$ and larger $m$ (for near-dense / dense $R$) and pick $b$ that fits the <|endoftext|> block requirements (usually even / powers-of-two blocks are used).

Why pick $m$ to ensure $R$ is dense? The paper states that <|endoftext|> produces a dense <|endoftext|> matrix when you take enough butterfly layers: specifically the “dense” regime is reached when
$m \approx \log_2(2d/b)$.
With $d = 4096$ this becomes
$m_{\text{dense}} = \log_2(8192/b) = 13 - \log_2(b)$.
So for common block sizes:
- $b = 2 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 12$,
- $b = 4 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 11$,
- $b = 8 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = <|endoftext|>
- $b = 16 \Rightarrow m_{\text{dense}} = 9$.

Interpretation and practical guidance: density of the final $R$ is controlled by $m$ (number of butterfly components): larger $m$ lets information flow across more levels and so the product of <|endoftext|> factors becomes dense. If you want a truly dense $R$ you should choose $m \ge m_{\text{dense}}$. Given the budget constraint $(b-1)\cdot m = 32$, the only choice that both matches the budget and meets typical $m_{\text{dense}}$ values is to pick a small block size ($b = 2$ is the simplest and commonly used) and a relatively large $m$. Concretely $(m,b) = (32,2)$ matches the <|endoftext|> budget and is well above $m_{\text{dense}} = 12$, so $R$ will be dense (but with higher runtime cost since you apply many factors). If you reduce $m$ to save compute <|endoftext|> $(m,b) = (4,9)$ or $(8,5)$), you still match the parameter budget but may not reach the dense regime unless the chosen $m \ge 13 - \log_2(b)$.

Trade-offs to keep in mind: smaller $b$ and larger $m$ give better <|endoftext|> (but more forward/backward <|endoftext|> steps), larger $b$ and smaller $m$ give fewer factor <|endoftext|> but induce more block structure (less global mixing). Choose according to whether you <|endoftext|> matching LoRA’s parameter count while getting a near-dense $R$ (pick $b$ small, $m$ large; <|endoftext|> $b = 2$, $m = 32$) or want fewer factor layers (pick larger $b$ but accept more <|endoftext|> / structured <|endoftext|> bias).
