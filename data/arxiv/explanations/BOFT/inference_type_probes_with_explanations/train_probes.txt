In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors note that increasing expressiveness without appropriate regularity during finetuning commonly leads to overfitting. This follows from their core theme that finetuning must balance expressivity and inductive bias by restricting the hypothesis class: while BOFT can be made highly expressive—up to representing the entire orthogonal group via kaleidoscope-style compositions $\\bm{R}^G$—the authors emphasize that “better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance,” noting that full finetuning (maximal expressiveness) often underperforms due to poor generalization. BOFT deliberately constrains $\\bm{R}$ to structured subsets of $\\mathsf{O}(d)$ (e.g., butterfly-composed orthogonals) that both preserve desirable spectral properties (left-multiplying by orthogonal $\\bm{R}$ keeps the spectral norm of $\\bm{W}^0$ unchanged) and impose inductive biases aligned with classic fast transforms (Fourier, Hadamard), thereby regularizing solutions and reducing overfitting relative to unconstrained parameterizations. Their identity initialization and multiplicative dropout further illustrate the need for regularity to keep updates close to the pretrained model and to avoid over-parameterized adaptations; conversely, increasing $m$ and $b$ to enlarge the parameter space without such structural priors would expand the function class, weaken these biases, and make it easier for the model to fit idiosyncrasies of the finetuning data, i.e., to overfit.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', replacing BOFT's structured product of sparse orthogonal factors with an unstructured dense orthogonal matrix while keeping the same number of effective parameters is likely to result in worse generalization. This follows from the paper’s central argument that BOFT constrains the finetuning map to a structured subset of the orthogonal group by parameterizing the update as a product of orthogonal butterfly components, i.e., \(\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\), which uses only \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm=O(d\log d)\) effective parameters yet preserves dense connectivity and yields a smooth interpolation between \(\bm{I}_d\) and the full orthogonal group; this structure recovers many classic fast linear transforms and thus induces a beneficial inductive bias that constrains the hypothesis class and mitigates overfitting, whereas allocating the same \(O(d\log d)\) degrees of freedom to an “unstructured” dense \(\bm{R}\in O(d)\) necessarily selects an irregular subset of the \(O(d^2)\) orthogonal degrees of freedom without these priors, enlarging effective search complexity and reducing alignment with known transforms. Under the paper’s information transmission view, the butterfly topology achieves dense end-to-end connectivity with the minimum number of “free” edges, while an unstructured construction with the same parameter budget must arbitrarily zero many entries to meet the parameter cap, producing sparsity patterns that neither approximate classic transforms nor provide the BOFT “smoother interpolation”; moreover, both BOFT and any orthogonal parameterization preserve the spectral norm of \(\bm{W}^0\), \(\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2=\|\bm{W}^0\|_2\), so the generalization advantage arises from BOFT’s structural regularization (identity initialization, factorwise multiplicative dropout, and constrained hypothesis class) rather than norm control. Consequently, for a fixed parameter budget, the paper argues that BOFT’s structured factorization offers a better expressivity–regularity trade-off than an unstructured dense parameterization, leading to improved generalization.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the proposed multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces a percentage of the butterfly components and diagonal blocks in each butterfly component with identity matrices $\bm{I}$, relying on the implicit property that replacing these blocks by $\bm{I}$ keeps the model valid, namely that this operation preserves orthogonality. This is sensible because each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is orthogonal by construction (via Cayley parameterization of $2b\times 2b$ blocks), and the components can be permuted into a block-diagonal form whose blocks are orthogonal; replacing some blocks with $\bm{I}$ leaves the component orthogonal since a block-diagonal matrix is orthogonal if and only if each block is orthogonal, and $\bm{I}$ is orthogonal because $\bm{I}^\top\bm{I}=\bm{I}$. Further, the full BOFT transform $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ remains orthogonal when some factors are set to $\bm{I}$, as the orthogonal group is closed under multiplication: if $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are orthogonal, then $(\bm{AB})^\top(\bm{AB})=\bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{B}^\top\bm{I}\bm{B}=\bm{I}$, and similarly $\bm{I}$ acts as a neutral orthogonal element. Therefore, the multiplicative Dropout’s identity replacements preserve the orthogonality constraints that underpin OFT/BOFT’s stability and generalization.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', for fixed $d$ and $m$, the effective number of trainable parameters is given by $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, so to minimize this quantity one should decrease $b$. This follows from the BOFT($m,b$) construction, where the orthogonal finetuning matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, each of which can be permuted into a block-diagonal form with $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks of size $2b\times 2b$; enforcing orthogonality within each block yields $(b-1)$ free parameters per $2b$-dimensional subspace (via $2\times 2$ rotations or Cayley transforms), giving $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ effective parameters per component and thus $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ overall. Because this count is linear in $(b-1)$ and $b\ge 2$, for fixed $d$ and $m$ it is minimized by choosing the smallest feasible block size, $b=2$, which also recovers the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ regime when $m=\log d$.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors explain that conceptually converting parameter complexity from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d\log d)$ changes the parameter growth class from quadratic to near-linear. This is sensible because BOFT replaces a single dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (which naively requires $\Theta(d^2)$ degrees of freedom) with a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse, orthogonal “butterfly components,” each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ trainable entries, yielding an overall count of $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective parameters; concretely, BOFT($m,b$) uses $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ parameters and the canonical butterfly setting $b=2,m=\log d$ gives $\Theta(d\log d)$, while full OFT uses $\Theta(d^2)$, and the information-transmission view clarifies why the butterfly topology achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges (trainable nonzeros), mirroring FFT’s trade of computation for space to transmit information globally via sparse factorization.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', the authors employ the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric; for the Cayley parameterization to be valid, the implicit mathematical condition on $\bm{Q}$ that must hold is that $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ is invertible. This condition is required because the mapping explicitly uses $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, and it also underpins the orthogonality guarantee: when $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$ and $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible, one can verify $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ via $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-\top}(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}=\bm{I}$ because $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$. Moreover, with $\bm{Q}$ real skew-symmetric, its eigenvalues are purely imaginary (or zero), so $1$ is never an eigenvalue of $\bm{Q}$ and hence $\det(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\neq 0$, which makes the condition generically satisfied; the paper’s identity initialization ($\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$) trivially meets it since $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}=\bm{I}$.

In the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', if BOFT's sparse factors were not constrained to be orthogonal, the stability benefit tied to SVD that would likely disappear is spectral norm preservation. The paper emphasizes that OFT/BOFT update a pretrained layer by left-multiplying an orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R} \), so if \( \bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \), then the finetuned weights are \( \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top \), which leaves the singular values—and in particular the spectral norm—unchanged because \( \bm{R} \) is orthogonal (\( \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I} \)), a property the paper links to improved training stability and generalization; BOFT ensures this by parameterizing each butterfly component as orthogonal (via Cayley transforms and block-wise permutations). If the sparse factors composing \( \bm{R} \) were not orthogonal, the product would no longer be orthogonal, and left-multiplication could rescale directions in \( \bm{U} \), altering the largest singular value of \( \bm{W}^0 \) (i.e., the spectral norm), thereby forfeiting the cited stability benefit.

According to the paper 'Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization', to obtain a dense $\bm{R}$ using fewer sequential multiplications (smaller $m$) without abandoning parameter-efficiency, you should increase the block size. This follows from their BOFT construction, where a dense orthogonal matrix is achieved by BOFT$(m,b)$ when $m=\log\frac{2d}{b}$, so reducing $m$ necessarily requires increasing $b$ to maintain dense connectivity in the information-transmission view; moreover, the total number of effective trainable parameters scales as $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, which for moderate $b$ and reduced $m$ remains $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ or otherwise far below $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (the cost of a full dense orthogonal parameterization), hence increasing block size compensates for fewer mixing stages while retaining parameter-efficiency.
