\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: Modeling Preferences: Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce

# Bradley–Terry (BT) model for pairwise comparisons
When we collect human judgments in the form “given the same context or prompt $x$, human raters prefer completion $y_1$ to $y_2$,” we can model the probability of that binary preference with the Bradley–Terry (BT) model. The BT model assumes there is an underlying scalar “score” or reward function $r^*(x,y)$ that reflects how desirable a completion $y$ is in context $x$. Under the BT model the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ is a logistic function of the difference of these scores:

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
= \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}
= \sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]

where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function. Intuitively, the BT model says that only the difference in rewards matters: if $r^*(x,y_1)$ exceeds $r^*(x,y_2)$ by a large margin, $y_1$ is highly likely to be preferred; if they are equal, the preference probability is 50/50.

This pairwise probabilistic formulation is appealing because it reduces preference judgments to a familiar binary choice model. It cleanly connects preference probabilities to a scalar latent utility, and this connection is differentiable and convenient for statistical estimation. In practice, when applying BT to language-model outputs, $r^*(x,y)$ is not observed; instead we learn a parametric approximation $r_\phi(x,y)$ from a dataset of comparisons, as described below.

# Plackett–Luce (PL) for listwise rankings
The Plackett–Luce (PL) family generalizes BT from pairwise comparisons to full or partial rankings over many alternatives. Under PL, the probability of any ranking over a list of completions depends on the same underlying scores $r(x,y)$, but in a sequential “choice” factorization: the probability that one particular item is ranked first is proportional to its exponentiated score, then conditioned on that choice the second is chosen from the remaining items proportionally to their exponentiated scores, and so on. For the special case of two items, PL reduces exactly to the BT formula above.

PL models therefore keep the same basic structure as BT — human ranking data is modeled by exponentiated scores that compete multiplicatively in probabilities — but they allow use of richer annotation types (e.g., ranked lists) without fundamentally changing the dependence on the latent reward function. This compatibility is why PL is often mentioned as a natural extension of BT in preference-learning work: if multiple completions are ranked, PL is the appropriate likelihood model, and if only pairwise preferences are available, BT is a convenient instance.

# Logistic likelihood and binary cross-entropy interpretation
Estimating a parametric reward model from a dataset of pairwise preferences is naturally cast as a maximum likelihood problem under the BT likelihood. Let the offline dataset of human comparisons be

\[
\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N,
\]

where $y_w$ denotes the human-preferred (winner) completion and $y_l$ denotes the dispreferred (loser) completion for prompt $x$. Under the BT model with parametric reward $r_\phi(x,y)$, the negative log-likelihood (i.e., the loss to minimize) is

\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi; \mathcal{D})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\right].
\]

This is exactly the binary cross-entropy (logistic) loss applied to pairwise comparisons: the model predicts the probability that the first item is preferred via a sigmoid of reward differences, and the standard BCE loss measures mismatch between predicted probabilities and observed labels. Minimizing this loss fits $r_\phi$ so that higher-scored completions are more likely to match the observed human preferences.

Because the BT likelihood is a logistic function of reward differences, gradients are straightforward to compute and optimization is stable in the same ways as training other classification models with cross-entropy. In practice for language models, $r_\phi(x,y)$ is typically implemented by taking a transformer language model (often initialized from a supervised fine-tuned model) and adding a linear head that maps the final representation of the completion to a scalar reward; optimization then proceeds by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_R$ with gradient-based methods.

# Underspecification: rewards identifiable only up to an x-dependent constant
A crucial structural property of BT and more generally of PL models is that the preference probabilities depend only on differences of the reward function across alternatives for the same context $x$. This induces an inherent under-specification: adding any function of the context alone to the reward does not change the preference probabilities. Formally, define an equivalence relation on reward functions by saying $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if there exists a function $f$ of the context $x$ such that

\[
r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x) \quad\text{for all }(x,y).
\]

Because the BT probability depends on $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, substituting $r'(x,y)$ cancels the added $f(x)$ and yields identical preference probabilities. Thus Lemma: under BT (and PL) two reward functions in the same equivalence class produce the same preference distribution. Equivalently, preference data identify a reward only up to an additive function of $x$.

This under-specification has two immediate implications. First, attempting to recover a unique scalar reward surface $r^*(x,y)$ from preference data alone is impossible without extra constraints; you can only recover a representative of the equivalence class. Second, when using reward estimates inside downstream optimization (for example, to produce a policy that maximizes reward subject to constraints), the particular representative matters unless the downstream use also depends only on reward differences. Many RLHF pipelines exploit that the constrained optimal policy depends effectively only on differences in reward or on a projected representative; the paper shows how to pick a canonical representative that makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

A common practical approach to mitigate variance and make reward outputs more interpretable is to normalize rewards, for example by subtracting a baseline so that rewards have mean zero over the data conditioned on $x$. Such normalizations do not change the modeled preferences (they correspond to choosing a particular $f(x)$) but can reduce variance when rewards are used as learning signals. The paper mentions normalizing rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_\phi(x,y)]=0$ for all $x$ as one practical choice.

# Fitting a reward model by maximum likelihood
Given the BT (or PL) likelihood and the underspecification described above, the standard pipeline fits a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed comparisons, i.e., by minimizing the negative log-likelihood $\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi;\mathcal{D})$ written above. In practice several implementation details matter:

First, model initialization. Reward models are often initialized from a supervised-fine-tuned language model that already generates reasonable completions. Concretely, a shared transformer backbone trained for next-token prediction or supervised fine-tuning can be reused, with a new scalar head trained on top to predict rewards. This initialization helps the reward model generalize across the high-dimensional space of possible completions.

Second, numerical stability and variance. Because the loss depends on differences of scalar outputs, it is common to normalize $r_\phi$ across examples (an $x$-dependent centering) to reduce signal variance during optimization. Without such normalization the magnitude of $r_\phi$ can grow and harm subsequent use of the reward.

Third, identifiability constraints. Since rewards are identifiable only up to an additive $f(x)$, practitioners often impose a constraint (explicitly or implicitly) to pick a representative: for example, fixing the mean reward under some baseline distribution to zero, or enforcing the partition-function-like normalization used in certain reparameterizations. These constraints do not change the likelihood but they pick a canonical member of the equivalence class for downstream tasks.

Finally, once $r_\phi$ is estimated by MLE under BT/PL, it is commonly used in a second stage to train a policy (a language model) to maximize expected reward subject to a KL or other constraint to a reference policy. That two-stage pipeline (fit reward, then optimize a policy with RL) is standard in RLHF. The paper you are studying observes that, because of the BT/PL structure and the under-specification property, one can reparameterize the reward in terms of an optimal policy so that the whole two-stage procedure can be collapsed into a single-stage maximum-likelihood fit of a policy — avoiding an explicit RL loop — while remaining equivalent under the same preference model assumptions.

Summary
The Bradley–Terry model and its generalization, the Plackett–Luce family, are natural probabilistic models for pairwise and listwise human preference data. Their likelihoods are logistic functions of differences in latent rewards, which makes maximum-likelihood estimation equivalent to minimizing a binary cross-entropy loss on pairwise data. A key structural consequence is under-specification: reward functions are only identified up to an additive function of the context $x$. In applied pipelines this is handled by choosing a representative (for example by normalization) and then using the learned reward in downstream policy optimization. The paper demonstrates that, exploiting these structural facts, one can directly parameterize and fit a policy that implicitly defines a canonical reward representative, enabling a simpler, RL-free route from preferences to an aligned language model.
