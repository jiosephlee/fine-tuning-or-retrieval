\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 11: Evaluation Methodology and Generalization

# Reward–KL frontier as objective-grounded evaluation
When measuring how well a preference-learning algorithm performs, it is important to evaluate it with respect to the actual objective it is intended to optimize. For RLHF-style setups the canonical optimization target combines two competing desiderata: maximize expected reward under the learned or true reward function, and constrain the policy not to deviate too far from a reference policy. This is commonly formalized as the KL-constrained reward maximization objective

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\beta$ controls the strength of the penalty. Reporting a single scalar (for example, only expected reward) ignores the degree of policy change; a policy that attains high reward only by deviating dramatically from the reference may be less desirable because it can enter regions where the reward model is unreliable, collapse diversity, or produce degenerate generations.

The reward–KL frontier provides a two-dimensional, objective-grounded evaluation that directly reflects this tradeoff. For a family of trained policies obtained by varying a conservativeness hyperparameter (e.g., different target KLs for PPO or different values of $\beta$ for DPO), we plot their achieved expected reward versus their sequence-level KL relative to the reference policy. The sequence-level KL is computed by summing per-token KLs over generated sequences, yielding a single divergence number per generation that matches the constrained objective used during training.

Evaluating algorithms via this frontier has two key benefits. First, it reveals which methods attain higher reward for a given allowable policy deviation. A method whose frontier dominates another achieves higher (or equal) reward at every KL level and is therefore strictly preferable under the RLHF objective. Second, it exposes inefficiencies: methods that reach similar rewards but at much higher KL are less sample- and compute-efficient or less stable in practice, even if they achieve comparable task metrics. In the experiments described in the paper, DPO produced a frontier that strictly dominated PPO and even PPO with access to ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT) in the controlled sentiment setting: DPO achieved higher reward while maintaining lower KL across operating points, demonstrating that it optimizes the intended constrained objective more efficiently and stably than the compared actor-critic approach.

# Win-rate against references; temperature sensitivity analyses
In many practical tasks (summarization, dialogue), the ground-truth scoring function is not available, and human judgments are the ultimate measure of quality. A common operational evaluation in these settings is a pairwise win-rate against a reference policy: for each evaluation prompt, generate a completion from the model under test and compare it to a reference completion (often the preferred completion in a held-out dataset). Counting how often the model's completion is judged better yields a win-rate that reflects relative quality under human (or proxy) judgment.

The win-rate metric is particularly useful because it ties directly to the preference data collected for training: the goal of preference optimization is to produce outputs that would be preferred by humans (or a reasonable proxy) to baseline outputs. In the paper's experiments, win-rate against reference completions was used for both Reddit TL;DR summarization and the Anthropic Helpful & Harmless single-turn dialogue dataset, with GPT-4 acting as the evaluator. Win-rate allows direct comparison between methods like SFT, Preferred-FT, PPO, Best-of-N baselines, and DPO.

An important, practical consideration when reporting win-rates is sampling temperature. Sampling temperature affects the randomness and diversity of generated outputs: lower temperatures reduce randomness and concentrate mass on higher-probability tokens, while higher temperatures increase diversity. In the experiments, completions were sampled at a range of temperatures (e.g., 0.0 to 1.0), and win-rates were reported across temperatures to reveal robustness. DPO was found to be more robust to temperature changes than PPO in the Reddit TL;DR experiments: while PPO's performance degraded at higher temperatures and could revert to base-model quality, DPO maintained higher win-rates across temperatures and achieved higher maximum win-rate at the best temperatures. Reporting win-rates as a function of temperature therefore gives a fuller picture of practical behavior under various decoding settings and helps identify how sensitive a fine-tuned policy is to sampling procedures at test time.

# GPT-4 as an automated evaluator: prompt design and limitations
Large LLMs like GPT-4 can be used as automated judges to score pairwise comparisons between candidate outputs. The paper used GPT-4 to estimate win-rates in place of—or to scale beyond—human evaluation, and devoted care to prompt design because the phrasing of the evaluation prompt materially affects the model's judgments. Two example prompts used in the paper were:

- GPT-4 (S): a simple prompt that asks which summary better captures the important information in the post.
- GPT-4 (C): a concise prompt that asks which summary better captures the important information while preferring concision as well.

These prompt variants reflect different evaluation emphases (importance vs. concision), and the choice between them changes win-rate outcomes because GPT-4 may prefer longer, more repetitive summaries when only asked about informativeness, and prefer shorter summaries when concision is explicitly requested.

There are several limitations inherent in using GPT-4 as an evaluator. First, automated evaluators are themselves models trained on large corpora and have their own biases; their preferences do not perfectly match human raters and will be sensitive to prompt wording. Second, the evaluation distribution matters: a GPT-4 prompt tuned for forum posts may not transfer exactly to news articles, even if the task is nominally the same. Third, automated evaluation can over-reward certain surface traits (length, repetitive phrasing) unless prompts are carefully constructed to penalize them. Despite these caveats, the paper finds that GPT-4 can be a practical, scalable proxy when validated appropriately against human judgments.

# Human study: agreement with GPT-4 vs. inter-rater reliability
Because automated evaluators are imperfect, validating them with human judgments is essential. The paper carried out a human study to measure how often GPT-4's pairwise judgments agree with human raters and to compare that agreement to inter-human agreement. The human evaluation used the TL;DR summarization comparisons and the two GPT-4 prompt variants (S and C). The study design included choosing representative method pairs at different quality levels (e.g., DPO high-performing vs. PPO low-performing, and an intermediate case) and collecting human judgments for the same pairs.

The findings were that GPT-4's agreement with humans was comparable to, and in some cases higher than, inter-human agreement. That is, the proportion of times GPT-4 picked the same winner as a human rater was similar to the proportion of times two human raters agreed with each other. This suggests that GPT-4 can be a reasonable proxy for human judgments, provided the prompt is carefully chosen. Empirically, the GPT-4 (C) prompt (the concise prompt) tended to align better with human preferences in these experiments and thus was used for the main automatic evaluations.

It is important to note practical limitations of the human study described in the paper: due to limited rater availability, multiple human judgments were collected only for selected comparisons, not exhaustively for all runs. Nonetheless, validating GPT-4 against human judgments and comparing that agreement to inter-rater reliability is a critical step before trusting automated win-rate measurements.

# OOD evaluation: TL;DR-trained policies on CNN/DailyMail
An important question for any policy learned from preferences is how well it generalizes to inputs drawn from a different distribution than the one used for training. The paper examined this by evaluating policies trained on Reddit TL;DR summarization preferences on the CNN/DailyMail news summarization dataset, a substantially different input distribution, using the same GPT-4 (C) prompt to judge win-rates against ground-truth summaries.

This out-of-distribution (OOD) experiment revealed that the DPO-trained policy generalized better to the CNN/DailyMail dataset than the PPO-trained policy learned from the Reddit preferences. Specifically, under the same sampling temperatures that were optimal on Reddit, DPO produced higher win-rates on the news-domain data than PPO. This provides initial evidence that DPO's direct policy optimization from preferences can generalize at least as well as actor-critic RLHF methods, despite DPO not using additional unlabeled prompts from the Reddit training distribution that PPO used.

While promising, the OOD evaluation in the paper is presented as initial evidence rather than a comprehensive generalization claim. DPO's favorable behavior in this experiment suggests that the method's implicit reward parameterization and stable optimization can produce policies that are not narrowly overfit to the prompts seen during preference collection, but broader investigations across more domain shifts and scales are needed to fully characterize generalization.

# Interpreting results and threats to validity
Interpreting experimental results from preference-learning evaluations requires careful attention to several potential threats to validity. First, the choice of evaluator—human raters versus an automated model like GPT-4—and the specific prompt used for automated evaluation can shift apparent performance rankings between models. The paper highlights this by showing that GPT-4's judgments vary with the prompt and validating the automated judgments against human raters; nevertheless, sensitivity to prompt design remains a threat.

Second, win-rate comparisons against reference completions depend on the quality and origin of the reference. If the reference completions in the test set were produced under different generation or sampling regimes than those used to collect training preferences, then win-rates may reflect mismatches in generation style rather than objective quality. In the paper, care is taken to use the preferred completions from held-out datasets as references and to initialize reference models so that distribution shifts are mitigated, but users should be aware of this subtlety.

Third, the OOD experiments provide encouraging signs but are limited in scope. Generalization claims based on a small number of cross-dataset evaluations should be considered preliminary. The paper itself frames the CNN/DailyMail experiment as initial evidence and points to the need for wider study of distribution shift and self-labeling strategies.

Fourth, comparison baselines can introduce confounding factors. For instance, Best-of-N methods decouple reward-model quality from policy optimization by returning the best-scoring sample among many at test time; they can be computationally impractical yet strong in absolute quality. PPO implementations require substantial hyperparameter tuning and variance reduction techniques to perform well; differences in implementation and tuning effort can affect conclusions about relative method performance. The paper addresses this by comparing across a suite of baselines (Preferred-FT, Unlikelihood, PPO with learned rewards, PPO-GT, Best-of-N) and by noting practical complexities like PPO's instability.

Finally, the datasets and model scales used in experiments (models up to 6B parameters, specific summarization and dialogue datasets) limit the immediate generality of results to larger architectures or other application domains. The paper acknowledges these limitations and frames DPO's empirical findings as strong evidence of practical viability while calling for future work to explore scaling and broader generalization.

In sum, the evaluation methodology in the paper combines objective-grounded frontiers, practical win-rate measurements with sensitivity analyses over sampling temperature, careful use of automated evaluators validated by human studies, and an initial OOD probe. Together these elements provide a balanced assessment of algorithmic performance while making explicit the limitations and assumptions behind the reported conclusions.
