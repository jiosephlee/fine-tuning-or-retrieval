\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 10: Experiments: Sentiment, Summarization, and Dialogue

# Controlled sentiment generation: reward–KL frontier comparisons
The controlled sentiment generation experiment provides a well-specified, reproducible environment to measure how well algorithms trade off reward maximization against staying close to a reference policy. In this setting, prompts $x$ are prefixes from the IMDb movie-review dataset and the objective is for the policy to produce continuations $y$ with positive sentiment. Importantly, because the experiment uses a pre-trained sentiment classifier as the ground-truth reward, we can compute both (1) the expected reward achieved by a policy using that oracle classifier and (2) the sequence-level KL divergence between the policy and the reference policy. This permits plotting a reward–KL frontier: for a family of trained policies produced by varying a conservativeness hyperparameter, we can visualize how much reward each algorithm gains for a given amount of deviation from the reference model.

All algorithms in the experiment were initialized from the same supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) model, which was obtained by fine-tuning GPT-2-large on training reviews from IMDb. To construct preference data, completions were sampled from the SFT model and labeled using the sentiment classifier so that each preference pair satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. The set of methods compared included DPO, PPO with a learned reward, PPO-GT (an oracle variant that has access to the ground-truth sentiment reward), Unlikelihood fine-tuning, Preferred-FT (supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions), and Best-of-$N$ baselines. For each algorithm we ran multiple training runs sweeping over a relevant hyperparameter that controls conservativeness (for PPO a target KL, for DPO the $\beta$ parameter, for Unlikelihood the unlikelihood coefficient $\alpha$, and random seeds for Preferred-FT). During training we evaluated policies periodically and computed average reward under the true reward function and the sequence-level KL with respect to the reference policy.

The empirical result is striking: the DPO-trained policies form a reward–KL frontier that strictly dominates the frontiers produced by the other methods in this setting. In other words, for any given KL budget (amount of allowed deviation from the reference), DPO achieves equal or higher ground-truth reward than PPO (even PPO-GT that uses the true sentiment reward during optimization). This indicates that although DPO and PPO are conceptually optimizing the same constrained objective (maximize expected reward subject to a KL penalty), DPO attains a more efficient trade-off in practice in this controlled setting. The paper attributes this improved efficiency to DPO’s direct, closed-form reparameterization of the optimal policy and the stable, low-variance training signal given by the binary cross-entropy objective, whereas actor-critic RL methods like PPO must fit a separate reward (and often a value baseline), sample from the policy in the loop, and manage high-variance gradients and delicate hyperparameter tuning.

Beyond the average frontier position, the analysis highlights that DPO’s per-example weighting (in the loss gradient, examples are weighted by $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$) prevents pathological updates that could arise from na\"ive likelihood ratios or unbounded push-away terms. The controlled sentiment experiment therefore demonstrates both the empirical effectiveness and a practical robustness advantage of DPO in a setting where ground-truth rewards are available for evaluation.

# Summarization on Reddit TL;DR: GPT-4 win rates, temperature robustness
The Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment evaluates DPO on real human preference data and uses an automated evaluator (GPT-4) as a proxy for human judgments. The task is standard: given a forum post $x$, generate a concise summary $y$ of the post’s main points. The preference dataset used for training comes from prior work where humans labeled pairwise comparisons of summaries sampled from an SFT model. For this experiment the SFT model is a GPT-J model fine-tuned on human-written forum-post summaries; DPO and PPO both fine-tune this same SFT checkpoint to permit a fair comparison.

Because human judgments are expensive, evaluation uses GPT-4 to compute win rates: for each test instance the candidate summary is compared against a reference completion (the test-set preferred completion) and GPT-4 is asked which summary is better. The experiment reports win rates across different sampling temperatures (including the greedy temperature 0.0). Temperature matters because generative policies produce varying quality and diversity depending on decoding temperature; it is therefore informative to compare algorithms across a temperature sweep.

DPO achieves a clear improvement over PPO on this task. At greedy sampling (temperature $0.0$) DPO attains approximately a $61\%$ win rate versus about $57\%$ for PPO at its best temperature. DPO also achieves a higher maximum win rate compared to the Best-of-$N$ baseline that scores many SFT samples under a learned reward. Importantly, DPO is more robust to sampling temperature: its win rate changes less across temperatures than PPO’s, whose performance degrades substantially at higher temperatures, sometimes falling to the performance level of the base SFT model. This robustness implies that DPO produces a learned policy whose sampling distribution is qualitatively better calibrated for multiple decoding regimes, which is a desirable property for practical deployment when one may vary decoding temperature to control creativity or determinism.

The experiment also validated the use of GPT-4 as an automated evaluator by conducting a separate human study. Two GPT-4 prompts (a simple prompt and a concise prompt) were used in the automatic evaluation; comparisons between GPT-4 judgments and human judgments showed that GPT-4’s preferred summaries correlate with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each other. The concise prompt tended to reflect human preferences most faithfully and was adopted for the main TL;DR evaluations. Taken together, these results show DPO’s ability to scale to real preference datasets and to improve summarization quality in a way that generalizes across decoding temperatures.

# Single-turn dialogue on Anthropic HH: setup without SFT, Preferred-FT reference
The single-turn dialogue experiment uses the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) dialogue dataset, which contains human interactions where each final pair of assistant responses was labeled with a human preference. In contrast to the Reddit summarization task, there is no publicly available SFT model pre-trained specifically for this dataset. To address this, the experimental pipeline constructs a reference model as follows: start from a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B language model, then perform supervised fine-tuning (Preferred-FT) on the chosen (preferred) completions in the preference dataset so the reference model assigns high likelihood to the preferred responses. This Preferred-FT model is used as the $\pi_\text{ref}$ required by DPO.

With the Preferred-FT reference in hand, DPO is applied using the existing preference labels to fine-tune the policy directly via the DPO cross-entropy loss. The empirical comparisons include DPO, Preferred-FT, zero- or few-shot prompted baselines (e.g., 2-shot Pythia-2.8B), PPO-trained models from available open-source RLHF implementations, and Best-of-$N$ baselines that sample many completions and pick the highest-scoring one under a learned reward model.

On this dialogue task, DPO is the only computationally efficient method that reliably improves over the Preferred-FT reference model. DPO matches or exceeds the performance of the Best-of-128 baseline while being much cheaper at inference time: Best-of-128 requires generating and scoring 128 completions per query, whereas DPO requires only a single sampled completion per query. The PPO model checked from an available implementation did not outperform the base Pythia-2.8B under any prompt or temperature found by the authors, which suggests PPO can be brittle or sensitive to tuning in this particular dataset and codebase. DPO converges relatively quickly to its best performance on this task, demonstrating both sample efficiency and stability during training.

This setup underscores a practical use-case of DPO: even when a purpose-built SFT model is not available, one can obtain a usable reference by supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions and then apply DPO to further refine the policy using the same preference dataset. The simplicity of this two-step pipeline—Preferred-FT to form $\pi_\text{ref}$ followed by DPO—contrasts with the heavier engineering and tuning typically required to get PPO-based RLHF to work well on such datasets.

# Baselines: SFT, Preferred-FT, Unlikelihood, PPO, PPO-GT, Best-of-N
The experiments compare DPO to a coherent set of baselines chosen to represent the pragmatic alternatives practitioners might use when learning from preferences.

- SFT (supervised fine-tuning) is the baseline reference model obtained by fine-tuning a pre-trained LM on human-written demonstrations (or on a dataset of target completions). SFT serves as the starting point for many RLHF pipelines and as a lower-bound baseline for preference-driven improvements.

- Preferred-FT is supervised fine-tuning on the human-preferred completions found in the preference dataset. Rather than optimizing a ranking objective, Preferred-FT simply trains the model to reproduce the chosen completion for each prompt. This is a common and simple approach when the preferred responses are available, and it is also used in this paper to construct $\pi_\text{ref}$ when a dedicated SFT checkpoint is absent.

- Unlikelihood training directly increases the probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. Concretely, it is implemented by maximizing $\log \pi(y_w\mid x)$ and minimizing $\alpha \log \pi(y_l\mid x)$ for some coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$. The paper reports that a na\"ive unlikelihood or probability-ratio style objective can cause model degeneration unless carefully weighted; this observation motivated DPO’s per-example weighting that stabilizes updates.

- PPO is the proximal policy optimization algorithm used in many prior RLHF works. In the standard pipeline PPO requires fitting a reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to the preference data (under the Bradley–Terry model), and then optimizing the policy to maximize expected reward with a KL constraint to the reference policy. PPO is computationally heavier because it requires sampling from the policy in the inner training loop, managing actor-critic bookkeeping, and tuning RL-specific hyperparameters.

- PPO-GT is an oracle variant of PPO that is given access to the true reward (available only in the controlled sentiment experiments). PPO-GT provides an upper bound of what PPO could achieve under perfect reward estimation, illustrating how much suboptimality comes from RL optimization versus reward learning.

- Best-of-$N$ is a strong but computationally expensive baseline: at inference time, sample $N$ completions from an SFT or Preferred-FT model and score them with a reward function (typically a learned reward model), returning the highest-scoring completion. Best-of-$N$ decouples the quality of the reward model from the stability of RL optimization, often producing high-quality outputs when $N$ is large. However, it requires generating and scoring many samples per query at inference time, which is impractical in many real-world settings.

Across the experiments, DPO typically matches or outperforms these baselines. In the controlled sentiment task DPO dominates PPO and even PPO-GT on the reward–KL frontier. In RLHF-style tasks where human labels are involved (Reddit TL;DR and Anthropic HH), DPO produces higher or comparable win rates to PPO and Best-of-$N$ while being substantially cheaper computationally at inference time than Best-of-$N$ and simpler to train than PPO.

# Compute and tuning effort: DPO vs. PPO
One of the paper’s central practical claims is that DPO substantially reduces computational complexity and hyperparameter tuning compared to PPO-based RLHF. There are several interrelated aspects to this claim that the experiments make concrete.

First, DPO eliminates the need for an explicit RL loop that samples from the policy during training. PPO requires generating rollouts, computing policy gradient estimates, fitting a value function baseline, and monitoring additional RL stability mechanisms (clipping, entropy bonuses, etc.). These steps are computationally expensive and sensitive to many hyperparameters (learning rates, clipping parameters, KL targets, trust-region sizes, reward normalization strategies, and more). By contrast, DPO reduces preference learning to a simple binary cross-entropy objective (Equation 8 in the paper), which can be implemented as standard supervised learning on pairs (preferred vs. dispreferred) with no policy sampling during training. This avoids the inner-loop sampling cost and reduces variance in gradient estimates.

Second, hyperparameter sensitivity is lower for DPO in practice. The main DPO hyperparameter is $\beta$, which controls the effective KL penalty in the underlying constrained objective. The experiments used only a small set of $\beta$ values (e.g., $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$) and report strong performance without extensive tuning. PPO, however, required sweeping target KLs, reward normalization strategies, and other RL-specific knobs to find stable runs, and even then the results were not uniformly better than DPO. The controlled-sentiment results showed that over a large suite of runs (22 different runs across those hyperparameters and seeds), DPO’s frontier was superior to PPE’s best outcomes.

Third, the inference-time cost is smaller for DPO compared to expensive baselines like Best-of-$N$. Best-of-$N$ requires evaluating many samples through the reward model at test time; in contrast, DPO produces a single policy that can be sampled once to produce a response. This translates to lower latency and lower compute cost per user query.

Finally, DPO’s stability during training reduces total compute required. Because it operates with a standard cross-entropy loss and does not require value function fitting or on-policy sampling, runs are less likely to fail catastrophically or require repeated restarts with different random seeds and hyperparameters. The empirical sections highlight that DPO converged quickly to good performance in the dialogue experiments, and that it was robust across decoding temperatures in summarization, suggesting that practitioners can expect fewer expensive reruns and less hyperparameter search when adopting DPO.

In sum, the experimental evidence presented in the paper supports the conclusion that DPO achieves competitive or superior policy quality with substantially lower computational and tuning overhead relative to PPO-based RLHF and to inference-heavy baselines like Best-of-$N$. This makes DPO an attractive, practical method for training language models from human preferences.
