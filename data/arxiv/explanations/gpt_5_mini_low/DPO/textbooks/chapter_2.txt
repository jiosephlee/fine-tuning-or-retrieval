Chapter 2: Background: Learning from Preferences and the RLHF Pipeline

# Three-phase RLHF: SFT → reward modeling → RL fine-tuning

A commonly used pipeline for steering large pre-trained language models toward desired behavior is known as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). The pipeline decomposes into three main phases that are typically executed sequentially.

The first phase is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Here, a large pre-trained language model is adapted to the downstream task domain (dialogue, summarization, code generation, etc.) by maximizing likelihood on a corpus of high-quality input–output examples. The resulting model, which we denote $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, serves as both a better initial policy for later optimization and a natural reference distribution for modeling how far a tuned model may safely deviate from the original behavior.

The second phase is reward modeling. Because collecting scalar reward labels for every possible model output is costly and noisy, practitioners instead collect pairwise or comparative judgments: for a given prompt $x$ the annotator chooses which of two candidate completions $(y_1,y_2)$ they prefer. These relative judgments are then used to fit a scalar reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ whose ordering of candidate completions should explain the human preferences. A standard statistical preference model used here is the Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) model, which expresses the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ as a logistic function of the difference in latent rewards. Concretely, if $r^*(x,y)$ denotes the (unknown) ground-truth scoring function underlying human preferences, the Bradley–Terry model assumes
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)} = \sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid. A parametric reward model $r_\phi$ is trained by maximum likelihood on a static dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ (here $y_w$ and $y_l$ are the human-preferred and dispreferred completions). The usual negative log-likelihood loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi,\mathcal{D})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr].
\]
Because the Plackett–Luce family depends only on reward differences, the reward is identifiable only up to adding a function of $x$; practitioners often normalize rewards (e.g., zero mean per input) to reduce variance.

The third phase is RL fine-tuning: using the learned reward function to directly optimize a policy that generates higher-reward completions. Due to the discrete nature of text generation, this optimization is performed with reinforcement learning algorithms. A widely used formulation places a KL-divergence penalty that constrains the optimized policy to remain close to a reference policy (often the SFT model). The RL objective for a candidate policy $\pi_\theta$ is typically written as
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ denotes the chosen reference model (usually $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$) and $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. In practice, the reward used inside this objective is sometimes augmented with the policy log-probability correction to produce a shaped scalar reward for per-token RL algorithms. Optimizing this objective is the primary role of RL in RLHF.

# Data formats: prompts, completions, pairwise preferences

The data used throughout the RLHF pipeline has a simple, consistent structure. Inputs (prompts) are denoted $x$ and can be partial dialogues, forum posts, article text, or other contextual information that conditions generation. A completion or candidate response is denoted $y$; in language models this is a sequence of tokens generated autoregressively.

Preference annotations are collected as pairwise comparisons: for a given prompt $x$, the annotator is shown two candidate completions $(y_1,y_2)$ and indicates which completion they prefer. Each annotation thus yields a triple $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one. A collected dataset of such triples is denoted $\mathcal{D}$. This offline dataset is the primary learning signal for reward modeling and, in the DPO view, for policy fitting as well.

When training a reward model, typical preprocessing draws pairs of model-generated completions from a sampling policy (often $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$) and submits those pairs to human labelers. When the reward model is learned, it is used to score arbitrary completions at training or evaluation time. Note that the distribution of sampled completions matters: if preference data come from a particular model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, then that reference model becomes an important object in downstream optimization and evaluation because it defines the distribution of candidates that humans judged.

# Notation for policies, rewards, and datasets

It is important to fix consistent notation because the RLHF pipeline mixes models and objectives. We summarize the main symbols used in the paper and in this chapter:

- x: a prompt or context (input) presented to the model.
- y: a completion or generated response (sequence of tokens). When required, the probability of a full sequence under a policy is written as $\pi(y\mid x)$.
- $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$: the supervised fine-tuned model obtained in the first phase; this is commonly used as the reference policy.
- $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$: the reference policy used in the KL-regularized RL objective. In practice, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ whenever available.
- $\pi_\theta$: a parametric policy (language model) with parameters $\theta$ that we optimize in the RL phase.
- $r^*(x,y)$: the (unknown) ground-truth latent reward function that would explain human preferences.
- $r_\phi(x,y)$: a parametric reward model with parameters $\phi$ trained on pairwise preference data.
- $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$: the static dataset of pairwise preferences (the training data for reward modeling and, in alternative methods, for direct policy fitting).
- $\beta$: the KL regularization coefficient in the RL objective; it sets the tradeoff between maximizing expected reward and remaining close to the reference policy.

Sequence-level KL divergence between a candidate policy $\pi$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for a given $x$ is written as
\[
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr].
\]
This sequence-level KL is commonly used to constrain whole-output behavior rather than per-token differences.

# Common RL algorithms in RLHF (REINFORCE, PPO) and practical issues

Because the RL fine-tuning objective involves expectations over discrete sequences, it is not straightforward to apply gradient-based optimization directly. Two commonly used approaches in the RLHF literature are policy-gradient methods like REINFORCE and more sophisticated actor-critic or proximal methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

REINFORCE is the simplest policy-gradient algorithm: it estimates the gradient of expected reward by sampling trajectories and weighting log-probability gradients by the empirical reward. While simple and unbiased, REINFORCE suffers from very high gradient variance in high-dimensional sequence spaces (like language), and therefore it is usually impractical without strong variance-reduction techniques or shaping.

PPO is an actor-critic family method designed to stabilize policy updates by clipping the policy ratio or otherwise constraining how far the updated policy can move from the current policy. In the RLHF context, PPO has been popular because it is more stable than naive policy gradient in practice and allows use of learned value baselines. Typical PPO-based RLHF pipelines first fit a reward model $r_\phi$ and then run PPO to maximize the expected reward minus a KL penalty to the reference policy. However, several practical issues arise with PPO in RLHF:

- Complexity and computational cost. PPO-based RLHF requires an explicit reward model, a policy model, and typically a value function (critic) to estimate learning signals. The training loop needs repeated sampling from the policy during optimization; generating many lengthy sequences at each training iteration is computationally expensive compared to supervised learning.
- Instability and sensitivity. Actor-critic methods require careful tuning of many hyperparameters (learning rates, clipping parameters, value-function training schedules, advantage normalization, reward normalization, etc.). In RLHF this tuning is often brittle: poor choices can lead to policy collapse, exploitation of reward-model mistakes, or divergence.
- Baseline/value-function estimation and variance. Because sequence rewards can have high variance and because the normalizing partition function of certain objectives is intractable, PPO setups often require learned value functions or heuristic baselines to stabilize gradients. Learning these critics introduces additional sources of training instability and mismatch between the reward model and policy.
- Reward model errors and distribution shift. The reward model $r_\phi$ is trained on a finite sample of pairs produced by a reference policy. If the policy drifts into regions where the reward model was not trained, the reward estimates can be unreliable; without a strong KL constraint this can lead to degenerate or unsafe policy behavior.

These practical issues motivate alternative approaches that reduce complexity, avoid explicit RL loops, or more directly optimize the same underlying objective in a simpler way.

# Why KL regularization to a reference policy is essential

A central element of the RLHF formulation is the KL-divergence penalty that constrains the optimized policy to remain close to a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, usually the SFT model $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$. There are several connected reasons for including this term, both theoretical and practical.

First, the reward model is fitted on comparisons drawn from candidate completions sampled by a particular distribution (commonly the SFT model). The learned reward model is therefore most reliable on outputs that are similar to those seen during annotation. Without an explicit tether to the reference policy, an optimizing policy could drift into areas of the output space where $r_\phi$ is inaccurate or adversarially exploitable, producing outputs that obtain high (spurious) reward yet are undesirable.

Second, the KL penalty prevents extreme mode collapse and loss of generation diversity. In text generation, a pure reward-maximizer without regularization may converge to degenerate behavior such as repeatedly producing one high-reward sequence or excessively short or long sequences that exploit artifacts of the reward. Penalizing divergence from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ keeps the policy anchored to a distribution that retains fluency and diversity.

Third, the constrained optimization perspective provides a clean theoretical characterization of the optimal policy under the KL-regularized objective. When maximizing expected reward subject to a KL penalty, the optimal policy for a given reward function $r$ can be written in closed form as
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
where $Z(x)$ is a partition function that normalizes the distribution. This Boltzmann-style relationship shows explicitly that the optimal policy reweights the reference policy according to exponentiated reward: higher-reward completions become relatively more likely, but the amount of reweighting is controlled by $\beta$ and remains anchored by the reference probabilities. The KL term is therefore not merely an implementation trick but an integral part of modeling what it means to balance reward-seeking with conservatism.

Finally, in practice the KL-regularized form facilitates safer and more stable policy updates: it converts an unconstrained maximization problem into a well-behaved tradeoff that is more robust to reward model misestimation and to the high-dimensionality of language output spaces.

Taken together, these considerations explain why almost all modern RLHF methods include a KL regularizer and why the choice of reference policy and the hyperparameter $\beta$ are critical knobs controlling the behavior and safety of the fine-tuned language model.

# Concluding remarks

This chapter has laid out the canonical RLHF workflow: start with SFT to obtain a competent reference model, collect pairwise preference data over completions to learn a reward model under a preference model such as Bradley–Terry, and then use reinforcement learning with a KL constraint to optimize a policy that scores highly under the learned reward while remaining close to the reference. The notation and data formats introduced here are used throughout subsequent chapters, including the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) approach described elsewhere, which builds on the same preference model and KL-regularized objective but reparameterizes the problem so that the optimal policy can be learned directly without an explicit RL loop. Understanding the three-phase pipeline, the role of pairwise data, the meaning of the KL regularization, and the practical shortcomings of actor-critic RL methods is essential background for engaging with current research on aligning language models using human preferences.
