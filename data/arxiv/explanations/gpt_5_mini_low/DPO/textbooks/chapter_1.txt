\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: Motivation: Aligning Language Models with Human Preferences

# Capabilities vs. controllability in pretrained LMs
Large, unsupervisedly pretrained language models acquire a broad and surprising set of capabilities: factual knowledge, pattern completion, translation, coding, and some reasoning abilities. These capabilities arise because the models are trained on massive, heterogeneous corpora produced by humans with many different goals, preferences, and skill levels. That same breadth of data that gives models capability also undermines precise control: the distribution of behaviors the model can produce is large and contains both desirable and undesirable modes. A model may "know" both the correct and the incorrect answer to a question, or both excellent and poor ways to respond to a coding prompt, and there is nothing in the unsupervised objective that privileges one over the other.

Controllability therefore means selecting which of the many behaviors the pretrained model should express at inference time. For practical systems this includes safety (avoidance of harmful outputs), usefulness (helpful and on-topic responses), and conformity to norms or preferences specific to an application. Because pretraining is unsupervised and aggregates many human voices, it cannot by itself guarantee these preferences will be respected. Thus a second-stage procedure is required to steer the model toward the subset of behaviors we want while retaining the capabilities learned in pretraining.

# Why supervised demonstrations are insufficient or costly
A natural approach to steerability is supervised fine-tuning on examples of desired behavior: collect demonstrations of high-quality responses and train the model to imitate them. This "instruction-tuning" or supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can substantially improve usability and makes models follow instructions better than zero- or few-shot prompting. However, collecting high-quality demonstrations is often expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes infeasible at the scale or specificity required.

There are several reasons supervised demonstrations can be insufficient. First, the space of prompts and contexts an interactive model must handle is enormous; obtaining expert demonstrations that cover this space well is costly. Second, demonstrations provide absolute targets, which may be hard to specify precisely for soft preferences (e.g., "more helpful", "less toxic") or for trade-offs between attributes. Third, demonstrations do not naturally capture the comparative notion of quality: humans can often say which of two responses is better more easily than they can craft a single perfect response. Finally, with only supervised examples, models can overfit or fail to generalize the subtle trade-offs implicit in human judgments of quality, especially when such judgments are context dependent.

# Relative preferences as scalable supervision
Because of the limitations above, learning from relative human preferences—labels that indicate which of two (or more) candidate completions is preferred—has emerged as a practical and scalable alternative. Preference data are attractive because: (1) they are often easier for annotators to provide (choosing between two responses is simpler than composing one), (2) they naturally express relative trade-offs (one reply could be more helpful while shorter), and (3) they can be gathered at scale even when absolute, expert demonstrations are scarce.

A formal, common statistical model for such pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) model. Under Bradley–Terry, an unknown latent scoring or reward function $r^*(x,y)$ induces pairwise choice probabilities via

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\]

A dataset of comparisons sampled from human annotators can therefore be viewed as samples from this probabilistic preference model. The standard route is to fit a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to maximize likelihood under the Bradley–Terry model, which reduces to a simple binary cross-entropy objective:

\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\bigr],
\]

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and each datapoint provides a preferred $y_w$ and a dispreferred $y_l$.

Preference learning thus converts human judgments into a learning signal that is well matched to questions of "which response is better" and avoids some of the costs of producing fully specified demonstrations.

# Limitations of traditional RLHF pipelines (complexity, instability, cost)
The dominant operational approach to using preference data with large language models has been Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). The typical RLHF pipeline includes three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to produce a reasonable initial policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$, (2) reward modelling: fit a reward network $r_\phi$ to preference comparisons using the Bradley–Terry loss above, and (3) reinforcement learning: optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize expected reward subject to a trust-region or KL constraint that prevents the policy from drifting far from a reference (usually $\pi^\text{SFT}$).

A commonly used constrained objective is

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\; y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\; \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\beta$ trades off reward versus closeness to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (often set to $\pi^\text{SFT}$). Because the expectation is over discrete text sequences, this objective is typically optimized using policy-gradient RL methods such as PPO.

This multi-stage RLHF approach has practical drawbacks. First, it is complex: it requires training multiple networks (reward model, policy, and often a value/baseline network), maintaining a sampling loop to produce trajectories for policy updates, and ensuring stable interplay between reward estimation and policy improvement. Second, it can be unstable: actor-critic methods used in RLHF can have high variance and require careful tuning of baselines, normalization, and hyperparameters. Third, it is computationally expensive: sampling many trajectories during RL fine-tuning and training auxiliary networks increases wall-clock time and infrastructure cost. Finally, because the reward model is learned separately from the policy, there can be issues with distributional shift—if the policy strays into regions where the reward model is inaccurate, optimization can produce degenerate behavior unless extra safeguards are applied.

Taken together, these limitations motivate looking for simpler, more stable algorithms that retain the advantages of preference supervision but avoid the burdens of RL.

# High-level promise of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a conceptually simple and computationally lightweight alternative to the standard RLHF pipeline. The main insight of DPO is a change of variables: instead of first learning a reward function $r_\phi$ from preferences and then using RL to find a policy that maximizes that reward under a KL constraint, DPO parameterizes the reward function via the policy itself and directly fits the policy to match the observed preferences.

A key fact underpinning DPO is that, for the constrained objective above, the optimal policy induced by a reward $r$ has the closed-form form

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

where $Z(x)$ is a partition function ensuring normalization. Rearranging gives a reparameterization of the reward in terms of the optimal policy:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\]

Crucially, Bradley–Terry preferences depend only on reward differences between two completions, so the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when modeling pairwise choice probabilities. Substituting the reparameterized reward into the Bradley–Terry model yields a preference model that depends directly on the policy ratios:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\bigl(\beta \log \tfrac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \tfrac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\bigr)}.
\]

This expression enables a maximum-likelihood objective for the policy itself. DPO trains a parametric policy $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing a simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs:

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\right].
\]

Two observations clarify DPO's promise. First, it bypasses both fitting a standalone reward network and running an RL optimization loop: the policy both defines an implicit reward and is directly trained to match human preferences. Second, the training objective is a simple supervised-style cross-entropy loss (over preference labels) that is straightforward to optimize with standard gradient-based methods and avoids sampling from the policy during training or learning separate value baselines. The resulting algorithm is thus conceptually simple, more stable, and much less computationally demanding than RLHF while targeting the same KL-constrained reward-maximization goal.

Intuitively, DPO updates increase the relative log-probability that the policy assigns to preferred completions and decrease it for dispreferred completions, but with example-specific weights that depend on how poorly the policy currently orders the pair. This weighting (coming from the logistic factor in the gradient) helps avoid degenerate updates that would otherwise push the model too aggressively away from the reference distribution.

Empirically, when applied to sentiment-controlled generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue tasks, DPO matches or improves on PPO-based RLHF in achieved trade-offs between reward and KL, in win rates against baselines, and in generalization to some out-of-distribution inputs—while being substantially simpler to implement and train. These properties make DPO a promising, lower-cost route to aligning large language models with human preferences.
