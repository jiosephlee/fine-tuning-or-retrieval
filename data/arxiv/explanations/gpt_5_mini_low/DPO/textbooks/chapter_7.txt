\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 7: Practical DPO: Data, Training, and Implementation

# Constructing the preference dataset \mathcal{D} from human/AI feedback
The DPO pipeline assumes an offline dataset of pairwise comparisons \mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N where for each prompt x a preferred completion y_w and a dispreferred completion y_l are recorded. The paper follows the standard RLHF data collection procedure used by prior work and the practical rules below summarize the approach and important considerations.

Data-generation flow. In practice the typical flow is:
1) Choose a reference policy \pi_\text{ref} (see next section).  
2) For each prompt x, sample two completions y_1,y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x).  
3) Present the pair (y_1,y_2) (and the prompt x) to a human labeler and record which completion is preferred: either y_1 \succ y_2 or y_2 \succ y_1. Convert the labeled pair to the canonical ordered form (y_w,y_l). Repeat to build \mathcal{D}.

Human vs synthetic labeling. The same framework supports human preference labels or labels generated by another model under a rubric (e.g., constitutional/LLM-based labeling). The paper uses both: real human labels for summarization and dialogue datasets, while in a controlled sentiment task they generate preferences using a ground-truth sentiment classifier (so the dataset is synthetic but controlled). If you plan to reuse public preference datasets, be aware the dataset was sampled from some \pi^\text{SFT}; matching or approximating that reference helps (see the pi_ref section).

Dataset diversity and sampling. Because DPO optimizes a conditional policy, the dataset should cover the kinds of prompts you expect at inference. The canonical RLHF procedure samples prompts from a validation/interactions distribution and draws completions from the SFT/reference policy. If you want to expand coverage, collect prompts from multiple sources or intentionally sample underrepresented prompts. The paper notes that many practitioners reuse existing preference datasets rather than re-collecting.

Formatting and storage. Store each example as (prompt x, preferred y_w, dispreferred y_l). You will need to compute \log \pi(\cdot\mid x) (sequence log-probabilities) for both \pi_\theta and \pi_\text{ref} during training, so keeping the raw tokenization or the raw strings is sufficient — tokenization can be done in the training loop.

# Choosing and initializing \pi_\text{ref} (SFT model or preferred-only MLE)
The reference policy \pi_\text{ref} plays two roles in DPO: (1) it appears explicitly in the DPO objective as the distribution to which we penalize divergence, and (2) it is the distribution used to sample candidate completions when constructing preference data. Choosing and initializing \pi_\text{ref} correctly is therefore important.

If an SFT model is available. The standard choice is to set \pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}, the supervised-fine-tuned model used to produce the data. This is the setup assumed in much RLHF work and in the paper's summarization experiments. Using the same SFT that generated the comparison pairs minimizes distribution shift between the data-collection policy and the reference used in the DPO objective.

If an SFT model is not available. The paper gives a practical fallback: fit \pi_\text{ref} by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions in \mathcal{D}. Concretely,
\pi_\text{ref} \leftarrow \arg\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)].
This produces a model that assigns higher probability to the human-preferred completions and mitigates mismatch between the unknown original sampling policy and the \pi_\text{ref} you use for the DPO loss.

Fixed vs. trainable reference. In DPO the reference policy is held fixed during DPO training. The DPO objective measures the log-probability ratio between \pi_\theta and a fixed \pi_\text{ref}; the derivation and theoretical guarantees assume \pi_\text{ref} is fixed. Therefore, if you retrain or update \pi_\text{ref}, recompute any dataset sampling procedures accordingly and treat the new \pi_\text{ref} as a new fixed reference.

Practical tip. If you initialize \pi_\text{ref} from a public SFT checkpoint, ensure tokenizers and sequence handling match between \pi_\text{ref} and your training implementation to avoid mismatched log-probability calculations.

# Training loop: batching, tokenization, loss computation, optimization
At training time DPO reduces to standard supervised learning building blocks, with the single per-example loss expressed directly in terms of log-probabilities under \pi_\theta and \pi_\text{ref}. The DPO loss (Eq. 9 in the paper) is:

\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\biggl(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\biggr)\right],

where \sigma is the logistic function and \beta>0 is the hyperparameter controlling conservativeness (discussed later).

Computing sequence log-probabilities. For each (x,y) pair you must evaluate \log \pi(y\mid x) for both \pi_\theta and \pi_\text{ref}. In autoregressive LMs this is the sum of token log-probabilities given the prefix x and any earlier tokens in y. Practically:
1) Tokenize the concatenated input (prompt x and target y) in the same way for both models.
2) Feed x+y into the model, mask to compute only the log-probabilities of the y tokens conditioned on x.
3) Sum token log-probabilities across the generated tokens of y to obtain \log \pi(y\mid x).
Because both \pi_\theta and \pi_\text{ref} are typically transformer LMs, the same tokenization and batching scheme can be reused.

Batching. A typical training batch contains multiple preference pairs. For each batch:
- Tokenize and pad prompts and both completions so forward passes can be batched.
- Compute \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x), \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) in one forward pass (or two, if memory forces it) and likewise compute \log \pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x), \log \pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x) using the fixed reference model.
- Compute the pairwise scalar inside the logistic: \Delta_\theta = \beta(\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)) - \beta(\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)). The loss per example is -\log\sigma(\Delta_\theta).

Gradient and effective weighting. The paper derives the gradient structure which is instructive for implementation: the scalar weight \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)), where \hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)), multiplies the usual log-prob gradient term. You do not need to implement the gradient by hand; standard autodiff over the scalar loss computes the correct gradient. But understanding the weighting explains stability: examples where the implicit reward mistakenly prefers the dispreferred response have larger weights, so the model focuses corrective updates there.

Optimization loop. Because DPO training is just minimizing \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}, you can use standard optimizers (Adam/AdamW). The paper emphasizes DPO requires little hyperparameter tuning compared to PPO. Keep \pi_\text{ref} fixed, run minibatch SGD/Adam steps on \pi_\theta, and periodically evaluate held-out metrics (win rate, validation loss, approximate KL — see monitoring).

Sequence length and padding. Ensure sequence-length limits (max tokens) are sufficient to cover prompt+completion pairs. Truncate the few longest examples or bucket by length to reduce padding waste. The paper worked with tasks up to 6B parameters; memory-efficient batching and mixed precision (bfloat16/FP16) are standard practical measures.

No separate reward model or actor-critic. Note that no separate reward model, value network, or on-policy sampling loop is required. You only need to load \pi_\text{ref} in evaluation mode for log-prob computations and train \pi_\theta on the DPO loss.

# Hyperparameters: \beta, learning rate, batch size, sequence length
The DPO algorithm introduces one new, important hyperparameter compared with pure supervised fine-tuning: \beta, which controls how strongly the learned policy is penalized for deviating from \pi_\text{ref} and appears in the implicit reward \hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)). The paper provides practical guidance and experimental values:

Interpreting \beta. Smaller \beta makes the KL penalty effectively stronger (more conservative), because the implicit reward scales with \beta. Larger \beta allows the policy to depart more from \pi_\text{ref} to increase reward. In experiments the authors tried values \beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\} in their hyperparameter sweeps; different tasks favored different \beta. If you want conservative changes from the SFT model, start with a small \beta; if you want more aggressive optimization toward preferences, increase \beta.

Other optimizer hyperparameters. The paper does not prescribe exact learning rates, batch sizes or sequence lengths (these depend on model size and infra). Practical rules of thumb consistent with training large LMs remain:
- Use AdamW with weight decay for stability.
- Use learning rates tuned to model size (smaller for larger models); DPO tended to require less tuning than PPO.
- Choose a batch size that balances gradient noise and available memory; accumulate gradients if needed.
- Use sequence length high enough to cover prompts and completions in your dataset.

Empirical search. The experimental comparison in the paper used modest sweeps (different \beta values and other method-specific hyperparameters). Because DPO is a simpler supervised-style objective, hyperparameter searches are typically faster than PPO experiments.

# No on-policy sampling or value baselines; compute and infra savings
One of the main practical benefits of DPO is infrastructural simplicity and computational efficiency compared to RLHF pipelines that use PPO or other policy-gradients.

No on-policy rollouts. PPO-based RLHF requires sampling from the current policy during training to estimate gradients and compute returns, which frequently requires repeated autoregressive decoding and careful rollout management. DPO uses an offline dataset \mathcal{D} and never samples new trajectories during training. This eliminates the inner RL sampling loop and the cost of autoregressive decoding during optimization.

No reward model training loop. DPO avoids the two-stage reward-model + RL training pipeline. Instead of first fitting a reward function r_\phi and then running RL against that learned reward, DPO optimizes \pi_\theta directly against the preference data. There is therefore no separate reward-model checkpoint or the engineering to manage reward normalization and baselines.

No value networks or baselines. Actor-critic methods require a critic/value network and careful handling of baseline subtraction or advantage estimation to control variance. DPO's loss is a simple binary cross-entropy formulation and does not rely on learned value baselines, reducing both code complexity and sources of instability.

Practical savings. These differences translate to real savings: fewer networks to maintain and checkpoint, no online sampling infrastructure, fewer correlated hyperparameters to tune, and faster wall-clock training for the same amount of human-labeled preference data. The paper reports that DPO trains stably and efficiently with substantially less hyperparameter tuning than PPO.

# Troubleshooting: collapse, over-optimization, and monitoring KL
Although DPO is simpler than RLHF, there are failure modes and diagnostics you should monitor. The paper highlights several practical failure modes and their causes, along with appropriate monitoring metrics.

Mode collapse / degeneracy. A naive objective that simply increases \log\pi(y_w\mid x) and decreases \log\pi(y_l\mid x) without the DPO weighting can cause degeneration, where the model assigns near-zero probability to many sequences (mode collapse). The DPO formulation avoids this by optimizing the Bradley–Terry-derived likelihood in terms of the log-probability ratio with an inherent, per-example importance weight. Empirically the authors note a naive unweighted probability-ratio objective led to degeneration (see their Appendix). If you see collapse (e.g., the model outputs trivial repeated tokens or assigns extremely small likelihoods broadly), verify you are using the full DPO loss as written, and not a simplified unlikelihood-style loss without the logistic weighting.

Over-optimization relative to human preferences. Because DPO optimizes toward the empirical preference data, aggressive training or very large \beta could cause the model to overfit to artifacts in the preference dataset or exploit annotation quirks. To detect over-optimization, monitor held-out preference loss, human- or proxy-evaluated win rate, and qualitative behavior on a held-out set of prompts. If win rate on a validation set stalls or declines while training loss continues to improve, that is a signal of overfitting.

Monitoring KL. Although DPO does not explicitly constrain KL during training (it incorporates \pi_\text{ref} into the implicit reward), you should still track the sequence-level KL divergence \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\mid\mid\pi_\text{ref}) on a validation prompt set. This provides the same interpretability used in RLHF evaluations: it lets you observe how far the policy has moved from the reference. If KL grows faster than desired, reduce \beta or stop training earlier.

Metric checklist for monitoring
- Training loss: \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} on the held-out portion of \mathcal{D}.
- Validation win rate: automatic (GPT-4 proxy) or human-based win rate against reference completions.
- Sequence-level KL: \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\mid\mid\pi_\text{ref}) averaged across validation prompts.
- Sample quality: inspect model outputs for repetition, unsafe content, or collapsed outputs.
- Reward (if you have a ground-truth surrogate, e.g., sentiment classifier in controlled tasks): mean reward vs. KL frontier.

Practical mitigation strategies
- If collapse appears: reduce learning rate, reduce update frequency, or verify you are using the full DPO logistic loss (not a naive unlikelihood loss). Smaller \beta will also make updates more conservative.
- If overfitting to preference artifacts: reduce \beta, add weight decay, early-stop based on validation win-rate, or expand preference data diversity.
- If KL drifts too far: reduce \beta or add an explicit KL-monitoring stopping rule (stop when KL exceeds a threshold).
- If instability in gradients: consider gradient clipping and smaller batch sizes with gradient accumulation to stabilize updates.

# Putting it together: a minimal implementation sketch (conceptual)
1) Prepare \mathcal{D} of labeled pairs and choose \pi_\text{ref} (SFT or preferred-only MLE).  
2) Initialize \pi_\theta from \pi_\text{ref} (common practice). Keep \pi_\text{ref} in evaluation mode.  
3) For each training minibatch:
   a) Tokenize batch of (x,y_w,y_l) triples and compute \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x), \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x).  
   b) Compute \log \pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x), \log \pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x) from the fixed model.  
   c) Compute \Delta_\theta = \beta[\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)] - \beta[\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)].  
   d) Loss = -\mathrm{mean}(\log\sigma(\Delta_\theta)). Backprop and optimizer step on \pi_\theta parameters.  
4) Periodically evaluate validation metrics: DPO loss on held-out pairs, win rates (GPT-4 or human), and sequence-level KL.  
5) Early-stop or adjust \beta / learning rate based on monitored metrics.

# Final practical notes and takeaways
- DPO replaces the two-stage RLHF pipeline (reward model + RL) with a single supervised-style objective that is simple to implement and computationally cheaper. It requires computing log-probabilities under a fixed \pi_\text{ref} and the current \pi_\theta for each labeled pair but eliminates online rollouts and value networks.  
- \beta is the main knob to trade off conservativeness vs. preference adherence; a small sweep over a few orders of magnitude (as in the paper: 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5) is typically sufficient to find a useful setting.  
- Use the full logistic DPO loss; naïve unlikelihood or ratio-only objectives can cause degeneration. The implicit per-example weighting is important for stability.  
- Monitor validation win rate and sequence-level KL to ensure improvements are real and the policy is not drifting excessively from the reference.  
- DPO is especially attractive when you have a static preference dataset to reuse: it greatly reduces engineering complexity and compute cost relative to PPO-style RLHF while achieving comparable or better empirical results in the experiments reported in the paper.

The descriptions above summarize the practical details, design choices, and failure modes that are discussed or used in the paper. For implementation-specific micro-optimizations (exact batching strategies, mixed-precision config, or distributed training tricks), follow standard large-language-model training best practices appropriate for your model size and infrastructure.
