\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: KL-Constrained Reward Maximization in RLHF

# Objective: maximize $ \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]$
A central optimization problem in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is to update a language model policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ so that its generations score highly under a reward function $r(x,y)$ inferred from human preferences, while preventing the policy from drifting too far from a trusted reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$. This tradeoff is naturally expressed as a regularized expected-reward objective:

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\mathcal{D}$ is the distribution of prompts $x$ (or the dataset of prompts used for fine-tuning), and $\beta>0$ is a scalar penalty coefficient. The expectation over $x$ and $y$ captures average-case performance across prompts and stochastic model generations, respectively. The KL term is computed between the policy distributions over complete sequences conditioned on $x$, and the objective trades off immediate reward gain against divergence from the reference behavior.

Framing the problem this way has two consequences. First, the objective directly encodes the desire to improve reward while keeping the policy anchored to the reference distribution. Second, it admits an analytic characterization of the optimal policy under mild assumptions: the optimal $\pi$ for a fixed reward $r$ and fixed $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ takes a form obtained by exponential tilting of the reference policy by the reward (see the final section). This equivalence is a useful theoretical tool for analyzing RLHF and also motivates alternative algorithms that exploit the analytic mapping between rewards and optimal policies.

# Role of $\beta$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$
The hyperparameter $\beta$ controls the strength of the regularization that penalizes deviation from the reference policy. When $\beta$ is large, the KL penalty term dominates, and the optimization favors policies close to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ even at the expense of reward; as $\beta\to\infty$, the objective strongly prefers to keep $\pi_\theta\approx\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Conversely, when $\beta$ is small, the KL constraint is weak and the optimizer is freer to pursue higher reward even if it requires large departures from the reference. Thus $\beta$ parameterizes the conservativeness of policy updates and is a key knob when trading off reward-seeking versus staying within the distribution where the reward model is reliable.

The reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ itself is typically the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ used prior to preference learning. Its role is twofold. First, it provides a natural baseline distribution of plausible, high-quality generations rooted in human-written training data. Second, the reference defines the geometry of the KL penalty: the KL divergence measures how much the updated policy changes the probability assigned to full sequences relative to that baseline. Using $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ helps ensure that the learned policy does not assign excessive mass to unlikely or degenerate sequences that the reward model may spuriously favor. In practice, if the preference dataset was generated by sampling from a particular model, initializing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to that same SFT model reduces distribution shift between the preference collection process and the policy being optimized.

# Why sequence-level KL matters (prevent drift, retain diversity)
The KL divergence in the RLHF objective is computed at the sequence (or trajectory) level: it compares the full-sequence distributions $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$. This sequence-level KL is crucial for several reasons highlighted in the paper.

First, it prevents the learned policy from drifting into parts of sequence space where the reward model is untrustworthy. The reward model is trained on a finite set of comparisons (pairs of completions), and it can produce arbitrarily high scalar scores for sequences far outside the reference distribution. Penalizing the policy based on sequence-level KL discourages assigning mass to such out-of-distribution sequences that might exploit reward-model artifacts.

Second, sequence-level KL preserves generation diversity. Without a constraint on entire sequences, optimization can collapse the policy to a few high-reward sequences (mode collapse). The KL penalty penalizes any reduction in entropy relative to the reference at the sequence level, encouraging the policy to retain the variety of outputs the reference provided. This is important in language modeling, where diversity often correlates with utility across diverse prompts and user intents.

Finally, the sequence-level KL naturally captures accumulated per-token deviations: because the sequence distribution factorizes across generation steps, the sequence KL is the sum of per-step KLs. Thus the penalty reflects aggregate departure across the whole response, which matters more than per-token or locally constrained penalties for preserving coherent global behavior.

# Challenges: discrete actions, variance, and on-policy sampling
Although the KL-constrained objective is conceptually straightforward, optimizing it in the context of language generation brings practical challenges emphasized by standard RLHF pipelines.

A primary difficulty is discreteness: language is generated via discrete tokens, and the expectation of reward under a parameterized policy is not differentiable through the sampling operation. This necessitates use of reinforcement learning estimators (e.g., policy gradients, PPO) or other gradient estimators to update $\pi_\theta$, which introduces variance and instability. Policy-gradient methods require sampling from the current policy during training so that gradients of the expected reward can be estimated; in turn, this on-policy sampling makes training computationally expensive, since many complete sequences must be generated and scored each update.

Variance of gradient estimates is another practical issue. The reward signal—especially when learned from human preferences—can have high variance across sequences, and gradients from REINFORCE-style estimators are noisy. To counteract this, actor-critic methods introduce learned value functions or baselines to reduce variance, but these introduce additional models to train and can be a source of instability if the value estimates are poor. In the RLHF pipeline, practitioners often normalize rewards or employ other tricks to stabilize PPO, yet significant hyperparameter tuning and careful engineering are typically required.

On-policy sampling itself is expensive in language settings because generating many full sequences from large LMs is computationally heavy. Training loops that require repeated sampling, reward evaluation, and policy updates (as in PPO) incur large compute and implementation complexity compared to standard supervised finetuning.

These challenges motivate the search for alternative formulations and algorithms that avoid heavy on-policy sampling and reinforcement learning while still optimizing the KL-constrained objective implied by human preference models.

# Optimal policy form under KL constraint (exponential tilting)
A powerful analytical result for the KL-constrained objective is that, for a fixed reward function $r(x,y)$ and fixed reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the optimal policy that maximizes

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]
\]

has a closed-form exponential-tilting solution. Concretely, the optimal policy $\pi_r(y\mid x)$ is

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right),
\]

where the partition function (normalizer)

\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right)
\]

ensures the distribution sums to one. This form is sometimes called exponential tilting or a Boltzmann-rational policy: the reference policy is reweighted multiplicatively by the exponentiated scaled reward. The factor $1/\beta$ controls the sharpness of reweighting: smaller $\beta$ (weaker KL penalty) makes the exponential factor more influential, concentrating mass on high-reward sequences; larger $\beta$ dampens the influence of reward, leaving the policy closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

This analytic form provides several important insights. First, it clarifies that the effect of the KL term is to modulate reward-based reweighting by the prior probability under the reference model: sequences unlikely under $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ require exponentially larger reward to become likely under the optimal policy, preventing pathological exploitation of the reward model unless the reward evidence is strong. Second, it shows the optimal policy depends on the partition function $Z(x)$, which is generally intractable to compute because it sums over an exponential number of possible sequences. That intractability is one reason standard approaches proceed by retaining an explicit reward model and optimizing the policy via RL rather than directly computing $\pi_r$.

However, the exponential-tilting relation also underlies the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) insight: by algebraically inverting the relation, we can express reward values up to an additive function of $x$ in terms of the log-ratio between the optimal policy and the reference: rearranging gives

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\]

Because preference models such as Bradley–Terry depend only on reward differences between two completions for the same prompt (the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels), one can parameterize the reward implicitly via the policy log-ratio and form a maximum-likelihood objective for the policy directly using preference pairs. This change of variables removes the need to explicitly fit a standalone reward model and also avoids RL sampling in the inner loop: the policy parameters can be trained with a simple binary cross-entropy loss that encourages higher log-probability (relative to the reference) for preferred sequences. The exponential-tilting form is therefore both the canonical solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem and the key bridge enabling RL-free preference optimization methods like DPO.

In summary, KL-constrained reward maximization formalizes the practical desiderata of improving expected reward while respecting a trusted reference policy. The scalar $\beta$ and the choice of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ control how conservative updates are. Sequence-level KL is essential to avoid reward exploitation and preserve diversity. Although discreteness and on-policy variance complicate direct optimization with RL methods, the optimal policy has a closed-form exponential-tilting expression that can be algebraically manipulated to design alternative, simpler optimization approaches that still respect the same theoretical objective.
