\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: Mechanics and Intuition: What the DPO Update Does

# Implicit reward: $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) frames the policy itself as defining an implicit reward. Instead of first fitting a separate scalar-valued reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ and then running reinforcement learning to find a policy that maximizes that reward subject to a KL constraint, DPO uses a closed-form relation between optimal reward and optimal policy to reparameterize the reward in terms of the policy. Concretely, for a given policy $\pi_\theta$ and a chosen reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the implicit reward that corresponds to $\pi_\theta$ under the KL-constrained objective is
\[
\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This equation arises from rearranging the known closed-form solution of the KL-regularized reward-maximization problem (see the paper's Eq.~(3) to Eq.~(5) derivation). Intuitively, $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)$ measures how much more (or less) the current policy prefers output $y$ relative to the reference: positive values indicate that the learned policy assigns higher probability than the reference, negative values indicate lower probability. The scalar $\beta$ simply scales this log-probability ratio and therefore sets the units of the implicit reward.

Viewing the policy as a reward model has two important consequences. First, it means that preferences (which depend only on reward differences) can be expressed purely in terms of ratios of policy probabilities, removing the explicit partition-function dependence that makes reward-based approaches expensive to evaluate. Second, it makes the optimization objective a standard binary cross-entropy on preference pairs, but where the modeled log-odds are functions of probability ratios of the policy and the reference (the DPO loss is the paper's Eq.~(8)). This reparameterization keeps the expressive power needed to represent the relevant class of reward functions (up to the usual Plackett–Luce / Bradley–Terry under-specification), while enabling direct gradient updates on the policy parameters.

# Gradient analysis: upweight examples where the model’s ordering is wrong
To understand what each gradient step does, consider the DPO loss specialized to a single labeled comparison $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$. The loss contribution is
\[
-\log\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)\bigr)
\]
with $\hat{r}_\theta$ defined above and $\sigma$ the logistic function. Differentiating with respect to $\theta$ gives the gradient form presented in the paper:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \;=\; -\beta\,\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\!\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\right],
\]
where we emphasize the multiplicative scalar
\[
w(x,y_w,y_l;\theta) \;=\; \sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr).
\]
This scalar acts as an example-dependent weight. Its value is large when the implicit reward currently ranks the dispreferred completion $y_l$ above the preferred $y_w$ (i.e., when $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)$ is large and positive). Conversely, the weight is small when the model already ranks $y_w$ higher than $y_l$. Therefore, each gradient step does two things simultaneously: (1) it increases the log-probability of preferred completions, and decreases the log-probability of dispreferred completions (the bracketed term), and (2) it concentrates more update mass on the comparisons that the model currently gets wrong (the multiplicative $\sigma(\cdot)$ factor). In other words, DPO performs targeted corrections where the implicit reward’s ordering conflicts with the human preference, and applies smaller corrections where the model already agrees with the preference.

This behavior is analogous to margin-based updates in classification: the model focuses on examples near or on the wrong side of the decision boundary, applying less force when the margin is already large and correct. But unlike a fixed-margin classifier, the DPO weight is smooth and depends on the model’s current implicit rewards, giving a continuous notion of how wrong an ordering is and smoothly scaling the gradient accordingly. The factor $\beta$ scales the entire update (see the next section for its role), so both the direction (promote $y_w$, demote $y_l$) and the magnitude (how strongly to do so) are controlled by the DPO gradient.

# Why naive unlikelihood without weighting can degenerate generations
A naive alternative to DPO is to apply an "unlikelihood" style objective that simply increases probability of the preferred completion and simultaneously decreases probability of the dispreferred one with fixed coefficients, for example minimizing
\[
-\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) + \alpha\,\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)
\]
for some constant $\alpha>0$. This unweighted or fixed-weight approach lacks the adaptive, ordering-dependent weighting of DPO. The DPO paper emphasizes empirically that such naive objectives can cause degeneration: the model can collapse probabilities in harmful ways or mode-collapse to short, generic, high-probability outputs.

Mechanistically, the problem arises because constant-magnitude pushes on probabilities ignore how badly the model presently ranks the pair. If the model already ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$, applying the same fixed push as when it ranks them incorrectly can needlessly move mass away from other desirable outputs to satisfy the artificial objective. Repeated application across many pairs can concentrate probability mass on a few tokens or phrases that were repeatedly pushed up, reducing generation diversity and producing degenerate or repetitive outputs. In contrast, DPO's weighting dampens updates when the current implicit reward ordering agrees with preferences; it does not punish already-correct orderings with the same strength as misorderings. This adaptive behavior prevents the unnecessary and cumulative shifts that lead to degeneration.

Put differently, unlikelihood applies unsigned forces uniformly, while DPO applies signed, magnitude-modulated corrective forces concentrated on problematic orderings. The logistic weight in DPO smoothly interpolates between no update (when $\hat{r}_\theta(y_w)\gg \hat{r}_\theta(y_l)$) and a full-strength update (when $\hat{r}_\theta(y_l)\gg \hat{r}_\theta(y_w)$), which stabilizes learning and preserves the diversity and calibration properties of the base model.

# Effect of $\beta$ on the strength of the KL constraint and learning dynamics
The scalar $\beta$ plays two roles in the DPO formulation. First, it appears in the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta = \beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ and thereby sets the scale of reward differences implied by a given probability ratio. Second, $\beta$ multiplies the entire gradient in the DPO update, so it directly scales update magnitudes.

Interpretationally, $\beta$ corresponds to the trade-off parameter in the original KL-constrained reinforcement objective:
\[
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}],
\]
so smaller $\beta$ places a larger penalty on KL deviation (more conservative policy), while larger $\beta$ relaxes the KL penalty (allowing more deviation to chase higher reward). When we reparameterize reward in terms of the policy, $\beta$ still encodes the same conservativeness. Concretely, for a fixed change in $\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$, the implied reward difference is scaled by $\beta$, so a larger $\beta$ makes the same probability ratio count as a larger reward gap; this in turn changes how the logistic weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(y_w))$ behaves because the argument is proportional to $\beta$. Therefore:

- Small $\beta$ (strong KL regularization): implicit reward differences $\hat{r}_\theta$ are small in magnitude for typical probability ratios, so the logistic weight is closer to 0.5 for mildly misordered pairs, producing more conservative updates that require stronger misordering to produce a large weight. This encourages the policy to move slowly away from the reference and prioritize staying close to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

- Large $\beta$ (weak KL regularization): implicit reward differences are larger for the same probability ratio, making the weight more extreme (closer to 0 or 1) for a given ordering error. This produces larger corrective updates and allows the policy to change more aggressively away from the reference. If too large, it can risk instability or over-optimization.

Because $\beta$ scales the gradient directly, tuning it controls both the magnitude of updates and the effective margin the model uses to decide whether an ordering is sufficiently correct. In practice (as reported in the paper), DPO is robust and needs less careful tuning than RLHF approaches; nevertheless $\beta$ remains the primary knob controlling conservativeness versus responsiveness in learning.

# Connection to classification with example-dependent margins
There is a close conceptual connection between the DPO update and margin-based classification with example-dependent margins. In margin classifiers (such as SVMs or margin-based logistic regression), an example contributes to the objective according to how close it is to the decision boundary; examples within the margin receive stronger gradient signals than those far from the margin. In DPO, each preference pair $(y_w,y_l)$ induces a scalar margin-like quantity
\[
\Delta_\theta(x;y_w,y_l) \;=\; \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l) \;=\; \beta\Bigl(\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Bigr) \;-\; \beta\Bigl(\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)\Bigr).
\]
The loss is effectively a logistic loss applied to this margin:
\[
\ell(\Delta) \;=\; -\log\sigma(\Delta).
\]
Crucially, the margin is model- and example-dependent: it depends not only on the current policy's log-probability difference between $y_w$ and $y_l$, but also on the reference policy's log-probability difference. This dependence is meaningful: if the reference already strongly favored $y_w$ (i.e., the reference margin is large and positive), then the DPO margin is easier to make large and the model needs less change; if the reference favored $y_l$, the policy must overcome that prior via larger updates. This is analogous to classification where some examples are weighted or have different target margins; here the reference creates a baseline margin that must be overcome for the policy to match preferences.

Because the weighting term $\sigma(-\Delta)$ is a smooth, example-dependent function of the margin, DPO can be seen as performing logistic regression over pairwise comparisons with adaptive margins and example-dependent importance. This viewpoint explains why DPO inherits desirable properties from margin-based learning: it focuses capacity on the hardest comparisons, produces calibrated updates, and avoids wasting learning signal on comparisons that are already confidently correct. Viewed this way, DPO is not only a policy optimizer but also an implicitly regularized classifier over preference orderings, where the reference policy provides the per-example baseline margin and $\beta$ sets the desired operating margin.

In summary, the DPO gradient is a principled, conservative, and adaptive corrective rule: it pushes the policy probabilities toward human-preferred orderings, concentrates updates where the model is most mistaken, avoids unnecessary changes when the model already agrees with preferences, and uses $\beta$ to trade off conservativeness against responsiveness. This combination of features explains why DPO avoids the instability of naive unlikelihood objectives and why it can outperform more complex RLHF pipelines in practice while remaining simple to implement and tune.
