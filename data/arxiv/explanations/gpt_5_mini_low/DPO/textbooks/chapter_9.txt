\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 9: RLHF Through the Control-as-Inference Lens: Why PPO Can Be Unstable

# Minimizing KL($\pi_\theta\parallel\pi^*$) leads to an objective with a soft value baseline

One useful way to view the RLHF fine-tuning step is as minimizing the divergence between a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ and the optimal constrained-RL policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes expected (learned) reward under a KL penalty to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Concretely, if $\pi^*$ is the optimizer of the KL-constrained reward maximization problem, then minimizing $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\parallel \pi^*(\cdot\mid x)]$ yields an objective that an actor-critic implementation will try to approximate. By carrying out the algebra (as sketched in the paper), this KL-minimization can be written as an expected objective of the form

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\,f(r_\phi, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x,y)\;-\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}\,\Big],
\]

where $r_\phi$ is the learned reward model, $\beta$ is the KL regularization weight, and

\[
f(r_\phi, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x,y) \;=\; r_\phi(x,y) \;-\; \beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y')\Big).
\]

The second term in the expectation, $-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, is the sequence-level KL penalty. The first term, $f(\cdot)$, is crucial: it equals the learned reward $r_\phi(x,y)$ minus the log-partition function (scaled by $\beta$) over the reference policy. Because the log-partition depends on $x$ but not on $y$, it acts like a value or baseline term that must be estimated or accounted for when computing policy gradients.

# Normalization term as the soft value of $\pi_\text{ref}$ and its variance issues

The subtraction of $\beta\log\sum_{y'}\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y'))$ is not an arbitrary algebraic artifact; it has a natural interpretation as a soft value of the reference policy. Viewed through the control-as-inference lens, the partition function inside the logarithm aggregates the exponentiated rewards of all completions under the reference policy, producing a soft (log-sum-exp) value estimate for the conditional context $x$. This is the quantity that normalizes the Plackett–Luce / Bradley–Terry preference model so that the induced policy $\pi_r$ in closed form (Eq. 7 of the paper)

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Big),
\]

sums to a valid distribution. When we optimize policies by estimating gradients of the expectation above, the partition function term plays the role of the baseline/value that reduces variance in the policy gradient. However, it is not directly known or cheaply computable because it requires summation (or sampling) over the huge discrete space of completions $y'$.

Practically, actor-critic algorithms must handle this normalization term somehow. If they ignore it, the gradients can have large variance and point in misleading directions. If they attempt to estimate it naively (for example, with Monte Carlo samples from $\pi_\text{ref}$ or $\pi_\theta$), the estimator inherits high variance because the log-sum-exp is dominated by rare high-reward completions and the sampling is from a potentially different distribution. This high-variance estimation makes the actor updates noisy and unstable, especially when reward magnitudes vary or when only a few samples are available to approximate the partition function.

# Difficulties of learning baselines/value functions in practice

A common remedy in reinforcement learning for high-variance policy gradients is to subtract a baseline or to learn a value function that approximates expected return. In actor-critic implementations applied to RLHF (PPO-style pipelines), practitioners introduce value networks to estimate the expected reward (or soft value) and subtract that estimate to reduce variance. In principle this mirrors subtracting the log-partition normalization above.

In practice, several issues complicate this approach in the RLHF setting. First, the value or baseline network is itself learned from noisy, off-policy data: the reward model $r_\phi$ is only an estimate of human preferences and the data distribution for training the value function may not match the distribution encountered during policy updates. Second, the value function must approximate a sequence-level log-sum-exp over a vast discrete output space; this is a challenging function class to fit, and small approximation errors produce biased gradient estimates. Third, learning a stable value estimator requires careful tuning (optimizer, learning rates, clipping, target networks, etc.), and errors in the value estimate can lead to pathological actor updates (for instance, pushing probability mass to improbable completions that the estimated value erroneously favors).

These practical difficulties translate to increased instability and sensitivity in PPO-based RLHF pipelines. The actor and critic interact: a poorly estimated critic increases actor gradient variance and can drive the actor into regimes where the critic's predictions are even less accurate, creating a feedback loop. The paper highlights that the normalization/soft-value term is precisely the culprit that needs either accurate estimation or proper accounting for; when it is mishandled, learning can be unstable.

# Reward normalization tricks vs. DPO’s baseline-free reparameterization

Because a direct computation of the partition (soft-value) is intractable, previous RLHF work often resorts to heuristic reward normalization techniques. One such trick is to normalize per-prompt rewards by subtracting the reward assigned to a baseline completion (for example, the human-preferred completion or a single Monte Carlo sample). This baseline acts as a crude, single-sample estimate of the log-partition term and can reduce some variance. However, it is only a rough approximation and introduces its own bias: the single-sample baseline may be an unrepresentative estimate of the true soft value, and reliance on that sample's properties can encourage overfitting to the specifics of the sampled completions.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) takes a different route: instead of estimating or approximating the partition function and then running RL, DPO reparameterizes the reward in terms of the policy itself. Using the closed-form mapping between rewards and the optimal policy (Eq. 9 in the paper),

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x),
\]

and noting that preference labels depend only on reward differences so that the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels, DPO writes the preference likelihood directly in terms of the policy ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}$:

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
\]

Because this loss eliminates the partition function algebraically (it cancels in reward differences), DPO avoids any explicit baseline, value function, or Monte Carlo estimate of the log-partition. The training signal is a simple classification-style objective that raises the relative log-probability of preferred responses while preserving the KL regularization implicitly through the $\pi_\text{ref}$ ratios and the choice of $\beta$. In other words, DPO is baseline-free by construction: the problematic normalization term that actor-critic methods must approximate never appears in the gradients.

# When PPO may underperform despite optimizing the same target

At first glance it may seem that PPO-based RLHF and DPO are optimizing the same underlying KL-constrained reward objective, so they should yield similar solutions. The paper shows they are optimizing equivalent objectives in theory; the difference in practice comes down to how the normalization (soft-value) term is handled and the resulting statistical and optimization difficulties.

PPO and similar actor-critic methods need to estimate or learn the normalization/baseline term (the soft value) to produce low-variance, accurate gradients. In realistic language-modeling settings this estimation is noisy, unstable, and sensitive to hyperparameters. PPO further requires sampling from the policy in the inner loop, clipping or trust-region-like mechanisms, and careful tuning of learning rates, batch sizes, and clipping coefficients. All of these practical requirements make PPO pipelines fragile: they can fail to converge to good policies, suffer from mode collapse, or diverge if the critic is inaccurate. The paper empirically demonstrates that DPO attains a strictly better reward–KL frontier than PPO in a controlled sentiment task and matches or outperforms PPO on real preference datasets, despite PPO optimizing the same theoretical objective in expectation.

In short, PPO can underperform not because of a discrepancy in the theoretical target, but because of the difficulty of accurately estimating the soft value/normalization term and managing the high-variance gradients that result. DPO circumvents these issues by algebraically removing the normalization term from the learning problem via a reparameterization that casts the reward differences purely in terms of policy ratios, yielding a stable, low-variance, and simple classification-style optimization that achieves the same final goal without the RL loop or learned baselines.

# Summary

Viewing RLHF through the control-as-inference lens reveals that the RL objective inherently contains a normalization (log-partition / soft-value) term tied to the reference policy. Actor-critic methods like PPO must handle this term via value estimation or heuristics, which introduces variance, bias, and a sensitivity to hyperparameters that commonly causes instability in practice. Reward normalization heuristics mitigate but do not eliminate these problems. DPO avoids the issue entirely by reparameterizing rewards in terms of the policy and cancelling the partition function in reward differences, producing a baseline-free training objective that is simple, stable, and empirically effective while still targeting the same KL-constrained reward maximization.
