Chapter 5: From Rewards to Policies: Deriving the DPO Objective

# Optimal policy under KL constraint: $\pi_r \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$

When we frame policy optimization for language models with a constraint that the learned policy should not drift too far from a reference policy, a common objective is to maximize expected reward while penalizing deviation via a KL divergence. Concretely, for a reward function $r(x,y)$ and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$, the constrained objective optimized in RLHF-style work is

\[
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\beta>0$ is a Lagrange multiplier (or temperature-like parameter) controlling the tradeoff between reward and closeness to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This objective balances two forces: it encourages the policy to place mass on high-reward completions, but resists changing the distribution too drastically from the reference.

A standard, straightforward calculation (see Appendix references in the paper) shows that the optimal policy for this objective has a closed-form Gibbs/tilted form. For each prefix $x$ the optimal policy $\pi_r$ induced by reward $r$ is

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

with partition function

\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

Intuitively, this expression says the optimal policy is a reweighting of the reference policy: each candidate continuation $y$ keeps its relative ordering from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ but is exponentially reweighted by its reward scaled by $1/\beta$. When $\beta$ is large, the penalty for deviating from the reference is strong and the policy remains close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; when $\beta$ is small, the policy concentrates more on high-reward continuations.

This closed form is central because it provides an explicit mapping from any reward function $r$ to its corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$. The mapping is analytic up to the partition function $Z(x)$, which depends on $r$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

# Reparameterizing rewards: $r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$

The expression for $\pi_r$ can be inverted to express the reward function in terms of the optimal policy. Starting from

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

take logarithms and rearrange to solve for $r(x,y)$. Doing so yields the reparameterization identity:

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x).
\]

This equality is exact: any reward $r$ that induces $\pi_r$ via the KL-constrained optimization satisfies it. The decomposition separates the reward into two parts. The first term, $\beta\log\frac{\pi_r}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$, captures the relative log-probability of $y$ under the optimal policy versus the reference. The second term, $\beta\log Z(x)$, is a normalization that depends only on the prefix $x$ (it is independent of $y$). Because it is a function of $x$ alone, this second term shifts every $r(x,y)$ for fixed $x$ by the same constant.

Why is this form useful? Many preference models and downstream algorithms depend only on reward differences between two completions for the same prefix. When only differences matter, any additive function of $x$ in $r(x,y)$ cancels out. Thus the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ is an acceptable degree of freedom: it is an under-specified part of the reward that does not affect pairwise comparisons for fixed $x$. The reparameterization shows that, up to that per-prefix constant, rewards can be read off directly from the policy via a scaled log-ratio against the reference.

# Partition function cancellation inside BT/PL preference likelihoods

Human preference datasets in RLHF typically consist of pairwise comparisons: for a given prompt $x$, annotators indicate that completion $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$, denoted $y_1 \succ y_2$. The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is a common probabilistic model of such pairwise preferences. Under BT, the probability that $y_1$ beats $y_2$ for prefix $x$ is

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)},
\]

where $r^*$ is the latent, true scoring function that generated the preferences. Crucially, BT depends only on the difference $r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)$; adding any function of $x$ to both rewards cancels in the ratio.

Plugging the reparameterization for $r^*$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and partition function $Z(x)$ into the BT expression, we get:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
= \frac{\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\bigr)}
{\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
+\exp\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\bigr)}.
\]

Because the $\beta\log Z(x)$ term is identical for both numerator and denominator, it cancels, leaving an expression that depends only on the relative log-ratios of the optimal policy to the reference:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
= \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\Bigr)}.
\]

Thus the unknown partition function $Z(x)$—which is expensive or impossible to compute in practice—drops out of the pairwise preference model. The cancellation is what makes it possible to express the BT likelihood purely in terms of policies, without ever explicitly estimating the reward or the partition function.

The same cancellation logic carries over to the more general Plackett–Luce (PL) ranking family: because PL likelihoods depend only on reward differences across items, any per-prefix additive constant in the reward vanishes from the likelihood. Therefore a reparameterization that differs from the true reward by a function of $x$ does not change the modeled preference probabilities.

# Policy-only likelihood of preferences

The cancellation above enables a shift of perspective: instead of fitting a separate reward model $r_\phi$ to the human comparison dataset and then running reinforcement learning to find a policy that maximizes that reward under a KL constraint, we can directly define a likelihood over policies. The idea is to treat the optimal policy (the one that would be obtained by maximizing reward with the KL penalty) as the object to be fit to the observed human comparisons.

Concretely, suppose we parameterize a policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and take a fixed reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ (for example, the supervised fine-tuned model used to generate the preference pairs). Under the BT model and the reparameterization, the probability that annotators prefer $y_w$ to $y_l$ becomes a logistic function of the difference of scaled log-ratios:

\[
p_\theta(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)
= \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr),
\]

where $\sigma(u)=1/(1+\exp(-u))$ is the logistic function. This expression is exactly the BT likelihood that would result from some reward in the equivalence class that our policy represents; by optimizing the policy to maximize this likelihood we implicitly recover a reward-class-consistent policy that explains the human preferences.

Given a dataset of preference triples $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, the (negative) log-likelihood for the policy becomes

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]

This is a policy-only likelihood: it directly measures how well a candidate policy's log-probability ratios relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ predict the human preferences. Optimizing this likelihood with respect to $\theta$ therefore seeks a policy that, under the DPO reparameterization, matches the distribution of human comparisons.

Two important conceptual points justify this approach. First, BT/PL models are under-specified up to additive functions of $x$ in the reward; that under-specification means there is no unique scalar reward assigned to each $(x,y)$ by preferences alone—only differences are identifiable. Second, for every reward function in the equivalence class consistent with preferences, Theorem 1 in the paper shows there exists a policy $\pi$ and the given $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ such that the reward can be written as $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ plus a per-prefix constant. Thus optimizing the policy likelihood is not restrictive: it searches the complete space of reward classes consistent with the preference model and directly targets the optimal policy that would have emerged through an RL step.

# DPO loss: logistic loss on relative log-probability ratios to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

Putting the previous steps together yields the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss, which is a simple, standard binary cross-entropy (logistic) loss applied to per-example scores built from the policy's log-probabilities relative to a reference. Writing $\widehat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ as the implicit reward induced by a candidate policy, the DPO loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma\!\bigl(\widehat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\widehat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr].
\]

This loss is algebraically identical to fitting a Bradley–Terry model where the scoring function is the implicit reward $\widehat{r}_\theta$. Crucially, because $\widehat{r}_\theta$ is constructed from the policy's log-probabilities, optimizing this loss over $\theta$ directly trains the language model to be the optimal policy under the KL-constrained reward maximization objective—without ever explicitly training a separate reward model or running reinforcement learning.

Examining the gradient provides further intuition for the dynamics of DPO. Differentiating the loss with respect to $\theta$ yields (up to the scalar $\beta$):

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sigma\bigl(\widehat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\widehat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

This gradient has a clear interpretation. For each preference pair, the update increases the log-probability of the preferred completion and decreases that of the dispreferred completion, exactly like a pairwise ranking objective or contrastive fine-tuning. The per-example multiplier $\sigma(\widehat{r}_\theta(y_l)-\widehat{r}_\theta(y_w))$ acts as a dynamic importance weight: when the model's current implicit reward already ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$ (i.e., the term is small), the weight is small; when the model mistakenly prefers $y_l$, the weight is larger, producing a stronger corrective push. The scalar $\beta$ scales the magnitude of these gradients, consistent with its role as a KL-temperature parameter.

This form also explains why a naive supervised objective that simply increases the probability of preferred completions and decreases that of dispreferred completions with a fixed weight (an "unlikelihood" style loss without the dynamic sigmoid weighting) can cause degeneration or instability. The dynamic weight in DPO enforces a principled per-example step size that depends on the model's current implicit ordering and the KL-penalty strength encoded by $\beta$, preventing excessive updates where the model is already consistent with preferences.

Operationally, DPO is simple to implement and train: given a dataset of preference pairs and a reference policy (typically the SFT model used to sample the labeled pairs), compute log-probabilities of each completion under the current model and the reference, form the pairwise score difference scaled by $\beta$, and optimize the logistic loss by standard gradient descent. There is no need to sample from the model during training for on-policy RL, no need to learn a separate value function or baseline, and no need to approximate partition functions.

In summary, the DPO derivation leverages three linked facts: (1) the KL-constrained optimal policy has an analytic Gibbs form; (2) the reward can be re-expressed as a scaled log-ratio between optimal and reference policies plus a per-prefix additive constant; and (3) under pairwise preference models like Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce that depend only on reward differences, that per-prefix constant cancels. These facts jointly allow reformulating preference learning as a direct supervised-like learning problem over policies, yielding the DPO loss: a simple logistic loss on differences of relative log-probabilities to the reference.
