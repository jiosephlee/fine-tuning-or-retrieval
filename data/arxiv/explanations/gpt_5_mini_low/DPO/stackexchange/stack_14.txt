\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why do the authors say DPO does not require a baseline term while actor–critic does?
Question:
In Eq. (10), the paper argues that actor–critic methods need a baseline (soft value) to reduce variance, while DPO’s reparameterization avoids this. Could someone explain more concretely why the term $-\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta} r_\phi(x,y))$ acts as a baseline in actor–critic, and why optimizing the pairwise logistic in Eq. (8) obviates any separate baseline/value function for variance reduction?
Answer:
Short answer: The term $-\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_\phi(x,y))$ in the actor–critic/RLHF formulation is the log of the partition function for the Boltzmann-optimal policy and plays the role of a soft value / baseline (it depends only on $x$, not on a particular sampled $y$). Actor–critic needs such a baseline when optimizing an expectation over stochastic samples because the return (or reward-to-go) has high Monte‑Carlo variance; estimating a good baseline/value reduces that variance but requires learning or Monte‑Carlo estimation. DPO avoids needing a separately estimated baseline because it reparameterizes the reward in terms of the policy, and the Bradley–Terry pairwise likelihood depends only on reward differences. The partition function cancels in those differences, so the loss becomes a deterministic pairwise binary‑classification objective (Eq. (8)) that does not require sampling under the current policy or estimating a value function for variance control.

A bit more detail and intuition, tied to the paper equations.

1) Why the normalization term is a baseline / soft value in actor–critic.  
Start from the constrained RL objective (Eq. (9) / Eq. (10) in the paper). The optimal policy given a reward $r$ is
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
$$
and rearranging gives the identity used in the paper (Eq. (7)):
$$
r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x),
\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
$$
When you write the actor–critic objective as an expectation over the policy (the form the paper gives in Eq. (AC)), the term
$$
f(r_\phi,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x) \;=\; r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\bigr)
$$
appears inside an expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\cdot]$. Because the second term depends only on $x$ (not on sampled $y$), it is exactly a baseline / value term: subtracting it from $r_\phi(x,y)$ centers the per-sample ``advantage’’ and therefore reduces the variance of the Monte‑Carlo estimate of the policy gradient. Intuitively it is a "soft value" because it is the log-partition (softmax) over rewards under the reference policy — the analogue of the expected value of future returns, but in the entropy-regularized/KL-constrained formulation.

2) Why actor–critic needs to estimate that baseline and why that can be unstable.  
In actor–critic / PPO you optimize an expectation over $y\sim\pi_\theta$. The gradient involves terms like $(\text{reward} - \text{baseline})\nabla \log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$. Monte‑Carlo samples of $r_\phi(x,y)$ are noisy, so without a good baseline the gradient variance is large. You can reduce variance by subtracting a learned value or by using a single-sample baseline (e.g., a human completion), but: (a) the true soft value here depends on $r_\phi$ through the partition function, so you would need to estimate it, and (b) learning that value function jointly with the critic and policy can be unstable (errors in the value affect the policy updates and vice versa). The paper points this out when it says the normalization term “does not affect the optimal solution, but without it, the policy gradient of the objective could have high variance” and that accommodating it requires a learned value function or Monte‑Carlo approximations.

3) Why DPO obviates a separate baseline/value.  
DPO performs a reparameterization: it represents the reward class by
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x),
$$
but crucially the Bradley–Terry preference probability depends only on reward differences:
$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr).
$$
When you substitute the reparameterized reward, the $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms cancel, leaving a preference model that depends only on the differences $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. Hence the DPO loss (Eq. (8) in the paper)
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\big)\Big]
$$
is a supervised pairwise logistic objective that does not contain $Z(x)$ at all. Two consequences follow. First, there is no partition-function / soft‑value term to estimate, so no separate value/baseline parameter is needed. Second, the loss is evaluated on a fixed offline dataset of (prompt, preferred, dispreferred) pairs sampled from the reference policy, so you are not taking a Monte‑Carlo expectation over $y\sim\pi_\theta$ during training; instead you directly optimize a log‑likelihood on observed pairs. The training signal is therefore a lower‑variance, supervised binary classification gradient rather than a high‑variance REINFORCE‑style return estimate.

The paper’s gradient expression makes this explicit: the DPO gradient is proportional to
$$
-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big],
$$
where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. This is a deterministic weighting (computed from the current $\pi_\theta$ and the observed pair) applied to two log‑probability gradients. There is no stochastic return sample whose variance must be reduced by a baseline; the only randomness is the offline dataset and any minibatch sampling, so variance is much lower and no learned baseline is required.

Summary: the normalization term in the actor–critic objective is a soft value / baseline needed to center high‑variance Monte‑Carlo rewards when optimizing an expectation under $\pi_\theta$. DPO’s reparameterization moves the optimization into a pairwise supervised likelihood that uses reward differences (where the partition function cancels). That cancellation both removes the need to estimate a baseline/value and replaces a high‑variance policy‑gradient update with a lower‑variance pairwise classification update.
