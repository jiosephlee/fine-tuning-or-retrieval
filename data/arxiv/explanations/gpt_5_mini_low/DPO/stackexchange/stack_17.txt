\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does DPO require on-policy data at any point, or is it purely offline once $\mathcal{D}$ is fixed?
Question:
One big appeal is removing the RL loop. But I want to confirm: after constructing $\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}$ offline (e.g., from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$), can DPO training proceed entirely offline with no need to resample new pairs from the evolving $\pi_\theta$? If so, are there any known failure modes from distributional shift during training (e.g., learned policy moving into regions unsupported by $\mathcal{D}$) and how are they mitigated?
Answer:
Short answer: Once you have your preference dataset $D = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$ built (typically by sampling $y$ from a fixed reference policy $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ and asking humans to compare pairs), DPO training can proceed entirely offline — you do not need to resample from the evolving $\pi_\theta$ during fine‑tuning. The paper explicitly designs DPO to avoid an RL sampling loop and shows the optimization is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss over the fixed dataset (Eq. 10 / Eq. $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$).  

Why this is possible (intuitive+technical): DPO reparameterizes the usual KL‑constrained RL objective so that the policy itself implicitly encodes the reward: the implicit reward is
$\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)}$.
Under the Bradley–Terry preference model the partition function cancels when you take reward differences, so the likelihood of a human pref pair can be written purely in terms of $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ (Eq. 9). That yields the offline loss
$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim D}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w|x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l|x)}\right]\right)\right]$,
so you only need the fixed triples in $D$ and the fixed $\pi_\text{ref}$ to compute gradients. No on‑policy sampling is required during gradient updates.

Known failure modes and mitigations (as discussed in the paper): The authors discuss several practical concerns that amount to distributional shift / instability risks, and how DPO mitigates them.

1) Drift into regions unsupported by $D$. Because $D$ is sampled from $\pi_\text{ref}$, preference labels are informative mainly about areas where $\pi_\text{ref}$ produces candidates. If $\pi_\theta$ drifts far away, its implicit reward estimates become unconstrained by data. DPO explicitly anchors $\pi_\theta$ to $\pi_\text{ref}$ via the $\beta$ parameter: the implicit reward is proportional to the log ratio to $\pi_\text{ref}$, so the KL penalty (controlled by $\beta$) penalizes large deviations. In practice the authors recommend initializing $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ (the SFT used to generate $D$) and selecting $\beta$ to enforce conservativeness; this is the main mechanism preventing catastrophic drift (Sec. 3, Sec. 4.1, Theorem 1 intuition).

2) Degeneration from a naive ratio objective. The paper shows that a naïve objective that only increases preferred likelihood can cause degeneration (mode collapse / unlikelihood issues). DPO’s gradient contains a per‑example weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l) - \hat r_\theta(y_w))$ which upweights examples where the current implicit reward misorders the pair; this dynamic weighting is necessary to stabilize learning (Sec. 3, gradient discussion). Appendix experiments show that leaving out this weighting harms stability.

3) Under‑specification of rewards and partition normalization concerns. In actor‑critic / PPO pipelines, the normalization term (partition function / soft value) must be estimated and can destabilize RL. DPO sidesteps explicit reward fitting and value estimation entirely by reparameterizing into the policy, removing the need for on‑policy rollouts to compute or bootstrap that normalization term (Sec. 4.2). That is a mitigation relative to RLHF.

4) OOD generalization is still a concern. The authors ran preliminary transfer experiments (Reddit → CNN/DM) and found DPO generalizes at least as well as PPO in their tests (Sec. 5.3). Still, they explicitly call out that more work is needed to understand how DPO behaves out of distribution (Discussion / Limitations). If you expect queries far from the prompts used to form $D$, the safe actions are: (a) keep $\beta$ conservative so $\pi_\theta$ stays near $\pi_\text{ref}$, (b) ensure $\pi_\text{ref}$ is fitted to the same distribution that generated $D$ (they suggest re‑fitting $\pi_\text{ref}$ to preferred completions if the original SFT is unavailable), (c) monitor sequence‑level KL and sampling behavior, and (d) collect new preference labels in newly encountered regions (i.e., iterate the data collection) or use controlled self‑labeling only cautiously.

Practical recipe based on the paper: sample candidate pairs from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ and collect preferences once; set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ (or fit $\pi_\text{ref}$ to the preferred completions if needed); run DPO offline on $D$ with a $\beta$ controlling how much you allow $\pi_\theta$ to deviate; monitor $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_\text{ref})$ and sample quality; if you see drift or poor OOD behavior, either increase conservativeness (larger effective KL penalty via $\beta$), refit $\pi_\text{ref}$ to preferred data, or collect additional preference labels for the problematic regions.

Bottom line: DPO is intended and shown to work as an offline algorithm once $D$ is fixed, and its formulation includes an explicit anchoring to the reference policy that mitigates, but does not eliminate, distributional‑shift failure modes. The remaining failure modes (reward over‑optimization, OOD generalization) are acknowledged by the authors and are handled in practice by choosing $\beta$, keeping $\pi_\text{ref}$ aligned with the data, monitoring KL, and, if necessary, collecting more labeled pairs.
