\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How are sequence probabilities computed in Eq. (8) for autoregressive LMs, and does DPO suffer from length bias?
Question:
Eq. (8) uses terms like $\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$. For an autoregressive LM, is this the sum of token-level log-probabilities along $y$ including an end-of-sequence token, i.e. $\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})$? If yes, does DPO inherit length biases (e.g., longer $y$ accumulating larger-magnitude log-prob sums)? Do we need any normalization (per-token averaging) or special handling (like explicit length penalties or EOS handling) to make the pairwise comparisons in Eq. (8) well-behaved?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — treat the sequence log-probability as the usual autoregressive sum over token log-probabilities (including EOS). DPO operates on sequence-level log-probabilities, so no extra per-token normalization is required by the algorithm as presented, but you should take practical care to avoid systematic length-mismatch artifacts (use a matching reference model and consistent EOS handling). Below I explain why length effects are largely handled by the DPO formulation, what to watch out for, and simple mitigations.

What "$\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$" means
In an autoregressive LM this is
$$
\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
$$
where the sequence $y$ normally includes the generated EOS token (so the sum runs through EOS). The DPO objective in Eq. (8) (Eq. (11) in the paper’s notation) uses those sequence-level log-probabilities.

Why DPO does not force you to average per-token
DPO compares two completed sequences by the difference of their sequence-level log-probabilities relative to a reference:
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
The loss uses the difference $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)$. Because this is a log-ratio, many simple length effects cancel: if both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ score longer sequences proportionally lower (or higher) just because they are longer, the same multiplicative length factor will appear in numerator and denominator and cancel in the ratio. In other words, DPO is operating on the relative sequence probability under the current model versus the reference, not on raw magnitudes of the log-probability alone.

When length can still matter
If preferred and dispreferred sequences have different lengths and the model and reference assign length-dependent penalties differently (e.g., $\pi_\theta$ becomes systematically more/less confident on longer outputs than $\pi_{\text{ref}}$), then the log-ratio will reflect that mismatch and could produce a length-dependent bias. Similarly, if the dataset of preference pairs is systematically biased by length (labelers prefer longer summaries, or the sampled $y_w$/$y_l$ construction encourages certain lengths), that can show up in learned behavior. The paper acknowledges sequence-level KL is computed as the sum of per-timestep KLs (footnote), so they are explicitly working at sequence granularity.

Why DPO still avoids some common instabilities
A key point of DPO is that it parameterizes the implicit reward as the log-ratio against a reference, so it does not require fitting an explicit reward baseline or estimating a partition function. The DPO gradient also includes a sigmoid weight on how badly the implicit reward orders the pair, which downweights extreme pushes and helps avoid collapse that naive unweighted pairwise objectives can cause (paper and Appendix note this empirically). Those design choices help stability even without per-token normalization.

Practical recommendations
1. Include EOS consistently: compute $\log \pi(y\mid x)$ through the EOS token for both model and reference. Inconsistent EOS handling between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ will create artifacts.  
2. Use a matched reference model: the paper recommends (and uses) $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^\text{SFT}$ or a model fit to the preferred completions so the reference distribution matches how the dataset was collected. This reduces distributional/length mismatches that could otherwise bias the log-ratio.  
3. Monitor length-related signals: during development, check whether DPO is systematically increasing or decreasing generation lengths compared to SFT; if you detect unwanted length bias, two reasonable mitigations are (a) adjust $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (train it on preferred completions so length behavior matches the data), or (b) add an explicit length penalty term at evaluation/training time (e.g., include length as an auxiliary feature in your preference sampling or apply minimal per-token normalization) — but these are engineering fixes, not required by the DPO derivation.  
4. Don’t naively replace DPO’s weighting: the paper shows that removing the sigmoid/weighting term leads to degeneration. Keep the formulation that uses the log-ratio and its sigmoid weighting (i.e., the loss in Eq. (8)/(11)).

Summary
Yes — use the usual autoregressive sum including EOS. DPO’s use of log-ratios versus a reference largely cancels out trivial length-scaling effects, so no per-token averaging is mathematically required by the method. Still, in practice you must ensure consistent EOS handling and a well-matched reference model (as the paper emphasizes) to avoid systematic length-dependent artifacts; if such artifacts appear, address them by matching the reference distribution or applying a simple length penalty rather than altering the core DPO objective.
