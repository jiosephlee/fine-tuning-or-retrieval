\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the precise relationship between DPO and PPO with a learned reward—do they optimize the same fixed point?
Question:
The paper states DPO "optimizes the same objective" as PPO-based RLHF with a KL constraint. However, PPO typically alternates between policy updates and value/baseline estimation and may use clipped objectives. Under idealized assumptions (infinite data, exact optimization, no clipping), do DPO and PPO converge to the same $\pi^*$ that satisfies Eq. (7)? If so, can someone outline a proof sketch that the DPO MLE solution corresponds to the fixed point of the KL-regularized policy improvement used by PPO when the reward comes from Bradley–Terry-consistent preferences?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — under the idealized assumptions you list (infinite data, exact optimization, no clipping, unlimited model capacity, and that preferences truly follow a Bradley–Terry (BT) model) DPO’s MLE solution and the (exact) solution of the KL‑regularized RL objective optimized by PPO coincide: both recover the same policy $\pi^*$ that satisfies Eq. (7). The paper gives the change‑of‑variables that makes this equivalence transparent; below is a concise proof sketch that connects the two viewpoints and shows they share the same fixed point.

Proof sketch (intuitive + technical):

1) KL‑constrained RL has an analytic optimal policy for a given reward. Start from the RLHF objective (Eq. (3) in the paper)
$$
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[\,r(x,y)\,]-\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)).
$$
For fixed reward function $r$ and fixed $\beta$, standard variational calculus / control-as-inference arguments yield the closed‑form optimizer (Eq. (4) in the paper)
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z_r(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
$$
where $Z_r(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y))$. So for any reward $r$, the policy that maximizes the constrained objective is $\pi_r$.

2) BT preferences only depend on reward differences, so the partition term cancels when expressing pairwise preference probabilities. The Bradley–Terry model says
$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
$$
where $\sigma$ is the logistic. Substitute the reparameterization obtained by inverting the optimal policy (take logs of $\pi_r$ and rearrange):
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z_r(x).
$$
Because BT uses differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, the $\beta\log Z_r(x)$ cancels and you obtain (Eq. (7) in the paper)
$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
= \sigma\!\bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\bigr).
$$

3) DPO fits $\pi$ directly by MLE under the BT model reparameterized in terms of $\pi$. The DPO loss (Eq. (9) in the paper) is the negative log‑likelihood of human pairwise data under the BT model after the change of variables:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\big)\Big].
$$
With infinite data and exact optimization (and sufficient model capacity), MLE returns the unique $\pi^*$ whose induced pairwise probabilities match the true $p^*$. By step (2), that $\pi^*$ satisfies the identity in Eq. (7), i.e. it is the $\pi_r$ corresponding to some reward in the BT equivalence class.

4) PPO (or any exact optimizer of the KL‑regularized RL objective) recovers the same $\pi^*$ when it is given the (MLE) reward consistent with the preferences. Suppose we first fit an (exact) reward $r_\phi$ from the same pairwise data by maximizing the BT likelihood (the usual reward‑model step). Under infinite data and exact reward fitting, $r_\phi$ is in the correct equivalence class of $r^*$ (differing only by an $x$-dependent constant). Then the optimal policy for that reward via the KL‑constrained objective is $\pi_{r_\phi}$ from step (1). But by the reparameterization algebra in step (2) / (3), $\pi_{r_\phi}$ must satisfy the same implicit equation as the DPO MLE solution. Thus the fixed point policy returned by exact optimization of the RL objective (i.e., the idealized PPO) equals the DPO MLE solution $\pi^*$.

5) Put together: DPO directly optimizes the same self‑consistency condition that the KL‑regularized RL objective enforces via the reward model + RL loop. Under the assumptions (BT‑consistent preferences, infinite data, exact reward estimation, exact optimization of the policy objective, same $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\beta$), both procedures have the same fixed point $\pi^*$ characterized by Eq. (7).

Remarks and caveats (why this is “idealized”): The equivalence depends on multiple idealizations made explicit in the paper. In practice PPO is implemented with function approximation, alternating optimization, a learned value/baseline, clipping, finite and noisy reward estimates, and sample‑based policy gradients; these practicalities can prevent PPO from reaching the exact fixed point and introduce instability or bias (the paper discusses these practical instabilities). DPO sidesteps the explicit reward step and the RL loop by optimizing the same consistency condition directly, which is why the paper finds DPO empirically more stable and efficient even though the underlying objectives coincide in the ideal limit.

Summary: The paper’s change of variables shows the mapping $r \leftrightarrow \pi_r$ and that BT depends only on reward differences, so the partition cancels. Therefore an MLE over $\pi$ (DPO) finds the same policy that would be the exact solution of KL‑regularized reward maximization (the idealized PPO solution) when preferences are BT‑consistent and all optimization and estimation are done perfectly.
