# The Theory in Plain English: Equivalence Classes, Identifiability, and Stability

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is an elegant simplification of the usual RL-from-human-preferences pipeline. The empirical results are compelling, but the paper’s cleanest contribution is theoretical: a change of variables that turns reward learning + RL into a single, well-behaved supervised objective on the policy itself. This post walks through the key theory intuitively—what the authors mean by reward equivalence classes, why the DPO reparameterization doesn't restrict what you can learn, and how this change removes common sources of instability in actor–critic approaches such as PPO.

## The setup in one paragraph

Start with what people normally do in RLHF:

- Collect human pairwise comparisons (for prompt x, humans prefer y_w to y_l).
- Fit a reward function r(x,y) so that preference probabilities follow a Plackett–Luce / Bradley–Terry model:
  p(y1 ≻ y2 | x) = σ(r(x,y1) − r(x,y2)), where σ is the logistic function.
- Use the learned reward rφ to optimize a policy πθ to maximize expected reward subject to a KL penalty that keeps π close to a reference (SFT) model π_ref:
  maximize E_{y∼π}[rφ(x,y)] − β KL[π || π_ref].

Two big practical complications come from this decomposition: (1) the reward is identified only up to an additive function of x, and (2) optimizing π with RL typically needs a value baseline / critic and on-policy sampling, which introduces variance and instability.

DPO’s core trick is to reparameterize the reward class so the optimal policy can be written analytically in terms of the reward, invert that relationship, and then directly fit π using a simple cross-entropy style objective derived from the Bradley–Terry model. No separate reward network and no RL loop.

## Reward equivalence classes — what they are and why they matter

A central technical observation is that, under the Plackett–Luce (and hence Bradley–Terry) family of preference models, certain changes to the reward do not affect preferences. Specifically, if you add any function f(x) that depends only on the prompt x (and not on the completion y), the pairwise differences r(x,y1) − r(x,y2) stay the same. So two reward functions r and r' are indistinguishable from preference data when

r(x,y) − r'(x,y) = f(x) for some f.

This defines an equivalence relation that partitions reward functions into equivalence classes. Important consequences:

- Any reward in the same equivalence class yields the same pairwise preference probabilities under Bradley–Terry (Lemma 1 in the paper).
- For the KL-constrained optimization problem used in RLHF, all rewards in the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy (Lemma 2).

So preference data alone cannot identify a unique reward function—only the equivalence class matters. That under-specification is not a bug; it’s a property of the problem we must handle correctly.

## The DPO reparameterization — represent any reward class without loss

DPO chooses a specific canonical representative from each equivalence class. The key algebraic fact is that, for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem, the optimal policy corresponding to a reward r has the closed-form:

π_r(y | x) = (1 / Z(x)) π_ref(y | x) exp(r(x,y) / β),

where Z(x) is the partition function (normalizer) depending on r. Rearranging, one gets

r(x,y) = β log [π_r(y | x) / π_ref(y | x)] + β log Z(x).

Crucially, preferences (Bradley–Terry) depend only on reward differences, and the additive β log Z(x) cancels when we look at r(x,y1) − r(x,y2). So we can represent any reward equivalence class by the simpler form

r(x,y) = β log [π(y | x) / π_ref(y | x)],

where π is now interpreted as the optimal policy associated with that class. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that this representation can produce a member of every equivalence class (i.e., no loss of generality): for any reward r, you can subtract a prompt-dependent constant (the log partition function) to get a reward of exactly this form.

Why this matters: instead of fitting rφ and then running RL to get π, DPO directly optimizes π in the maximum-likelihood objective implied by Bradley–Terry, substituting r(x,y) with β log [π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)]. That yields a simple cross-entropy-style loss on the policy:

L_DPO(πθ; π_ref) = −E_{(x,y_w,y_l)∼D} [ log σ( β log [πθ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)] − β log [πθ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)] ) ].

Because the change of variables covers every reward class, optimizing this objective recovers the same optimal policies that the full RLHF pipeline could produce—without ever explicitly estimating r or sampling trajectories in an RL loop.

## What the DPO update does (intuitively)

Take the gradient of L_DPO with respect to θ. You can rewrite it to show a few clear effects:

- The update increases log π(y_w | x) (makes preferred completions more likely) and decreases log π(y_l | x) (penalizes dispreferred completions).
- Each pairwise example is weighted by σ(ĥrθ(x,y_l) − ĥrθ(x,y_w)), where ĥrθ(x,y) := β log [πθ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)] is the implicit reward corresponding to the current policy.

This weighting has a natural interpretation: examples where the model currently rates y_l higher than y_w (i.e., when the implicit reward ordering is wrong) receive larger gradient weight. The β factor controls how strongly the implicit reward shapes updates, mirroring the KL penalty in the original RL formulation. Practically, this per-example adaptive weighting prevents collapse modes that would happen if you simply tried to maximize log π(y_w) and minimize log π(y_l) with a fixed coefficient (the paper observes that the naive unweighted "unlikelihood" objective can make models degenerate).

## Why this avoids instabilities tied to actor–critic value baselines

In actor–critic style RLHF (e.g., PPO), after fitting a reward model rφ, you optimize a policy πθ with a KL penalty. Written differently, the objective being minimized can be expressed as

E_{y∼πθ} [ rφ(x,y) − β log Σ_y π_ref(y|x) exp(rφ(x,y)/β) − β log (πθ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) ].

The normalization term inside the expectation, f(rφ, π_ref, β) := rφ(x,y) − β log Σ_y π_ref(y|x) exp(rφ(x,y)/β), behaves like a soft value function. In practice you either:

- Estimate that normalization / value term with a learned critic, or
- Use Monte Carlo baselines (single-sample approximations), or
- Normalize rewards heuristically.

All of these introduce variance or bias: learning a critic is another unstable supervised regression that must stay synchronized with the actor; single-sample baselines have high variance; reward normalization can be ad-hoc.

DPO sidesteps this entire sub-problem. By parameterizing the reward via the policy (r = β log π/π_ref + const), the normalization term cancels analytically when converting to pairwise preference probabilities. The policy objective becomes a straightforward supervised objective with low-variance gradients (no return estimation, no value learning). In short:

- No separate reward network → no reward misfit between reward model and policy optimization.
- No critic / baseline → fewer moving parts and reduced variance.
- Optimization is just maximum-likelihood-like training of πθ, which is stable and familiar.

This explains the empirical finding that DPO obtains a much more favorable reward-vs-KL frontier than PPO, and that it can even outperform PPO that has access to ground-truth reward in some controlled experiments.

## Practical implications and caveats

- DPO preserves representational generality: because it picks a canonical representative in each equivalence class, it does not restrict the set of achievable optimal policies under the Bradley–Terry model.
- The β hyperparameter still matters: it sets the effective strength of the KL constraint (how much the policy is allowed to deviate from π_ref). In practice it needs to be tuned, but the training is otherwise far simpler than RLHF.
- DPO relies on the assumption that preferences follow a model like Bradley–Terry. This is the same assumption used by prior RLHF reward-fitting methods, so it is not an extra limitation compared to the standard pipeline.
- DPO removes the need to estimate a value baseline, but it still needs a good reference policy π_ref. In practice π_ref is the SFT model used to generate the comparison dataset, or it can be fit to preferred completions when unavailable.

## Bottom line

DPO’s theory reframes the preference-learning problem: since preferences only identify rewards up to prompt-dependent constants, use that freedom to reparameterize rewards in terms of the optimal policy. This yields a policy objective that (a) is equivalent to optimizing the original KL-constrained RL objective over the appropriate reward class, (b) avoids reward-model misalignment and the variance of actor–critic training, and (c) reduces learning to a supervised, low-variance optimization problem. The result is a method that is simpler, more stable in practice, and theoretically grounded: you haven't thrown away power, you've just removed an underdetermined middleman.
