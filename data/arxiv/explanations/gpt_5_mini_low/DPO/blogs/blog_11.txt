# Case Study: How DPO Improves Summarization Quality

In this post I walk through how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) improves summarization on the TL;DR task from the paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model". I cover the dataset setup, how win-rates were computed against reference summaries, the empirical win-rate comparisons (DPO vs PPO and other baselines), why DPO is more robust to sampling temperature than PPO, and the initial evidence that DPO generalizes better across domains (TL;DR → CNN/DailyMail).

## TL;DR dataset and evaluation setup

- Dataset: Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset and the human preference annotations collected by prior work (Stiennon et al.). The preference dataset contains triples (prompt x, preferred summary y_w, dispreferred summary y_l) sampled from some SFT model. The SFT model used in experiments was a GPT-J model fine-tuned on human-written TL;DR summaries (the same SFT checkpoint used by the TRLX framework).
- Training objective: DPO directly optimizes a policy \(\pi_\theta\) to explain the human preference data under a Bradley–Terry preference model, using the loss
  \[
  \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\big)\Big],
  \]
  where \(\pi_\text{ref}\) is the reference (SFT) model and \(\beta\) controls the KL-style conservativeness.
- Evaluation method: For TL;DR the authors measure win-rate of model-generated summaries against the reference summaries in the test set. Win-rate judgments are made using GPT-4 as an automated judge (two GPT-4 prompts were studied; the "concise" variant GPT-4 (C) was used for main results). The authors validated GPT-4 judgments against human raters and report that GPT-4 agreement with humans is roughly comparable to inter-human agreement, supporting using GPT-4 as a proxy.
- Sampling: Completions were sampled at a range of temperatures (0.0 → 1.0) to compare how models behave under different decoding stochasticity.

## Win-rate comparisons: DPO vs PPO and baselines

Key empirical findings reported for TL;DR (same SFT model for DPO and PPO):

- DPO achieves approximately 61% win-rate (GPT-4 judged) at temperature 0.0, exceeding PPO's best performance of ~57% at its optimal temperature of 0.0.
- DPO attains a higher maximum win-rate than the Best-of-\(N\) baseline (where \(N\) responses are sampled and the top-scoring one under a learned reward model is chosen). This is notable because Best-of-\(N\) decouples reward-model quality from RL optimization and is computationally expensive at inference time.
- Preferred-FT (supervised fine-tuning on chosen completions) did not significantly outperform the SFT model on this task.
- The authors did not perform extensive tuning of DPO's \(\beta\), so reported DPO performance is likely conservative.

These results show DPO matching or beating PPO on real summarization preferences while being much simpler to implement and train.

## Why DPO handles sampling temperature better than PPO

Empirically, the paper finds that DPO’s performance is more robust to sampling temperature: PPO’s win-rate can degrade toward the SFT baseline at higher temperatures, while DPO maintains stronger performance across temperatures. There are several complementary reasons for this robustness, grounded in the algorithmic differences highlighted in the paper:

1. Direct policy objective vs. actor-critic loop. PPO-based RLHF follows a two-step process: (a) fit an explicit reward model \(r_\phi(x,y)\) from preferences, then (b) run RL (PPO) to optimize a policy for expected reward with a KL penalty to \(\pi_\text{ref}\). DPO instead parameterizes the reward implicitly as
   \[
   \hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}
   \]
   and fits \(\pi_\theta\) directly to maximize likelihood of observed preferences (see the DPO loss above). This avoids the delicate actor-critic dynamics (policy updates + learned value baseline) that can destabilize PPO and affect how the trained policy behaves under different decoding temperatures.

2. Built-in conservativeness and dynamic weighting. The DPO gradient (from the paper) can be written informally as
   \[
   \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} \propto -\beta\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\big)\right].
   \]
   The sigmoid factor re-weights each pair's contribution based on how much the current implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta\) misorders the pair. This per-example importance weighting reduces catastrophic pushes on probability mass (preventing overconfidence) and implicitly regularizes distance from \(\pi_\text{ref}\) via the \(\hat r_\theta\) definition. In contrast, naive unlikelihood objectives (or poorly tuned PPO) can either under-correct or over-correct and produce policies that are brittle under sampling.

3. No need for Monte Carlo value normalization / baselines. PPO training requires sampled rollouts, value estimation, and reward normalization; mistakes or variance in those components can produce a policy that performs well at greedy decoding (temperature near 0) but degrades quickly as sampling temperature increases. Because DPO directly fits the policy to match observed pairwise preferences (and its reparameterization removes the need to estimate a partition function), it avoids those sources of variance.

Taken together, these points explain why DPO produces summaries that remain high-quality both when decoded greedily and when some sampling is used, whereas PPO-trained policies are more sensitive to decoding temperature.

## Cross-domain generalization: TL;DR → CNN/DailyMail

The paper includes an experiment testing out-of-distribution generalization: policies trained on Reddit TL;DR preference data were evaluated on the CNN/DailyMail test set (news articles), with win-rate measured by GPT-4 against ground-truth news summaries. Using the best sampling temperatures chosen from the TL;DR validation (temperatures 0 and 0.25), DPO outperformed the PPO policy by a significant margin on CNN/DailyMail.

This initial result provides early evidence that DPO policies generalize at least as well as PPO policies across input distributions, despite DPO not using additional unlabeled prompts that PPO uses in its training pipeline. The takeaway is that the stability and directness of DPO's objective may help produce policies whose preference-consistent behavior transfers better to new domains, though the authors emphasize that more comprehensive studies are needed.

## Practical takeaways

- If you have a preference dataset (triples of prompt, preferred completion, dispreferred completion) and an SFT reference model, DPO offers a simple, stable way to obtain a preference-aligned policy without fitting a separate reward model or running RL.
- For TL;DR summarization with GPT-J SFT models, DPO achieved higher GPT-4 win-rates (≈61%) than PPO (≈57%) at their best sampling settings, and is less sensitive to decoding temperature.
- DPO also showed promising cross-domain results when evaluated on CNN/DailyMail despite being trained on Reddit TL;DR preferences, indicating initial robustness to domain shift.
- The DPO objective is straightforward to implement (a binary cross-entropy on transformed log-prob ratios) and avoids the sampling, value-function estimation, and hyperparameter tuning inherent to PPO-style RLHF.

## Closing note

These TL;DR results are a strong case that the DPO reparameterization — treating the policy as implicitly defining the reward via \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}\) and optimizing the induced Bradley–Terry likelihood — is not just theoretically elegant but practically effective for preference-guided summarization. The paper highlights that DPO reduces engineering complexity while matching or exceeding PPO on real preference datasets; the TL;DR case study is a concrete demonstration of that claim.
