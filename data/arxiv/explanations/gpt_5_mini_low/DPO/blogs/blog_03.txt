# Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model

## TL;DR
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) shows that you don't need a separate reward model plus an RL loop to learn from pairwise human preferences. If you parameterize the reward the right way, you can read the reward off the policy. Concretely, under the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective, an optimal policy corresponds to a reward of the form
r(x,y) = β log(π(y|x) / π_ref(y|x)),
and that reparameterization lets you train the policy directly with a simple binary cross-entropy loss on preference pairs. This yields the same optimal solution as RLHF but without reward fitting, sampling-in-the-loop, or PPO-style instability.

## The setup in plain language
RLHF workflows typically have three steps:
1. Start from a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) language model π_ref.
2. Generate pairs of candidate completions for prompts, collect human pairwise preferences (y_w preferred over y_l).
3. Fit a reward model r_φ to explain those preferences (often with a Bradley–Terry / logistic model), then run RL (e.g., PPO) to obtain a policy π_θ that maximizes reward while staying close to π_ref (controlled by a KL penalty).

This two-stage approach (learn reward, then optimize policy with RL) works, but it's complex and computationally heavy. DPO asks: can we skip the explicit reward fitting and RL step while still optimizing the same constrained objective?

## The key identity: reward ↔ policy
Start from the standard KL-constrained objective used in RLHF:
maximize over policies π:
E_{x, y∼π(·|x)}[ r(x,y) ] − β D_KL(π(·|x) || π_ref(·|x)).

For this objective one can show the optimal policy (for fixed reward function r) has the Boltzmann form
π_r(y|x) = (1 / Z(x)) · π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β ),
where Z(x) is a partition function that normalizes the distribution.

Re-arrange that equation to express r in terms of the optimal policy π_r:
r(x,y) = β log( π_r(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + β log Z(x).

The additive term β log Z(x) depends only on x and not on y. This is important in the next step.

## Preferences depend only on reward differences
Human pairwise preferences are modeled with Bradley–Terry (logistic) style probabilities:
P(y_1 ≻ y_2 | x) = σ( r(x,y_1) − r(x,y_2) ).

Because this depends only on reward differences, the additive term β log Z(x) cancels out when you substitute the reparameterization for r. After cancellation you get a preference model directly in terms of the optimal policy and π_ref:

P(y_1 ≻ y_2 | x) = σ( β log( π(y_1|x) / π_ref(y_1|x) ) − β log( π(y_2|x) / π_ref(y_2|x) ) ).

This expression says: if the policy assigns relatively higher probability (relative to π_ref) to y_1 than y_2, then y_1 is preferred. So the policy itself implicitly defines the reward differences that drive preferences.

## Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective
Given a dataset of preference triples (x, y_w, y_l), DPO directly fits the policy π_θ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences under the formula above. Equivalently, minimize the binary cross-entropy loss

L_DPO(π_θ; π_ref) = − E_{(x,y_w,y_l)} [ log σ( β log( π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x) ) − β log( π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x) ) ) ].

This is a supervised learning objective (no RL). Intuitively, it pushes up the policy probability of preferred completions and pushes down the dispreferred ones, but critically it does so in a way weighted by the current implicit reward gap (via the logistic).

## Why this doesn't reduce expressivity
A natural worry: are we restricting the class of reward functions by forcing r(x,y) = β log(π/π_ref) up to an x-dependent constant? The paper explains why this is not a real restriction for preference learning:

- Preference models like Bradley–Terry only depend on reward differences r(x,y1) − r(x,y2). Adding any function f(x) to r(x,y) (i.e., re-centering by an x-only term) does not change preferences. Thus rewards are identifiable only up to an additive function of x.
- The reparameterization r(x,y) = β log(π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β log Z(x) picks one representative from each equivalence class (it normalizes so the induced Boltzmann policy is a proper distribution). Theorem 1 in the paper shows that for any reward function in the equivalence class that matters for preferences, there exists a policy π such that its log ratio to π_ref recovers a member of that class.
- Therefore, DPO can represent any reward function that is meaningful for preference prediction (i.e., any reward up to an x-dependent baseline), and so it does not lose representational power relevant to the preference data.

In short: reward functions that differ only by an additive function of x are indistinguishable from preference data, and DPO's parameterization picks a canonical representative of each indistinguishable class.

## What's happening in the gradient
Differentiating the DPO loss shows the following behavior. Let the implicit reward estimate be
ĥr_θ(x,y) = β log( π_θ(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ).
Then the gradient of the loss (w.r.t. θ) for a pair (y_w, y_l) contains a multiplicative weight σ(ĥr_θ(x,y_l) − ĥr_θ(x,y_w)) times the usual log-probability gradients:
- +∇_θ log π_θ(y_w|x) (increase preferred example likelihood),
- −∇_θ log π_θ(y_l|x) (decrease dispreferred example likelihood).

The weight σ(ĥr_θ(y_l) − ĥr_θ(y_w)) is large when the model currently ranks the pair incorrectly (i.e., when ĥr_θ rates y_l higher than y_w), and small when the model already ranks them correctly. That dynamic per-example weight stabilizes updates compared with a naive likelihood-ratio style objective and prevents degenerative collapse.

## Practical benefits
Because DPO directly optimizes the policy via a standard classification loss, it has several practical advantages over the typical RLHF pipeline:
- No separate reward model to fit and no RL optimizer loop (no sampling from the policy during training).
- No need for value networks or baselines to estimate partition functions or stabilize actor-critic updates.
- Simpler implementation (standard MLE-style fine-tuning) and less hyperparameter tuning.
- Theoretically it optimizes the same KL-constrained objective as RLHF: it recovers the same optimal policy class.

The experiments in the paper show that DPO matches or improves on PPO-based RLHF across tasks like controlled sentiment, Reddit TL;DR summarization, and single-turn dialogue while being computationally simpler and more stable.

## Limitations and open questions
The paper notes several open points for further work:
- How do DPO-trained policies generalize out-of-distribution compared to policies trained via explicit reward + RL? Initial experiments indicate similar or better generalization in the tested setups, but more study is needed.
- How does reward over-optimization (i.e., exploiting weaknesses of the implicit reward) manifest in DPO?
- Scaling to very large models remains to be explored.
- The approach relies on the preference model (Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce). Extensions to other preference/noise models are possible but require care.

## Bottom line
If your goal is to train a language model from pairwise preferences under the common KL-constrained RLHF formulation, you can think of the policy as implicitly encoding reward differences via log-probability ratios to the reference model. DPO exploits this identity to train the policy directly with a simple cross-entropy loss, avoiding explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning while still targeting the same optimal solutions. In practice this yields a simpler, more stable, and computationally lighter path to preference-aligned language models.
