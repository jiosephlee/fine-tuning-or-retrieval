# Understanding the Reward–KL Trade-off Without RL

## Why a KL constraint matters when aligning LMs

When we fine-tune a language model to follow human preferences, the objective is intuitively simple: make the model produce outputs humans prefer. But optimizing for preference or reward alone can be dangerous in practice. If a model aggressively chases a learned reward function, two failure modes commonly appear:

- Mode collapse and loss of diversity: the model can assign extremely high probability mass to a small set of completions that score well under the learned reward, producing repetitive or overly specific outputs.
- Reward exploitation and misgeneralization: the policy can drift into regions of the output space where the learned reward is unreliable (because the reward model was trained on a limited distribution), producing superficially high-reward but low-quality or unsafe outputs.

To prevent these problems, RL-from-human-feedback (RLHF) methods typically add a KL-divergence penalty that constrains the fine-tuned policy to stay close to a reference policy (usually the supervised-finetuned model, SFT). The usual constrained objective is written as

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim \pi_\theta} [r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big),
\]

where $r_\phi$ is a learned reward model, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference (SFT) policy, and $\beta$ controls how costly divergence is. Intuitively:

- Small $\beta$ (weak KL penalty) allows the policy to diverge more to chase reward — higher reward but higher risk of degeneration or going off-distribution.
- Large $\beta$ (strong KL penalty) keeps the policy conservative and close to the reference — safer and more diverse, but potentially lower reward.

So the alignment problem becomes a trade-off: how much reward can we gain for a given amount of KL divergence from the reference policy?

## How RLHF (PPO-style) handles the trade-off — and why it's finicky

Most RLHF pipelines implement the above objective via an actor-critic RL algorithm (commonly PPO). The pipeline is typically two-stage:

1. Fit a reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to human pairwise preference data (via a Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce formulation).
2. Run an RL algorithm to update a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the reward minus KL penalty.

This works, but it introduces several practical complications:

- You must fit a separate reward model (and often normalize it), which can have high variance and identifyability issues.
- The RL loop requires sampling from the policy, estimating returns, and often training an auxiliary value network to reduce variance. These components make training unstable and require significant hyperparameter tuning.
- Actor-critic methods must estimate the normalizing/soft-value term (the partition function), which is expensive or noisy to approximate. If that term is handled poorly (e.g., by poor baselines), gradients get high variance and learning becomes brittle.

Empirically, PPO can succeed but is sensitive and computationally heavy. The reward–KL frontier achieved by PPO depends on careful tuning.

## DPO: directly optimizing the same constrained objective without RL

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a different route. Instead of (1) learning a reward model and then (2) running RL to optimize a policy, DPO reparameterizes the reward in terms of the policy and directly fits the policy to the human preference data with a single classification loss.

Key analytical facts used by DPO:

1. For the KL-constrained objective above, the *optimal* policy corresponding to any reward $r$ has the softmax-like closed form

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right),
\]

with partition function \(Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)\).

2. Rearranging that gives a reparameterization of the reward (up to an additive function of \(x\)):

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

3. Human pairwise preference models like Bradley–Terry (used to fit reward models) depend only on reward *differences*: \(p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\big)\). The additive \(\beta\log Z(x)\) drops out when taking differences.

Putting this together, DPO expresses the Bradley–Terry preference probability directly in terms of the policy:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big).
\]

Thus the policy that optimizes the KL-constrained RLHF objective can be learned by maximizing the likelihood of human preferences under this parameterization. Concretely, DPO minimizes a binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs:

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}} \Big[ \log \sigma\!\big( \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} \big) \Big],
\]

where \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) are the human-preferred and dispreferred completions.

What this gives you in practice:

- No explicit reward model to fit; the policy itself implicitly defines a reward via \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\).
- No RL sampling loop or auxiliary value function is needed — training is just supervised optimization of a cross-entropy loss.
- The KL penalty is built in via the \(\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) terms and the scaling parameter \(\beta\), so DPO directly targets the same reward–KL constrained objective as PPO.

## How DPO preserves the reward–KL trade-off and stabilizes training

Two important properties explain why DPO yields a favorable reward–KL frontier in practice:

1. Equivalent reward classes. Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce models only identify rewards up to an additive function of the prompt \(x\). DPO's reparameterization picks a particular representative from that equivalence class — the one for which the partition function is normalized — and this representative leads to the same optimal policy. So DPO is not constraining the space of achievable optimal policies compared to the usual RLHF formulation.

2. Built-in correct weighting of updates. The gradient of the DPO loss has the form (ignoring constants)

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} \propto -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[ \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\;(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x))\Big],
\]

where \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\). The logistic weight \(\sigma(\cdot)\) upweights pairs where the current implicit reward ordering is incorrect (i.e., the model presently favors the wrong completion), and downweights pairs already ordered correctly. This dynamic, per-example importance weight prevents destabilizing updates that a naive unlikelihood-style loss can incur, mitigating model degeneration and helping maintain generation diversity.

In contrast, actor-critic RL approaches must estimate the normalization/soft-value term (the partition function) implicitly and often rely on baselines or learned value networks to control variance. If those estimates are noisy, the actor's gradients become high-variance or biased, harming the resulting reward–KL trade-off.

## Empirical behavior: better reward–KL frontier with much less complexity

The paper shows that DPO achieves a strictly better reward–KL frontier than PPO on a controlled sentiment task (where the true reward is known). That is, for a given KL divergence from the reference, DPO reaches higher reward than PPO, and conversely can achieve the same reward with lower KL. This is notable because both methods optimize the same constrained objective in theory — but DPO does so more directly and with lower variance.

On real preference datasets (summarization and single-turn dialogue), DPO matches or outperforms PPO and several other baselines (Preferred-FT, unlikelihood, Best-of-N in some settings) when evaluated by proxy judges (GPT-4) and human raters. Importantly, DPO does this while being simpler to implement (no RL loop) and cheaper to run (no policy sampling during training, no value function to learn).

## Practical takeaways

- The KL penalty in RLHF is not just a regularizer: it encodes a safety/diversity trade-off by constraining how far you can push a policy away from a trusted reference. Tuning that trade-off is central to aligned fine-tuning.
- DPO is a simple, supervised-style way to directly optimize that same constrained objective. It replaces the reward-model-then-RL pipeline with a single cross-entropy loss over preference pairs and an explicit \(\beta\) hyperparameter controlling conservativeness.
- In practice, DPO tends to achieve a better reward–KL trade-off than PPO, and it avoids much of the instability and engineering complexity of actor-critic RLHF pipelines. That makes DPO attractive as a lighter-weight alternative for learning from pairwise preferences.

## Closing note

DPO leverages a neat analytic identity between constrained optimal policies and reward functions to bypass RL entirely while preserving the theoretically-motivated KL constraint. If you care about both maximizing what humans prefer and keeping your model close to a safe, diverse reference, DPO gives a practical and principled way to navigate that reward–KL trade-off with much less fuss.
