\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is GSPO’s sequence-level importance weight an unbiased off-policy correction?
Question:
The paper argues that GRPO misapplies importance sampling at the token level and proposes GSPO’s sequence-level weight

$\displaystyle s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|}$.

In standard importance sampling, an unbiased estimator of an expectation under $\pi_{\theta}$ using samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ requires the raw ratio $\pi_{\theta}(z)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(z)$, not a powered version. After clipping, the estimator is typically biased but stabilizes training.

Question: In GSPO, even before clipping, does taking the $1/|y_i|$ power introduce bias relative to the target gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[A(y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)]$? If so, what objective is GSPO optimizing exactly, and how does the introduced bias relate to sequence length?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — taking the $1/|y|$ power does introduce a bias relative to the standard importance-sampling correction for the target gradient. GSPO is not forming an unbiased estimator of the usual off‑policy policy gradient; instead it optimizes the sequence-level surrogate objective that the paper writes down, and the difference from the true importance-sampled gradient depends directly on sequence length.

Why, precisely. The standard target gradient you quoted is
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\big[A(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\big]
=
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y\mid x)}\,A(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right],
$$
i.e. the importance weight for a whole sequence is the raw ratio $\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)$.

GSPO instead defines the scalar sequence weight
$$
s(\theta;y)=\Big(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y|}
$$
and optimizes the surrogate objective (before clipping)
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\rm GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\rm old}}\big[\,s(\theta;y)\, \widehat A(y)\,\big].
$$
Differentiating (paper Eq. (10)) gives the GSPO gradient estimator
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{J}_{\rm GSPO}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\rm old}}\Big[
s(\theta;y)\,\widehat A(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log s(\theta;y)
\Big]
=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\rm old}}\Big[
\Big(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\rm old}(y)}\Big)^{\!1/|y|}\widehat A(y)\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)
\Big].
$$

Compare this to the (importance-sampled) policy gradient written with token-sums:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\rm old}}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\rm old}(y)}\,\widehat A(y)\,\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
So per sampled sequence the multiplicative discrepancy (scalar factor) between GSPO’s per-sample contribution and the standard IS contribution is
$$
\Delta(y,\theta)=\frac{(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm old})^{1/|y|}\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}}{(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm old})}
= \frac{1}{|y|}\bigg(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\rm old}(y)}\bigg)^{\,\frac{1}{|y|}-1}.
$$
Because $\Delta$ depends on $|y|$ and on the raw sequence ratio, GSPO’s gradient is a biased estimator of the usual importance-sampled gradient (even before any clipping).

Intuition and length dependence. There are two linked effects that produce the bias and explain its dependence on sequence length:

1) Exponentiating by $1/|y|$ dampens the raw importance ratio. For long sequences $1/|y|$ is small, so $(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm old})^{1/|y|}$ is close to 1 even when the full sequence ratio is large or small. Thus GSPO applies a much weaker off‑policy correction for long responses; it moves the estimator toward treating the sample as nearly on‑policy (modulo the overall $1/|y|$ scaling). This reduces variance but introduces systematic under‑correction (bias) for off‑policy samples.

2) The extra factor $1/|y|$ multiplying the token-sum further scales down the whole gradient contribution of longer sequences. In effect GSPO weights per-token gradient contributions by the average per-token log‑likelihood change rather than the full sequence ratio. As $|y|$ grows the per-sample contribution tends to shrink (both because of $1/|y|$ and because the powered ratio tends to 1), so long sequences are pulled closer to zero relative to the unbiased IS estimator.

Trade-off the paper is implicitly making. The paper motivates these choices as variance‑reducing and numerically stable: length normalization keeps sequence importance ratios in a controlled range so clipping and optimization behave well (and empirically this stabilizes MoE training). That comes at the cost of bias: GSPO optimizes the concrete surrogate objective $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\rm old}}[s(\theta;y)\,\widehat A(y)]$ (with clipping), not the importance-sampled expected-policy gradient. The bias decreases for short sequences (where $1/|y|$ is large so $s$ is closer to the raw ratio) and increases for long sequences (where $s$ approaches 1 and the correction vanishes).

Additional note on clipping. The clipping operation the paper uses introduces a further (and standard) bias because it truncates ratios; that is separate from and compounds the bias coming from the $1/|y|$ power. The paper’s empirical claim is that the practical reduction in variance and the better matching of the optimization unit (sequence reward ↔ sequence clipping) lead to superior stability and performance despite the theoretical bias.

Summary. GSPO is biased relative to the canonical importance‑sampled policy gradient: it explicitly optimizes the surrogate objective with sequence‑level, length‑normalized importance weights $s(\theta;y)$. The bias scales with sequence length: for long responses the powered ratio and the $1/|y|$ factor make GSPO under‑correct (more biased but lower‑variance); for short responses the scheme is closer to the unbiased correction. This is the trade‑off the paper documents and leverages for practical stability.

### Why is the sequence-level ratio length-normalized as the geometric mean?
Question:
GSPO defines the importance ratio as

$\displaystyle s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$,

which is the geometric mean of token-level ratios. Conceptually, importance sampling would use the product (no $1/|y_i|$), and PPO typically clips token-wise ratios directly.

What is the theoretical justification for preferring the geometric mean over the product in GSPO? Is there an equivalent variational objective or divergence that makes the geometric mean the “right” scaling, or is this purely a variance-control heuristic?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO’s sequence importance ratio is length-normalized (the geometric mean) because it is the numerically stable, variance-controlled way to turn a sequence likelihood ratio into a per-token signal that (a) matches a sequence-level reward, (b) keeps the scale comparable across different response lengths, and (c) yields gradients that weight every token equally. This choice is primarily motivated by variance and numerical-stability considerations plus a desire to match the unit of correction (sequence) to the unit of reward — it is not the direct consequence of a new variational bound or a different exact importance-sampling identity, but it does have a clear probabilistic interpretation and meaningful gradient-level consequences.

Why the geometric mean is natural and useful
- Probabilistic interpretation. The sequence likelihood ratio is
  $$
  \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
  = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
  $$
  Taking the geometric mean (equivalently exponentiating the per-token average log-ratio)
  $$
  s(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}
  =\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}\Big)
  $$
  is exactly the exponentiated average per-token log change. That makes $s(\theta)$ a per-token-scale measure derived from the true sequence ratio.

- Numerical stability and variance control. Using the raw product (no $1/|y|$) makes importance weights scale exponentially in sequence length. Small per-token changes multiply into huge (or tiny) sequence ratios, producing extreme variance and numerical overflow/underflow. The geometric mean tames this by converting multiplicative accumulation into an additive average of log-ratios, which grows only linearly in the average per-token change and is comparable across lengths. Empirically the paper highlights that raw token-ratio based weighting accumulates noise across long sequences and can induce catastrophic instability; length-normalization prevents that accumulation.

- Makes clipping and hyperparameters length-agnostic. If you clip the raw product you must set clipping bounds that depend strongly on $|y|$; length-normalizing yields an importance signal that lives in a unified numerical range for different lengths so one clipping range can be meaningful across responses.

- Gradient/weighting consequences. With the geometric-mean definition the gradient term becomes
  $$
  s_i(\theta)\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot),
  $$
  so every token in the sampled sequence is weighted equally by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$. By contrast, token-level ratios (or using the raw product without normalization) produce unequal per-token multipliers that vary per position and can amplify instability. Equal per-token weighting aligns with the fact that the reward is sequence-level: you want the off-policy correction to say “how much does this whole sequence come from the new policy?” and then distribute that correction uniformly over the sequence when applying policy gradients.

Relation to importance sampling, divergences, and theory
- It does not invent a new importance-sampling identity: importance sampling for the sequence expectation legitimately uses the product ratio $\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)$. GSPO instead uses a length-normalized form because we want a per-sequence scalar that is stable and interpretable per-token. So GSPO remains consistent with sequence-level importance sampling in spirit: the geometric mean is a reparameterization of the same sequence ratio that is more usable in practice for long sequences.

- There is no hidden variational objective in the paper that uniquely mandates the geometric mean as “the” objective in the sense of deriving a different divergence; rather, the geometric mean corresponds to constraining average per-token log-prob changes (which is directly related to per-token KL behavior). Viewing $\log s(\theta)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t\Delta\log\pi_t$ makes controlling $\log s(\theta)$ akin to constraining the average log-prob shift — i.e., a per-token KL-like control — which is a sensible regularization when rewards are defined at sequence level.

Summary
Length-normalizing the sequence ratio (geometric mean) is primarily a practical, theoretically consistent choice: it converts the true sequence importance ratio into an interpretable per-token scale, dramatically reduces variance and numerical instability for long sequences, makes clipping hyperparameters meaningful across lengths, and yields gradients that equally weight each token in a response. The paper treats this choice as principled variance/scale control that aligns the unit of correction (sequence likelihood) with the unit of reward, rather than as arising from a novel variational divergence.

### Does GSPO’s gradient correspond to REINFORCE with a modified baseline or objective?
Question:
Ignoring clipping, the GSPO gradient is

$\displaystyle \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[s(y;\theta)\, \widehat{A}(y)\, \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right]$.

Can this be derived as REINFORCE on some transformed objective like $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\tilde{A}(y)]$ with a particular choice of $\tilde{A}$ or baseline? If yes, what is $\tilde{A}$ explicitly, and how does the $1/|y|$ interact with the baseline to keep the estimator unbiased (prior to clipping)?
Answer:
Short answer: No — GSPO’s gradient cannot be written as a plain REINFORCE gradient $ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y)] $ with a $\tilde{A}$ that is independent of $\theta$ (or with a simple transformed baseline). You can algebraically solve for a candidate $\tilde{A}$, but it depends on $\theta$ (through the importance ratio), so the equality does not reduce GSPO to standard REINFORCE without extra terms. Below I show the algebra, give the explicit ($\theta$‑dependent) expression, and explain why the $1/|y|$ and baseline issues prevent an unbiased REINFORCE interpretation.

Derivation and intuition grounded in the paper
The paper gives the unclipped GSPO gradient (writing $s(y;\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ and $\hat{A}(y)$ for the group advantage):

$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\Big[ s(y;\theta)\, \hat{A}(y)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|\cdot)\Big].$

Note that $\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|\cdot) = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$, so this is

(1) $\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\Big[ s(y;\theta)\, \hat{A}(y)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\Big].$

Compare to REINFORCE: for any function $\tilde{A}(y)$ that does not depend on $\theta$, we have

$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\Big[ \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\Big)\, \tilde{A}(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\Big].$

To make the REINFORCE-form integrand match (1), we would need

$\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\Big)\, \tilde{A}(y)
= s(y;\theta)\, \hat{A}(y)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}
= \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|}\, \hat{A}(y)\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}.$

Solving algebraically for $\tilde{A}(y)$ yields

(2) $\tilde{A}(y) = \frac{1}{|y|}\, \hat{A}(y)\, \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|-1}.$

This is the explicit expression you asked for. But notice the crucial point: $\tilde{A}(y)$ depends on $\theta$ through the factor $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\right)^{1/|y|-1}$. That dependence breaks the usual REINFORCE identity, because when $\tilde{A}$ depends on $\theta$ the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y,\theta)]$ is

$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y,\theta)] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta] + \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \tilde{A}],$

so there is an extra $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \tilde{A}]$ term which is not present in the GSPO gradient (and in general is nonzero). Therefore GSPO is not equivalent to vanilla REINFORCE on a $\theta$‑independent advantage; the best you can do algebraically is (2), which makes $\tilde{A}$ $\theta$‑dependent and thus changes the differentiation.

Intuition: why the mismatch arises
REINFORCE works through the identity $\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[f(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[f(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)]$ only when $f$ does not depend on $\theta$. GSPO inserts a per-sequence scale $s(y;\theta)$ which is a fractional power of the full importance weight, i.e., an atypical dependence on $\pi_\theta$. To convert that scale into the prefactor $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$ that REINFORCE produces when changing measure from $\pi_\theta$ to $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, you must move a factor of $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\right)^{\text{something}}$ into $\tilde{A}$; that necessarily makes $\tilde{A}$ depend on $\theta$. So GSPO’s gradient is the gradient of its own objective $J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)$ (as the paper shows), but not the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y)]$ for any $\tilde{A}$ that is independent of $\theta$.

Role of the $1/|y|$ and baselines (unbiasedness prior to clipping)
The factor $1/|y|$ in the GSPO gradient is a per‑sequence length normalization that turns the sum of token log‑gradients into an average token gradient. Algebraically it is just a scalar factor that can be absorbed into any candidate advantage (see (2) above) — it does not change the core argument about $\theta$‑dependence. From the unbiasedness perspective (before clipping), subtracting a baseline $b(x)$ that depends only on the query $x$ (and not on the token sequence $y$) is safe: $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[b(x)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)] = b(x)\, \nabla_\theta \int \pi_\theta(y)\, dy = 0$, so the estimator remains unbiased. However, any baseline that depends on $y$ or on $\theta$ in a way that correlates with $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$ will in general introduce bias unless the baseline’s $\theta$‑dependence is accounted for in the gradient (i.e., you include the extra $\nabla_\theta \tilde{A}$ term). In GSPO the group advantage $\hat{A}(y)$ is computed from the sampled group and does not depend on $\theta$, so subtracting a group mean baseline is valid and keeps the estimator unbiased (again, ignoring clipping). But trying to absorb the $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\right)^{1/|y|-1}$ factor into a “baseline” that is independent of $\theta$ is impossible, because that factor is inherently $\theta$‑dependent.

Summary
You can write a formal equality by defining

$\tilde{A}(y,\theta) = \frac{1}{|y|}\, \hat{A}(y)\, \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|-1},$

and then GSPO’s integrand equals the REINFORCE-type term $\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\Big)\, \tilde{A}(y,\theta)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$. But $\tilde{A}$ depends on $\theta$, so the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\tilde{A}(y,\theta)]$ contains extra terms and therefore GSPO is not REINFORCE on a $\theta$‑independent transformed objective. The $1/|y|$ is simply a length normalization you can fold into the advantage; unbiasedness (before clipping) is preserved only when baselines do not depend on the action sequence $y$ (or on $\theta$) in a way that would introduce extra gradient terms.

### How exactly does token-level importance weighting in GRPO lead to variance that accumulates with length?
Question:
The paper claims GRPO’s token-wise ratios $w_{i,t}$ inject high variance that accumulates across long sequences and is amplified by clipping.

Could someone provide a concrete variance analysis that contrasts

- GRPO: $\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t w_t \widehat{A}\, \nabla\log\pi(y_t)$
- GSPO: $s^{\phantom{.}}\widehat{A}\, \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \nabla\log\pi(y_t)$

under a simple model (e.g., independent token logits with small perturbations)? I’m looking for a derivation that shows how the variance scales with $|y|$ for each method, and how clipping further changes that scaling.
Answer:
Short answer: under a simple i.i.d. per‑token noise model you can show both GRPO and GSPO gradients have a leading variance term that decays like $1/|y|$ (because you average $|y|$ noisy token contributions), but GRPO carries an extra $O(\sigma^2)$ variance term that does not shrink with length as fast as GSPO’s corresponding term. Concretely, if the per‑token log‑ratio perturbations are small and independent, the extra noise from token‑level importance ratios in GRPO scales as $O(\sigma_\delta^2/|y|)$ while in GSPO the analogous term scales as $O(\sigma_\delta^2/|y|^2)$. Thus for long sequences the relative penalty of GRPO’s token‑wise weighting remains $O(\sigma_\delta^2)$ while GSPO’s penalty vanishes like $1/|y|$. Clipping makes this worse for GRPO because clipping is applied per token and creates biased, highly asymmetric noise that effectively increases the variance term $\sigma_\delta^2$ and prevents cancellation across tokens — this is why the paper says variance “accumulates across long sequences and is amplified by clipping.”

Below is a short, concrete derivation that makes the statements above explicit. The math follows the setup and notation of the paper.

Setup and simplifying assumptions
- Consider a single response $y$ of length $|y|$. For simplicity analyze the scalar projection of the gradient on some direction (so gradients become scalars). Let $g_t$ denote the scalar random variable representing the token gradient contribution at position $t$ (this is the scalar version of $\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_t|\cdot)$ projected on a direction of interest). Assume $g_t$ are i.i.d. with mean $0$ and variance $\mathrm{Var}[g_t] = \sigma_g^2$.  
- Let the token importance be $w_t = \pi_\theta(y_t|\cdot)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|\cdot)$. For small changes of policy we write the small additive perturbation in log‑space: define $\delta_t$ by $\log w_t \approx \delta_t$ and assume $\delta_t$ are i.i.d., mean $0$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\delta_t] = \sigma_\delta^2$ (this is natural if the new logits differ slightly per token). For small $\delta_t$ we have $w_t \approx 1 + \delta_t$ (first order). We assume independence between $\{g_t\}$ and $\{\delta_t\}$ to keep algebra simple (this isolates the effect of importance noise).
- Advantages are the same for all tokens in a response: $\widehat A$ (scalar). For variance scaling we can drop the common $\widehat A$ factor (it scales all variances).

GRPO estimator and its variance
GRPO uses the token‑wise estimator (scalarized):
$$
\hat G_{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} w_t\, g_t.
$$
With $w_t \approx 1 + \delta_t$ and $\mathbb{E}[g_t]=0$, $\mathrm{Var}[g_t]=\sigma_g^2$, and independence:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}[\hat G_{\text{GRPO}}]
&= \frac{1}{|y|^2}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \mathrm{Var}[w_t g_t]
\quad\text{(cross terms zero by independence / zero mean)}\\
&= \frac{1}{|y|^2}\cdot |y| \; \mathbb{E}[w_t^2]\,\sigma_g^2
\quad\text{(since }\mathrm{Var}[w g]=\mathbb{E}[w^2]\mathrm{Var}[g]\text{ when }\mathbb{E}[g]=0)\\
&\approx \frac{1}{|y|}\,(1+\sigma_\delta^2)\,\sigma_g^2,
\end{aligned}
$$
where we used $w_t \approx 1+\delta_t$ so $\mathbb{E}[w_t^2]\approx 1 + \mathrm{Var}[\delta_t] = 1+\sigma_\delta^2$ (neglecting higher moments). So GRPO variance scales as
$$
\mathrm{Var}_{\text{GRPO}} \approx \frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\,(1+\sigma_\delta^2).
$$

GSPO estimator and its variance
GSPO forms a length‑normalized sequence ratio
$$
s = \exp\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log w_t\Big)\approx 1 + \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\delta_t,
$$
and multiplies the averaged token gradients:
$$
\hat G_{\text{GSPO}} = s\cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} g_t.
$$
Define $D=\sum_{t}\delta_t$ and $G=\sum_{t} g_t$. Then
$$
\hat G_{\text{GSPO}} \approx \frac{G}{|y|} + \frac{D\,G}{|y|^2}.
$$
Compute variance ($\mathbb{E}[G]=\mathbb{E}[D]=0$, independence assumed):
$$
\mathrm{Var}[\hat G_{\text{GSPO}}]
= \mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{G}{|y|}\right) + \mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{D\,G}{|y|^2}\right)
$$
(the cross term has zero mean and can be neglected under independence). We have $\mathrm{Var}[G]=|y|\sigma_g^2$ and $\mathrm{Var}[D]=|y|\sigma_\delta^2$. Using the standard product variance result for independent zero‑mean variables $\mathrm{Var}[DG]=\mathrm{Var}[D]\mathrm{Var}[G]$ we get
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}[\hat G_{\text{GSPO}}]
&\approx \frac{|y|\sigma_g^2}{|y|^2} + \frac{(|y|\sigma_\delta^2)(|y|\sigma_g^2)}{|y|^4}\\
&= \frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|} + \frac{\sigma_\delta^2\,\sigma_g^2}{|y|^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
So GSPO variance scales as
$$
\mathrm{Var}_{\text{GSPO}} \approx \frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\Big(1 + \frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{|y|}\Big).
$$

Comparison and interpretation
Both estimators have the same leading term $\sigma_g^2/|y|$ coming from averaging $|y|$ independent token gradients. The crucial difference is the next‑order term coming from importance noise:

- GRPO has an additive factor $(1+\sigma_\delta^2)$ multiplying the leading $1/|y|$ term; the extra contribution from importance‑ratio noise stays $O(\sigma_\delta^2/|y|)$.
- GSPO’s extra contribution is $O(\sigma_\delta^2/|y|^2)$, i.e., suppressed by another factor of $|y|$.

For long responses (large $|y|$) the ratio of variances behaves roughly like
$$
\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\text{GRPO}}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\text{GSPO}}} \approx \frac{1+\sigma_\delta^2}{1 + \sigma_\delta^2/|y|} \approx 1 + \sigma_\delta^2\Big(1 - \frac{1}{|y|}\Big) ,
$$
so GRPO retains an $O(\sigma_\delta^2)$ extra noise that does not vanish with length, while GSPO’s importance‑noise contribution vanishes like $1/|y|$. Intuitively: GRPO multiplies each token gradient by a different noisy factor $w_t$, so the noise components do not average away as efficiently; GSPO averages the log‑ratios first and therefore shrinks importance noise by $1/|y|$ before applying it to the averaged gradient.

Effect of per‑token clipping (why it amplifies GRPO variance)
The paper applies clipping to importance ratios to prevent extreme off‑policy weights. Clipping at token level changes the distribution of $w_t$ in two harmful ways for GRPO. First, clipping is asymmetric with respect to the advantage sign (the paper uses $\min( w_t \hat A, \operatorname{clip}(w_t) \hat A )$ ), so the gradient estimator becomes biased and the clipped $w_t$ have a mass at the clip boundary; this increases $\mathbb{E}[w_t^2]$ relative to the unclipped case and therefore directly increases $\mathrm{Var}[w_t g_t]$. Second, token‑wise clipping breaks any residual cancellation across tokens: many $w_t$ might be clipped at their bounds, producing correlated extreme terms in the token sum rather than benign independent small perturbations. Both effects raise the effective $\sigma_\delta^2$ (or make higher moments larger), so the $O(\sigma_\delta^2/|y|)$ GRPO variance term becomes numerically large and no longer shrinks effectively with $|y|$. In contrast, GSPO clips a single sequence‑level $s$, so the clipping operates after averaging and is far less likely to push many independent small perturbations to the clip boundary; hence clipping hurts GSPO much less.

Summary (intuitive)
Under small, independent per‑token perturbations the sequence‑level weighting of GSPO averages the importance noise before it multiplies gradients, producing an extra variance term that shrinks like $1/|y|^2$ and is negligible for long sequences. GRPO instead multiplies each token by its own noisy importance weight; those per‑token noises produce an extra variance contribution that decays only like $1/|y|$ and can be large in practice. Per‑token clipping amplifies GRPO’s problem by increasing the variance and introducing bias at the token level, so the practical effect is that GRPO’s noise “accumulates” with length and becomes unstable — precisely the phenomenon highlighted in the paper.

### What objective does GSPO optimize when the group standard deviation of rewards is near zero?
Question:
The advantage is defined as

$\displaystyle \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}$.

What happens when all responses in a group have nearly identical rewards so that the denominator is zero or numerically tiny? Does the implementation include a small $\epsilon$ in the denominator? If yes, how sensitive is GSPO to this $\epsilon$ and could it bias learning toward groups with larger within-group variance?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s GSPO objective uses the group z-score

$$
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r}{\operatorname{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)},
$$

so if the denominator is (nearly) zero the z‑score is mathematically undefined (or numerically enormous). The paper does not explicitly state an $\epsilon$ in the published equations, but in any real implementation you must prevent divide‑by‑zero. How you do that determines behavior and possible bias.

Why this matters intuitively: the z‑score rescales within a group so that relative differences matter, not absolute magnitudes. If all group rewards are identical, there is no within‑group signal to learn from; dividing by (near) zero would amplify tiny numerical differences into extremely large advantages and destabilize gradients. If you clamp the denominator instead, you effectively say “no useful signal from this group” (small advantage magnitudes), which is stable but reduces that group’s contribution.

Practical choices and consequences
1. eps in denominator. The common implementation is to compute
$$
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r}{\max(\operatorname{std},\epsilon)}
$$
(or add as $\operatorname{std}+\epsilon$). The paper doesn’t specify the exact $\epsilon$, but since rewards are in $[0,1]$ a typical range is $\epsilon\in[10^{-6},10^{-3}]$. A very small $\epsilon$ lets groups with tiny but real differences keep a large normalized signal (risking instability); a larger $\epsilon$ suppresses the signal (safer but may ignore small genuine differences).

2. Sensitivity and bias. Using an $\epsilon$ (or clamping) does bias how much each group contributes: when you increase the denominator (via a larger $\epsilon$) you reduce the magnitude of all advantages coming from low‑variance groups, so those groups influence the update less. Conversely, if you use a tiny $\epsilon$, low‑variance groups can produce huge advantages from tiny noise and dominate (causing instability). In short, there is a tradeoff: tiny $\epsilon$ risks instability and spurious large gradients; large $\epsilon$ systematically downweights low‑variance groups and can bias learning toward groups with larger apparent within‑group variance (because their normalized advantages remain larger relative to clamped low‑variance groups).

Recommended safeguards (practical, grounded in the paper’s setup)
- Add a small clamping constant, e.g. use $\max(\operatorname{std},\epsilon)$ or $\operatorname{std}+\epsilon$. Since rewards are in $[0,1]$, start with $\epsilon\approx 10^{-6}$ and tune up to $10^{-3}$ if instability is observed. The GSPO paper’s other numeric choices (very small clipping ranges like $3\times10^{-4}$, $4\times10^{-4}$) suggest training is sensitive to small scales, so validate $\epsilon$ empirically.
- Alternatively, treat groups with $\operatorname{std}<\tau$ as “no signal” and either skip advantage normalization for that group (set $\widehat A_i=0$) or use a robust normalization (e.g., median absolute deviation) or rank‑based advantages (which avoid division by tiny std).
- Monitor diagnostics: fraction of groups with std below threshold, distribution of $|\widehat A_i|$, and gradient norms. If tiny fractions produce huge $|\widehat A_i|$, increase $\epsilon$ or switch to robust scaling.
- Because GSPO already averages over groups and clips sequence‑level importance ratios, downweighting low‑variance groups (via an $\epsilon$) is often preferable to allowing unstable huge advantages.

Conclusion: the paper’s formula omits an explicit $\epsilon$, but a stable implementation must include one (or an equivalent safeguard). The choice of $\epsilon$ trades off between instability from inflated advantages (if too small) and under‑utilization of low‑variance groups (if too large), and it can therefore bias effective learning toward groups whose within‑group variance remains larger. With bounded rewards ($[0,1]$) a small epsilon (order $10^{-6}$–$10^{-3}$) and empirical checks (or robust normalization/ranking) are practical ways to avoid both extremes.

### How should I choose GSPO’s clipping range when sequence lengths vary widely?
Question:
The paper notes that GSPO’s clipping bounds are numerically very different from GRPO because $s_i$ is a geometric mean. In practice, if $|y|$ ranges from very short to very long within a batch, should the clipping thresholds depend on $|y|$ (e.g., bounds on the log-ratio average) or remain fixed? Is there a principled way (perhaps via concentration bounds) to set $\varepsilon$ as a function of $|y|$ and expected token-level log-ratio variance?
Answer:
Short answer: because GSPO uses a length-normalized importance ratio
$s_i = \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t} Z_{t} \right),\; Z_{t} := \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|\ldots)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{t}|\ldots)}$,
you can meaningfully use a fixed clipping interval on $s_i$ across different $|y|$. If you want a principled, length-aware choice that controls tail probability of extreme $s_i$, set the clipping on the log scale and scale the threshold like $1/\sqrt{|y|}$ (up to model/token correlation corrections). Concretely, estimate the token-level variance and choose the clip via a concentration/CLT bound; this gives a simple, interpretable rule and is what I recommend in practice.

Why this works (intuitive + technical): GSPO already averages the token log-ratios, so the distribution of the average
$L_i := \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} Z_t$
concentrates as $|y_i|$ grows. If the $Z_t$ were iid with $\mathrm{Var}(Z_t)=\sigma^2$, then $\mathrm{Var}(L_i)=\sigma^2/|y_i|$ and $L_i$ is approximately Gaussian for moderate-to-large $|y_i|$ (CLT). So long responses naturally have much smaller deviation of $L_i$ from its mean than short responses. Because $s_i = \exp(L_i)$, clipping directly on $s_i$ without accounting for length implicitly clips more tightly (in probability) for long sequences than for short ones. That is why the paper normalizes by length: it brings $s_i$ into a unified numerical range so a single small clip value (they report $O(10^{-4})$ for $s_i$) is meaningful.

A principled recipe (using CLT / sub-Gaussian/Hoeffding bounds)
1) Empirically estimate token log-ratio variance $\hat\sigma^2$ from your rollout data (compute $Z_t$ per token under the sampled pairs and take variance; do this per query-type or globally as you prefer). If tokens are not iid (correlation across positions), either estimate effective variance from the empirical distribution of $L_i$ directly or inflate $\hat\sigma^2$ by an empirical factor to account for positive correlation.
2) Decide a desired tail probability $\delta$ that you want to permit (e.g., $0.01$ or $0.001$) for $|L_i|$ exceeding the threshold $a$.
3) Use a normal/CLT approximation: $a \approx z_{1-\delta/2}\, \hat\sigma / \sqrt{|y|}$. Equivalently, solve Hoeffding (if $Z_t$ bounded by $[A,B]$): $a = (B-A)\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(2/\delta)}{2|y|}}$. Then set the multiplicative clip around $s_i$ as
   $s_i \in [ \exp(-a), \exp(+a) ]$.
If you prefer to express in the form $s_i \in [1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, use $\varepsilon \approx \exp(a)-1$ (for small $a$, $\varepsilon\approx a$).

Practical notes and caveats: the iid assumption is only an approximation. Token log-ratios are often correlated (conditioning, common context), so the naive $\sigma^2/|y|$ can under-estimate tail mass; either (a) estimate $\mathrm{Var}(L_i)$ directly from collected sequences and use its empirical standard deviation to set $a$, or (b) multiply $\hat\sigma$ by a safety factor $(1.5\text{--}3)$ before applying $1/\sqrt{|y|}$ scaling. For very short sequences the CLT is poor; use empirical percentiles of $L_i$ for those lengths. If you want a single universal clip, choose $a$ so that long sequences (largest $|y|$ you expect) have the desired tight tail; short sequences will then be clipped less aggressively in probability, which is often acceptable. The paper’s choice to normalize by length is precisely to make fixed, very small clip values on $s_i$ meaningful across lengths (they report left/right $3\times10^{-4}/4\times10^{-4}$ on $s_i$ in experiments).

Example quick rule you can implement:
• collect $Z_t$ and compute either empirical sd of per-token $Z$ ($\hat\sigma$) or empirical sd of $L_i$ for each length bin;
• pick $\delta$ (e.g., $0.01$) and set $a(|y|)=z_{1-\delta/2}\, \hat\sigma / \sqrt{|y|}$;
• set clip: $s \in [\exp(-a(|y|)), \exp(+a(|y|))]$, or use $\varepsilon(|y|)=\exp(a)-1$ if you want the $1\pm\varepsilon$ form.
If you want a single $\varepsilon$ across lengths, choose $\varepsilon = \exp(z\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{L_{\max}})-1$ where $L_{\max}$ is the largest sequence length you must support.

Summary: keep using length-normalized $s_i$ so a fixed clip is meaningful; for principled control of tail risk, set clipping on the log scale using variance estimates and scale the threshold approximately like $1/\sqrt{|y|}$ (or use empirical $\mathrm{Var}(L_i)$ per length bin to avoid iid assumptions). This matches the GSPO design in the paper and gives an interpretable way to set $\varepsilon$.

### Why does clipping many more tokens/responses in GSPO still yield higher sample efficiency?
Question:
The experiments show GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (effectively, entire responses) yet trains more efficiently than GRPO. I’m confused because clipping usually discards learning signal.

Is the explanation that GRPO’s per-token ratios create noisy or even adversarial weights so that ‘using more tokens’ actually harms signal-to-noise, while GSPO’s sequence-level filter removes off-policy outliers and yields a cleaner gradient? Can the authors quantify this by comparing effective sample sizes or gradient SNR between the two methods?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — GSPO clips many more tokens/responses but still improves sample efficiency because it removes high-variance, effectively adversarial off‑policy samples at the sequence level and replaces many noisy per‑token importance corrections with a single, length‑normalized sequence weight. That yields a cleaner, lower‑variance gradient (higher effective use of the kept samples) even though fewer raw tokens contribute.

Why this happens (intuitively tied to the paper)
The paper’s core diagnostic is that GRPO applies importance weights at each token, where each per‑token weight
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$
is computed from a single sample of the next‑token distribution. Because that single sample is a noisy draw, the per‑token weight often does not perform the role of importance sampling (it does not average over the next‑token distribution) but rather injects high variance and possibly large, unbalanced reweighting into the gradient. Those unequal token weights accumulate across long sequences, and the clipping interacts badly with this accumulation—tokens with positive advantage can get aggressively upweighted on a per‑token basis while negative‑advantage tokens can be effectively amplified in the other direction—leading to noisy or even adversarial gradient components and ultimately instability.

GSPO replaces those many per‑token weights by one sequence‑level, length‑normalized importance ratio
$s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$
(which the paper motivates as the correct importance measure when the reward is given per sequence). Two practical consequences follow. First, sequence weights better match the unit of reward and importance sampling: they measure how off‑policy the entire response is, which is what matters for that response’s reward. Second, GSPO’s gradient (Equation (12) in the paper) weights all tokens in a response equally by $s_i$ rather than introducing unequal, per‑token multipliers. The result is much lower variance in the weighting applied to gradient components and far fewer pathological gradient directions even though many entire responses (hence many tokens) get clipped and excluded.

Why clipping more can still improve sample efficiency
Clipping is a tool to exclude samples whose importance weights are so extreme that they would dominate or destabilize gradient estimation. In GRPO, token‑level clipping keeps many token contributions but those surviving tokens may still be individually noisy and carry conflicting reweightings across the same sequence; thus the effective gradient signal per retained token can have poor signal‑to‑noise. In GSPO, clipping at sequence level excludes entire outlier responses (highly off‑policy or unstable), leaving a smaller but much cleaner set of sequences. Because the retained sequences have coherent, properly scaled weights and advantages, the gradients computed from them have lower variance and higher directional fidelity. In other words, GSPO increases the usefulness per retained sample even while decreasing the number of retained tokens, so effective sample efficiency (how much useful gradient you extract per rollout) increases.

How you would quantify/verify this (what the paper suggests and what to measure)
The paper does not provide explicit numbers for ESS or gradient SNR, but it gives the analytical and empirical reasons to expect better effective sample usage for GSPO. If you want to quantify this empirically (and the paper’s design invites exactly these checks), measure two diagnostics per mini‑batch for each algorithm:

1) Effective sample size (ESS) using the sequence weights $s_i$ for GSPO and an appropriate aggregation for GRPO. A standard ESS definition is
$${\rm ESS} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2},$$
where $w_i$ are the importance weights used in the objective. For GSPO use $w_i = s_i$ (or the clipped $s_i$); for GRPO you can either (a) use sequence‑aggregated token weights (e.g., the geometric mean of token weights) to get comparable sequence weights, or (b) compute a token‑level ESS and then aggregate—expect GRPO’s token ESS to be misleadingly large but not reflective of coherent sequence correction.

2) Gradient signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR): compute the batch mean gradient vector $\bar{g}$ and its sample covariance; report
$\displaystyle {\rm SNR} = \frac{\|E[g]\|}{\sqrt{\operatorname{trace}(\operatorname{Var}[g])}}$
(or equivalently $\displaystyle \frac{\|\bar{g}\|}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{B}\sum_b\|g_b-\bar{g}\|^2}}$). The paper’s gradient expressions (Equations (11)–(13)) imply GSPO will show a larger SNR because tokens in a sequence are coherently weighted by $s_i$ rather than multiplied by independent noisy $w_{i,t}$ factors.

Empirical expectation: you should observe that even though GSPO has far fewer tokens surviving clipping, it achieves larger ESS at the sequence level (or larger per‑unit ESS) and larger gradient SNR. That aligns with the paper’s empirical claim that GSPO produces more stable, efficient learning and eliminates the need for Routing Replay in MoE models.

Extra evidence from the paper’s MoE discussion
The paper also argues GSPO is less sensitive to MoE expert‑activation volatility because sequence likelihoods are comparatively stable even when token‑level activated experts flip between old and new policies. In GRPO, those expert changes make per‑token weights highly unstable; in GSPO, the sequence weight aggregates over tokens and is length‑normalized, reducing sensitivity to local routing changes. That mechanism further increases the “quality” of retained samples and thereby the effective sample efficiency.

Takeaway
Clipping more tokens (or entire responses) in GSPO is not wasteful: it removes harmful, high‑variance outliers and leaves a smaller set of coherent, properly weighted sequences whose gradients carry much higher value per token. To make this exact, compute sequence ESS and batch gradient SNR (as above) and you will see GSPO trades quantity for higher‑quality signal, resulting in higher sample efficiency — precisely what the paper reports.

### What guarantees prevent GSPO from mode collapse when rewards are sparse or heavy-tailed?
Question:
In tasks like math/code, reward distributions can be highly skewed (many zeros, few ones). With sequence-level clipping and $s_i$ weighting, could GSPO over-amplify a small set of high-reward sequences and collapse exploration?

Are there theoretical or empirical safeguards (e.g., KL penalties not shown in the main equations, per-query normalization across batches, entropy bonuses) that ensure sufficient exploration and prevent premature collapse, especially when $G$ is small?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO does not come with a formal proof that it can never collapse under arbitrarily sparse or heavy‑tailed rewards. What the paper does provide (and what practically reduces the risk of collapse) are several interacting design choices that limit the ability of a tiny number of high‑reward sequences to dominate updates: sequence‑level clipping, group normalisation of advantages, length‑normalised sequence importance ratios, and averaging over group members. These are empirical and algorithmic safeguards rather than a mathematical safety guarantee. Below I explain how each component helps intuitively and technically, how risks grow when the group size $G$ is small, what the paper does and does not explicitly include ($\mathrm{KL}$/entropy), and practical mitigations you should consider.

Why the mechanism reduces collapse risk
1) Sequence‑level clipping controls amplification. GSPO replaces token‑level importance weights with a sequence‑level importance ratio
$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$,
and applies PPO‑style clipping to $s_i(\theta)\cdot \hat A_i$. That clipping directly bounds how much a single sampled response can increase (or decrease) its contribution to the objective during one update. Intuitively, even if a response has a huge advantage (e.g., reward 1 while others are 0), the clipped multiplier prevents the gradient contribution from being arbitrarily large.

2) Group standardisation reduces heavy‑tail influence. The advantage $\hat A_i$ in GSPO is computed per query by standardising rewards across the group:
$\hat A_i = \dfrac{r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}(\{r\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r\})}$.
This mean/std normalisation converts raw, skewed rewards into z‑scores inside the mini‑group. For heavy‑tailed reward vectors this trades raw magnitude for relative standing inside the group, so an isolated high reward is turned into a large but scaled advantage. Concretely, when most rewards are identical (many zeros) and one is high, the standardisation makes that one stand out but not infinite; the clipping on $s_i$ then caps how much the standout item affects the update.

3) Length normalisation stabilises importance ratios. Taking the $|y|$‑th root (equivalently exponentiating the mean log ratio) keeps $s_i(\theta)$ numerically comparable across different sequence lengths and prevents a handful of token likelihood changes from exploding the sequence weight. That reduces variance in $s_i$ and therefore reduces the chance that a single sample produces an enormous multiplicative gradient.

4) Averaging over $G$ responses and over batches reduces variance. The objective averages the clipped term over the $G$ responses for each query and across queries/batches. This dilution means a single high‑reward sequence must repeatedly appear (or be repeatedly up‑weighted) to cause a runaway effect.

What the paper explicitly says (and what it omits)
The paper explicitly presents the items above: sequence‑level importance ratios, length normalisation, group advantage standardisation, and clipping. It also shows empirical evidence that training is stable and that GSPO often clips a large fraction of tokens yet still improves performance—an empirical indicator that the clipped updates are effective but not destructive.

The paper omits detailed $\mathrm{KL}$ or entropy regularizers in the main objective "for brevity" (they explicitly said they omitted the $\mathrm{KL}$ regularization term earlier in the PPO preliminaries). That means you should not assume GSPO as written in the main equations necessarily includes a $\mathrm{KL}$ penalty or entropy bonus, although such regularizers are standard in practice and the omission was deliberate for presentation clarity. The authors do report tuned (very small) clipping ranges used in experiments (e.g., $\sim 3\times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\sim 4\times 10^{-4}$ in GSPO experiments), which are themselves a strong practical regulariser.

Limits and the role of $G$
When $G$ is small (e.g., $G = 1$ or very small per‑query sample counts), the group standardisation becomes unstable: the sample mean and std are noisy and an outlier reward produces a very large z‑score. With small $G$ the variance of $\hat A_i$ increases and the per‑query averaging weakens, so the algorithm is more susceptible to over‑amplifying isolated high‑reward sequences. The paper’s stability claims rely on using nontrivial group sizes and large rollout batches (they mention partitioning rollouts into multiple minibatches). In short, small $G$ increases collapse risk; the design of GSPO reduces but does not eliminate it.

Practical safeguards to add (consistent with the paper’s spirit)
Because the paper provides algorithmic but not formal guarantees, in practice you should combine GSPO’s built‑in mechanisms with additional safeguards if you expect extremely sparse/heavy‑tailed rewards:

- Increase group size $G$ and/or batch size so the per‑query standardisation is reliable. Larger $G$ reduces the chance a single sample dominates.
- Use a $\mathrm{KL}$ penalty to the reference policy or an explicit trust‑region term (the paper already notes $\mathrm{KL}$ regularisation is standard in PPO but omitted in presentation). This directly constrains drift from the behavior policy.
- Add an entropy bonus (or minimum entropy constraint) to preserve exploration if you see premature narrowing of the policy.
- Use reward clipping or robust baselines (e.g., running winsorised/std‑stabilised baselines) before computing advantages to tame extreme reward values.
- Use adaptive or annealed clipping ranges: smaller ranges early on to guard against big jumps, or adapt clipping based on observed $s_i$ variance.
- Monitor per‑query statistics (mean/std, fraction clipped, effective sample weights) and abort / reduce learning rate if a tiny fraction of samples accounts for most weight.
- If using MoE models, the paper shows GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay; still, monitor expert activation volatility as an extra check.

Conclusion
GSPO gives stronger practical protection against collapse than GRPO because it (1) matches the unit of importance weighting to the unit of reward (sequence), (2) length‑normalises the ratio, (3) standardises advantages across a group, and (4) clips sequence multipliers. These mechanisms together reduce the probability that a handful of rare high‑reward responses will overthrow the policy. However, the paper does not offer a formal impossibility guarantee: when $G$ is very small or rewards are pathologically heavy‑tailed you can still get risky updates. In practice you should combine GSPO with sensible choices (sufficient $G$/batch size, $\mathrm{KL}$ or entropy regularisation, reward preprocessing, monitoring) to ensure robust exploration and avoid premature collapse.

### Does GSPO-token remain numerically identical to GSPO when advantages are per-token but sum to the sequence advantage?
Question:
The paper states GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$. Suppose I choose token-level advantages that decompose the sequence advantage, e.g., $\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t} = |y_i|\,\widehat{A}_i$ or a credit assignment based on stepwise rewards.

Do GSPO-token and GSPO still match in this case, or do they start optimizing different objectives? Is there a recommended constraint on $\{\widehat{A}_{i,t}\}$ to maintain theoretical equivalence or at least preserve the variance benefits claimed for GSPO?
Answer:
Short answer: They generally do NOT remain identical unless each token advantage is exactly the same as the sequence advantage. GSPO-token and GSPO become different objectives as soon as the per-token advantages vary across positions, even if those per-token advantages sum (or average) to the sequence advantage. To preserve exact theoretical equivalence you must set $\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$ for every token; if you only enforce $\sum_t \widehat A_{i,t}=|y_i|\,\widehat A_i$ you will be optimizing a different (and generally non‑equivalent) objective.

Why (intuitively and technically). Compare the gradients the paper gives. GSPO (sequence-level) yields, ignoring clipping,
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\widehat A_i\;\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
$$
GSPO-token gives (again ignoring clipping)
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token} \propto
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\widehat A_{i,t}\;\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
$$
Both share the same multiplicative sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$, so clipping and sequence reweighting remain sequence-level. The difference is the per-token multiplier inside the sum: GSPO uses the same scalar $\widehat A_i$ for every token, while GSPO-token multiplies each token gradient by $\widehat A_{i,t}$. If $\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$ for all $t$ the two gradients are identical; if the $\widehat A_{i,t}$ vary, then the token gradients are reweighted non‑uniformly and the update direction changes. The condition $\sum_t\widehat A_{i,t}=|y_i|\,\widehat A_i$ only matches the total weight, not the per-token weighting pattern, so it does not guarantee equality of the vector-valued gradients.

Consequences for objective and variance. When per-token advantages differ you are unambiguously optimizing a different objective: the learning signal allocates more or less emphasis to specific token logits. That can be intentional (fine-grained credit assignment), but it also reintroduces some of the instability/new variance the paper warns about for token-level importance weighting: even though GSPO-token keeps the sequence-level importance factor $s_i(\theta)$ detached and identical across tokens, skewed $\widehat A_{i,t}$ can amplify gradients for tokens where the model already has large or noisy logits, changing both bias and variance of updates relative to GSPO.

Practical recommendation to maintain equivalence / preserve variance benefits. If you want the theoretical equivalence and the variance/stability behavior the paper highlights, enforce the constraint
$$
\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i\quad\text{for every token }t.
$$
If you need per-token credit assignment, consider these compromises: (1) keep the per-token advantages zero-mean around $\widehat A_i$ with small variance (i.e., $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$ and $\mathrm{Var}_t(\widehat A_{i,t})$ small), so updates remain close to GSPO while allowing mild token-level modulation; (2) use GSPO sequence-level optimization to get stable global learning and apply token-level adjustments in a secondary stage or via a smaller learning-rate multiplier for the tokenized component; or (3) explicitly accept you are optimizing a different objective and monitor stability (clipping, gradient norms, expert-activation volatility in MoE) carefully, since per-token variation can reintroduce instability.

Bottom line: GSPO-token preserves the desirable sequence-level importance reweighting (so it keeps many of GSPO’s stability benefits), but it only equals GSPO exactly when every token’s advantage equals the sequence advantage. If you decompose $\widehat A_i$ into varying $\widehat A_{i,t}$ you will change the objective and can increase variance; to retain theoretical equivalence enforce uniform per-token advantages (or keep per-token deviations tiny and controlled).

### Is the stop-gradient trick in GSPO-token mathematically sound or an implementation hack?
Question:
GSPO-token defines

$\displaystyle s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}$.

Since this equals $s_i(\theta)$ numerically, the gradient only flows through $\log\pi_\theta$ terms while keeping the geometric-mean ratio as a scalar weight. Is there a principled derivation (e.g., from a surrogate objective or a control variate argument) that justifies this stop-gradient decomposition? Or is it primarily a practical device to avoid coupling higher-order terms that would increase variance?
Answer:
Short answer: In the GSPO paper the stop-gradient in the GSPO-token definition is a principled implementation trick that is algebraically exact (under the paper’s assumptions) and intentionally removes problematic higher-order coupling between per-token likelihoods and the sequence-level importance weight. It is not an ad-hoc biasing of the objective in that setting, but it is an implementation choice whose correctness depends on the specific identity and assumptions used in the paper (not a generic estimator that you can drop into arbitrary objectives without checking those assumptions).

Why this is true (intuitive + technical). The paper defines the sequence-level importance ratio
$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/\lvert y_i\rvert}
= \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{\lvert y_i\rvert} \sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}\right).$

GSPO optimizes at the sequence level and derives the gradient (omitting clipping) as
$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ s_i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{\lvert y_i\rvert}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot) \right].$
This is the clean sequence-level form: a single scalar weight per response, times the usual score-function terms for each token.

GSPO-token writes a per-token scalar $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ but defines it with a stop-gradient:
$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\text{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]},$
so numerically $s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta)$ (because the ratio evaluates to $1$), but autograd only lets gradients flow through $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ in the denominator/ratio part, which is then cancelled by the numerator when differentiated. The paper shows that with this construction the resulting gradient is exactly the same as the sequence-level gradient above when the token advantages within a sequence are identical ($\hat{A}_{i,t} = \hat{A}_i$). In other words, the stop-gradient is an algebraic device that yields the desired gradient expression: the importance scalar behaves like a detached multiplicative weight while the gradient flows only through the log-probability terms. So, for GSPO-token as defined and used in the paper, the stop-gradient is mathematically sound: it implements the sequence-level gradient using tokenwise autograd in a way that avoids producing extra terms.

What the stop-gradient is buying you (practical / variance reasons). Without the stop-gradient you would let $s_i(\theta)$ (which depends on $\sum_t \log \pi_\theta$ terms) also propagate gradients, producing coupling terms like $s_i(\theta)$ times gradients of $s_i(\theta)$ (i.e., second-order / higher-order interactions among token-wise log probabilities). Those additional terms are real derivatives of a differently written objective, but they are undesirable here because they (a) introduce extra variance and (b) weight tokens unevenly in ways the authors argue lead to instability (especially for long sequences or MoE models with routing volatility). By detaching the scalar $s_i(\theta)$ you intentionally remove those higher-order contributions so each token’s gradient ends up being weighted by the sequence scalar but has the simple score-function form. That both matches the sequence-level derivation and reduces variance relative to the fully-coupled derivative you would get if you allowed full backprop through $s_i$.

Caveats and boundaries of validity. The equivalence (and thus correctness of the detach trick) relies on the construction and on the assumptions used in the paper:

- Numerical equality: $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ as defined is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. That guarantees that clipping and the forward computation behave like the sequence-level scheme.
- Gradient equality: the paper shows the gradients coincide under the case where token advantages are the same across a response. If you give different per-token advantages, the GSPO-token derivation still yields a sensible gradient (it allows per-token advantages while still using the sequence scalar), but you should re-check whether any desired theoretical identity is preserved.
- Clipping: clipping is applied to $s_{i,t}$ numerically equal to $s_i$. Because detaching affects only gradient flow, clipping decisions are the same on the forward pass. But be mindful: if you change the algebraic form or place the detach in a different spot, you can alter gradients or clipping behavior.
- Numerical and autograd subtleties: stop-gradient is implemented by detaching tensors (e.g., torch.detach). That is exact in floating point arithmetic up to usual precision limits; however, using inference vs training engines with different numerical precision could create tiny numerical mismatches—this is an engineering issue, not a conceptual one.

Relation to surrogate objectives / control variates. The stop-gradient here is not presented as a control variate in the usual variance-reduction sense (it does not subtract a baseline to reduce mean squared error of an estimator). Instead it implements a surrogate gradient: you replace the full derivative of a composite function by the derivative of a simpler surrogate that is algebraically equivalent in value and whose gradient matches the intended sequence-level score-function gradient. In that sense it is a principled surrogate implementation rather than an ad-hoc variance-bias tradeoff. It removes higher-order coupling rather than adding an explicit variance-canceling term. So it’s different in spirit from typical control-variates / baselines, but it does serve the same practical ends: lower variance and more stable updates.

Bottom line. The stop-gradient in GSPO-token is a principled implementation trick in the specific setting of the paper: it is an algebraically exact way to implement the sequence-level weighted score-function gradient while avoiding higher-order coupling and variance. It is not a generic, blind trick to be applied everywhere; you must preserve the algebraic identities and assumptions (how clipping and advantages are used). When used as in the paper, it produces the intended gradient and improves stability and variance properties compared to the fully coupled token-level derivative.

### How does GSPO handle EOS tokens and variable truncation during log-likelihood computation?
Question:
For the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$, does $y$ include the EOS token in practice? If responses are truncated by a max length, how is $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ computed—do we treat the final token as EOS, or ignore the missing EOS probability? Could inconsistent handling of EOS across $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$ bias the sequence-level ratios or the clipping decisions?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO’s math treats a response $y$ as the full token sequence used to compute the sequence likelihood $ \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$. In practice you must be consistent about whether that sequence includes the EOS token. If you truncate sampling at a max length you must still compute the sequence-likelihoods for both $ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $ \pi_{\theta}$ in the same, explicit way (either include the EOS probability by evaluating it at the final context, or deliberately omit EOS for both policies). Any inconsistent handling of EOS/truncation between the old and new policy likelihood computations will bias the sequence-level ratio $s(\theta)$ and therefore affect clipping/optimization.

Why this matters (intuitively and technically). GSPO forms the importance ratio from whole-sequence likelihoods (length-normalized). That ratio is sensitive to whatever set of token probabilities you include in the product (or sum of log-probabilities). If for one policy you include an EOS-probability term and for the other you do not, the numerator and denominator are not computing the same event and the ratio is wrong. The error is systematic (not zero-mean noise) and will push $s_i(\theta)$ away from 1 in a biased way, which directly influences whether a sequence is clipped or how much it contributes to the gradient.

Concrete safe implementations
1) Prefer to include EOS explicitly whenever possible. If generation finished by producing EOS, include that token in $y$ and in $|y|$, and multiply by its probability under both policies when forming $\pi(y\mid x)$.
2) If sampling was truncated by a hard max length (no EOS produced), evaluate the model(s) once more at the final context to get $p(\mathrm{EOS}\mid x, y_{<T})$. You can then either (a) append that EOS probability and treat the sequence as if EOS would follow (so you include $p(\mathrm{EOS})$ in both $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$), or (b) explicitly decide to treat truncated sequences as “EOS absent” and compute likelihoods over only the observed tokens — but you must do the same for old and new policies.
3) Always compute likelihoods for both $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$ with identical tokenization, same stop/termination rules, same routing or inference-mode determinism (for MoE), and (ideally) the same numerical precision/back-end. GSPO is more tolerant than token-level methods to small numerical differences, but EOS treatment is a discrete choice that causes non-negligible bias if inconsistent.
4) If you cannot recompute $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ with the training engine, store the full sampled token sequence including EOS-flag at sampling time (or store the final-context logits or $p(\mathrm{EOS})$) so you can re-evaluate consistently later.

Relation to GSPO specifics. The paper already length-normalizes the sequence ratio to control variance, but normalization does not remove bias from inconsistent sequence definitions. Because GSPO uses sequence-level ratios, a missing or extra EOS term affects the whole-sequence ratio (exponentiated average log-probability), so it matters for clipping decisions and gradients. The recommended consistent handling above prevents such bias.

Practical rule of thumb: treat sampled sequences and their likelihoods as the same atomic event for both policies. Either always include EOS (by appending $p(\mathrm{EOS})$ when truncated) or always omit it, but never mix choices between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$. Doing so keeps $s_i(\theta)$ meaningful and avoids spurious clipping/optimization bias.

### What precisely causes MoE ‘expert-activation volatility’ to destabilize GRPO but not GSPO?
Question:
The paper reports that, under GRPO, about 10% of experts change after each update for the same sample, making token-level ratios unstable. GSPO allegedly avoids this because it only cares about sequence likelihood, which fluctuates less.

Can you provide a more formal argument or diagnostic showing that changes in routing primarily inject multiplicative noise into token-wise ratios but average out in the log-likelihood sum? For example, do you observe that per-token log-ratio variance is large while the average log-ratio across tokens remains stable across updates in MoE models?
Answer:
Short answer: MoE routing changes multiply token probabilities, so they are multiplicative noise on token importance ratios but become additive, averaging noise in the log domain. Because GSPO uses the sequence-level (geometric mean) ratio, those multiplicative perturbations largely cancel across many tokens; GRPO uses the raw token ratios, so it directly injects the large per-token multiplicative noise into gradients. A simple diagnostic (compute per-token log-ratio statistics across updates for the same sample) will reveal large per-token variance but a much smaller variance of the sequence-mean log-ratio, consistent with the paper’s observation that $\sim 10\%$ of experts change per update.

Formal, intuitive argument
Let a fixed rollout sample be $y = (y_1 \ldots y_T)$ and compare two parameter states, old and new. Define the token importance ratio and its log:
$w_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid \text{context}_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \text{context}_t)},\quad \delta_t := \log w_t.$

GRPO uses $w_t$ directly in token-weighted gradients; GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence ratio
$s = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/T} = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \delta_t\right).$
Thus per-token routing-induced multiplicative changes are $w_t$ (multiplicative), while GSPO effectively uses $\exp(\text{mean }\delta_t)$ (geometric mean).

If routing changes act approximately multiplicatively and independently across tokens (or at least are weakly correlated across positions), then $\delta_t$ behave like additive noise terms. By basic statistics, the variance of the sample mean is
$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \delta_t\right)=\frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t,t'}\operatorname{Cov}(\delta_t,\delta_{t'}).$
If covariances are small compared to per-token variance, the variance of the mean scales roughly like $\operatorname{Var}(\delta)/T$. Therefore the sequence-level log-ratio’s variance shrinks with sequence length $T$, while the per-token log-ratios retain the larger $\operatorname{Var}(\delta)$. Converting back to importance ratios, large variance in $\delta_t$ corresponds to large multiplicative fluctuations in $w_t$, but those fluctuations largely average out in $s$.

Why MoE routing specifically magnifies per-token multiplicative noise
In MoE layers the active expert set for a token determines the token’s conditional probability density because different experts implement different feedforward transforms. If routing changes between $\theta_{\text{old}}$ and $\theta$ produce a different expert for a token, $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid \text{context})$ can jump by a factor (multiplicative change). The paper reports roughly $\sim 10\%$ of experts change per update for the same sample; that means a non-negligible fraction of tokens can see large multiplicative changes (heavy tails in $w_t$). GRPO multiplies gradients by these $w_t$ at every token and then clips per-token—so the token-level multiplicative noise is injected directly and nonlinearly into the gradient estimator. GSPO, by using $\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \delta_t\right)$, turns those multiplicative jumps into additive deltas in the mean; many such additive terms cancel or average down.

Concrete diagnostics to show this effect (what to compute)
1) For a fixed set of rollout samples, after each SGD update compute for every token $t$ in each sample:
$\delta_t = \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \text{context}) - \log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \text{context}).$
Collect these deltas across updates (or over a window of recent updates).

2) Report per-token statistics: $\text{mean}(\delta_t)$, $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_t)$ and the histogram (heavy tail / outliers). This displays how large and skewed the per-token log changes are due to routing.

3) Compute sequence-level statistics: $\delta_{\text{seq}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \delta_t$ for each sample; report $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_{\text{seq}})$ and compare it to $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_t)$. Under the averaging argument you should see $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_{\text{seq}}) \ll \operatorname{Var}(\delta_t)$, roughly $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_t)/T$ if correlations are small.

4) Compare gradient-noise proxies: for GRPO use the per-token weighted quantity $w_t \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid\text{context})$; for GSPO use $s \cdot \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid\text{context})$. Compute the empirical variance over minibatches of these gradient estimators (or of their norms). GRPO should show substantially higher variance.

5) Routing-specific check: mark tokens whose expert assignment changed between $\theta_{\text{old}}$ and $\theta$; compare distributions of $|\delta_t|$ for tokens with changed expert vs unchanged. You should see much larger $|\delta_t|$ for changed-expert tokens, confirming routing-induced multiplicative jumps.

How this ties to the paper’s observations
The paper states that about $\sim 10\%$ of experts change per update for the same sample, and that this “expert-activation volatility” makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically and invalidates GRPO’s token-level correction. The diagnostics above will precisely quantify that claim: a heavy-tailed per-token $\delta$ distribution (driven by expert flips) together with a small variance of $\delta_{\text{seq}}$ demonstrates that routing injects multiplicative noise at the token level which is largely averaged out by GSPO’s sequence log-mean. That is exactly why GSPO is robust without routing replay, while GRPO needs Routing Replay to stabilize token-level ratios by holding expert assignments fixed.

Practical notes and caveats
The “averaging down” effect assumes deltas are not perfectly positively correlated across tokens. If many tokens change together (e.g., a global model shift) covariances will reduce cancellation and sequence variance will be larger. Also, extreme outliers (few tokens with enormous $|\delta_t|$) can still influence the mean; clipping and length-normalization (as GSPO uses) help control that. Finally, even when $\operatorname{Var}(\delta_{\text{seq}})$ is small, GRPO’s per-token clipping can interact pathologically with the heavy-tail multiplicative noise (the paper points this out), producing biased gradient updates; GSPO avoids that by clipping at sequence level.

Summary
Mathematically, routing changes multiply token likelihoods ($w_t$), which become additive in log space ($\delta_t$). GSPO uses the average log change, so the variance of the sequence log-ratio falls roughly as $1/T$ (if per-token changes are not strongly correlated), while GRPO exposes the optimizer to the full per-token multiplicative noise. The diagnostics I described (per-token delta histograms, per-sequence mean and variances, expert-change tagging, and gradient-noise estimates) will empirically demonstrate the paper’s claim that per-token log-ratio variance is large while average log-ratio across tokens remains much more stable.

### How does batch partitioning into mini-batches create off-policy drift within a rollout, and how does GSPO mitigate it?
Question:
The paper mentions that partitioning a large rollout batch into multiple mini-batches makes the learning off-policy relative to $\pi_{\theta}$.

Could you clarify the timeline? If $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ generated the entire batch, but we update $\theta$ across several mini-batches, then later mini-batches are more off-policy than earlier ones. In this setting, does GSPO’s sequence-level clipping adaptively downweight later mini-batches more strongly than GRPO’s token-wise clipping, and is there empirical evidence that gradient norms or losses differ systematically across mini-batches within the same rollout?
Answer:
Short answer: Partitioning a rollout into mini-batches makes the optimization progressively more off‑policy within the same rollout because you are applying multiple parameter updates after the data were collected from a single behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Later mini‑batches see a target policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that has moved farther from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, so their importance ratios tend to deviate more. GSPO mitigates this by measuring and clipping importance at the sequence level (the sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta)$), which directly reflects how “off‑policy” a whole response is under the current $\theta$; empirically GSPO clips many more token contributions but still yields more stable and efficient training than GRPO.

Explanation and timeline
When you generate a large rollout with $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, all samples in that rollout are draws from the same behavior distribution. If you split that rollout into $K$ mini‑batches and perform parameter updates after each mini‑batch, the parameter vector $\theta$ changes between mini‑batches. Concretely: you start with $\theta^{(0)}=\theta_{\text{old}}$, take mini‑batch 1 and update to $\theta^{(1)}$, then take mini‑batch 2 (whose data were still sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) and update to $\theta^{(2)}$, and so on. Each update makes the current policy $\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$ a worse match to the original sampling policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Thus later mini‑batches are more off‑policy relative to the current $\theta$ (larger discrepancy between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ when evaluating importance ratios).

Why this matters for GRPO vs GSPO
GRPO uses token‑level importance weights
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$
and clips each token’s weight. Because updates change $\theta$ between mini‑batches, these per‑token ratios can fluctuate a lot, and since GRPO reweights tokens individually, the noise introduced by single‑sample token weights accumulates across long sequences and across updates. In MoE models this is amplified by “expert‑activation volatility” (the paper observed about 10\% routing changes after updates), which makes token probabilities unstable and invalidates the per‑token importance correction.

GSPO instead computes a sequence‑level, length‑normalized ratio
$s_i(\theta)=\big(\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\big)^{1/|y_i|}$
and clips at the response (sequence) level. Intuitively, $s_i(\theta)$ measures how much the entire response $y_i$ becomes less (or more) probable under the current policy; it therefore tracks the true off‑policy gap for the unit that receives the reward (the whole sequence). Because GSPO uses this single scalar per response to weight or exclude that response, it more directly and coherently downweights samples that have become very off‑policy after intervening updates.

Does GSPO downweight later mini‑batches more strongly than GRPO?
Yes, implicitly. Later mini‑batches, being evaluated under a $\theta$ that has moved farther from $\theta_{\text{old}}$, will tend to have larger deviations in their sequence ratios $s_i(\theta)$, so the clipping operation in GSPO is more likely to clip (or strongly downweight) those entire responses. GRPO’s token clipping acts locally and individually; it can sometimes allow inconsistent partial contributions from the same response (some tokens clipped, others not), which both dilutes the intended sequence‑level correction and introduces high variance. The paper reports a striking empirical signature consistent with this explanation: GSPO ends up clipping a much larger fraction of tokens (two orders of magnitude difference in clipped‑token fraction compared to GRPO) yet achieves better, more stable learning. That observation shows GSPO is more aggressive at excluding highly off‑policy signal (which will often come from later, more off‑policy mini‑batches) but does so at the sequence granularity that matches the reward.

Empirical evidence about mini‑batch differences
The paper does not report per‑mini‑batch gradient‑norm or loss time‑series explicitly, so there is no direct plotted curve of “gradient norm vs mini‑batch index” in the text. However, the paper gives several empirical findings that imply systematic differences across mini‑batches: GSPO’s much higher fraction of clipped tokens (Figure referenced in the paper), superior overall stability and sustained improvement across extended training and longer generations, and the noted expert‑activation volatility under GRPO (roughly 10\% routing changes after each update) that destabilizes token‑level ratios. Together these provide indirect empirical support that later mini‑batches become more off‑policy and that GSPO’s sequence clipping is a more effective adaptive correction than GRPO’s token clipping. The paper also shows that GRPO needed the Routing Replay trick to converge on MoE models, whereas GSPO removed that requirement—another empirical sign that sequence‑level correction handles the within‑rollout drift better in practice.

Summary
Partitioning a rollout into mini‑batches creates within‑rollout off‑policy drift because $\theta$ changes between mini‑batches while all samples were drawn from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. GSPO mitigates this by using a sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and clipping whole responses; later mini‑batches—which are more off‑policy—are therefore more likely to be clipped/ downweighted under GSPO than under token‑wise GRPO. The paper’s empirical findings—much larger token clipping fractions under GSPO, stable and better training outcomes, and elimination of Routing Replay for MoE—support that GSPO adapts more effectively to the mini‑batch-induced off‑policy drift, even though explicit per‑mini‑batch gradient norms are not reported.

### Can GSPO directly use inference-engine likelihoods without recomputation—what errors are tolerable?
Question:
The paper claims GSPO is tolerant to precision discrepancies between training and inference engines because it only uses sequence-level likelihoods.

Practically, what level of numerical discrepancy (e.g., absolute error in average log-prob per token) is acceptable before training degrades? Are there recommended checks, such as monitoring the distribution of $\log s_i = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}$, to detect when inference-computed likelihoods are too noisy to trust for clipping and weighting?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — GSPO is much more tolerant than token-level methods, but you must ensure the inference-computed sequence log-likelihoods are numerically accurate compared to the training-engine recomputation at a scale that is small relative to your sequence-level clipping tolerance. Practically you should monitor length-normalized log-importance errors (the paper’s “log $s_i$”) and alarm if their bias or variance causes many examples to cross the clipping thresholds or materially change the weighted gradients.

Why, in one paragraph: GSPO clips and weights by the sequence-level importance ratio
$s_i = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\right)$, i.e. it really relies only on the length-normalized sequence log-likelihood
$\log s_i = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$.
Because GSPO operates at sequence level (and the paper uses very small clipping windows around $1$), small numerical errors in log-likelihoods are damped by averaging across tokens. But if the inference-engine errors in $\log s_i$ are large enough to push $s_i$ across the clipping boundaries or to change the relative ordering/scale of the advantages, then the optimization sees biased weights and training can degrade.

Concrete practical checks and heuristics (grounded in the paper):
1) Always monitor the distribution of $\log s_i$ computed by the inference engine. Record $\text{mean}(\log s_i)$, $\text{std}(\log s_i)$, and the histogram (or percentiles) per mini-batch. The paper directly formulates and uses length-normalized $\log s_i$, so this is the natural diagnostic.

2) Periodically recompute $\log s_i$ on a small held subset (for many batches) using the training engine and compare inference vs.\ recomputed values. Report per-example differences
$\delta_i = \log s_i^{\text{(inference)}} - \log s_i^{\text{(training)}}$.
From these deltas compute $\text{mean}(|\delta_i|)$, median, and a tail metric (e.g., 99th percentile). Use these to judge whether inference numbers are acceptable.

3) Tie acceptance thresholds to your clipping window. In the paper GSPO’s clipping is applied around $s_i$ (numerically they used very small ranges; e.g., they stated left/right clipping ranges of order $3\times10^{-4}$ and $4\times10^{-4}$ around $1$). In log-space those are approximately $\pm 3\times10^{-4}$ (because for small $\epsilon$, $\log(1+\epsilon)\approx\epsilon$). A practical rule of thumb: require the per-token (length-normalized) absolute error in $\log s_i$ to be a small fraction of the clipping half-width. For safety use something like
$\text{mean}(|\delta_i|) < 0.1 \times \text{clipping\_half\_width\_in\_log}$
and tail $|\delta_i|$ (e.g., 99th percentile) $< 0.5 \times \text{clipping\_half\_width\_in\_log}$.
If you use the paper’s tiny clipping windows ($\sim 3\times10^{-4}$), this implies your allowable absolute error per-token should be on the order of $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$. If you choose a larger clipping window in your run, the allowable error scales accordingly.

4) Monitor derived training signals that show practical degradation: the fraction of sequences that are clipped (clipping fraction), the fraction whose clipping decision differs when using inference vs.\ training recomputed likelihoods, and the correlation between inference and recomputed $\log s_i$. If the inference engine causes large changes in clipping fraction or many examples cross from unclipped to clipped (or vice versa), stop and recompute more accurately. The paper highlights clipping fractions as an important observable (their Figs and discussion); GSPO still works well even when many tokens/responses are clipped, but you must ensure clipping isn’t being driven by numerical noise.

5) Watch advantage-weight interactions. Because GSPO multiplies $s_i$ by the group-normalized advantage $A_i$, noisy $s_i$ that correlate (or anti-correlate) with $A_i$ will bias gradient estimates. A useful operational check is to compute Pearson correlation between $\delta_i$ and $A_i$ across a batch. A statistically significant correlation suggests bias that will affect optimization and warrants recomputation or switching to the training engine.

6) Practical monitoring cadence and fallback: recompute on a small but representative sample every $N$ updates ($N$ might be $100$–$1000$, depending on throughput). If the mean absolute delta exceeds the threshold in (3), fall back to recomputing likelihoods for that period or increase clipping widths temporarily.

Why these numbers: the paper demonstrates GSPO uses very tight clipping ranges in $s$ (on the order of $10^{-4}$ around $1$). Because $s$ is exponentiated length-normalized log-prob, an error in $\log s_i$ of comparable magnitude will change whether a sample is clipped. Therefore the acceptable absolute error in average log-prob per token should be much smaller than the clipping half-width; otherwise clipping/weighting becomes unreliable. If you widen clipping windows in your training, you relax the requirement on likelihood precision proportionally, but that trades off off-policy safety.

Summary actionable recipe: compute and monitor $\log s_i$ (length-normalized) from your inference engine; periodically recompute with the training engine on a held mini-batch and compute per-example deltas; ensure mean and tail deltas are small fractions (e.g., $<10\%$ mean, $<50\%$ tail) of your clipping half-width in log-space; monitor clipping-fraction differences and correlation with advantages; if thresholds are violated, switch to training-engine recomputation or widen clipping until stability is restored. With these checks GSPO’s sequence-level formulation (as the paper argues) usually tolerates small inference/training engine discrepancies much better than token-level methods.

### How does KL regularization interact with GSPO’s sequence-level ratios and clipping?
Question:
The paper omits the KL term for brevity. If we add a KL penalty $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))$ at the sequence level, where does it enter the GSPO objective? Does the presence of $s_i(\theta)$ and sequence-level clipping change the usual PPO intuition that KL constrains policy drift? Any guidance on tuning $\beta$ jointly with the GSPO clipping bounds so they do not fight each other?
Answer:
Short answer: the KL penalty simply adds a sequence-level regularizer to GSPO (subtracted from the objective), it complements but does not replace GSPO’s sequence-level importance-ratio clipping, and you should tune the KL weight $\beta$ so that the empirical (length‑normalized) KL on your rollout batches sits at a small target that is consistent with your GSPO clipping range (monitor empirical KL and fraction clipped and adjust $\beta$ so they aren’t fighting).

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper

1) Where the KL term enters
The paper deliberately omits the PPO KL term. If you add a sequence-level penalty $\beta\;\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)$ you include it as a subtractive regularizer in the GSPO objective averaged over queries $x$. Using the paper’s notation (and keeping the existing GSPO clipping term) you get

$$
J_{\mathrm{GSPO\_with\_KL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\Bigg[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\big( s_i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}\big(s_i(\theta), 1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_i \big) \Bigg]  -  \beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[ \mathrm{KL}\!\big( \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x) \big) \big].
$$

Typically $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference/old policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) or an exponential moving average of past policies; choose whatever you use in PPO. The KL is computed at the sequence level (you can use the length‑normalized log likelihoods to keep scales comparable):

$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[ \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) \big].
$$

In practice when you only have samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ you will estimate this KL either by (a) sampling $y\sim\pi_\theta$ (requiring on‑policy recomputation) or (b) approximate it with an importance‑weighted or length‑normalized empirical estimate on your rollout batch. A common practical surrogate is the expected length‑normalized log ratio on the batch:
$$
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{est}}(x) \approx \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\bigg[ \frac{1}{|y|}\big( \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x) \big) \bigg],
$$
which matches how $s_i(\theta)$ is defined ($s_i$ is $\exp$ of that average log‑ratio).

2) How $s_i(\theta)$ and sequence‑level clipping interact with the KL intuition
PPO’s standard intuition is that a KL term softly penalizes policy drift (average divergence), while clipping enforces a per‑sample trust region (hard cap). In GSPO the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is already a sequence‑level, length‑normalized measure of drift:

$$
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\Big( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\ldots)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\ldots)} \Big).
$$

So GSPO’s clipping operates directly on the same length‑normalized log‑ratio that a sequence KL would control in expectation. Concretely, the clipping rejects or caps individual sampled responses whose sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$, whereas the KL penalty discourages the average (expected) length‑normalized log‑ratio from growing (softly, across all $x$ or $y$). They are complementary: clipping prevents any single sampled sequence from exerting excessive influence (hard local control), and KL prevents gradual systematic drift of $\pi_\theta$ away from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (soft global control).

Because GSPO already uses a sequence‑level ratio and length normalization, the usual PPO intuition about KL constraining drift still holds, but interpreted per sequence (or per token on average). There is no mysterious cancellation from $s_i(\theta)$—$s_i$ merely changes the unit of clipping from tokens to sequences; the KL term still measures expected log‑ratios and thus still constrains drift in expectation.

3) Practical guidance for tuning $\beta$ together with the clipping bounds
- Think of clipping as “local, per‑sample” and $\beta$‑KL as “global, averaged.” If clipping is very tight (small $\epsilon$) you already strongly limit per‑sample updates; a large $\beta$ is then redundant and can over‑regularize. If clipping is loose you may need a larger $\beta$ to prevent slow, systematic drift.

- Use a length‑normalized KL target. Because $s_i$ uses length normalization, measure KL on the same basis (per‑token averaged log‑ratio). Monitor the empirical length‑normalized KL on your rollout batches:
$$
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}} = \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\text{rollout}}\bigg[ \frac{1}{|y|}\big( \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) \big) \bigg].
$$

- Choose $\beta$ to hit a small KL target. A practical workflow is: start with $\beta = 0$ and your desired clipping $\epsilon$ (the paper reports very small GSPO clipping ranges numerically, e.g.\ left/right $\approx 3\mathrm{e}{-4} / 4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ because $s_i$ is length‑normalized). Run short experiments and observe $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ and the fraction of sequences that are clipped. Then increase $\beta$ until $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ approaches your target (typical per‑token targets are small—order of $1\mathrm{e}{-4}$ to $1\mathrm{e}{-3}$ per token in settings where the paper reports very small $s_i$ clipping numbers). The exact scale depends on your model, batch size, and sequence lengths; always use the length‑normalized KL to compare across lengths.

- Watch fraction clipped and training signal. If increasing $\beta$ reduces $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ but dramatically increases the fraction of sequences being clipped to zero contribution (or collapses learning), back off $\beta$ or tighten $\epsilon$ (smaller clipping) appropriately. Conversely, if very few sequences are clipped but $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ is large, increase $\beta$.

- Practical heuristics: because GSPO’s $s_i$ is already sequence‑level and length‑normalized, practitioners often can use a smaller $\beta$ than in token‑level PPO. If you have limited compute for tuning, a reasonable approach is to tune $\epsilon$ first to obtain a stable fraction clipped (not too large—the paper shows GSPO clips many tokens but that is measured differently; track fraction of sequences clipped), then tune $\beta$ to maintain $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ below a small threshold. Also prefer monitoring held‑out performance/validation reward: if KL reduction harms reward improvement, relax $\beta$.

Summary
Add the KL as a subtractive sequence‑level regularizer to the GSPO objective. Sequence‑level $s_i(\theta)$ and clipping are consistent with the usual PPO interpretation: clipping enforces a per‑sample trust region while KL softly constrains the average drift. Tune $\beta$ so that the empirical length‑normalized KL stays at a small target consistent with your clipping $\epsilon$ (monitor both $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{empirical}}$ and fraction clipped and adjust $\beta$ and $\epsilon$ jointly so they complement rather than fight).
