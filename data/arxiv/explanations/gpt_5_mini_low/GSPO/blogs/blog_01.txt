\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# From Tokens to Tales: Why Sequence-Level RL Makes LLMs More Stable

## TL;DR
Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) replaces token-level importance weights with a sequence-level importance ratio and clips at the full-response level. Because rewards are assigned to whole responses, this alignment reduces high-variance, accumulative noise from token-wise reweighting, stabilizes training (especially for large and sparse Mixture-of-Experts models), and improves sample efficiency compared to token-level methods like GRPO.

## The mismatch: rewards are for sequences, but some algorithms optimize tokens
Modern RL fine-tuning of language models treats the model as a policy \(\pi_\theta\). We sample responses \(y\) given a query \(x\) and score each (e.g., a verifier gives a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\)). Practically, these rewards apply to entire generated responses — not to individual tokens — yet some RL variants try to correct for off-policy sampling and optimize at the token level.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) is a recent approach that avoids using a value model by computing relative advantages within a group of responses for the same query. But GRPO still uses token-level importance ratios
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\)
and clips those ratios at every token position. Intuitively this is like grading every word in a sentence and re-weighting each word differently when computing gradients — while the final grade (reward) applies to the whole sentence.

## Why token-level importance ratios are problematic
The theoretical justification for importance sampling is that we reweight samples from a behavior distribution to estimate expectations under a target distribution:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
This relies on reweighting entire sampled items \(z\) (and averaging over many such samples) to correct distributional mismatch. In autoregressive generation, the natural "item" is the whole sequence \(y\), not each sampled token \(y_t\) in isolation.

Token-level weights in GRPO are computed from single-token samples drawn from the next-token distribution. Because each token weight is based on only one sample from that next-token distribution, the weight does not reliably correct distribution mismatch and instead injects high variance into gradient estimates. Two practical consequences follow:

- Variance accumulates across long sequences: multiplying or summing many noisy per-token contributions amplifies instability as response length grows.
- The clipping mechanism interacts badly with token-wise weights: clipping each token independently can produce unpredictable, disproportionate effects on gradients, further destabilizing training.

Empirically, this can lead to catastrophic, often irreversible collapse of the policy during RL fine-tuning of large models.

## GSPO: clip and optimize at the sequence level
GSPO fixes the mismatch by defining importance ratios on whole sequences and applying clipping at the response level. For a response \(y_i\), GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
The GSPO objective (with group-based advantages) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right],
\]
where \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the standardized reward of response \(y_i\) within its group.

Two important design choices:
- Sequence-level ratio: reweights whole responses, matching the reward unit.
- Length normalization: the exponent \(1/|y_i|\) keeps ratios comparable across different response lengths and reduces variance.

Because GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally (through the shared \(s_i(\theta)\)), it avoids the noisy, token-wise imbalance and produces more stable gradients.

## Why this shift reduces training noise and collapse
There are three complementary reasons GSPO improves stability:

1. Correct unit for importance sampling. Importance sampling theory applies naturally at the sequence level in autoregressive generation; reweighting whole sequences properly corrects for sampling from an older policy.

2. Reduced variance accumulation. Token-wise importance weights introduce independent noise per token that accumulates across long sequences. Sequence-level weights aggregate across tokens before reweighting, dampening this accumulation.

3. Cleaner interaction with clipping. Clipping a single scalar per response is less likely to introduce disproportionate, structured biases in the gradient than clipping many independent token weights.

As a result, GSPO removes a key source of unpredictable gradient noise that contributed to the catastrophic collapses observed with GRPO.

## Benefits for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models
MoE models add another layer of instability: expert-activation volatility. After a parameter update, the set of experts routed for the same input can change. In GRPO, token-level importance ratios depend on per-token likelihoods computed under potentially different active sub-networks (different routing), which can make those ratios swing wildly.

A previously used workaround, Routing Replay, cached routing choices from the old policy and replayed them to compute token likelihoods under the new policy. While helpful, Routing Replay increases engineering complexity, memory, and communication, and can limit effective model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entire problem: because it uses sequence-level likelihoods, it is much less sensitive to token-level routing changes. The sequence probability does not fluctuate as drastically under routing volatility, so GSPO stabilizes MoE RL without Routing Replay, simplifying infrastructure and letting the model utilize its full capacity.

## Empirical signals reported
The paper reports that GSPO:
- Trains stably where GRPO sometimes collapses.
- Improves training efficiency and downstream benchmark performance (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces) under comparable compute and queries.
- Allows stable RL scaling with MoE models without Routing Replay.
- Shows a counterintuitive but important observation: GSPO clips a far larger fraction of tokens (because clipping acts on whole responses) than GRPO, yet it still attains higher efficiency. This supports the interpretation that GRPO's token-level gradients are noisy and less useful.

## A token-level variant when you need it: GSPO-token
When per-token advantages are genuinely needed (e.g., multi-turn scenarios where you want finer credit assignment), the authors propose GSPO-token. It retains the sequence-level scale \(s_i(\theta)\) but allows token-by-token advantages \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\). Numerically, when token advantages are identical across a response, GSPO-token reduces to GSPO; when they differ, GSPO-token provides flexibility while preserving the more stable sequence-based reweighting.

## Practical takeaways
- Match the optimization unit to the reward unit: if rewards are computed per sequence, perform off-policy correction at the sequence level.
- For long responses and large/sparse models, per-token importance weighting can create high-variance gradients and cause training collapse.
- Using sequence-level importance ratios with length normalization and sequence clipping is a simple change with big stability and efficiency gains. It also reduces the need for MoE-specific hacks like Routing Replay.
- If you need token-level credit assignment, build it on top of a sequence-level reweighting scheme (GSPO-token) rather than replacing the sequence-level correction.

## Closing thought
The idea is simple but powerful: when you grade "tales" (full responses), don't try to retro-fit that grading into per-word reweighting. Align the importance-sampling correction with the unit that receives the reward. GSPO shows that this alignment is enough to dramatically reduce noise, prevent catastrophic collapse, and scale RL to larger and sparser language models.
