\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 12: Extensions, Limitations, and Open Questions

# When GSPO-token’s finer credit assignment helps or hurts

The paper introduces two closely related objectives: GSPO, which applies importance weighting and clipping at the sequence level, and GSPO-token, which is a token-level objective variant that embeds the sequence-level importance ratio into per-token weights while allowing token-wise advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Numerically, when all token advantages in a response are identical ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$), the two objectives coincide; their gradients reduce to the same expression (Equations (11) vs (14) in the paper). This identity highlights that GSPO-token is not a different algorithm in the aggregate, but a more flexible parameterization that permits finer credit assignment when advantages vary across tokens.

From the perspective developed in the paper, GSPO-token helps when the reward structure genuinely requires token-level differentiation. For example, in multi-turn settings where different tokens or turns correspond to different sub-goals (e.g., an intermediate clarification turn that should be rewarded differently from a final answer), allowing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to vary permits the optimizer to direct updates toward particular parts of the generated sequence without changing the overall clipping behavior: the sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ still determines whether a response is treated as overly off-policy. Practically, GSPO-token can therefore express structured credit assignment while retaining sequence-level off-policy correction and clipping.

However, the paper also illuminates important limitations and failure modes of token-level weighting that carry over to GSPO-token if used injudiciously. The core motivation for GSPO was that token-level importance ratios, as used in GRPO, are unreliable because each ratio is computed from a single sampled token and so cannot fulfill the variance-reduction role of importance sampling. GSPO-token avoids this by detaching the per-token likelihood magnitudes from the gradient path (the paper uses a stop-gradient, \mathrm{sg}[\cdot], in the definition of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$). Despite this, if token advantages are noisy, miscalibrated, or inconsistent with the sequence reward, per-token credit assignment can reintroduce instability. In particular, assigning large positive/negative $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to a few tokens can drive disproportionate updates to their conditional distributions through the shared factor $s_i(\theta)$ in the gradient (Equation (14)). Thus, GSPO-token can help when the token-level advantages are reliable and meaningful, but hurt when they are noisy or poorly estimated: it introduces more degrees of freedom that require trustworthy signals to exploit safely.

Design implication: when applying GSPO-token, one should carefully validate the quality and stationarity of token-level advantages (for example, by inspecting their variance across groups, or by sanity-checking that token-level reward proxies correlate with desired behaviors). If such checks fail, falling back to sequence-level advantages (GSPO) is the safer option, per the stability arguments made in the paper.

# Interaction with KL penalties, baselines, and other regularizers

The GSPO objective in the paper omits the KL regularization term for brevity, but the authors note that such terms are commonly used in PPO-style algorithms. The move from token-level importance ratios to sequence-level ratios changes the interpretation and practical role of any additional regularizers.

KL penalties typically constrain the updated policy to stay close to the behavior policy. In a token-level formulation like GRPO, a per-token KL or per-token penalty implicitly controls the local conditional distributions. In GSPO, a KL penalty applied at the sequence level (e.g., constraining $\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x) \,\|\, \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|x)\big)$) is naturally aligned with the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$. This consistency means that a sequence-level KL or KL-based trust-region interacts coherently with the clipping behavior in GSPO: both operate on the same unit (the full response) and so their effects are congruent.

Baselines (value functions or group-based baselines) are another common stabilizer. GRPO was introduced to eliminate the need for an explicit value function by using relative advantages across a group. GSPO inherits this group-based baseline mechanism: advantages are normalized across the generated group $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$. If one were to reintroduce an explicit baseline or value model (e.g., for variance reduction), it should be consistent with the unit of optimization: a sequence-level value estimate $V(x)$ or an estimate per response $V(x,y)$ would be more appropriate than a per-token value that tries to predict cumulative future reward at each token. Mixing mismatched baselines (token-level baseline with sequence-level importance ratios) risks reintroducing the instability GSPO avoids.

Other regularizers—entropy bonuses, parameter norm penalties, or trust-region (e.g., PPO clipping with additional KL constraints)—should likewise be reasoned about at the sequence level when used with GSPO. Practically, the paper’s observations imply that aligning the granularity of regularizers with the granularity of importance weighting reduces conceptual mismatch and the risk of high-variance, ill-posed updates.

# Reward model drift and non-stationarity

The paper emphasizes group-normalized advantages computed from verifier scores $r(x,y)$. In practice, RL for language models often uses learned reward models, which can drift over time as they are updated with new annotations or further training. Reward model drift creates a form of non-stationarity: the mapping $y \mapsto r(x,y)$ changes across optimization steps. The GSPO design mitigates some issues because it ties optimization to sequence-level likelihoods and normalizes rewards within the group; nevertheless, several open questions arise directly from the paper’s findings.

First, group normalization (subtracting mean and dividing by std across $G$ responses) reduces sensitivity to absolute reward scale and can absorb some gradual drift. But abrupt changes in the reward landscape could alter which sequences are considered advantageous within a group and thereby change the sign or magnitude of $\widehat{A}_i$. Because GSPO clips based on $s_i(\theta)$, sudden shifts in advantage ordering could cause previously safe sequences to be treated as off-policy or vice versa, possibly inducing transient instability.

Second, because GSPO avoids token-level importance ratios, it is less likely to amplify short-term fluctuations in per-token likelihoods that could interact badly with a drifting reward model. The sequence-level weighting is inherently more robust to small conditional-probability perturbations, which helps when reward updates are moderate. Yet, if reward model updates are large and frequent, GSPO’s reliance on group comparisons means that the training signal’s semantics can change quickly; this requires attention to training schedules and potentially the use of slower reward-model update cadences or holdout evaluations to detect destabilizing drift.

A practical mitigation strategy consistent with the paper’s approach is to control reward-model changes relative to policy updates: ensure reward-model updates are less frequent or constrained so that the distribution of $\{r(x,y_i)\}_i$ stays approximately stationary across the mini-batches used for gradient estimation. Another strategy is to combine group-normalization with temporal smoothing of advantages (e.g., exponential moving averages), though the paper does not empirically evaluate such schemes—so they remain open directions consistent with the paper’s motivations.

# Handling extremely long sequences and memory constraints

An important motivation for GSPO in the paper is improved stability for long responses and large rollout batch sizes. The algorithm uses a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\Big( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot)} \Big),

which the paper motivates as a way to control variance and unify numerical ranges across different lengths. This normalization reduces extreme multiplicative effects that would occur if one used raw sequence ratios without scaling.

Despite this, extremely long sequences pose practical and theoretical challenges not fully solved in the paper. Computing $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ requires summing or accumulating token log-likelihoods over $|y_i|$ tokens. For very long $|y_i|$, storing per-token quantities for many responses can strain memory, especially when rollouts are large or when models are MoE with large parameter counts. The paper notes that GSPO can partition rollout batches into mini-batches, but that introduces off-policy gaps that GSPO is designed to handle better than GRPO. Still, memory and compute for recomputing sequence likelihoods (under both $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) can be substantial.

Two practical observations in the paper point to solutions. First, GSPO is tolerant of inference-training engine precision mismatches because it only needs sequence-level likelihoods; this opens the door to using lower-precision or inference-optimized engines to compute likelihoods, reducing memory and recomputation costs. Second, the length-normalized exponentiation helps keep $s_i(\theta)$ numerically stable across lengths, simplifying clipping hyperparameter selection.

Open questions remain: how to scale GSPO to scenarios with extremely long sequences (e.g., many thousands of tokens) without recomputing or storing large per-token traces? Possible directions, consistent with the paper’s rationale, include streaming accumulation of log-probabilities (so only a scalar and running sum are stored), using blockwise or chunked evaluation with amortized recomputation, or employing online estimators that keep only the quantities needed for $s_i(\theta)$. The paper does not empirically explore these engineering trade-offs in depth, so they remain fruitful areas for further work.

# Theoretical analysis opportunities: variance bounds, convergence in off-policy regimes

A central conceptual contribution of the paper is the argument that token-level importance ratios violate the basic variance-reduction assumptions of importance sampling because each ratio is computed from a single sample of the next-token distribution. The natural follow-up is to develop formal theoretical analyses that quantify these intuitions.

First, one can analyze variance bounds comparing gradient estimators under GRPO, GSPO, and GSPO-token. The paper derives the gradients for GSPO and GRPO and points out that GRPO weights token gradients with ratios that vary across positions, thereby accumulating unpredictable contributions. These derivations invite precise variance comparisons: under mild probabilistic models of the policy distributions, one could compute or bound Var\!\left[\nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathcal{J}}\right] for each estimator and show conditions under which GSPO yields strictly lower variance—particularly as $|y|$ grows. The length-normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ is another ingredient to consider in such bounds.

Second, the paper motivates GSPO by appealing to importance sampling at the sequence level. There remains an opportunity to study convergence properties in off-policy regimes where samples come from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but the objective optimizes $\pi_\theta$. Formalizing convergence requires assumptions about clipping (which creates bias), group-size $G$ (which affects advantage estimation variance), and the magnitude of per-update policy shifts. One could aim to prove that under controlled step sizes or bounded $s_i(\theta)$ (via clipping or KL constraints), GSPO is a descent method in expectation or at least possesses monotonic improvement guarantees akin to PPO’s approximate policy improvement results. The paper’s gradient expressions (Equations (11) and (14)) provide a starting point for such analyses.

Third, studying the bias introduced by clipping at the sequence level and how it trades off with variance reduction is a concrete theoretical question suggested by the paper. Clipping excludes highly off-policy sequences but also introduces bias in gradient estimates. Characterizing this bias as a function of the clipping thresholds and sequence length, and optimizing clipping schedules, could lead to principled hyperparameter choices beyond the empirical tuning described in the paper.

These theoretical lines of work would strengthen the conceptual foundations of GSPO and clarify its behavior across model scales and task regimes.

# Generalization to non-autoregressive or structured generation tasks

The paper grounds GSPO in the autoregressive language modeling setting, where $\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and sequence likelihoods factor naturally. This structure enables a clear, interpretable sequence-level importance ratio and the length-normalized exponentiation that stabilizes numeric ranges.

Extending the central ideas of GSPO to non-autoregressive or more structured generation paradigms raises both opportunities and challenges. For models that do not factor into a simple product of conditionals—for instance, non-autoregressive sequence models that generate tokens in parallel, structured prediction models that use latent variables, or graph/structured outputs—the notion of a sequence likelihood still exists in principle, but its computation and interpretation can differ. The key requirement from the paper’s perspective is that we must be able to evaluate, under both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, a scalar probability or density for the entire generated structure $y$ conditioned on $x$.

If such evaluation is tractable and numerically stable, GSPO’s principle—define importance ratios at the unit of reward, and clip at that granularity—remains applicable. However, the engineering and statistical considerations change. For example, non-autoregressive models often employ approximate decoding methods or marginalize over latent alignment variables; computing exact sequence likelihoods can be intractable, necessitating approximations that could undermine the theoretical importance-sampling basis GSPO relies on. Similarly, structured outputs with combinatorial spaces may produce highly skewed likelihoods, and the length-normalization concept would need adaptation (e.g., normalization by some measure of structural size or effective degrees of freedom).

An alternative route consistent with the paper’s logic is to identify an appropriate granularity that matches the reward: for tasks where reward is attached to substructures (subtrees, spans, or turns), define importance weighting and clipping at that substructure level rather than at per-token or whole-sequence level. This mirrors the GSPO-token idea but in a structured-space setting: the importance ratio should be computed for the same unit that the reward evaluates.

In sum, generalizing GSPO beyond autoregressive models is feasible in principle when sequence/structure likelihoods are computable and when the optimization granularity is matched to reward granularity. Implementation and theoretical validation in these regimes remain open research directions stemming from the paper’s main arguments.

# Summary and prioritized open questions

The paper’s chief insight—importance weighting and clipping should be applied at the same unit as the reward, and sequence-level importance ratios are more stable than token-level ratios—opens several practical and theoretical avenues. From an applied standpoint, GSPO-token is a useful flexible variant when token-level advantages are reliable; KL and other regularizers should be aligned to the sequence granularity; reward-model drift must be monitored and controlled because group-normalization alone cannot eliminate all non-stationarity effects; extremely long sequences demand engineering solutions that exploit GSPO’s tolerance to inference/training precision mismatches; and GSPO naturally obviates MoE-specific routing replay strategies, simplifying infrastructure. From a theoretical standpoint, rigorous variance bounds, bias-variance trade-offs due to clipping, and convergence analyses under off-policy sampling are key open problems. Finally, adapting GSPO ideas to non-autoregressive and structured generation requires (a) tractable sequence/structure likelihoods or (b) choosing an appropriate substructure granularity that matches the reward.

Concretely, if a researcher or practitioner seeks immediate next steps based on the paper, the prioritized open questions would be:
1. Empirically and theoretically compare variance of gradient estimators for GRPO, GSPO, and GSPO-token as a function of sequence length and group size $G$.
2. Analyze bias introduced by sequence-level clipping and derive principled clipping schedules or KL penalties that balance bias and stability.
3. Evaluate GSPO in settings with learned reward models that are periodically updated, measuring sensitivity to reward-model drift and testing mitigation strategies.
4. Develop engineering patterns (streaming log-prob accumulation, inference-engine likelihood usage) for very long-sequence RL rollouts, quantifying memory/compute trade-offs.
5. Explore extensions to structured-generation tasks, defining appropriate normalization and importance-ratio formulations for non-token outputs.

Addressing these questions will build on the paper’s foundations and help determine the practical limits and broader applicability of GSPO.
