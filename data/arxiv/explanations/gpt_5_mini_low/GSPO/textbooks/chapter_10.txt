\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 10: RL Infrastructure Simplification with GSPO

# Precision mismatches between training and inference engines

In practical large-scale reinforcement learning for language models, two distinct execution environments commonly arise: the inference engine used to sample responses (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) and the training engine used to compute gradients and update model parameters (e.g., Megatron). The paper emphasizes that these engines can differ in numerical precision and implementation details. As a result, the likelihoods or log-likelihoods produced by the inference engine for sampled responses may not exactly match the values that would be produced by the training engine running the same model parameters. This discrepancy is a pragmatic source of error when an RL algorithm depends on likelihood values: if the optimization requires recomputing token-level probabilities under the training engine to obtain accurate importance weights, then the mismatch forces additional computation and data transfer to the training environment.

The GRPO formulation illustrates why this matters. GRPO constructs token-level importance ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]
and these ratios are applied at each token position. Because token-level ratios directly multiply gradients per token, any small numerical differences between engines can lead to inconsistent or unstable gradient estimates unless the token probabilities are recomputed precisely with the training engine. This is why, in practice, systems adopting token-level correction often recompute or re-evaluate token likelihoods in the training engine before optimization to ensure consistency. That recomputation step imposes extra latency, memory, and communication costs: responses sampled by an inference service must be sent or stored and then reprocessed by the training engine, increasing overhead in production RL pipelines.

# GSPO’s tolerance: using inference-engine sequence log-likelihoods directly

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level importance ratio built from sequence likelihoods:
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Because this quantity aggregates token log-probabilities across the entire response and then length-normalizes, it is numerically less sensitive to small per-token precision differences between engines. The paper argues that sequence-level likelihoods are a more natural object in the RL setting for language models because rewards are assigned at the sequence level; thus, the unit of importance sampling correction and clipping should match the unit of reward.

Practically, GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it much more tolerant of the precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. The inference engine can provide sequence log-likelihoods for sampled responses, and GSPO can use those values directly in its objective without an expensive recomputation step in the training engine. The paper explicitly notes this intuition: sequence-level likelihoods are "much more tolerant of precision discrepancies," so GSPO makes it possible to avoid recomputation and accept the inference-engine likelihoods for optimization.

# Benefits in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated systems

The toleration of inference-engine sequence likelihoods has concrete operational advantages across several RL scenarios highlighted in the paper.

In partial rollout settings—where the system produces and stores only parts of a long trajectory or where responses are collected in chunks—the ability to trust sequence-level likelihoods from the inference service avoids the need to reconstitute or re-evaluate entire rollouts in the training engine. Since GSPO operates on complete responses (or length-normalized sequence likelihoods) as its optimization unit, partial outputs that constitute full response units can be scored and used for optimization without additional training-side recomputation.

For multi-turn RL, where each turn may produce responses conditional on an evolving conversation state, token-level recomputation becomes especially costly and complex: the conditioning context can be large, and replaying routing or activation patterns across engines is onerous. Because GSPO matches rewards and corrections at the sequence level, it naturally fits multi-turn workflows where a complete turn (a response to a given query state) is the fundamental sample. The paper also presents a token-level variant (GSPO-token) that retains flexibility for per-token advantages while still basing the correction on a detached sequence-level factor; this allows fine-grained control when necessary without sacrificing the tolerance benefits of a sequence-based importance term.

In disaggregated training-inference frameworks—where inference services and training clusters are separate, possibly geographically or architecturally distinct—avoiding recomputation reduces cross-system data movement and round-trip latency. The paper suggests that GSPO's use of inference-provided sequence likelihoods enables such disaggregation: inference can produce sampled responses, sequence rewards, and sequence log-likelihoods, and the training system can consume these directly for optimization. This reduces the coupling between inference and training stacks and simplifies engineering around model serving, rollout collection, and replay.

# Operational simplifications and potential throughput gains

Adopting GSPO yields multiple operational simplifications, each with implications for throughput and engineering complexity. First, by removing the need to recompute token-level likelihoods in the training engine, the RL pipeline eliminates a costly recomputation step. That step otherwise duplicates the forward pass computation on the training side for each sampled response, adding GPU time and increasing I/O between inference collectors and training nodes. Eliminating it reduces end-to-end latency for rollouts to be available for gradient computation and reduces the demand on training GPUs for pure likelihood evaluation work.

Second, GSPO reduces the dependency on precise numerical parity between engines. When token-level ratios are required, teams must ensure that both inference and training engines use matching precision regimes and numerically consistent implementations; mismatches can force conservative and complex engineering workarounds (e.g., recomputation, routing replay). GSPO relaxes that requirement because sequence-level likelihoods are more robust to small per-token numeric differences. This allows organizations to use highly optimized inference engines for high-throughput rollout collection while keeping training on separate, possibly differently optimized, clusters.

Third, the paper demonstrates additional benefits specific to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. GRPO required a Routing Replay strategy to restore token-level importance ratio stability when expert-activation volatility caused token-likelihoods to shift after parameter updates. Routing Replay incurs memory and communication overhead and constrains model capacity. GSPO obviates Routing Replay because sequence likelihoods remain stable enough despite expert routing changes; this simplifies the RL training pipeline for MoE models and allows the model to use its capacity without forced routing reuse.

Collectively, these simplifications translate into potential throughput gains: fewer duplicate forward passes, reduced cross-engine communication, and less engineering effort devoted to preserving token-level parity. While the paper does not provide exact throughput numbers, it reports that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency compared to GRPO under the same compute and query budgets. The improved efficiency arises partly from using sequence-level corrections that give more reliable learning signals and partly from the operational savings described above. The net effect is a streamlined RL infrastructure that can scale rollouts and training more effectively.

# Final remarks

The central operational message from the paper is straightforward: aligning the unit of importance sampling and clipping with the unit of reward—i.e., moving from token-level to sequence-level corrections—yields both algorithmic robustness and pragmatic engineering benefits. By tolerating inference-traced sequence likelihoods and eliminating the necessity of retracing token probabilities in a separate training engine, GSPO simplifies distributed RL pipelines, reduces overhead in partial-rollout and multi-turn workflows, and removes brittle workarounds required for MoE models. For teams building large-scale RL systems for language models, GSPO therefore offers a path to simplify infrastructure while improving stability and efficiency.
