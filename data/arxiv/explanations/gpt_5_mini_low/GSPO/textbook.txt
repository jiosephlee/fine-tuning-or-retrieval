\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models: Context and Goals

# Why RL for LLMs: deep reasoning, math, coding, multi-step synthesis

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central ingredient for advancing the capabilities of autoregressive language models. In the setting considered in this paper, a language model parameterized by $\theta$ is treated as a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maps a query $x$ to a distribution over responses $y$. The reward function $r(x,y)$ evaluates full query–response pairs, producing a scalar in $[0,1]$ that reflects task success as judged by a verifier. This framing enables objectives that go beyond one-step next-token likelihood: instead of merely learning local continuation statistics, RL allows models to improve toward end-to-end task metrics that require multi-step planning, extended chain-of-thought, or program synthesis.

Concretely, when tasks require deep reasoning (for example, multi-step mathematics or programming), performance is determined not by any single token choice but by the entire generated sequence. Reinforcement learning with sequence-level rewards encourages the model to prefer trajectories that lead to high final reward even if some intermediate token probabilities are individually low under the behavior policy. This is crucial for tasks where correct solutions require coordinated sequences of tokens, long-range dependency handling, or multi-turn interactions. The paper emphasizes that large-scale RL equips language models with abilities to tackle competition-level mathematics and programming by incentivizing the model to execute longer, more coherent reasoning processes that maximize task-level success.

# Stability as the bottleneck when scaling compute, length, and model size

Scaling RL for language models means increasing model size, response length, rollout batch size, and computational investment. However, the authors identify training stability as the primary bottleneck in this scaling process. Several interacting factors make stability challenging. First, as response length $|y|$ grows, any variance in gradient estimation that accumulates across tokens can make optimization noisy and unpredictable. Second, large models—especially those with sparse architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)—exhibit additional sources of nonstationarity and volatility; small parameter changes can alter which experts are activated for the same input, which in turn changes token likelihoods in a non-smooth way. Third, practical engineering such as partitioning large rollout batches into mini-batches creates an effectively off-policy setting: samples are drawn from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while gradients are computed for a new policy $\pi_\theta$. Off-policy correction is therefore necessary, and the commonly used clipping mechanisms (originating from PPO) are intended to limit the influence of overly off-policy samples.

The combination of long sequences, off-policy sampling, and model nonstationarity leads to two intertwined problems: high-variance gradient estimates and difficulties in reliably estimating advantages. Methods that address only part of this problem can still leave the optimization prone to catastrophic instability. The paper makes the central claim that without a principled treatment of the importance-weighting unit and its interaction with the reward unit, accumulated variance and clipping artifacts can drive irreversible collapse in large-scale RL training.

# Observed collapse in state-of-the-art methods (e.g., GRPO) on large models

The paper documents that prior group-based methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) can exhibit severe instability and even catastrophic model collapse when applied to large models and long responses. GRPO avoids explicit value estimation by computing a relative advantage for each response within a group of $G$ responses to the same query. Its objective uses a token-level importance ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
and applies clipping on these token-level ratios inside the per-token loss. While this design removes the need for a separate value model, the paper argues it is ill-posed in practice for two reasons.

First, importance sampling theory relies on re-weighting entire-sample likelihoods to correct distributional mismatch between a behavior distribution and a target distribution. Applying importance weights computed from single-token samples of the next-token distribution does not perform the intended distribution correction: each ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is estimated from a single draw $y_{i,t}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, so it cannot reduce bias reliably and instead injects high variance. Second, these token-level weights can vary across positions inside a sequence and across tokens in a way that accumulates over long sequences. When combined with clipping, this accumulation of noisy weights can lead to unpredictable optimization dynamics; empirically, the authors report cases where training collapses irreversibly. For MoE models, the problem is aggravated: after some updates the set of activated experts for the same sample can change substantially, causing the token-level likelihoods (and hence importance ratios) to fluctuate dramatically. The paper reports roughly 10% expert-activation change after each gradient update in a deep MoE model, which further destabilizes token-weighted objectives like GRPO.

In short, GRPO's token-level importance weighting misaligns the unit of importance correction with the unit of reward (the sequence), producing high-variance gradients and practical training failure at scale.

# High-level preview of GSPO: sequence-level importance ratios and clipping

To address these failure modes, the paper introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). The core conceptual change is to move the importance weighting and clipping from the token level to the sequence level. GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio for a response $y_i$ to query $x$ as
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
This length-normalized form ties the importance weight directly to the likelihood of the entire generated sequence under the new and old policies, and the normalization by $|y_i|$ controls variance and places sequence importance ratios on a comparable numerical scale across different lengths.

GSPO optimizes a group-based sequence-level clipped objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right],
\]
where the group advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed across the $G$ responses to the same query:
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
\]
Because the reward is assigned at the sequence level, applying importance correction and clipping at the same sequence granularity aligns the optimization unit with the reward unit, which both reduces variance and avoids the accumulation of per-token noise. The authors show analytically that GSPO weights all tokens in a sequence equally in the gradient (after accounting for the length normalization), in contrast to GRPO where token gradients are weighted by potentially noisy per-token importance ratios. This equal weighting reduces another source of instability.

The paper also presents a token-level variant called GSPO-token, which is numerically equivalent to GSPO when per-token advantages are set equal across a sequence, but which allows per-token advantage customization in multi-turn settings. GSPO-token uses the sequence-level ratio as a detached scalar and multiplies it by token-level likelihoods so that the optimization still respects sequence-level correction while permitting finer advantage shaping.

# Roadmap of the textbook

This chapter has situated reinforcement learning in the context of large language models and explained why sequence-level reward structures and stability considerations motivate algorithmic changes. The paper’s central contribution, GSPO, replaces token-level importance weighting with a principled sequence-level importance ratio and sequence-level clipping, aligning the unit of importance sampling with the unit of reward. Subsequent chapters in this textbook will unpack the technical details and implications introduced here: a chapter on the formal derivation and gradient analysis of GSPO will walk through the equations for the objective and gradients, comparing them term-by-term with GRPO to make the source of variance explicit. Following that, an empirical chapter will explore experimental results, including the behavior of clipping fractions, training curves on benchmarks like AIME and CodeForces, and how GSPO affects MoE-specific phenomena such as expert-activation volatility and the need for Routing Replay. Finally, a chapter on practical engineering will discuss length normalization, hyperparameter choices for clipping ranges, and implications for RL infrastructure—how sequence-level likelihoods reduce sensitivity to engine precision differences and can simplify likelihood recomputation in production settings. Together, these chapters will give you the tools to understand the algorithmic reasoning, evaluate experimental claims, and apply GSPO-style principles to future RL problems with language models.

Preliminaries: Notation, Setup, and Baseline Algorithms

# Autoregressive policy, queries, responses, and sequence likelihood

We treat a large autoregressive language model as a stochastic policy in the reinforcement learning sense. The policy is parameterized by $\theta$ and denoted $\pi_{\theta}$. The environment provides a query (or prompt) $x$ drawn from a query set $\mathcal{D}$. Given $x$, the policy produces a response $y$, which is an ordered sequence of discrete tokens $y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{|y|})$ where $|y|$ denotes the response length.

Because the policy is autoregressive, the likelihood of a whole response under $\pi_{\theta}$ factorizes over tokens by the chain rule of probability:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{|y|}
\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]
where $y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$. This sequence likelihood is central to how we measure how probable a sampled response is under a policy and it will be used to form sequence-level importance ratios and training objectives.

Describing the model as a policy makes it straightforward to apply RL concepts: sampling responses corresponds to rollouts, and gradients with respect to $\theta$ are derived from log-likelihoods of sampled tokens or sequences. Importantly, whether we reason at the token level or the sequence level determines how we construct importance weights and how we correct for off-policy sampling; this distinction is a central theme when comparing baseline algorithms.

# Reward model / verifier

To convert generated responses into scalar signals suitable for learning, we use a reward model or verifier. The verifier is a deterministic scoring function $r$ that maps a query-response pair to a numerical reward in a bounded range:
\[
r: (x,y) \mapsto r(x,y) \in [0,1].
\]
This score can represent automated quality metrics, correctness checks, human preference models, or other utilities depending on the task. The reward is assigned to the whole response $y$ (sequence-level reward), not to individual tokens, unless an external token-level credit assignment mechanism is provided.

Because the reward is sequence-level, any learning objective that uses token-level importance corrections must justify the token-level decomposition. A core insight that motivates later algorithmic design is that the unit of optimization (token vs sequence) should align with the unit of reward; mismatches create theoretical and practical difficulties in importance weighting and variance control.

# Off-policy updates with rollout batches and mini-batching

In practice, RL optimization for large language models is performed using batched rollouts. We sample many responses from the current running policy (or from an older saved policy) to form a rollout batch. To maximize hardware utilization and stabilize training, practitioners typically split a large rollout batch into several mini-batches and perform multiple gradient updates per rollout batch.

This partitioning introduces an off-policy learning setting because the model parameters change between the time we sampled responses and the later gradient updates. Let $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ denote the policy that generated the rollouts, and $\pi_{\theta}$ denote the current policy at update time. When we compute gradients with the responses sampled under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ but optimize $\theta$, we must correct for the distributional mismatch between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Without correction, gradient estimates can be biased and unstable. Importance sampling provides a principled way to reweight off-policy samples, but its practical application depends on the choice of the importance weight (token-level vs sequence-level) and on effective variance control mechanisms such as clipping.

Because rollout batches are large and we often perform several epochs of optimization on the same data, we need mechanisms that prevent overly large updates driven by off-policy samples. Clipping of importance ratios, KL regularization, or trust-region constraints are commonly used to keep updates in a proximal neighborhood of the behavior policy and to limit the harmful effects of extreme importance weights.

# PPO objective: token-level importance ratio, clipping, and value model dependency

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely used on-policy RL algorithm that has been adapted to language-model fine-tuning. The PPO recipe enforces a proximity constraint to the behavior policy using clipped importance ratios. When adapted for autoregressive models, PPO commonly forms token-level objectives: each token prediction at position $t$ contributes a term to the objective, and the importance ratio is taken at the next-token distribution.

Concretely, given rollouts sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, PPO often optimizes an objective of the form
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_t\bigr)
\right],
\]
where the token-level importance ratio is
\[
w_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
and $\widehat{A}_t$ is an estimate of the advantage at token position $t$. The clip operator prevents the scaled advantage from changing sign due to large importance weights beyond the range $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$.

A central feature of PPO is that it relies on an advantage estimator, which typically comes from a learned value function. The value model estimates expected future reward from a given state (in the language setting, often a token-conditioned state) and supplies the baseline subtraction needed to compute the advantage $\widehat{A}_t$. This value component is critical for variance reduction and for providing a meaningful per-token credit assignment.

# Challenges of PPO for LLMs: compute, memory, and value accuracy

Applying PPO directly to large language models raises several practical challenges. First, the standard PPO pipeline requires maintaining a value model that is comparable in size to the policy model. For state-of-the-art LLMs, this doubles GPU memory consumption and computational requirements when the value network is architecturally similar to the policy. For models that are already pushing memory limits (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts with many experts), this extra cost can be prohibitive.

Second, effective PPO performance depends on the accuracy of the value estimates. Estimating token-conditional expected returns in long-horizon generation tasks is difficult: rewards are often sparse or delayed (sequence-level verifiers), and value learning becomes a hard supervised learning problem in its own right. Errors in the value model lead to biased advantage estimates, which in turn degrade policy updates and can destabilize training.

Third, as response lengths grow, the per-token credit assignment and the propagation of value estimates across many steps amplify variance and can result in noisy gradient signals. The practical consequence is that PPO may require careful hyperparameter tuning, extensive engineering, and additional infrastructure to scale reliably to the largest models and longest responses.

# GRPO objective: group-based advantages without a value model

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was developed to reduce dependence on a learned value model by computing relative advantages within a group of responses for the same query. Instead of training a value network, GRPO generates $G$ responses for each query under the behavior policy and uses the group statistics to derive advantage-like signals.

The GRPO objective is typically written as
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t},\; \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}\bigr)
\right],
\]
where the token-level importance ratio for the $i$-th response at position $t$ is
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
and the per-response advantage is derived from group normalization of rewards:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i}
=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)}{\mathrm{std}\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)}.
\]
GRPO typically sets $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$, so all tokens in the same response share the same advantage.

The idea behind GRPO is simple and pragmatic: by comparing responses sampled for the same prompt, we obtain a relative ranking (or z-scored) reward signal that serves the role of an advantage without needing a separately trained value function. This group advantage reduces infrastructure costs and can adapt quickly to changing reward distributions.

# Group size G, per-response z-score advantage, and token-level weighting in GRPO

Group size $G$ is a key hyperparameter in GRPO. For each query $x$, the algorithm samples $G$ responses from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. The per-response advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed by z-scoring the reward values within this group:
\[
\widehat{A}_i
=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G r(x,y_j)}
{\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G\bigl(r(x,y_j)-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{k=1}^G r(x,y_k)\bigr)^2}}.
\]
This normalization makes advantages zero-mean and unit-variance within each group, which stabilizes optimization and provides relative preference information among the group responses.

However, GRPO retains token-level importance weighting: each token gradient contribution is multiplied by the importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. The practical rationale is to apply importance sampling correction at the granularity where the model defines probabilities (the next-token distributions). But this design raises a conceptual tension: the reward is sequence-level (assigned to the whole $y_i$), while the importance correction is applied at the token level using single-token samples from each next-token distribution.

This mismatch has consequences. Importance sampling theory justifies re-weighting by the ratio between target and behavior densities when we average the re-weighted values over many samples of the random variable being corrected. In the token-level GRPO setting, the importance ratio at a given token position is computed from a single sampled token $y_{i,t}$ for the corresponding next-token distribution. Because the ratio is not aggregated over many samples of that next-token distribution, it does not robustly estimate the correction needed for the discrepancy between the full distributions. Instead, it injects high-variance correction factors into the gradient. Over long sequences, the accumulation of these token-level variance contributions can impair learning and even cause catastrophic instability.

In summary, GRPO is an appealing baseline because it removes the need for a value model by using group-based, per-response normalized rewards. But it continues to apply importance sampling at the token level, and that design choice interacts poorly with long sequences, large models, and architectures like Mixture-of-Experts where token-level likelihoods may be volatile between successive policy updates.

# Closing remarks

This chapter set up the notation and the key practical considerations for RL applied to autoregressive language models. We formalized policies $\pi_{\theta}$, sequence likelihoods $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, and the sequence-level reward function $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$. We discussed how large rollout batches and mini-batching create an off-policy learning problem that necessitates importance correction and clipping to stabilize updates.

We reviewed two baseline approaches: PPO, which uses token-level importance ratios together with a value model to compute token-wise advantages; and GRPO, which replaces the value model with group-based, per-response z-scored advantages but retains token-level importance weighting. The tension between the unit of reward (sequence) and the unit of importance correction (token) in PPO/GRPO motivates alternative designs that align the importance ratios and clipping with the sequence-level reward; such considerations lead directly to the sequence-level methods described later in the paper.

Diagnosing Instability: Why Token-Level Importance Weighting Breaks

# Importance sampling principle: estimating expectations under target vs behavior distributions

Importance sampling is a basic technique for estimating an expectation under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a different, behavior distribution. Formally, for a random variable \(z\) and a function \(f(z)\), the expectation under the target distribution \(\pi_\text{tar}\) can be written using samples from the behavior distribution \(\pi_\text{beh}\) as
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right].
\]
This identity is exact whenever the support of \(\pi_\text{tar}\) is contained in the support of \(\pi_\text{beh}\). The re-weighting factor \(\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\) corrects the distributional mismatch between where the samples actually came from and where we wish to evaluate expectations. In policy optimization for sequence generation, this same idea underlies on-policy correction when we optimize a new policy \(\pi_\theta\) using samples produced by an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\).

Two aspects are crucial for importance sampling to function well in practice. First, the importance weight must be appropriate to the unit of the sampled object \(z\): if we want to estimate expectations over whole sequences, the weight should reflect the whole-sequence likelihood ratio. Second, importance sampling tends to have high variance when individual weights vary widely; averaging across many independent samples mitigates that variance. The canonical statement of importance sampling therefore presumes many samples from the behavior distribution so that the re-weighted average converges and noise is reduced.

# Need for averaging over many samples per distribution to reduce variance

The variance properties of importance-sampled estimators are central to their practical use. When the importance weights \(\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\) fluctuate substantially, a few samples with very large weights can dominate the estimate, producing high-variance or unstable gradient estimates. A standard remedy is to average over a large number \(N\) of independent draws \(z_1,\dots,z_N \sim \pi_\text{beh}\). As \(N\) grows, the law of large numbers causes the empirical importance-weighted average to concentrate around the true expectation, and the effective noise contributed by the weight variability decreases.

In structured prediction or autoregressive generation, the same principle applies at whatever granularity we choose for \(z\). If \(z\) is an entire response \(y\), then many sampled responses are needed for stable sequence-level importance weighting. If \(z\) is a next-token sample from a single next-token distribution, then the weight for that next-token correction will be computed from a single sample of that local distribution. That single-sample construction cannot be averaged at that local position and therefore cannot play the role that importance sampling is intended to play: it cannot correct distributional mismatch by averaging out sampling noise. Put differently, importance weighting only reduces bias relative to the target distribution when its weights are estimated in a way that allows aggregation across many independent samples; single-sample weights do not provide that aggregation and thus are not effective importance-correction mechanisms.

# Token-level weighting in GRPO: single-sample next-token correction is ineffective

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) adopts token-level importance ratios. For a token \(y_{i,t}\) in the \(i\)-th response to query \(x\), GRPO defines the token importance weight
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
This weight is applied at each token position in the PPO-style objective and is intended to correct the fact that tokens were sampled from \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) while we update \(\pi_\theta\). However, each \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is constructed from exactly one sampled next token \(y_{i,t}\) drawn from the single next-token distribution \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})\). There is no within-position averaging across alternative next-token samples, so the token-level importance weight is effectively a single-sample ratio rather than an estimator that benefits from many independent draws.

Because importance sampling relies on averaging many samples to control variance and produce a reliable correction, the token-level correction in GRPO fails to implement the averaging that makes importance sampling useful. Instead, it introduces a noisy multiplicative factor tied to the randomness of a single next-token draw. That single-sample noise becomes a direct source of variance in the gradient estimator and is not reduced by the grouping or by other design choices in GRPO. Thus, the token-level weight is not an effective importance-correction term and should not be expected to produce the stable, low-variance corrections that importance sampling theory prescribes.

# Variance accumulation over long sequences and amplification by clipping

Autoregressive models generate sequences by multiplying many local next-token probabilities, so any per-token randomness can compound across a whole response. In GRPO, each token contributes an independent single-sample importance weight and a corresponding multiplicative factor inside the gradient. When sequences are long, these token-wise noisy contributions accumulate: small fluctuations at many positions can combine into large, unpredictable deviations in the gradient. The cumulative effect is that gradient noise grows with sequence length, making training progressively more unstable as the target tasks require longer responses.

Clipping, as used in PPO-style objectives, is intended to prevent overly off-policy samples from dominating the update by truncating extreme importance ratios. But in GRPO the clipping operates at the token level, truncating these single-sample ratios. That truncation does not remove the root cause (single-sample noise per token); instead, it interacts with the already high-variance token-weighting in two harmful ways. First, clipping introduces a nonlinearity that depends on the sign of the advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\): for positively advantaged tokens the weights are clipped on one side, and for negatively advantaged tokens on the other. When token-level ratios are noisy and vary heterogeneously across positions, clipping can systematically bias gradient directions in ways that amplify instability rather than control it. Second, because token-level clipping is applied independently at each position, the number of clipped tokens grows with sequence length; the accumulation of clipped positions further distorts the intended importance correction and injects structured, correlated noise into the optimization.

The combination of single-sample token weights that accumulate variance across long sequences and a clipping mechanism that nonlinearly interacts with that noise creates a pathway to catastrophic, hard-to-reverse mismatch between the intended update and its realized effect.

# Empirical symptom: irreversible model collapse despite hyperparameter rescue attempts

The paper reports a strong empirical manifestation of the theoretical diagnosis: when training large models (especially on long-response tasks) with GRPO, catastrophic model collapse occurs. Collapse here denotes abrupt, severe degradation of the model that cannot be recovered by conventional remedies. Critically, experiments showed that once such a collapse happens it is often irreversible in practice: simply reverting to an earlier checkpoint and re-running training with carefully tuned hyperparameters (for example, different clipping ranges), extending generation length, or switching the RL queries does not restore normal training behavior.

This irreversibility is consistent with the idea that token-level single-sample noise can, over multiple updates, push the model into regions where its likelihoods and routing behaviour (in the case of Mixture-of-Experts models) change dramatically and unpredictably. The clipping mechanism, rather than preventing harm, can turn the noisy importance weights into biased gradient contributions that drive the policy away from stable regions. Once the policy has been driven sufficiently far by these compounded and biased updates, the resulting parameter configuration may be outside the basin of attraction of desirable optima, making recovery by merely altering tuning parameters ineffective. The empirical collapse thus supports the theoretical critique: token-level importance weighting produces high-variance, accumulating, and potentially biased updates that can permanently degrade the model.

# Key design principle: align unit of optimization with unit of reward (sequence)

The central design lesson arising from this diagnosis is succinct: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. In the RL setup considered in the paper, rewards are assigned to complete responses \(y\). Therefore, applying off-policy correction and clipping at the token level is a mismatch: the reward signal aggregates over the whole sequence, but the importance correction attempts to operate locally at each token on the basis of a single sampled token. This mismatch invalidates the intended role of importance sampling and introduces harmful variance.

The natural remedy is to perform importance weighting and clipping at the same granularity as the reward — namely, the sequence level. Sequence-level importance ratios
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}
\]
correctly quantify how atypical the sampled response \(y\) is under the current policy relative to the older policy, and they align directly with the sequence-level reward. Moreover, sequence-level weights can be averaged across different sampled responses to reduce variance in the usual importance-sampling way. To control length-dependent effects, the paper proposes a length-normalized sequence ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
which keeps the numerical range more stable across response lengths and prevents a few token likelihood changes from causing dramatic fluctuations in the sequence weight.

By matching the unit of importance correction to the unit of reward (the entire sequence), we restore the theoretical legitimacy of importance sampling in this setting and avoid the single-sample, position-wise noise that undermines token-level approaches. This alignment is the conceptual foundation of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), which replaces token-level weighting with sequence-level weights, performs clipping per response, and estimates advantages across groups of responses to the same query. The result is a practical algorithm that reduces gradient variance accumulation, stabilizes training (including for large Mixture-of-Experts models), and empirically avoids the irreversible collapses observed under token-level importance weighting.

GSPO: Group Sequence Policy Optimization

# Sequence-level objective with response-wise clipping

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) formulates policy improvement at the level of whole responses (sequences) rather than at the token level. The central objective GSPO optimizes is an expectation over queries $x$ and groups of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Big( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}\big(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i\Big)
\right].
\]
Here the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is computed for the entire response $y_i$ and the clipping operator is applied to that single scalar per response. Applying clipping response-wise means that an entire sampled response is either trusted (possibly re-weighted within the clipping window) or effectively limited; we neither multiply nor clip individual token contributions separately. This design aligns the unit of optimization with the unit at which the verifier assigns reward: entire responses receive scores $r(x,y)$, so it is natural and coherent to perform off-policy correction and clipping at the same granularity.

The response-wise clipping serves two practical roles. First, it prevents excessively off-policy responses from dominating gradient estimation by bounding the sequence-level importance weight. Second, it preserves coherent gradients across all tokens within a response: all token log-likelihood gradients for $y_i$ are scaled uniformly by the same clipped factor, avoiding per-token imbalance that can lead to noisy and unstable updates.

# Group-based normalized advantages (z-score within a query’s responses)

GSPO uses a group-relative advantage estimate computed across the set of $G$ responses generated for the same query. For response $y_i$ to query $x$, the advantage is obtained by z-scoring the verifier reward within the group:
\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}
{\mathrm{std}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}.
\]
This group-based normalization has two intended effects. Intuitively, it removes per-query bias and rescales rewards to a common variance so that learning signals are comparable across different queries and groups. Because the responses in a group share the same query context, comparing them directly isolates the relative quality of model generations for that query, which is precisely the information needed to nudge the policy toward better outputs in that context.

Using the normalized advantage as the training signal also obviates reliance on a separate value model. Instead of predicting an absolute expected return for each prefix or token, GSPO relies on within-query ranking and scaling. This reduces memory and compute costs and avoids instabilities that come from training a large auxiliary value network, which is especially valuable when scaling RL to very large language models.

# Sequence-level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta) = \big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\big)^{1/|y|}\)

The importance ratio in GSPO is defined using sequence likelihoods. For response $y_i$ of length $|y_i|$, GSPO sets
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
This ratio measures how the full-response likelihood under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ compares to that under the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Importantly, GSPO uses this single scalar to reweight (and clip) the contribution of the entire response $y_i$ in the objective. In the unclipped gradient form (omitting the min/clip), the gradient contribution from response $y_i$ is proportional to
\[
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\cdot\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}\widehat{A}_i
\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]
Thus every token gradient inside a response is weighted equally by the same response-level factor, and that factor reflects the intended importance-sampling correction at the sequence scale.

# Length normalization: controlling variance and unified clipping scales

GSPO raises the sequence likelihood ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$, i.e., performs a per-token geometric mean of probability ratios. This length normalization has two pragmatic motivations described in the paper. First, it reduces variance: raw sequence likelihood ratios multiply token-level ratios across $|y_i|$ positions and can therefore explode or vanish for longer responses. Taking the $|y_i|$-th root moderates these multiplicative effects, yielding a more stable numerical quantity that does not systematically shrink or grow with sequence length.

Second, length normalization enables a unified clipping scale across responses of different lengths. If $s_i(\theta)$ were the raw product of token ratios, longer responses would naturally have much larger dynamic range and thus require different clipping magnitudes than short responses. By normalizing by length, GSPO places importance ratios from responses of varying lengths into comparable numeric ranges, allowing a single clipping interval $(1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)$ to be meaningfully applied to all responses. This simplification makes hyperparameter tuning and stable optimization more tractable.

# How GSPO matches reward granularity and off-policy correction

A core design principle emphasized in the paper is that the unit of importance sampling correction should match the unit at which the reward is provided. In the RL for language generation considered here, the verifier assigns a scalar reward $r(x,y)$ to an entire response $y$. Applying token-level importance weights, as in GRPO and token-wise PPO variants, attempts to correct distributional mismatch at each next-token distribution even though the reward is sequence-level. This creates a conceptual mismatch: importance sampling is intended to reweight whole-sample expectations, and estimating a sequence expectation by token-wise single-sample corrections undermines the statistical role of importance weights and introduces high variance.

GSPO resolves this by performing off-policy correction at the sequence level. The sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}$ correctly accounts for how much a sampled response $y$ is representative under the current policy relative to the behavior policy. Averaging many such sequence-weighted contributions in the expectation approximates the desired on-policy objective. Therefore, GSPO restores the intended semantics of importance sampling for sequence-valued samples, leading to more meaningful and lower-variance corrections and avoiding the accumulated noise and instability caused by token-wise ratios applied to single-token samples.

# Choosing clipping ranges: different magnitude vs token-level methods

Because GSPO defines importance ratios differently from token-level methods, the numerical scale and distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ differ substantially from token-wise ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. The paper emphasizes that clipping ranges appropriate for token-level importance weights (used in GRPO or PPO) are not directly transferable to GSPO. In experiments, clipping intervals for GSPO are set to extremely small numeric ranges (e.g., left and right clipping ranges like $3\times10^{-4}$ and $4\times10^{-4}$ in the paper’s presented runs), reflecting that $s_i(\theta)$ — particularly after length normalization — occupies a different numeric regime than per-token ratios.

Consequently, choosing clipping ranges in GSPO requires consideration of the distribution of sequence-level normalized ratios in practice. The critical point is not the absolute numeric magnitude but the consistency between the ratio definition and clipping: because GSPO clips entire-response ratios, the clipping should be set to effectively exclude responses that are too far off-policy at the sequence scale, while retaining most mildly off-policy but informative samples. In contrast, token-level clipping excludes or limits individual token contributions and therefore typically interacts with token-wise ratio magnitudes and their per-token variance, which behaves quite differently from the sequence-level case.

# Intuition: fewer, more meaningful weights; exclude overly off-policy responses

GSPO replaces many token-wise importance weights per response with a single, meaningful sequence-level weight. This design reduces the number of independent reweighting factors and enforces coherent treatment of all tokens in a single sampled response. The intuition is that when the reward is assigned at the sequence level, we care about whether the whole sampled sequence is likely under the current policy; token-level variations inside a single sampled sequence are not informative for correcting the off-policy bias of that sample.

By computing and clipping sequence-level importance ratios, GSPO effectively excludes or down-weights entire responses that are overly off-policy. This is preferable to token-wise clipping which may still allow a response with wildly mismatched overall likelihood to be partially counted via some tokens whose per-token ratios fall within the clipping window. GSPO’s approach therefore focuses learning on a smaller set of reliable, moderately off-policy sequences, yielding lower-variance gradient estimates and more stable training dynamics. The empirical findings in the paper show that, despite clipping a larger fraction of tokens when viewed token-wise, GSPO attains higher training efficiency than token-level methods: pruning whole responses that are too off-policy produces cleaner learning signals and avoids the noisy, accumulating interference that arises from token-wise importance corrections.

In sum, GSPO’s sequence-level reweighting and clipping, combined with group-based normalized advantages and length normalization, produce a coherent algorithmic picture: align the unit of importance sampling with the unit of reward, stabilize the numerical behavior across sequence lengths, and trade many noisy local corrections for a single, robust global correction per sampled response. This conceptual alignment is the core reason GSPO yields more stable and efficient RL training for large language models according to the paper.

Gradient Mechanics: GSPO vs GRPO

# Unclipped gradient of GSPO: response-level weight times average token log-gradients

We begin by writing the unclipped GSPO objective (restricting attention to the policy-dependent term and omitting any KL regularizer). For a batch of queries x and, for each query, a group of G sampled responses \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G from the behavior policy \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio and a group-based advantage \widehat{A}_i. The unclipped objective (per Equation (7) in the paper, without the \min/\mathrm{clip} for clarity) is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \right],
\]

where the sequence-level importance ratio is length-normalized:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Taking the gradient with respect to \theta and pushing the derivative inside the expectation gives (as in Equation (10) of the paper):

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\right].
\]

Expanding \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta) using the sequence factorization of an autoregressive policy yields

\[
\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Substituting this back produces the compact form:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This expression makes two structural points clear. First, each response y_i contributes a single scalar multiplier s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i. Second, that multiplier scales the average (over token positions t\in\{1,\dots,|y_i|\}) of the token log-gradients \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot). In other words, GSPO applies a response-level weight, and then treats all tokens within the response equally by averaging their log-gradients.

# Unclipped gradient of GRPO: per-token importance-weighted gradients

By contrast, GRPO formulates the objective and gradient at the token level (again omitting clipping for clarity; see Equation (4) and its gradient in the paper). The GRPO unclipped objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\right],
\]

with the per-token importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Differentiating produces (as in the paper)

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\widehat{A}_i \cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

Observe that in this form each token log-gradient \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot) is multiplied individually by its own importance weight w_{i,t}(\theta). There is no single response-level scalar applied uniformly to all token gradients; instead, the scaling is per-token and depends only on the pair of current and old token conditional likelihoods for that position.

# Consequence: GSPO equal token weights per response; GRPO unequal token weights

Comparing the two gradient formulas side by side highlights the essential structural difference:

- In GSPO, the contribution of response y_i to the parameter gradient is s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i times the average token log-gradient:
  \[
  \text{GSPO term for }y_i:\quad s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
  \]
  All token gradients inside the same response are weighted equally by the same scalar s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i.

- In GRPO, the contribution of response y_i is the average over token-wise scaled gradients:
  \[
  \text{GRPO term for }y_i:\quad \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
  w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
  \]
  Here different token gradients within y_i can receive different multiplicative factors w_{i,t}(\theta).

Thus GSPO enforces equality of token weights within each response; GRPO allows—and indeed applies—heterogeneous weighting across token positions.

# Why unequal token weights accumulate instability over time in GRPO

The paper identifies the root cause of severe instability and catastrophic collapse observed in GRPO training as a misapplication of importance sampling at the token level. Importance sampling is meant to reweight sample-level contributions so that an expectation under a target distribution can be estimated using samples from a behavior distribution. This requires that the importance weight correspond to the probability ratio of the entire sampled object (here, a next-token sample would need many alternative next-token samples to average over to reduce variance). In GRPO, the weight w_{i,t}(\theta) is computed using only a single observed token sample y_{i,t} drawn from \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t}). Because each token weight is based on a single sample from that conditionals distribution, it cannot effectively perform the role of correcting distributional mismatch with low variance.

Two interacting mechanisms make this particularly damaging over training time:

1. Accumulation over sequence length. Token-level weights are applied at every position, and their effects add (or multiply, in likelihood terms) across positions. Small, high-variance fluctuations in many token weights accumulate through long sequences, producing large unpredictable perturbations in the gradient estimator. As sequence length increases, the summed effect of many noisy per-token multipliers grows, increasing instability.

2. Interaction with clipping. GRPO applies clipping per token to prevent extremely off-policy token samples from driving the update. But clipping a noisy per-token weight truncates some contributions while leaving others, which biases and reshapes the gradient in a way that depends on token-level noise. Over many updates this non-uniform truncation accumulates into systematic, hard-to-predict shifts of the model parameters. Empirically the paper reports that such noise and bias can drive irreversible collapse: once the model collapses under GRPO, reverting to a checkpoint and adjusting hyperparameters often cannot recover training.

Moreover, in models with sparse activation like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, small parameter changes can alter which experts are routed for particular tokens. That means the token conditional likelihoods \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot) can change not only numerically but structurally under small parameter updates. This amplifies the per-token weight volatility: a token's w_{i,t}(\theta) may swing drastically because the forward network used to compute \pi_\theta at that position changes. In GRPO these swings produce wildly varying per-token multipliers; in GSPO the sequence-level likelihoods remain comparatively stable because they average over tokens and the overall language modeling capability is less sensitive to per-token expert routing volatility.

Taken together, unequal per-token weights introduce high variance and biased gradient estimates whose effects compound across tokens, updates, and long responses, producing training instability that is difficult to control.

# Effect on variance and sample efficiency

From a variance and sample-efficiency perspective, the two gradient forms behave differently because of how they incorporate importance ratios.

GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio s_i(\theta) is computed from the entire sampled response y_i and then length-normalized. By applying one scalar per response, GSPO concentrates the off-policy correction at the response granularity, which better respects the unit of the reward signal (the verifier score r(x,y) is assigned at the sequence level). This has several variance-reducing and sample-efficient consequences discussed in the paper:

- Averaging token log-gradients within a response reduces variance. Instead of many independently noisy per-token multipliers, GSPO multiplies the mean token gradient by a single scalar. The averaging inside the response smooths out token-level noise, making the learning signal more stable.

- Length normalization controls the numerical range of s_i(\theta). Without it, sequence likelihood ratios would grow (or shrink) exponentially in sequence length, requiring very different clipping ranges for responses of different lengths. Normalizing by |y_i| keeps s_i(\theta) in a unified range across lengths, reducing variance heterogeneity across samples.

- Clipping at the sequence level removes entire overly off-policy responses from contributing original-scale gradients. While that can discard many tokens (the paper notes GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO), it does so in a principled, coherent way: responses judged as too far-off-policy are excluded in full, avoiding the partial, positionwise distortions caused by token-level clipping. Empirically this leads to better sample exploitation despite the seemingly larger fraction of clipped tokens.

GRPO, with per-token importance weights, suffers higher variance in its gradient estimator for several reasons:

- Each per-token ratio w_{i,t}(\theta) is computed from a single token sample and so is a high-variance estimator of the true local probability-ratio correction. Summing many such noisy terms (one per token) inflates gradient variance proportionally to sequence length.

- Token-level clipping truncates a subset of token contributions, biasing the estimator in a complex, position-dependent way. This biased, high-variance signal reduces sample efficiency: more samples or more conservative learning rates/clipping are required to obtain stable progress.

- When structural changes to the model (e.g., routing volatility in MoE) alter the conditional computation used at token positions, the per-token ratios become even more volatile, further increasing variance and undermining sample reuse across mini-batches.

The net effect, as the experiments in the paper show, is that GSPO attains superior training stability and efficiency relative to GRPO: despite clipping more tokens at the token level (because clipping is performed per-sequence and thus tends to discard entire responses), GSPO achieves consistently better training reward curves and downstream benchmark improvements under the same training compute. This supports the theoretical intuition that aligning the importance weighting and clipping unit with the reward unit (sequence-level) yields lower-variance, more effective gradient estimates and better sample exploitation.

# Summary

The unclipped gradient forms make explicit the fundamental difference between GSPO and GRPO. GSPO applies a single, length-normalized sequence importance ratio s_i(\theta) to scale the average of token log-gradients inside each response. This enforces equal token weighting within each response, reduces variance through averaging and normalization, and aligns the optimization unit with the unit of reward. GRPO instead applies distinct token-level importance ratios w_{i,t}(\theta) to each token gradient, producing heterogeneous weighting across positions. Because each w_{i,t}(\theta) is based on a single token sample and can be volatile (especially in long sequences or MoE models), GRPO accumulates high variance and clipping-induced bias across tokens, which empirically leads to instability and poorer sample efficiency. GSPO's sequence-level design addresses these issues and yields more stable, efficient RL training for large language models.

GSPO-token: A Flexible Token-Level Variant

# Objective with per-token advantages and sequence-informed token weights
GSPO-token adapts the GSPO idea to permit token-level advantage shaping while preserving the stability benefits of sequence-level importance estimation. The optimization objective of GSPO-token is written as

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right],
\]

where each token $y_{i,t}$ inside response $y_i$ is allowed its own advantage estimate $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The defining element that differentiates GSPO-token from GRPO is the choice of the importance term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$: rather than using the per-token importance ratio directly as GRPO does, GSPO-token constructs token-wise factors that are numerically tied to a sequence-level quantity. Concretely,

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]  \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] },
\]

with the sequence-level term

\[
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

Thus the per-token scalar $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is arranged so that its numeric value coincides with the sequence-derived $s_i(\theta)$ (as shown below), but its construction allows a different gradient flow than simply using $s_i(\theta)$ multiplied by a per-token likelihood. This allows GSPO-token to apply per-token advantages while still grounding the importance scaling in sequence-level likelihoods.

# Stop-gradient trick: numerical equality to $s_i(\theta)$ with stable gradients
The key implementation device in GSPO-token is the stop-gradient (denoted here as $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$, corresponding to a detach operation in frameworks like PyTorch). The stop-gradient is applied so that some factors contribute numerically but do not pass gradients back during optimization. Examine $s_{i,t}(\theta)$:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]  \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }.
\]

Numerically, the ratio

\[
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
\]

evaluates to $1$ because the numerator and the detached denominator share the same numeric value. Consequently $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ evaluates to the numeric value of $\mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]$, i.e.,

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) \equiv s_i(\theta)\quad\text{(numerically)}.
\]

However, due to the stop-gradient placement, gradients do not flow through the components used to compute $s_i(\theta)$ inside $s_{i,t}(\theta)$; instead, gradients pass through the intended per-token log-probability terms when appropriate. This construction yields two important benefits:

1. Stability from a sequence-informed scale: Because the numeric scaling applied to each token in a response is derived from the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, GSPO-token retains the low-variance, sequence-grounded weighting that makes GSPO robust.

2. Controlled gradient paths: The stop-gradient prevents token-level importance ratios from contributing raw gradient signals that vary strongly across tokens (the very source of instability in GRPO). Instead, the gradient expressions become structured so that the sequence-level factor appears as a multiplicative scalar detached from the gradient path, while per-token log-likelihood gradients are handled in the standard way.

The gradient of the GSPO-token objective (omitting clipping for clarity) can be derived as

\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x, \{y_i\} }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right],
\]

which makes explicit that the sequence-level scalar multiplies the per-token log-probability gradients, while token-level advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ can vary arbitrarily.

# Equivalence to GSPO when token advantages are uniform
When every token in a response shares the same advantage, i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$ for all $t$, GSPO-token reduces to GSPO in both its numerical objective and its gradient. Starting from the GSPO-token gradient above, if we replace $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ by $\widehat{A}_{i}$ and pull that scalar outside the inner sum, we obtain

\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x, \{y_i\} }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
 \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right],
\]

which is identical to the GSPO gradient expression (again, ignoring clipping). Therefore GSPO-token is a strict generalization: it preserves GSPO’s behavior whenever token-level advantages are uniform, and extends it to support diverse token-level credit assignments otherwise.

# Use cases: multi-turn RL, localized credit assignment
GSPO-token is designed for settings where rewards or desiderata are not naturally or exclusively assigned to whole responses, but instead depend on local structure inside a response. Typical scenarios include multi-turn RL and cases where only a subset of tokens should receive credit or blame.

In multi-turn RL, an overall response is composed of segments corresponding to different turns. Some turns may be more critical or have separate evaluation signals. By assigning distinct per-token (or per-turn) advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, GSPO-token enables targeted reinforcement: tokens belonging to a favorable turn can receive positive advantage while tokens from a different turn receive smaller or even negative advantage. Because the multiplicative scaling remains sequence-informed (through $s_i(\theta)$), the algorithm still avoids the instability associated with raw token-level importance ratios.

Similarly, for localized credit assignment (for example, when only a specific clause, line of code, or answer fragment should be rewarded), GSPO-token allows the practitioner to shape benefits precisely where desired. The per-token advantages can be set by validators that score subcomponents of the response, or by heuristics that flag particular positions. This facilitates more granular policy shaping than pure sequence-level GSPO while leveraging the same stable weighting mechanism.

# Trade-offs and when to prefer GSPO vs GSPO-token
GSPO-token introduces flexibility at the cost of slightly more complexity in advantage estimation. The principal trade-offs are:

- Granularity vs. simplicity: GSPO (sequence-level) is simpler: a single advantage per response suffices, and that is often natural when the verifier assigns a single scalar reward to the whole response. GSPO-token is preferable when rewards or desired behavior vary across positions or turns and you need localized credit assignment.

- Stability considerations: Both GSPO and GSPO-token inherit the sequence-informed stability properties because the numeric scaling applied to tokens is derived from $s_i(\theta)$. GSPO-token uses stop-gradient to ensure gradients do not follow the per-token likelihoods in a way that reintroduces the instability seen in GRPO. Thus, in practice, GSPO-token retains GSPO’s stability while enabling token-wise advantage shaping.

- Implementation complexity: GSPO-token requires constructing per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ and ensuring correct placement of the stop-gradient. If your validation setup or infrastructure already provides per-token or per-turn signals, GSPO-token integrates naturally. If you only have a single scalar reward per response, GSPO is sufficient and simpler.

- Computational/estimation overhead: Estimating reliable per-token advantages can be more challenging than computing a single group-normalized reward per response. When per-token signals are noisy or unreliable, using GSPO-token could introduce variance through the advantage estimates. In such cases, falling back to GSPO (or aggregating token-level signals into a sequence-level advantage) may produce better practical results.

In summary, prefer GSPO when rewards are inherently sequence-level, the evaluation signal is reliable only at the response level, or you want minimal algorithmic complexity. Choose GSPO-token when you need finer-grained control over credit assignment (multi-turn dialogs, localized correctness, or structured responses) and you can provide meaningful per-token or per-segment advantages.

# Closing remarks
GSPO-token provides a principled middle ground: it keeps the low-variance, sequence-informed weighting that addresses the instability issues of per-token importance sampling, while enabling per-token advantage customization through a careful stop-gradient construction. It is mathematically equivalent to GSPO when token advantages are uniform, and otherwise furnishes the extra expressiveness needed for multi-turn or locally evaluated tasks without reintroducing the high-variance gradient noise that motivated GSPO in the first place.

Practical Training with GSPO

# Rollout generation under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and mini-batch updates (the off-policy reality)
In practical large-scale RL for language models you will almost always operate in an off-policy regime: responses are sampled (rollouts) from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, stored, and then used for multiple gradient updates of the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. This is especially true when you partition a large rollout batch into multiple mini-batches to keep GPUs/TPUs fully utilized. The GSPO paper explicitly describes this setting and notes that such partitioning "inevitably introduces an off-policy learning setting" and motivates the clipping mechanism.

When implementing GSPO, generate a group of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ per query $x$ using $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (the paper uses this notation throughout). Partition the collected rollouts into several mini-batches for gradient steps; the experiments partitioned each rollout batch into four mini-batches. Be prepared that this partitioning increases the off-policy gap between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$ during optimization, which is precisely why GSPO uses sequence-level importance ratios and sequence-level clipping to control how "off-policy" samples contribute to the gradient.

Keep in mind the conceptual point emphasized in the paper: clipping is necessary not just as a heuristic but to prevent overly off-policy samples from corrupting gradient estimates. GSPO implements this clipping at the sequence level so that each sampled response is either used (with a reweight) or clipped based on how far the whole response likelihood under $\pi_{\theta}$ departs from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

# Setting clipping ranges for sequence-level ratios
One of the central design differences in GSPO is that importance ratios are defined for whole sequences:
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
(with length normalization). Because the numerical scale and variance of these sequence-level ratios differ substantially from token-level ratios, the clipping ranges you choose should also differ in magnitude from typical PPO/GRPO token-level ranges.

The paper reports concrete clipping choices used in experiments: for GSPO the left and right clipping ranges were set to 3e-4 and 4e-4 respectively in Equation (6). For comparison, GRPO used much larger token-level clipping ranges (0.2 and 0.27). These values illustrate two points made in the paper:

- Sequence-level ratios tend to be numerically much smaller (after length normalizing and taking the geometric mean across tokens), so the clipping thresholds must be chosen in that regime.
- The absolute numeric ranges between GSPO and GRPO are not comparable directly — expect to tune GSPO clipping magnitudes on your data and model scale rather than reusing token-level defaults.

When tuning clipping ranges in practice, monitor the fraction of clipped responses and be prepared to tighten the range if training exhibits instability, or loosen it if learning stalls. The paper showed that GSPO still attains good sample efficiency despite clipping a much larger fraction of tokens/responses (in their experiments), so don't assume that high clipped fractions are necessarily harmful — they can indicate that noisy, overly off-policy sequences are being rightfully excluded.

# Group size $G$ and reward normalization effects
GSPO inherits the group-relative advantage estimation idea from GRPO: for each query $x$ you generate $G$ responses and compute a group-normalized advantage per response:
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}.

Group size $G$ controls how well the group statistics (mean and standard deviation) estimate relative preference among responses. The paper presents GSPO in this framework and uses the group-based normalized advantage in their objective (Equation (6)). Practical implications from the paper:

- Larger $G$ gives better estimates of relative advantage within each query (lower sample variance of the mean/std), but requires generating more responses per query (compute cost).
- Smaller $G$ reduces per-query compute but increases variance of the advantage estimates, which can degrade stability.

The paper does not prescribe a single optimal $G$; instead, use the GSPO formulation with the group-normalized advantages and tune $G$ in the trade-off between sample-efficiency and compute. Always normalize advantages per group as shown above; the paper emphasizes alignment between sequence-level rewarding and sequence-level optimization, and the group normalization is the mechanism for that alignment in GRPO/GSPO.

# KL regularization: role and typical settings
Although the derivations in the paper omit the KL regularization term for brevity, the authors acknowledge that typical PPO-style RL pipelines include a KL penalty or constraint between the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the reference policy (often the pretraining or prior policy). In practical RLHF-like training, a KL term is commonly kept to bound deviation from the base model and prevent reward hacking or degenerate outputs.

The paper's GSPO objective (Equation (6)) is written without the KL term for clarity, but when you implement GSPO in a production RL loop, include a KL regularizer or an explicit KL constraint (analogous to standard PPO usage). The magnitude of the KL coefficient depends on your safety/performance trade-offs and model scale; because GSPO already clips sequence-level ratios tightly, the KL coefficient may be set conservatively. The paper does not give a single recommended coefficient, so treat this as a standard PPO tuning knob and monitor behavior (reward improvement vs. divergence from the prior).

# Length control and generation constraints
GSPO uses length-normalized sequence importance ratios:
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
so generation length matters for numeric stability and variance. The paper explicitly argues for length normalization to reduce variance and keep ratios in a unified numerical range; without it, a few token likelihood changes could produce dramatic fluctuations.

Operationally this suggests you should:

- Use consistent generation length constraints during rollouts when computing the importance ratios (e.g., fixed max length or a well-defined stopping condition).
- If you change max generation length during training (the paper notes GSPO continues to improve when extending generation length), retune clipping ranges because the distribution of normalized sequence ratios may shift.
- Consider normalizing or bucketing by length if you have very heterogeneous response lengths; GSPO's length normalization already mitigates much of the problem, but extreme length variation may still warrant extra care.

# Compute/memory considerations: no value model, sequence-likelihood computation
A major practical advantage of GSPO emphasized in the paper is that it removes the need for a separate value model. PPO/GRPO typically rely on a value function to compute per-token or per-time-step advantages; GRPO avoids a value model with group-relative advantages, and GSPO follows that route. As the paper points out, the value model is costly (memory and compute), and its accuracy is often a bottleneck.

In GSPO you only need to compute sequence likelihoods under $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and group-normalized rewards. Two practical consequences follow from points in the paper:

- Memory and compute footprint are lower because you don't train or evaluate a value-function network of comparable size to the policy.
- Sequence-likelihood computation is required for importance weights. The paper notes that, in practice, likelihoods of sampled responses under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ are often recomputed with the training engine to avoid precision mismatch between inference and training engines. However, GSPO's sequence-level ratios are more tolerant to precision discrepancies than token-level ratios, enabling the possibility of using inference-engine likelihoods directly in some setups (see the Monitoring section below).

Be aware that if you do recompute likelihoods with the training engine, doing so for whole sequences (rather than token-by-token routing manipulations) is simpler and cheaper than the additional machinery required to stabilize token-level ratios (see Routing Replay below).

# Monitoring: sequence ratio statistics, clipped fraction of responses, rewards
The paper emphasizes specific diagnostics that reveal GSPO's practical behavior and stability advantages. During training, track these quantities:

- Distribution of sequence importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$ (or log-$s_i(\theta)$). Look at mean, variance, and tails. GSPO's length-normalized ratios should be concentrated; large variance or heavy tails indicate possible instability or an inappropriate clipping range.
- Fraction of clipped responses. GSPO clips at the sequence level; the authors observed that GSPO clips a much higher fraction of tokens/responses (two orders of magnitude difference compared to GRPO token-level clipping), yet still showed superior training efficiency. That means a large clipped fraction is not necessarily bad, but you should track whether excessive clipping correlates with stalled reward improvement. If clipping fraction is near 100% (practically none used for update), you should loosen clipping or reduce the learning rate.
- Reward and normalized advantage statistics per group. Monitor group means and standard deviations of rewards within each query; anomalous patterns (e.g., near-zero std across groups) will make advantage estimates noisy and uninformative.
- Training reward curves and downstream benchmark performance over compute. The paper shows that GSPO yields stable, monotonic improvements across training compute whereas GRPO suffered catastrophic collapses in some settings.
- For MoE models specifically, monitor "expert-activation volatility": how often activated experts for the same sample change after updates. The paper reports roughly 10% expert changes per update under GRPO for a 48-layer MoE model and points out this volatility destabilizes token-level ratios.

If you have infrastructure with separate inference and training engines, compare likelihoods produced by the inference engine and those recomputed by the training engine for a sample of responses. GSPO's authors note that sequence-likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies, and using inference-engine likelihoods directly can avoid recomputation overhead when acceptable.

# Special considerations for MoE models: routing replay and how GSPO helps
The paper documents a concrete stability problem with MoE models under token-level importance-weight algorithms: expert-activation volatility. When the activated experts for the same response differ between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$, token-level likelihoods under different activated sub-networks fluctuate drastically, invalidating the token-level importance ratios and destabilizing training.

Previously, the authors used a Routing Replay strategy for GRPO: cache the activated experts under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and force the same routing (replay routes) when computing $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ so that token-level likelihoods are computed on the same activated network. This fixes convergence but adds memory, communication, and capacity constraints.

GSPO fundamentally alleviates this need: because its importance ratios are sequence-level likelihoods (and length-normalized) rather than token-level ratios, they are much less sensitive to token-level routing changes. The paper reports GSPO converged normally and stably for MoE RL without Routing Replay. Thus, when training MoE models, prefer GSPO's sequence-level approach to avoid the added complexity and overhead of routing replay. If you must use token-level objectives, routing replay (or other routing-stabilization strategies) becomes essential to obtain convergence.

# Practical checklist when launching GSPO training
Summarizing actionable items grounded in the paper's findings:

1. Rollout and mini-batching: sample groups of responses per query from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$; partition rollouts into several mini-batches (the paper used four) for gradient updates, accepting the off-policy setting and using sequence-level clipping to control it.

2. Importance ratio: compute sequence-level, length-normalized ratios
   s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
   Use these ratios for clipping and weighting.

3. Clipping ranges: initialize clipping magnitudes appropriate to sequence-level ratios (the paper used left/right = 3e-4 / 4e-4 in experiments). Expect these to be orders of magnitude smaller than token-level clipping ranges. Tune by monitoring clipped-fraction and learning progress.

4. Group size and advantage: choose group size $G$ to balance per-query compute vs. stability; always apply group normalization:
   \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r\})}.

5. KL regularization: include a KL penalty or constraint in production runs (omitted in the derivations), and tune its coefficient as an additional safety/control knob.

6. Length control: use length normalization in ratios (as in GSPO) and keep generation length decisions consistent; if you change max length, revisit clipping and other hyperparameters.

7. MoE models: prefer GSPO to avoid Routing Replay. If you are forced into a token-level approach, be prepared to use routing replay to stabilize token-level likelihoods.

8. Monitoring: continuously log sequence-ratio distributions, clipped response fraction, group reward statistics, reward curves, and expert-activation volatility for MoE models. Compare inference-engine likelihoods vs. training-engine recomputed likelihoods if precision mismatch is a concern.

9. Compute savings: take advantage of not using a value model — less memory and compute — and consider using inference-engine likelihoods directly when acceptable, since GSPO is more tolerant to precision differences.

Following these guidelines — which are direct consequences and recommendations from the GSPO paper — should help you run stable, efficient, and scalable RL on large language models while avoiding the high-variance pitfalls associated with token-level importance weighting.

Empirical Results and Interpretation

# Benchmarks: AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces (Elo)

The paper evaluates GSPO against GRPO on three downstream benchmarks that exercise different reasoning and coding capabilities of the language model: AIME’24 (mathematical reasoning, measured by average Pass@1 over 32 samplings), LiveCodeBench (coding, measured by average Pass@1 over 8 samplings on the 202410–202502 slice), and CodeForces (competitive programming, measured by Elo rating). These benchmarks serve as practical performance proxies: improvements in reward during RL training should translate into better Pass@1 on AIME’24 and LiveCodeBench and higher Elo on CodeForces.

Empirically, GSPO-trained models show superior performance on these benchmarks compared to GRPO when consuming the same amount of training compute and query budget. The paper reports that GSPO not only attains higher training reward curves (discussed below) but that these gains carry through to improved Pass@1 and Elo metrics. This alignment between RL training reward and downstream benchmark performance validates the algorithmic design principle of matching the unit of optimization to the unit of reward: since verifier rewards are sequence-level, optimizing with sequence-level importance ratios and clipping produces practical gains on real evaluation tasks.

# Training curves: stability over long runs, scaling with more compute and length

A central empirical claim is that GSPO produces stable training curves over extended RL runs. The paper contrasts GSPO’s behavior to GRPO’s documented catastrophic instabilities: GRPO can suffer irreversible model collapse that persists despite rollbacks and hyperparameter adjustments. In contrast, GSPO exhibits steady increases in reward throughout extended training, with no observed catastrophic collapses.

Beyond mere stability, GSPO scales sensibly with increased training compute and with longer generation lengths. The authors emphasize three scaling behaviors: (1) increasing the amount of training compute leads to continued performance improvement rather than divergence; (2) regularly updating the query set (i.e., changing the distribution of prompts used in rollouts) does not destabilize training under GSPO; and (3) extending generation length produces continued improvements rather than accumulating variance that causes collapse. These observations support the theoretical motivation in the paper: by applying importance correction at the sequence level (the same granularity as the reward), GSPO avoids the high-variance gradient noise that accumulates in token-level schemes as sequence length grows.

# Efficiency: higher training accuracy and downstream metrics vs GRPO under equal compute

The experiments compare GSPO and GRPO under matched compute and consumed-query budgets, with rollout batches partitioned into four minibatches for gradient updates (introducing an off-policy update necessity). Under this controlled comparison GSPO achieves higher training accuracy (reward) and correspondingly higher benchmark scores with the same resources. That is, GSPO is more sample-efficient and training-efficient: given identical data and compute constraints, GSPO extracts better optimization signal and makes more progress.

This empirical efficiency ties directly to the algorithmic change: GRPO’s token-level importance weighting injects high-variance noise into gradient estimates because each token’s importance ratio is computed based on a single sampled token from the next-token distribution. GSPO replaces those noisy per-token ratios with a single length-normalized sequence-level ratio per response,
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
\]
which reduces variance and yields more reliable gradient directions. The observed improvements in both training reward and downstream metrics under equal resource consumption empirically validate that GSPO’s sequence-level weighting produces more efficient learning.

# Cold-start on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base: practical implications

The experimental setup uses a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. “Cold-start” here indicates RL fine-tuning beginning from a supervised-finetuned base rather than continuing an RL-trained checkpoint. The practical implication is that GSPO can be applied effectively starting from a large pre-trained and finetuned model without specialized warm-start tricks.

This is practically significant because many production pipelines perform RL fine-tuning from a supervised checkpoint. The paper shows that GSPO yields stable and improving training curves from that starting point, enabling teams to apply RL at scale to existing large models. Moreover, because GSPO avoids the catastrophic collapse modes observed with GRPO in large models and long-response settings, it reduces the operational risk when launching large-scale RL jobs on architectures such as Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base.

# Clipping fraction observation: GSPO clips far more tokens yet learns faster

One of the more surprising reported empirical observations is that GSPO clips a substantially larger fraction of tokens compared to GRPO—two orders of magnitude more—yet nonetheless achieves better training efficiency and downstream performance. Here “clipping” refers to the PPO-style clipping that prevents overly off-policy importance ratios from dominating the objective; GSPO performs clipping at the sequence level, while GRPO clips at each token.

Intuitively, clipping a large fraction of tokens might be expected to reduce effective sample utilization and slow learning. The reported finding is the opposite: GSPO, despite discarding many token contributions via sequence-level clipping, learns faster in practice. The key interpretive point offered by the authors is that GRPO’s token-level gradients are inherently noisy and unreliable: they may appear to use more tokens, but those token-level weighted updates contain high-variance errors that degrade optimization. By contrast, GSPO’s sequence-level approach discards many individual token contributions but preserves a cleaner, lower-variance training signal that yields net faster learning.

# Interpretation: GRPO’s token-level gradients are noisy; GSPO’s signal is cleaner

The experimental results are consistent with and reinforce the paper’s theoretical analysis of gradient structure. For GRPO the gradient takes the form (ignoring clipping and normalization constants)
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
so each token’s gradient is reweighted by a token-level importance ratio computed from a single sample. Because importance sampling requires averaging across multiple samples to correct distribution mismatch reliably, a single-sample token-level ratio produces high variance and can systematically bias or destabilize updates, especially as sequence length increases and the number of such single-sample ratios to aggregate grows.

By contrast GSPO’s unclipped gradient (before clipping and with length normalization) is
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
where the single multiplicative weight $s_i(\theta)$ applies uniformly to all token log-probability gradients within the response. This uniform weighting removes per-token variance introduced by single-sample importance ratios and aligns the optimization granularity with the sequence-level reward signal. Length normalization within $s_i(\theta)$ further stabilizes its numerical range across different response lengths.

The empirical phenomena—the higher training reward, improved benchmark scores, stable long runs, and faster learning despite more aggressive clipping—are all explained by this variance reduction and signal alignment. GRPO’s token-wise reweighting makes its gradient estimator noisier and more sensitive to fluctuations (exacerbated in sparse-activation MoE models by expert-activation volatility). GSPO’s sequence-level weighting yields a cleaner, lower-variance gradient that produces more reliable policy improvement steps.

# Additional interpretation: MoE stability and infrastructure benefits

Two additional empirical consequences emerge from the same interpretation. First, GSPO resolves expert-activation volatility problems in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without ad-hoc remedies. GRPO required “Routing Replay” to force consistent routing across old and new policies when computing token-level ratios; this added memory/communication overhead and restricted effective model capacity. Because GSPO uses sequence likelihoods, which are less sensitive to which individual expert was active at a particular token, it avoids the instability induced by routing changes. Empirically this means GSPO converges stably on MoE models without Routing Replay.

Second, GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods makes the algorithm more tolerant of precision mismatches between inference and training engines. In practice, many systems recompute likelihoods with the training engine to avoid precision discrepancies. GSPO’s sequence-level signals are empirically observed to be robust enough that one can often use likelihoods returned by inference engines directly, simplifying RL infrastructure and reducing the need for recomputation.

# Summary

The reported empirical results strongly support the paper’s core theoretical claims. GSPO produces more stable and efficient RL training than GRPO: it achieves higher training rewards, better downstream benchmark performance, and reliable long-run scaling behavior while avoiding catastrophic collapse. The counterintuitive observation that GSPO clips many more tokens yet learns faster is explained by variance considerations: GRPO’s token-level importance ratios, computed from single-token samples, inject high-variance noise that harms learning, whereas GSPO’s sequence-level importance ratios provide a cleaner, better-aligned optimization signal. These empirical findings also have practical implications: GSPO simplifies MoE training by removing the need for Routing Replay and can reduce engineering complexity in RL infrastructure by allowing safer use of inference-engine likelihoods.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): Stabilizing Expert Activation

# Sparse activation and expert-activation volatility across updates

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures route different tokens through different expert subnetworks, so at inference or rollout time only a sparse subset of the full parameter set is activated for any single token or sequence. This sparse activation is a core strength of MoE: it enables very large model capacity while keeping computation per token manageable. However, that same sparsity introduces a new source of instability in reinforcement learning (RL) training that is not present in dense models.

Concretely, when RL performs gradient updates, the parameters that determine routing decisions and expert behavior change. Because only a subset of experts are used for any given rollout sample, small parameter updates can alter which experts are selected for the same input/query on subsequent passes. The paper documents this phenomenon quantitatively: with a deep MoE model (48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), after a single RL gradient update roughly 10% of the experts activated for the same rollout sample under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This degree of expert-activation volatility grows with model depth and becomes an important practical failure mode in RL.

This volatility matters because RL algorithms rely on comparing the old policy that generated the data and the new policy being optimized. When the activated computation paths (i.e., the experts) differ between the policies, many of the assumptions underlying off-policy corrections and importance weighting break down or become noisy. Thus, sparse activation—which otherwise scales capacity efficiently—introduces an extra axis of distributional shift between behavior and target policies that must be managed for stable RL.

# Effect on token-level ratios: unstable per-token likelihoods with changing experts

GRPO and related algorithms (and PPO variants when implemented token-wise) use token-level importance ratios of the form
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
applied at each token position $t$ and then combined across a response. These ratios are intended to correct for the fact that samples were drawn from the old policy rather than the current policy, following the principle of importance sampling. For dense models and modest distributional shifts, token-level ratios can be informative and reasonably stable. However, in MoE models the volatility of expert activation undermines this stability.

When routing decisions change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$, the conditional probability assigned to a token $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ may change not only because model parameters changed, but because a different expert subnetwork is now computing that token’s logits. Thus the numerator and denominator in $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can correspond to different computational graphs and different effective parameterizations. The result is that $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can fluctuate drastically and unpredictably across updates. This makes the token-level importance weight a very noisy and sometimes invalid correction: rather than estimating how the distribution changed, it reflects routing discontinuities. In turn, the noisy per-token weights introduce high-variance gradient estimates, which can accumulate over long token sequences and precipitate catastrophic instability in training.

The paper emphasizes that this instability is not only theoretical: in practice, the fluctuating token-level ratios due to expert-changes materially hindered GRPO training of MoE RL, preventing normal convergence unless special mitigations were applied.

# Routing Replay: caching old routing to restore comparability in GRPO

To cope with the inconsistency between activated experts under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$, the authors previously developed the Routing Replay strategy. The core idea of Routing Replay is simple: when computing token likelihoods under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ for importance weights, reuse the routing pattern (the specific experts) that were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Concretely, for each token $y_{i,t}$ in a rollout sample, the routing decisions recorded during rollout under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ are cached; during optimization, these cached routing modes are "replayed" in $\pi_{\theta}$ so that both numerator and denominator of the token-level ratio are computed using the same activated subnetwork.

This re-alignment restores a meaningful comparison between $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ because the same computational path is used in both evaluations. By removing routing-induced discrepancies, Routing Replay stabilizes the token-level importance weights and thus helps GRPO’s gradient estimates behave more regularly. The paper shows that Routing Replay is essential for GRPO to converge normally on MoE RL experiments: without it, expert-activation volatility makes token-level ratios too noisy and training fails to converge.

# Costs of Routing Replay: memory, communication, capacity constraints

Although Routing Replay can restore comparability and enable GRPO to converge on MoE models, it comes with nontrivial practical costs. First, caching and replaying routing decisions requires additional memory: the routing modes for all rollout samples must be stored and then supplied back to the model during optimization. Second, replaying cached routing modes during training introduces extra communication overhead across devices and systems, because the routing metadata must be transferred and aligned with training compute. Third, and perhaps more subtly, replaying a fixed routing pattern constrains the effective capacity and dynamics of the MoE model during optimization. By forcing the new policy to use the old policy’s routing graph for likelihood computation, Routing Replay prevents the model from fully leveraging newly learned routing behaviors during that optimization step; this can limit the expressive or adaptive benefits of MoE during RL.

In short, Routing Replay is a practical but heavyweight workaround: it recovers stability at the expense of memory, communication, and potentially underutilized model capacity. These costs motivate seeking algorithmic alternatives that do not rely on replaying routing modes.

# GSPO’s robustness: sequence likelihoods remain stable despite token-level routing changes

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a principled alternative that inherently mitigates routing volatility without Routing Replay by changing the granularity of importance weighting and clipping from token level to sequence level. GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
\]
and applies clipping, advantage weighting, and optimization at the whole-response level rather than per token.

The crucial observation underpinning GSPO’s robustness is that while individual token likelihoods can swing due to changes in activated experts, the overall sequence likelihood under the policy tends to be far more stable. Even if some token-level contributions change because different experts are used, the model’s language modeling competence is preserved and sequence-level probabilities do not typically exhibit the extreme discontinuities that token-level ratios can. By using a length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$, GSPO further reduces variance and places importance ratios for responses of different lengths on a comparable numerical scale.

Because GSPO weights the entire token-gradient sum for a response by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$ (and the clipped variant), the algorithm avoids relying on per-token ratios that may be invalid when routing changes. In effect, GSPO decouples the optimization correction from the precise per-token routing pattern and instead measures how much the response as a whole is favored under the new policy relative to the old. This aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward (the sequence) and removes the sensitivity to routing-induced token-level instability.

# Empirical outcome: normal convergence without Routing Replay

Empirically, the paper reports that GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay in MoE RL training. When applied to the same MoE models for which GRPO required routing replay to converge, GSPO converged stably using conventional likelihood computation under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$—no cached routing modes or replay mechanism were necessary. The authors emphasize that GSPO’s sequence-level approach resolves the expert-activation volatility problem because sequence likelihoods remain comparatively stable despite token-level routing changes.

Beyond simply enabling convergence, GSPO also demonstrated superior training efficiency and better downstream performance compared to GRPO (with Routing Replay). By avoiding the memory and communication costs of Routing Replay and by permitting the model to use its native routing during optimization, GSPO simplifies RL infrastructure and allows MoE models to leverage their full capacity more readily. These empirical outcomes support GSPO as a practical and theoretically grounded solution to the stability issues that MoE architectures introduce in token-level importance-weighted RL methods.

RL Infrastructure Simplification with GSPO

# Precision mismatches between training and inference engines

In practical large-scale reinforcement learning for language models, two distinct execution environments commonly arise: the inference engine used to sample responses (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) and the training engine used to compute gradients and update model parameters (e.g., Megatron). The paper emphasizes that these engines can differ in numerical precision and implementation details. As a result, the likelihoods or log-likelihoods produced by the inference engine for sampled responses may not exactly match the values that would be produced by the training engine running the same model parameters. This discrepancy is a pragmatic source of error when an RL algorithm depends on likelihood values: if the optimization requires recomputing token-level probabilities under the training engine to obtain accurate importance weights, then the mismatch forces additional computation and data transfer to the training environment.

The GRPO formulation illustrates why this matters. GRPO constructs token-level importance ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]
and these ratios are applied at each token position. Because token-level ratios directly multiply gradients per token, any small numerical differences between engines can lead to inconsistent or unstable gradient estimates unless the token probabilities are recomputed precisely with the training engine. This is why, in practice, systems adopting token-level correction often recompute or re-evaluate token likelihoods in the training engine before optimization to ensure consistency. That recomputation step imposes extra latency, memory, and communication costs: responses sampled by an inference service must be sent or stored and then reprocessed by the training engine, increasing overhead in production RL pipelines.

# GSPO’s tolerance: using inference-engine sequence log-likelihoods directly

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level importance ratio built from sequence likelihoods:
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Because this quantity aggregates token log-probabilities across the entire response and then length-normalizes, it is numerically less sensitive to small per-token precision differences between engines. The paper argues that sequence-level likelihoods are a more natural object in the RL setting for language models because rewards are assigned at the sequence level; thus, the unit of importance sampling correction and clipping should match the unit of reward.

Practically, GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it much more tolerant of the precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. The inference engine can provide sequence log-likelihoods for sampled responses, and GSPO can use those values directly in its objective without an expensive recomputation step in the training engine. The paper explicitly notes this intuition: sequence-level likelihoods are "much more tolerant of precision discrepancies," so GSPO makes it possible to avoid recomputation and accept the inference-engine likelihoods for optimization.

# Benefits in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated systems

The toleration of inference-engine sequence likelihoods has concrete operational advantages across several RL scenarios highlighted in the paper.

In partial rollout settings—where the system produces and stores only parts of a long trajectory or where responses are collected in chunks—the ability to trust sequence-level likelihoods from the inference service avoids the need to reconstitute or re-evaluate entire rollouts in the training engine. Since GSPO operates on complete responses (or length-normalized sequence likelihoods) as its optimization unit, partial outputs that constitute full response units can be scored and used for optimization without additional training-side recomputation.

For multi-turn RL, where each turn may produce responses conditional on an evolving conversation state, token-level recomputation becomes especially costly and complex: the conditioning context can be large, and replaying routing or activation patterns across engines is onerous. Because GSPO matches rewards and corrections at the sequence level, it naturally fits multi-turn workflows where a complete turn (a response to a given query state) is the fundamental sample. The paper also presents a token-level variant (GSPO-token) that retains flexibility for per-token advantages while still basing the correction on a detached sequence-level factor; this allows fine-grained control when necessary without sacrificing the tolerance benefits of a sequence-based importance term.

In disaggregated training-inference frameworks—where inference services and training clusters are separate, possibly geographically or architecturally distinct—avoiding recomputation reduces cross-system data movement and round-trip latency. The paper suggests that GSPO's use of inference-provided sequence likelihoods enables such disaggregation: inference can produce sampled responses, sequence rewards, and sequence log-likelihoods, and the training system can consume these directly for optimization. This reduces the coupling between inference and training stacks and simplifies engineering around model serving, rollout collection, and replay.

# Operational simplifications and potential throughput gains

Adopting GSPO yields multiple operational simplifications, each with implications for throughput and engineering complexity. First, by removing the need to recompute token-level likelihoods in the training engine, the RL pipeline eliminates a costly recomputation step. That step otherwise duplicates the forward pass computation on the training side for each sampled response, adding GPU time and increasing I/O between inference collectors and training nodes. Eliminating it reduces end-to-end latency for rollouts to be available for gradient computation and reduces the demand on training GPUs for pure likelihood evaluation work.

Second, GSPO reduces the dependency on precise numerical parity between engines. When token-level ratios are required, teams must ensure that both inference and training engines use matching precision regimes and numerically consistent implementations; mismatches can force conservative and complex engineering workarounds (e.g., recomputation, routing replay). GSPO relaxes that requirement because sequence-level likelihoods are more robust to small per-token numeric differences. This allows organizations to use highly optimized inference engines for high-throughput rollout collection while keeping training on separate, possibly differently optimized, clusters.

Third, the paper demonstrates additional benefits specific to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. GRPO required a Routing Replay strategy to restore token-level importance ratio stability when expert-activation volatility caused token-likelihoods to shift after parameter updates. Routing Replay incurs memory and communication overhead and constrains model capacity. GSPO obviates Routing Replay because sequence likelihoods remain stable enough despite expert routing changes; this simplifies the RL training pipeline for MoE models and allows the model to use its capacity without forced routing reuse.

Collectively, these simplifications translate into potential throughput gains: fewer duplicate forward passes, reduced cross-engine communication, and less engineering effort devoted to preserving token-level parity. While the paper does not provide exact throughput numbers, it reports that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency compared to GRPO under the same compute and query budgets. The improved efficiency arises partly from using sequence-level corrections that give more reliable learning signals and partly from the operational savings described above. The net effect is a streamlined RL infrastructure that can scale rollouts and training more effectively.

# Final remarks

The central operational message from the paper is straightforward: aligning the unit of importance sampling and clipping with the unit of reward—i.e., moving from token-level to sequence-level corrections—yields both algorithmic robustness and pragmatic engineering benefits. By tolerating inference-traced sequence likelihoods and eliminating the necessity of retracing token probabilities in a separate training engine, GSPO simplifies distributed RL pipelines, reduces overhead in partial-rollout and multi-turn workflows, and removes brittle workarounds required for MoE models. For teams building large-scale RL systems for language models, GSPO therefore offers a path to simplify infrastructure while improving stability and efficiency.

Implementation Blueprint

# End-to-end loop: sample with $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} \to$ score rewards $\to$ compute $s_i(\theta), \widehat{A}_i \to$ clip $\to$ update $\theta$
An implementation of GSPO follows a clear sequence of stages. At high level, each RL iteration (or training step) does the following:

1. Rollout / sampling. For each query $x$ in a batch drawn from the query set $\mathcal{D}$, sample $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ from the current behavior policy, which in our notation is the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Keep and store each entire response $y_i$ and the corresponding token sequence used to produce it.

2. Reward scoring. For each sampled pair $(x,y_i)$ compute the scalar reward $r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$ using the verifier/scoring function in your system. These rewards will be the basis for the group advantage estimates.

3. Compute group-normalized advantages. For the $G$ responses to the same query $x$, compute the group mean and standard deviation of rewards and form the normalized advantage
   \[
   \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}.
   \]
   This is the advantage term used in Equations (4) and (5) of the paper; all tokens of response $y_i$ share this same $\widehat{A}_i$ for the base GSPO objective.

4. Compute sequence-level importance ratios. For each response $y_i$ compute the sequence-level importance ratio (length-normalized) between the target policy $\pi_\theta$ and the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$:
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
   =\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
   \]
   This is the fundamental importance weight used by GSPO (Equation (7) in the paper). Note the length normalization by $|y_i|$ is important to reduce variance and unify numerical range.

5. Clipping. Apply sequence-level clipping to each term according to the clipped objective used in GSPO:
   \[
   \min\big( s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i\big).
   \]
   Use the clipping bounds appropriate for $s_i(\theta)$. The paper reports example clipping ranges for GSPO that are orders of magnitude different from token-level clipping used by GRPO (e.g., very small left/right ranges such as $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ in their experiments). Choose clipping magnitudes consistent with the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ and validated on your data.

6. Form objective and gradient update. Average across group members and across queries to form the empirical estimate of
   \[
   \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
   \min\big( s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right],
   \]
   then compute gradients and update $\theta$. The unclipped gradient form (before applying clipping) is (Equation (10)):
   \[
   \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
   =\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}\widehat{A}_i
   \cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   \right].
   \]
   Apply the clipping mask so that clipped terms use the clipped scalar in place of $s_i(\theta)$ when forming gradients.

7. Replace $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ as needed. After some number of optimization steps (or following your existing checkpointing/rollout schedule), set $\theta_\text{old}\leftarrow\theta$ and repeat the next rollout/optimization cycle.

This end-to-end loop emphasizes that sampling, reward assignment, importance ratio computation, and clipping are all performed at sequence granularity so that the optimization unit matches the reward unit.

# Efficient sequence log-prob computation and caching
GSPO depends only on sequence-level likelihoods (and their length-normalized exponents). Practical implementation should therefore be organized to compute and store sequence log-probabilities efficiently:

- During rollout, record both the token sequence $y_i$ and the per-token log-probabilities under the behavior policy $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Summing these per-token log-probs yields $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)$. Keep these stored alongside the sampled response so you do not need to recompute them later for $s_i(\theta)$ denominator.

- When computing $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)$ under the current policy, you can either recompute the full token log-probs by running the model in likelihood-evaluation mode on the stored sequences, or, if your inference/training engine returns sequence log-likelihoods directly, use those (the paper notes that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies across engines).

- Use vectorized batched likelihood evaluation: evaluate many stored sequences in a batch to amortize per-call overhead. Compute per-token log-probs and sum along the token axis to get sequence log-likelihoods.

- Cache the behavior-policy sequence log-probs and token log-probs at rollout time so you do not need to recompute them later for the denominator in $s_i(\theta)$. The numerator must be computed under the current policy at update time unless you are using a replay scheme.

- If you use GSPO-token (the token-level variant that still bases scaling on $s_i(\theta)$), you will still need the sequence-normalized $s_i(\theta)$; GSPO-token then attaches these scalars to token-level terms (see the GSPO-token definition). Keep the caching strategy identical.

# Numerical stability: log-space sums, length normalization, epsilon handling
GSPO depends on ratios of sequence likelihoods, which can be extremely small or large in raw probability space. The paper prescribes and employs numerically stable transformations; implement the same:

- Work in log-space. Compute
  \[
  \log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\big(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)-\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)\big),
  \]
  then exponentiate only when necessary to form $s_i(\theta)$. Many places in the implementation (for clipping, for inspecting distributions) can be done equivalently on $\log s_i(\theta)$ to avoid overflow/underflow.

- Length normalization. Use the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ factor in the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ exactly as in the paper. This reduces variance and keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a consistent numerical range across sequences of differing length.

- Clipping epsilon. Choose the clipping range $\varepsilon$ appropriate to $s_i(\theta)$ (the paper demonstrates clipping ranges for GSPO that are far smaller than token-level clipping used in GRPO because the definitions differ by scale). Implement clipping on $s_i(\theta)$ using numerically stable routines, e.g.
  \[
  s_i^\text{clipped}=\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon),
  \]
  and be mindful if you clip in log-space: clipping $s$ corresponds to clipping $\log s$ at $\log(1\pm\varepsilon)$.

- Handle divide-by-zero and zero-std. When computing $\widehat{A}_i$, guard against numerical edge cases: if the standard deviation across group rewards is zero (all group rewards equal), define $\widehat{A}_i$ in a consistent fallback manner (the paper uses standardization; your implementation must ensure no NaN is produced).

- Use small epsilons when computing $\log$ of probabilities if your model might produce exact zeros (practical language models normally do not, but implement a safety floor like adding a tiny value before taking $\log$ when absolutely necessary).

# Choice of optimizer and schedule; gradient accumulation with large batches
The paper focuses on algorithmic changes rather than particular optimizer choices; however, it mentions that practical RL training with large models usually partitions a large rollout batch into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates, which introduces an off-policy setting. Thus implementers should:

- Support splitting each rollout batch into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. The GSPO objective is designed to be robust to this off-policy setting provided sequence-level importance ratios are used and clipping is applied.

- Implement gradient accumulation if you need to emulate very large batch sizes but are constrained by device memory. Accumulate gradients across mini-batches before applying an optimizer step so that each optimization step reflects the intended large-batch estimate.

- Use an optimizer and learning-rate schedule appropriate to your model and existing training recipes. GSPO replaces the importance-weighting scheme but does not mandate a specific optimizer; ensure the learning rate, weight decay, and other hyperparameters are tuned for stable training.

- Update $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ on a schedule that balances how stale your behavior policy becomes versus the computational cost of frequent snapshotting. The paper implies standard practice: sample from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, perform multiple optimization steps, then update $\theta_\text{old}\leftarrow\theta$ per your rollout/checkpoint cadence.

# Diagnostics: distribution of $s_i(\theta)$, clipped ratios, reward stats, loss decomposition
To monitor and debug training, instrument the following diagnostics derived directly from the paper’s analysis and experiments:

- Distribution of sequence-level importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$. Track histograms or summary statistics (mean, median, variance) of $s_i(\theta)$ and/or $\log s_i(\theta)$. This reveals how off-policy your sampled sequences are relative to the current model and indicates whether your clipping range is being hit frequently.

- Clipping fraction. Measure the fraction of sequences whose $s_i(\theta)$ values are clipped on either side. The paper found that GSPO clips far more tokens (sequences) than token-level clipping approaches but still yields better training efficiency. Monitoring clipping fractions helps diagnose whether your clipping range is too narrow or too wide.

- Reward statistics per group. Track the group means and standard deviations of rewards, and the distribution of normalized advantages $\widehat{A}_i$. Verify that group standardization is producing a reasonable spread of advantages and that you are not losing signal due to extremely small std or degenerate groups.

- Loss decomposition. For each mini-batch, log the average unclipped term, average clipped term, and the difference. Also log the mean per-sequence contribution:
  \[
  \ell_i(\theta)=\min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big),
  \]
  and aggregate statistics across the batch. This helps identify whether gradients are dominated by clipped terms or by particular examples.

- Gradient-norm statistics. Monitor the norm of gradients contributed by each sequence (or per-layer summaries) to detect instability or exploding/vanishing gradient problems.

- For MoE models: routing volatility metrics. If you train Mixture-of-Experts models, monitor the fraction of experts activated before and after updates for the same responses. The paper identifies that GSPO obviates the need for Routing Replay because sequence-level likelihoods are insensitive to token-level expert-activation volatility. Logging this metric confirms that GSPO is functioning as intended in the MoE setting.

# Common errors: mixing token- and sequence-level ratios; mis-scaled clipping; missing stop-gradient in GSPO-token
There are several common pitfalls to avoid, drawn from the paper’s explanations and contrast with GRPO:

- Mixing token-level and sequence-level importance ratios. Do not combine token-level importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$ with the GSPO objective. The fundamental motivation for GSPO is that token-level importance ratios are a misapplication of importance sampling when your reward is sequence-level. Using token-level $w_{i,t}$ in a sequence-reward setting re-introduces the high-variance instability GSPO is designed to avoid.

- Mis-scaled clipping ranges. Remember that clipping ranges appropriate for token-level ratios (as used in GRPO/PPO) are not appropriate for the length-normalized sequence ratios $s_i(\theta)$. In practice the magnitudes differ by orders of magnitude. Set and tune clipping bounds according to the distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ you observe; monitor clipping fractions to guide adjustments.

- Missing stop-gradient/detach in GSPO-token. If you implement GSPO-token (the token-level objective variant that still scales tokens by the sequence scalar), be careful to implement the stop-gradient (detach) semantics as specified: $s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]}$. Numerically this yields $s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)$, but the detach prevents double-counting gradients through both the token probability and the global sequence scalar. Omitting this stop-gradient will change the theoretical gradient and can introduce instability. In practice treat $s_i(\theta)$ as a scalar importance weight that does not backpropagate through the per-token numerator when forming token-wise terms.

- Incorrect advantage standardization. When computing $\widehat{A}_i$, ensure the mean and std are taken only across the $G$ responses for the same query $x$. Using an incorrect grouping (e.g., across the entire batch mixing different queries) breaks the group-relative advantage design and will alter the learning signal.

- Not caching behavior-policy likelihoods. Recomputing $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)$ incorrectly (or recomputing it under $\pi_\theta$ by mistake) will corrupt the importance ratios. Store the behavior-policy log-likelihoods at rollout time or implement a reproducible way to recompute them exactly under $\theta_\text{old}$.

- Forgetting length normalization. Leaving out the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ factor in $s_i(\theta)$ leads to sequence-length dependent scales and much higher variance. The paper emphasizes length normalization as important for variance reduction and unified clipping behavior.

# Implementation checklist (concise)
- Sample $G$ responses per query from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and store token sequences and per-token log-probs from the rollout engine.
- Score $(x,y_i)$ with verifier to get $r(x,y_i)$.
- Compute per-query group mean/std and normalized advantages $\widehat{A}_i$.
- Compute $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)$ (numerator) at update time; fetch cached $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)$ (denominator).
- Form $\log s_i(\theta)=\frac{1}{|y_i|}(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)-\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x))$ and exponentiate only as needed.
- Clip $s_i(\theta)$ to $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ using numerically stable operations; build the clipped objective per Equation (8).
- Average per-query/group terms to form the minibatch loss, accumulate gradients if necessary, then take optimizer step and optionally update $\theta_\text{old}$ on your chosen cadence.
- Monitor diagnostics (distribution of $s$, clipping fraction, reward stats, gradients) and adjust clipping, learning rate, or batch partitioning as needed.

Following these steps implements GSPO as described in the paper: sequence-level importance ratios, group-relative advantages, sequence clipping, and sequence-level optimization — which together address the stability issues identified with token-level importance weighting and enable robust scaling (including for MoE models).

Extensions, Limitations, and Open Questions

# When GSPO-token’s finer credit assignment helps or hurts

The paper introduces two closely related objectives: GSPO, which applies importance weighting and clipping at the sequence level, and GSPO-token, which is a token-level objective variant that embeds the sequence-level importance ratio into per-token weights while allowing token-wise advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Numerically, when all token advantages in a response are identical ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$), the two objectives coincide; their gradients reduce to the same expression (Equations (11) vs (14) in the paper). This identity highlights that GSPO-token is not a different algorithm in the aggregate, but a more flexible parameterization that permits finer credit assignment when advantages vary across tokens.

From the perspective developed in the paper, GSPO-token helps when the reward structure genuinely requires token-level differentiation. For example, in multi-turn settings where different tokens or turns correspond to different sub-goals (e.g., an intermediate clarification turn that should be rewarded differently from a final answer), allowing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to vary permits the optimizer to direct updates toward particular parts of the generated sequence without changing the overall clipping behavior: the sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ still determines whether a response is treated as overly off-policy. Practically, GSPO-token can therefore express structured credit assignment while retaining sequence-level off-policy correction and clipping.

However, the paper also illuminates important limitations and failure modes of token-level weighting that carry over to GSPO-token if used injudiciously. The core motivation for GSPO was that token-level importance ratios, as used in GRPO, are unreliable because each ratio is computed from a single sampled token and so cannot fulfill the variance-reduction role of importance sampling. GSPO-token avoids this by detaching the per-token likelihood magnitudes from the gradient path (the paper uses a stop-gradient, \mathrm{sg}[\cdot], in the definition of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$). Despite this, if token advantages are noisy, miscalibrated, or inconsistent with the sequence reward, per-token credit assignment can reintroduce instability. In particular, assigning large positive/negative $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to a few tokens can drive disproportionate updates to their conditional distributions through the shared factor $s_i(\theta)$ in the gradient (Equation (14)). Thus, GSPO-token can help when the token-level advantages are reliable and meaningful, but hurt when they are noisy or poorly estimated: it introduces more degrees of freedom that require trustworthy signals to exploit safely.

Design implication: when applying GSPO-token, one should carefully validate the quality and stationarity of token-level advantages (for example, by inspecting their variance across groups, or by sanity-checking that token-level reward proxies correlate with desired behaviors). If such checks fail, falling back to sequence-level advantages (GSPO) is the safer option, per the stability arguments made in the paper.

# Interaction with KL penalties, baselines, and other regularizers

The GSPO objective in the paper omits the KL regularization term for brevity, but the authors note that such terms are commonly used in PPO-style algorithms. The move from token-level importance ratios to sequence-level ratios changes the interpretation and practical role of any additional regularizers.

KL penalties typically constrain the updated policy to stay close to the behavior policy. In a token-level formulation like GRPO, a per-token KL or per-token penalty implicitly controls the local conditional distributions. In GSPO, a KL penalty applied at the sequence level (e.g., constraining $\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x) \,\|\, \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|x)\big)$) is naturally aligned with the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$. This consistency means that a sequence-level KL or KL-based trust-region interacts coherently with the clipping behavior in GSPO: both operate on the same unit (the full response) and so their effects are congruent.

Baselines (value functions or group-based baselines) are another common stabilizer. GRPO was introduced to eliminate the need for an explicit value function by using relative advantages across a group. GSPO inherits this group-based baseline mechanism: advantages are normalized across the generated group $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$. If one were to reintroduce an explicit baseline or value model (e.g., for variance reduction), it should be consistent with the unit of optimization: a sequence-level value estimate $V(x)$ or an estimate per response $V(x,y)$ would be more appropriate than a per-token value that tries to predict cumulative future reward at each token. Mixing mismatched baselines (token-level baseline with sequence-level importance ratios) risks reintroducing the instability GSPO avoids.

Other regularizers—entropy bonuses, parameter norm penalties, or trust-region (e.g., PPO clipping with additional KL constraints)—should likewise be reasoned about at the sequence level when used with GSPO. Practically, the paper’s observations imply that aligning the granularity of regularizers with the granularity of importance weighting reduces conceptual mismatch and the risk of high-variance, ill-posed updates.

# Reward model drift and non-stationarity

The paper emphasizes group-normalized advantages computed from verifier scores $r(x,y)$. In practice, RL for language models often uses learned reward models, which can drift over time as they are updated with new annotations or further training. Reward model drift creates a form of non-stationarity: the mapping $y \mapsto r(x,y)$ changes across optimization steps. The GSPO design mitigates some issues because it ties optimization to sequence-level likelihoods and normalizes rewards within the group; nevertheless, several open questions arise directly from the paper’s findings.

First, group normalization (subtracting mean and dividing by std across $G$ responses) reduces sensitivity to absolute reward scale and can absorb some gradual drift. But abrupt changes in the reward landscape could alter which sequences are considered advantageous within a group and thereby change the sign or magnitude of $\widehat{A}_i$. Because GSPO clips based on $s_i(\theta)$, sudden shifts in advantage ordering could cause previously safe sequences to be treated as off-policy or vice versa, possibly inducing transient instability.

Second, because GSPO avoids token-level importance ratios, it is less likely to amplify short-term fluctuations in per-token likelihoods that could interact badly with a drifting reward model. The sequence-level weighting is inherently more robust to small conditional-probability perturbations, which helps when reward updates are moderate. Yet, if reward model updates are large and frequent, GSPO’s reliance on group comparisons means that the training signal’s semantics can change quickly; this requires attention to training schedules and potentially the use of slower reward-model update cadences or holdout evaluations to detect destabilizing drift.

A practical mitigation strategy consistent with the paper’s approach is to control reward-model changes relative to policy updates: ensure reward-model updates are less frequent or constrained so that the distribution of $\{r(x,y_i)\}_i$ stays approximately stationary across the mini-batches used for gradient estimation. Another strategy is to combine group-normalization with temporal smoothing of advantages (e.g., exponential moving averages), though the paper does not empirically evaluate such schemes—so they remain open directions consistent with the paper’s motivations.

# Handling extremely long sequences and memory constraints

An important motivation for GSPO in the paper is improved stability for long responses and large rollout batch sizes. The algorithm uses a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\Big( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot)} \Big),

which the paper motivates as a way to control variance and unify numerical ranges across different lengths. This normalization reduces extreme multiplicative effects that would occur if one used raw sequence ratios without scaling.

Despite this, extremely long sequences pose practical and theoretical challenges not fully solved in the paper. Computing $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ requires summing or accumulating token log-likelihoods over $|y_i|$ tokens. For very long $|y_i|$, storing per-token quantities for many responses can strain memory, especially when rollouts are large or when models are MoE with large parameter counts. The paper notes that GSPO can partition rollout batches into mini-batches, but that introduces off-policy gaps that GSPO is designed to handle better than GRPO. Still, memory and compute for recomputing sequence likelihoods (under both $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) can be substantial.

Two practical observations in the paper point to solutions. First, GSPO is tolerant of inference-training engine precision mismatches because it only needs sequence-level likelihoods; this opens the door to using lower-precision or inference-optimized engines to compute likelihoods, reducing memory and recomputation costs. Second, the length-normalized exponentiation helps keep $s_i(\theta)$ numerically stable across lengths, simplifying clipping hyperparameter selection.

Open questions remain: how to scale GSPO to scenarios with extremely long sequences (e.g., many thousands of tokens) without recomputing or storing large per-token traces? Possible directions, consistent with the paper’s rationale, include streaming accumulation of log-probabilities (so only a scalar and running sum are stored), using blockwise or chunked evaluation with amortized recomputation, or employing online estimators that keep only the quantities needed for $s_i(\theta)$. The paper does not empirically explore these engineering trade-offs in depth, so they remain fruitful areas for further work.

# Theoretical analysis opportunities: variance bounds, convergence in off-policy regimes

A central conceptual contribution of the paper is the argument that token-level importance ratios violate the basic variance-reduction assumptions of importance sampling because each ratio is computed from a single sample of the next-token distribution. The natural follow-up is to develop formal theoretical analyses that quantify these intuitions.

First, one can analyze variance bounds comparing gradient estimators under GRPO, GSPO, and GSPO-token. The paper derives the gradients for GSPO and GRPO and points out that GRPO weights token gradients with ratios that vary across positions, thereby accumulating unpredictable contributions. These derivations invite precise variance comparisons: under mild probabilistic models of the policy distributions, one could compute or bound Var\!\left[\nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathcal{J}}\right] for each estimator and show conditions under which GSPO yields strictly lower variance—particularly as $|y|$ grows. The length-normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ is another ingredient to consider in such bounds.

Second, the paper motivates GSPO by appealing to importance sampling at the sequence level. There remains an opportunity to study convergence properties in off-policy regimes where samples come from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but the objective optimizes $\pi_\theta$. Formalizing convergence requires assumptions about clipping (which creates bias), group-size $G$ (which affects advantage estimation variance), and the magnitude of per-update policy shifts. One could aim to prove that under controlled step sizes or bounded $s_i(\theta)$ (via clipping or KL constraints), GSPO is a descent method in expectation or at least possesses monotonic improvement guarantees akin to PPO’s approximate policy improvement results. The paper’s gradient expressions (Equations (11) and (14)) provide a starting point for such analyses.

Third, studying the bias introduced by clipping at the sequence level and how it trades off with variance reduction is a concrete theoretical question suggested by the paper. Clipping excludes highly off-policy sequences but also introduces bias in gradient estimates. Characterizing this bias as a function of the clipping thresholds and sequence length, and optimizing clipping schedules, could lead to principled hyperparameter choices beyond the empirical tuning described in the paper.

These theoretical lines of work would strengthen the conceptual foundations of GSPO and clarify its behavior across model scales and task regimes.

# Generalization to non-autoregressive or structured generation tasks

The paper grounds GSPO in the autoregressive language modeling setting, where $\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and sequence likelihoods factor naturally. This structure enables a clear, interpretable sequence-level importance ratio and the length-normalized exponentiation that stabilizes numeric ranges.

Extending the central ideas of GSPO to non-autoregressive or more structured generation paradigms raises both opportunities and challenges. For models that do not factor into a simple product of conditionals—for instance, non-autoregressive sequence models that generate tokens in parallel, structured prediction models that use latent variables, or graph/structured outputs—the notion of a sequence likelihood still exists in principle, but its computation and interpretation can differ. The key requirement from the paper’s perspective is that we must be able to evaluate, under both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, a scalar probability or density for the entire generated structure $y$ conditioned on $x$.

If such evaluation is tractable and numerically stable, GSPO’s principle—define importance ratios at the unit of reward, and clip at that granularity—remains applicable. However, the engineering and statistical considerations change. For example, non-autoregressive models often employ approximate decoding methods or marginalize over latent alignment variables; computing exact sequence likelihoods can be intractable, necessitating approximations that could undermine the theoretical importance-sampling basis GSPO relies on. Similarly, structured outputs with combinatorial spaces may produce highly skewed likelihoods, and the length-normalization concept would need adaptation (e.g., normalization by some measure of structural size or effective degrees of freedom).

An alternative route consistent with the paper’s logic is to identify an appropriate granularity that matches the reward: for tasks where reward is attached to substructures (subtrees, spans, or turns), define importance weighting and clipping at that substructure level rather than at per-token or whole-sequence level. This mirrors the GSPO-token idea but in a structured-space setting: the importance ratio should be computed for the same unit that the reward evaluates.

In sum, generalizing GSPO beyond autoregressive models is feasible in principle when sequence/structure likelihoods are computable and when the optimization granularity is matched to reward granularity. Implementation and theoretical validation in these regimes remain open research directions stemming from the paper’s main arguments.

# Summary and prioritized open questions

The paper’s chief insight—importance weighting and clipping should be applied at the same unit as the reward, and sequence-level importance ratios are more stable than token-level ratios—opens several practical and theoretical avenues. From an applied standpoint, GSPO-token is a useful flexible variant when token-level advantages are reliable; KL and other regularizers should be aligned to the sequence granularity; reward-model drift must be monitored and controlled because group-normalization alone cannot eliminate all non-stationarity effects; extremely long sequences demand engineering solutions that exploit GSPO’s tolerance to inference/training precision mismatches; and GSPO naturally obviates MoE-specific routing replay strategies, simplifying infrastructure. From a theoretical standpoint, rigorous variance bounds, bias-variance trade-offs due to clipping, and convergence analyses under off-policy sampling are key open problems. Finally, adapting GSPO ideas to non-autoregressive and structured generation requires (a) tractable sequence/structure likelihoods or (b) choosing an appropriate substructure granularity that matches the reward.

Concretely, if a researcher or practitioner seeks immediate next steps based on the paper, the prioritized open questions would be:
1. Empirically and theoretically compare variance of gradient estimators for GRPO, GSPO, and GSPO-token as a function of sequence length and group size $G$.
2. Analyze bias introduced by sequence-level clipping and derive principled clipping schedules or KL penalties that balance bias and stability.
3. Evaluate GSPO in settings with learned reward models that are periodically updated, measuring sensitivity to reward-model drift and testing mitigation strategies.
4. Develop engineering patterns (streaming log-prob accumulation, inference-engine likelihood usage) for very long-sequence RL rollouts, quantifying memory/compute trade-offs.
5. Explore extensions to structured-generation tasks, defining appropriate normalization and importance-ratio formulations for non-token outputs.

Addressing these questions will build on the paper’s foundations and help determine the practical limits and broader applicability of GSPO.

Summary and Key Takeaways

# Sequence-level importance weighting aligns with sequence rewards

A central insight of the paper is that the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward. In typical RL for language models the reward is assigned to an entire generated response (sequence) rather than to individual tokens. Therefore, importance sampling corrections and the clipping mechanism used to limit off-policy bias should be defined at the same granularity. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) implements this alignment by defining importance weights on full responses: for a response $y$ to query $x$ sampled from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO uses the sequence-level importance ratio
\[
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\right),
\]
with a length normalization exponent $1/|y|$. This choice directly reflects how far the sampled response under the old policy deviates from the current policy, and is therefore a meaningful indicator for whether that entire response should be trusted for gradient estimation or clipped out as overly off-policy.

The motivation for this shift comes from the mechanics of importance sampling. To correct an expectation under a target distribution using samples from a behavior distribution, one must reweight whole samples by the ratio of target to behavior densities. Applying a per-token importance weight based on a single sampled next-token (as done in GRPO) breaks that principle: a token-level weight computed from a single sampled token is not an effective correction for the whole next-token distribution and thus acts more like high-variance noise than a proper off-policy correction. By restoring the importance ratio to the sequence level, GSPO restores the theoretical intent of importance sampling in the sequence generation context and aligns clipping, reward assignment, and optimization on the same object: the sequence.

# Equalized per-token gradients within a response reduce variance and instability

A second key difference between GSPO and token-level variants like GRPO is how token gradients are weighted inside a response. When deriving the GSPO gradient (ignoring the min/clip for clarity), we obtain
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
where $s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ and $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-normalized advantage of that response. GSPO multiplies the average token log-gradient by a single sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$, so all tokens in the same response are weighted equally in their contribution to the parameter update.

By contrast, GRPO produces
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\right].
\]
Here each token gradient is multiplied by its own importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$, which may differ substantially across token positions and responses. Those unequal, per-token weights introduce an additional source of variance that accumulates over long sequences. Because clipping in GRPO operates at the token level, clipping interacts with these per-token ratios in complex ways and can amplify instability. The paper argues and provides empirical evidence that this mechanism is a key contributor to catastrophic, often irreversible, collapse observed in large-scale GRPO training.

Equalizing the token contributions within each response by using the same sequence-level multiplier removes this unstable degree of freedom. The averaging across tokens together with the group-normalized sequence advantage yields a lower-variance and more stable gradient estimate for long responses, which is particularly important when scaling RL to very large models and long generations.

# Improved stability, efficiency, and performance vs GRPO

Empirically, GSPO demonstrates clear advantages over GRPO across several axes reported in the paper. Training curves on a Qwen3-30B-based cold-start model show that GSPO proceeds stably over extended RL training, whereas GRPO is prone to sudden collapses or degraded progress unless augmented with additional stabilization measures. Under matched training compute and consumed queries, GSPO achieves higher training rewards and better downstream benchmark performance (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces Elo) than GRPO in the experiments described.

One striking empirical observation concerns clipping behavior. GSPO clips at the sequence level and, as a result, clips a much larger fraction of tokens (by several orders of magnitude) than GRPO's token-level clipping. Intuitively one might expect that clipping more tokens reduces sample utilization and hurts learning. Instead, GSPO still attains superior efficiency: despite discarding more token-level contributions, the retained sequence-level gradients are less noisy and more informative, leading to faster and more reliable progress. This counterintuitive result highlights that noisy token-level importance correction in GRPO wastes gradient signal; in practice, removing that noise by operating at the sequence level yields better effective sample efficiency.

The paper also reports that GSPO continues to improve model performance when training compute is increased, queries are updated more frequently, or generation length is extended — all behaviors consistent with a more robust algorithmic foundation that scales well with computational investment.

# MoE training simplified by removing Routing Replay

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures introduce another practical complication: sparse routing causes which experts are activated to depend on the model parameters. During RL, parameter updates can change routing, so the network used to compute the current token likelihood may differ from the one used when the sample was generated. Under token-level importance weighting, this expert-activation volatility makes token-level ratios fluctuate drastically and can invalidate the off-policy correction. To stabilize GRPO for MoE models, the authors had previously adopted a Routing Replay strategy: cache the routing choices from the old policy and replay them under the new policy when computing token likelihoods, ensuring the same activated network is used for numerator and denominator in each token ratio.

Routing Replay restores convergence for GRPO at the cost of memory, communication overhead, and an implicit constraint on model capacity (by forcing reuse of past routing decisions). GSPO fundamentally reduces or eliminates the need for this workaround. Because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods, it is far less sensitive to changes in individual token-level activations. The sequence likelihood of a response under the current policy does not fluctuate as wildly as individual token likelihoods when sparse routing changes, so the sequence-level importance ratio remains a stable and meaningful correction. In practice reported in the paper, GSPO converges for MoE models without Routing Replay, simplifying the training pipeline and removing the extra overhead associated with caching and replaying routing modes. This allows the MoE model to use its capacity more naturally and reduces engineering complexity.

# Practical benefits for RL infrastructure and scaling

GSPO produces additional pragmatic advantages for RL infrastructure in large-scale language-model training. In many production settings the rollout sampling is handled by inference engines (e.g., SGLang, vLLM) while training and likelihood recomputation are performed on training engines (e.g., Megatron). Discrepancies in numerical precision and implementation can make it necessary to recompute token likelihoods on the training side to get consistent token-level ratios for GRPO. Because GSPO only requires sequence-level likelihoods, it is more tolerant of such precision or engine differences; sequence probabilities aggregate many token contributions and thus are empirically less affected by small numerical mismatches. This tolerance opens the possibility of using likelihoods obtained directly from the inference engine for optimization, avoiding expensive recomputation on the training engine. That can simplify data pipelines, reduce computation and communication overhead, and improve throughput in partial-rollout, multi-turn, or disaggregated training-inference setups.

Finally, since GSPO obviates complex stabilization techniques (like Routing Replay) and relies on a conceptually simpler, sequence-aligned importance weighting and clipping rule, it has the potential to make RL at very large scale both more stable and easier to operate. The paper links these practical improvements to the successful application of GSPO in the development of the Qwen3 models, where it helped stabilize and scale RL training.

# Overall takeaway

Group Sequence Policy Optimization enforces a simple but powerful design principle: match the unit of off-policy correction and clipping to the unit of reward — the entire generated sequence. By using length-normalized sequence-level importance ratios, equalizing per-token gradient weight within each response, and employing group-normalized sequence advantages, GSPO reduces variance, avoids destabilizing token-level noise, and better respects importance-sampling principles in the language-generation setting. These choices yield improved stability, efficiency, and empirical performance over GRPO, simplify MoE training by removing the need for Routing Replay, and provide practical infrastructure benefits by tolerating inference/training engine differences. Taken together, GSPO offers a theoretically motivated and empirically validated foundation for scaling RL-based fine-tuning of large language models.
