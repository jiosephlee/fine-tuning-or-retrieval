# From Tokens to Tales: Why Sequence-Level RL Makes LLMs More Stable

## TL;DR
Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) replaces token-level importance weights with a sequence-level importance ratio and clips at the full-response level. Because rewards are assigned to whole responses, this alignment reduces high-variance, accumulative noise from token-wise reweighting, stabilizes training (especially for large and sparse Mixture-of-Experts models), and improves sample efficiency compared to token-level methods like GRPO.

## The mismatch: rewards are for sequences, but some algorithms optimize tokens
Modern RL fine-tuning of language models treats the model as a policy \(\pi_\theta\). We sample responses \(y\) given a query \(x\) and score each (e.g., a verifier gives a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\)). Practically, these rewards apply to entire generated responses — not to individual tokens — yet some RL variants try to correct for off-policy sampling and optimize at the token level.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) is a recent approach that avoids using a value model by computing relative advantages within a group of responses for the same query. But GRPO still uses token-level importance ratios
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\)
and clips those ratios at every token position. Intuitively this is like grading every word in a sentence and re-weighting each word differently when computing gradients — while the final grade (reward) applies to the whole sentence.

## Why token-level importance ratios are problematic
The theoretical justification for importance sampling is that we reweight samples from a behavior distribution to estimate expectations under a target distribution:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
This relies on reweighting entire sampled items \(z\) (and averaging over many such samples) to correct distributional mismatch. In autoregressive generation, the natural "item" is the whole sequence \(y\), not each sampled token \(y_t\) in isolation.

Token-level weights in GRPO are computed from single-token samples drawn from the next-token distribution. Because each token weight is based on only one sample from that next-token distribution, the weight does not reliably correct distribution mismatch and instead injects high variance into gradient estimates. Two practical consequences follow:

- Variance accumulates across long sequences: multiplying or summing many noisy per-token contributions amplifies instability as response length grows.
- The clipping mechanism interacts badly with token-wise weights: clipping each token independently can produce unpredictable, disproportionate effects on gradients, further destabilizing training.

Empirically, this can lead to catastrophic, often irreversible collapse of the policy during RL fine-tuning of large models.

## GSPO: clip and optimize at the sequence level
GSPO fixes the mismatch by defining importance ratios on whole sequences and applying clipping at the response level. For a response \(y_i\), GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
The GSPO objective (with group-based advantages) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right],
\]
where \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the standardized reward of response \(y_i\) within its group.

Two important design choices:
- Sequence-level ratio: reweights whole responses, matching the reward unit.
- Length normalization: the exponent \(1/|y_i|\) keeps ratios comparable across different response lengths and reduces variance.

Because GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally (through the shared \(s_i(\theta)\)), it avoids the noisy, token-wise imbalance and produces more stable gradients.

## Why this shift reduces training noise and collapse
There are three complementary reasons GSPO improves stability:

1. Correct unit for importance sampling. Importance sampling theory applies naturally at the sequence level in autoregressive generation; reweighting whole sequences properly corrects for sampling from an older policy.

2. Reduced variance accumulation. Token-wise importance weights introduce independent noise per token that accumulates across long sequences. Sequence-level weights aggregate across tokens before reweighting, dampening this accumulation.

3. Cleaner interaction with clipping. Clipping a single scalar per response is less likely to introduce disproportionate, structured biases in the gradient than clipping many independent token weights.

As a result, GSPO removes a key source of unpredictable gradient noise that contributed to the catastrophic collapses observed with GRPO.

## Benefits for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models
MoE models add another layer of instability: expert-activation volatility. After a parameter update, the set of experts routed for the same input can change. In GRPO, token-level importance ratios depend on per-token likelihoods computed under potentially different active sub-networks (different routing), which can make those ratios swing wildly.

A previously used workaround, Routing Replay, cached routing choices from the old policy and replayed them to compute token likelihoods under the new policy. While helpful, Routing Replay increases engineering complexity, memory, and communication, and can limit effective model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entire problem: because it uses sequence-level likelihoods, it is much less sensitive to token-level routing changes. The sequence probability does not fluctuate as drastically under routing volatility, so GSPO stabilizes MoE RL without Routing Replay, simplifying infrastructure and letting the model utilize its full capacity.

## Empirical signals reported
The paper reports that GSPO:
- Trains stably where GRPO sometimes collapses.
- Improves training efficiency and downstream benchmark performance (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces) under comparable compute and queries.
- Allows stable RL scaling with MoE models without Routing Replay.
- Shows a counterintuitive but important observation: GSPO clips a far larger fraction of tokens (because clipping acts on whole responses) than GRPO, yet it still attains higher efficiency. This supports the interpretation that GRPO's token-level gradients are noisy and less useful.

## A token-level variant when you need it: GSPO-token
When per-token advantages are genuinely needed (e.g., multi-turn scenarios where you want finer credit assignment), the authors propose GSPO-token. It retains the sequence-level scale \(s_i(\theta)\) but allows token-by-token advantages \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\). Numerically, when token advantages are identical across a response, GSPO-token reduces to GSPO; when they differ, GSPO-token provides flexibility while preserving the more stable sequence-based reweighting.

## Practical takeaways
- Match the optimization unit to the reward unit: if rewards are computed per sequence, perform off-policy correction at the sequence level.
- For long responses and large/sparse models, per-token importance weighting can create high-variance gradients and cause training collapse.
- Using sequence-level importance ratios with length normalization and sequence clipping is a simple change with big stability and efficiency gains. It also reduces the need for MoE-specific hacks like Routing Replay.
- If you need token-level credit assignment, build it on top of a sequence-level reweighting scheme (GSPO-token) rather than replacing the sequence-level correction.

## Closing thought
The idea is simple but powerful: when you grade "tales" (full responses), don't try to retro-fit that grading into per-word reweighting. Align the importance-sampling correction with the unit that receives the reward. GSPO shows that this alignment is enough to dramatically reduce noise, prevent catastrophic collapse, and scale RL to larger and sparser language models.



# The Hidden Instability in Today’s RL for LLMs—and How GSPO Fixes It

## TL;DR
State-of-the-art group RL methods for language models like GRPO apply importance sampling at the token level. That seems natural for an autoregressive model, but it breaks a core requirement of importance sampling: you need many samples to correct a distribution mismatch. Token-level weights computed from single next-token samples inject high-variance noise, which accumulates over long responses and can cause catastrophic, often irreversible model collapse—especially for very large or sparse (MoE) models. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fixes this by (1) computing importance ratios at the sequence level, (2) clipping at the sequence level, and (3) computing advantages over groups of full responses. The result: far more stable, efficient RL for large LLMs and MoE models, with fewer hacks in the training stack.

## The problem in one picture
When you run RL on language models you generate whole responses (sequences) but often update the policy using mini-batches and off-policy samples. Importance sampling corrects for this mismatch by weighting behavior samples by how likely they are under the new policy. GRPO applied this idea at the token level: for each token $y_{t}$ in a sampled response $y$, it uses the per-token importance weight
$w_{t}(\theta) = \dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\!\mid\!x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{t}\!\mid\!x, y_{<t})}$
and then clips and multiplies by an advantage shared across the tokens of the response.

That looks plausible, but there's a subtle mismatch: importance sampling’s corrective power comes from reweighting over many samples of the same random variable. In GRPO’s token-level scheme, each $w_t$ is computed from exactly one sampled token from the next-token distribution. That single-sample weight cannot reliably estimate the ratio between whole next-token distributions. The consequence: high-variance gradient estimates that compound across tokens and across long responses. Empirically this can lead to training collapse that is hard or impossible to recover from.

## Why token-level importance ratios fail (intuitively)
Importance sampling is designed to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by averaging weighted samples from a behavior distribution:
$\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\left[\dfrac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].$
This relies on the weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ correcting the distribution mismatch across many draws $z$. In the token-level setup, the “random variable” is the next-token draw, but for each token position we only have a single draw $y_{t}$ to compute $w_{t}$. That single value is a noisy, unreliable estimator of how the whole next-token distributions differ.

Two practical consequences make this worse:
- Noise accumulation: Per-token weight noise stacks up over sequence length. Even small variance per token can explode across hundreds of tokens.
- Clipping amplification: GRPO uses per-token clipping to limit off-policy effects. But clipping interacts poorly with high-variance, single-sample weights, creating biased and unstable gradient signals.

For large models, long responses, and sparse architectures (MoE), these problems magnify, producing the catastrophic collapses reported in practice.

## GSPO’s core idea: make the unit of correction match the unit of reward
GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization) adopts a straightforward, well-motivated change: perform importance weighting and clipping at the sequence (response) level, not the token level. Since rewards are assigned to whole responses $y$, the sequence is the natural optimization unit.

Define the sequence-level importance ratio (length-normalized) as
$s(\theta) = \left(\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y\!\mid\!x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\!\mid\!x)}\right)^{1/|y|}
= \exp\!\left(\dfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\!\mid\!x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\!\mid\!x,y_{<t})}\right).$
GSPO then forms group-based advantages across $G$ responses to the same query:
$\widehat{A}_i = \dfrac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}$
and optimizes the clipped sequence objective
$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\dfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right].$

Two practical details matter:
- Length normalization in $s(\theta)$ reduces variance and makes clipping ranges comparable across different response lengths.
- Group advantages avoid training a separate value model: each response’s reward is normalized within the group, giving a relative advantage estimate.

## What the gradient analysis shows
Dropping clipping for clarity, GSPO’s gradient looks like
$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\dfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)\right]$
which expands to
$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\dfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G 
\left(\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\!\mid\!x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\!\mid\!x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_i\cdot\dfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\!\mid\!x,y_{i,<t})
\right].$

Compare this to GRPO’s gradient, which attaches per-token multiplicative weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ directly inside the per-token sum. In GRPO, tokens within the same response can receive different importance scalings, derived from single-token draws. GSPO, by contrast, multiplies the entire response’s token gradients by a single sequence-level scalar. This equal weighting of the tokens in a response removes a major source of noisy, inconsistent gradient scaling and stabilizes learning.

## Why this matters for MoE models
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts models add another failure mode. Expert routing can change between policy updates, so the activated subnetwork for a given token under $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ may differ. This makes per-token likelihood ratios volatile and unreliable.

Workarounds like Routing Replay (cache the old routing and replay it under the new policy when computing token likelihoods) mitigate the issue but add complexity, memory, and communication overhead, and they constrain the MoE to operate under old routing choices.

GSPO avoids this entirely: sequence-level likelihoods are much less sensitive to individual expert reroutings. Because GSPO does not rely on accurate per-token ratios, it converges stably for MoE models without Routing Replay, simplifying training and letting the model fully exploit its capacity.

## Surprising empirical observation about clipping
GSPO clips whole responses; GRPO clips at the token level. That leads to a counter-intuitive empirical fact: GSPO ends up clipping a much larger fraction of tokens (because if a sequence is clipped, all its tokens are effectively clipped), yet GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency than GRPO. This suggests that GRPO’s token-level signal is so noisy that keeping more token gradients (by clipping fewer tokens) does not translate into better learning. GSPO’s cleaner, lower-variance sequence-level signals, even when fewer tokens are used, are more effective.

## Other practical benefits
- Reduced sensitivity to precision mismatches between rollout/inference engines and training engines. Sequence-level likelihoods tolerate small numeric differences better than token-by-token likelihoods, which eases recomputation needs and simplifies distributed training-inference setups.
- No need for a separate value model (same as GRPO): GSPO pairs naturally with group-based advantage estimation, avoiding the memory and engineering costs of training a value network at scale.

## When to use GSPO
GSPO is particularly compelling when:
- You train very large or deep LLMs where long responses cause token-level noise to accumulate.
- You train sparse/MoE models and want to avoid routing replay complexity.
- You want a more robust RL pipeline that tolerates off-policy mini-batching and engine precision differences.

If you need token-level control over advantages (e.g., multi-turn RL with local rewards), GSPO-token is a variant that detaches the sequence-level scalar but allows per-token advantage shaping while preserving the low-variance, sequence-based correction principle.

## Bottom line
Token-level importance weights computed from single next-token samples violate the practical assumptions of importance sampling. That creates high-variance gradient noise that accumulates with sequence length and can crash RL training for large language models, especially MoE. GSPO brings the unit of importance correction in line with the unit of reward—full sequences—using length-normalized sequence likelihoods, group-based advantages, and sequence-level clipping. The result is markedly more stable and efficient RL for LLMs with simpler infrastructure and fewer brittle hacks.



# Mixture-of-Experts, Minus the Mayhem: Stable RL for MoE with GSPO

## Why MoE training gets messy under RL

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures scale model capacity by routing tokens to different expert sub-networks. That sparsity gives huge parameter counts without proportionally larger FLOPs, but it also makes RL training brittle in practice. The core problem observed in large MoE RL runs is expert-activation volatility: after a single gradient update, the set of experts activated for the same input can change substantially. The Qwen3 experiments report that for a 48-layer MoE model, about 10% of experts activated for a given response under the new policy differ from those under the old policy after an RL gradient step.

Why does that matter? Most RL algorithms for language models (e.g., GRPO, and token-level PPO variants) rely on per-token importance ratios
w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}
to correct for the fact that rollout samples come from an old policy. Those ratios implicitly assume the network computing the numerator and denominator is comparable across the two policies. But in MoE, a different set of experts can be activated under \pi_{\theta} than under \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} for the same token. That changes the functional mapping used to compute token probabilities, so the ratio w_{t} becomes noisy and can fluctuate wildly. This invalidates the intended importance-sampling correction and injects high-variance noise into gradients — a major source of instability and even catastrophic collapse in RL training of large MoE models.

## The Routing Replay workaround (and its costs)

To stabilize GRPO-style, token-level importance ratios on MoE models, the paper's prior approach was Routing Replay. The idea is simple in concept: when computing the importance ratio for a token, force \pi_{\theta} to use the same routing decisions (the same activated experts) that \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} used when the sample was collected. Concretely, you cache the routing choices (which experts were selected) for each rollout sample and then “replay” those choices under the new policy when evaluating \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid \cdot).

Routing Replay works because it removes the source of mismatch in the numerator and denominator of w_{t}: both probabilities are evaluated on the same subnetwork, so the ratio becomes meaningful again. Empirically, this strategy was essential for getting GRPO to converge when training MoE RL in prior work.

But Routing Replay is far from free:

- Memory overhead: You must cache routing choices (typically per-token or per-layer per-sample) for the entire rollout buffer or at least for mini-batches. For large batches and long responses this can be substantial.
- Communication/synchronization overhead: In distributed training, routing metadata must be stored and re-applied across machines, adding complexity and bandwidth costs.
- Reduced effective capacity: Replaying old routing prevents the model from using its current routing policy during likelihood evaluation, artificially constraining how experts can be used in optimization and limiting the natural capacity benefits of MoE.

So Routing Replay stabilizes training at the expense of infrastructure complexity and possibly under-utilizing the model.

## GSPO: sequence-level importance ratios that sidestep volatility

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) takes a different angle: follow the basic importance sampling principle but apply it at the sequence level, not the token level. Instead of forming per-token ratios w_{t}, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio for an entire response y to query x:

s(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\right).

Two design choices matter:

1. Sequence-level weighting and clipping: GSPO applies clipping and the optimization objective at the response level, i.e., whole responses are scaled by s(\theta) and possibly clipped if s(\theta) moves too far from 1. This aligns the unit of importance weighting with the unit of reward (rewards are sequence-scoped).
2. Length normalization: The exponent 1/|y| keeps the numerical range of s(\theta) reasonable across different response lengths.

Why does this help with MoE volatility? Sequence-level likelihoods are much less sensitive to small per-token routing changes. Even if a few token-level likelihoods jump because different experts are used, the aggregated sequence likelihood remains a stable quantity: the model's overall generative capacity is still intact and does not flip dramatically due to localized routing changes. Thus s(\theta) provides a meaningful measure of how “off-policy” a whole response is, while being robust to the volatility of individual expert activations.

Crucially, GSPO can compute s(\theta) using the model's normal forward pass under \pi_{\theta}, without replaying past routings. That means the numerator and denominator in the sequence ratio are evaluated on their respective, naturally selected expert sets — which is fine because the ratio's purpose is to measure distributional shift at the sequence scale.

## What GSPO removes — and what it gives back

By moving to sequence-level importance ratios, GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay when training MoE models. The practical consequences documented in the paper are:

- No routing metadata caching: You no longer need to store and reapply per-token routing decisions for rollout samples, cutting memory use and simplifying data pipelines.
- Less communication/synchronization overhead: Without replaying routing across devices, distributed setups are simpler and require less coordination.
- Full use of MoE capacity: The model can use its current routing policy naturally during optimization. There is no artificial constraint forcing optimization to reuse old routing patterns, which lets the MoE exploit its capacity rather than being partially constrained by replay artifacts.
- Stable convergence: GSPO empirically stabilized RL for large MoE models where GRPO required Routing Replay to converge. The paper reports that with GSPO, normal convergence is achieved using standard likelihood evaluation under \pi_{\theta}.

An additional practical advantage noted is that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant to precision differences between inference and training engines. That makes it easier to use likelihoods returned by inference stacks (e.g., vLLM) directly rather than recomputing with training engines — further simplifying RL infrastructure.

## A concise comparison

- GRPO + token-level ratios
  - Pros: Fine-grained per-token adjustment; fits a token-wise view of autoregressive models.
  - Cons: Token likelihoods vary wildly when expert activations change; requires Routing Replay to be stable on MoE; high memory and communication overhead; possible under-use of model capacity.

- GSPO + sequence-level ratios
  - Pros: Robust to token-by-token routing volatility; removes the need for Routing Replay; reduces memory/communication costs; allows MoE to use full capacity; aligns optimization unit with reward unit.
  - Cons: Coarser granularity (but GSPO-token variant exists if token-wise advantages are needed while retaining sequence-level weighting mechanics).

## Takeaway

Expert-activation volatility is an inherent source of instability when you try to apply token-level importance sampling on MoE models. Routing Replay was a pragmatic fix but adds heavy infrastructure and constrains the model. GSPO reframes the problem: do importance weighting and clipping at the sequence level, where likelihoods are stable despite per-token routing changes. That simple shift removes the need for Routing Replay, reduces system complexity, and lets MoE models realize their capacity during RL — enabling more stable and effective large-scale RL training in practice.



# Clipping Fewer Signals, Learning More: The Counterintuitive Efficiency of GSPO

## TL;DR

When you move clipping from the token level to the sequence level, something surprising happens: you end up clipping far more tokens overall, yet the model learns faster and more stably. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) does exactly that. By defining importance weights on whole-response likelihoods and clipping at the response level, GSPO produces cleaner gradient signals than token-level methods (e.g., GRPO), reduces harmful variance, stabilizes Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training, and delivers better sample efficiency in practice.

Below I unpack why clipping "more" can lead to learning "more", keeping the explanation rooted in the GSPO paper's analyses and empirical findings.

## What does "clipping" do in RL fine-tuning of LMs?

Clipping (from PPO-style objectives) is a safeguard against large off-policy corrections: when samples were generated by an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\), we reweight them by importance ratios to approximate expectations under the current policy \(\pi_\theta\). Clipping limits how far those importance ratios can push the gradient, preventing unstable updates from overly off-policy samples.

Two design choices matter:

- The unit over which you compute the importance ratio (token vs. sequence).
- The unit on which you apply clipping (per-token clipping vs. per-sequence clipping).

GRPO and related token-level variants compute importance ratios per token and clip each token's contribution. GSPO computes a sequence-level importance ratio and clips entire responses.

## The surprising empirical fact

In the GSPO experiments, the authors report a striking empirical observation: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (by roughly two orders of magnitude) compared to GRPO, yet achieves faster training progress and better downstream benchmark performance under the same computation budget.

Concretely, GSPO in the experiments used very tight sequence-level clipping ranges (left/right around 3e-4 and 4e-4), whereas GRPO used token-level clipping ranges tuned around 0.2 and 0.27. Even with GSPO discarding or down-weighting many more token contributions, it produced cleaner learning and superior efficiency.

Why does that happen? The intuition and the math in the paper point to variance and correctness of importance sampling.

## Why token-level clipping can be misleading and noisy

Importance sampling rests on a core principle: to estimate an expectation under a target distribution, you reweight samples drawn from a behavior distribution by the ratio of target to behavior probabilities. Crucially, those importance weights are meaningful when they correct for distribution mismatch across many samples from the same distribution.

In the token-level scheme used by GRPO, the importance ratio is
\(w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\),
which is computed from a single sampled token \(y_{i,t}\) for each next-token distribution \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). That single-sample ratio is a poor estimator for correcting distributional mismatch: it injects high variance instead of correcting bias. Over long sequences these per-token variances accumulate, and the clipping mechanism interacts with those noisy ratios in complex ways that can amplify instability. Empirically, the paper reports that this accumulation can lead to catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse during large-scale RL.

The gradient-level distinction is instructive. GRPO effectively weights each token gradient \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)\) by the token-level ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), allowing token-wise unequal weights that vary unpredictably. Those unequal per-token weights are the source of the high-variance, unstable signal.

## Why GSPO's sequence-level clipping is cleaner

GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio and uses it for clipping:
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\right).
\]
Two practical choices make this work well:

- The ratio is taken over the whole sequence likelihood, which aligns with the fact that rewards are assigned at the sequence level.
- The ratio is length-normalized (exponent \(1/|y_i|\)) to control numerical range and variance across different response lengths.

The resulting gradient effectively multiplies the entire sequence's token gradients by a single scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) instead of applying different scalars per token. That equal-weighting within a response removes the per-token importance noise that plagues GRPO, yielding a much cleaner learning signal. In short: GSPO corrects (and clips) at the same granularity as the reward, which is what importance-sampling theory suggests.

## Why clipping "a lot" can still be better

At first glance it seems paradoxical: GSPO clips far more tokens (fewer tokens contribute to gradient estimation), yet it learns faster. The resolution is that the quality of the gradients matters more than their quantity. GRPO's per-token gradients are noisy and biased by single-sample importance ratios; they can drown signal in variance. GSPO, though it ignores or down-weights many tokens via sequence-level clipping, preserves low-variance, coherent updates for the remaining responses. Those higher-quality updates are more informative per unit of compute and lead to better sample efficiency.

Put another way: throwing away many noisy signals (tokens) can be better than using many noisy signals that pull the model in inconsistent directions.

## Extra wins: MoE stability and simpler infrastructure

The paper also observes two important practical benefits of GSPO that follow from the cleaner, sequence-level treatment.

First, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models suffer from "expert-activation volatility": after a gradient update, the set of experts activated for the same response can change, causing token-level likelihoods to fluctuate drastically. GRPO needs a Routing Replay workaround (replaying old routing modes) to stabilize token-level ratios; this adds memory and communication overhead and constrains model capacity. GSPO, by relying on sequence likelihoods rather than fragile token-level probabilities, naturally avoids this instability and eliminates the need for Routing Replay.

Second, training and inference engines can have precision differences. Recomputing token-level likelihoods on the training engine is commonly used to avoid mismatches. GSPO's tolerance for sequence-level likelihood noise makes it feasible to use likelihoods returned by inference engines directly, which simplifies RL infrastructure and reduces recomputation.

## Takeaway

- Token-level importance ratios computed from single sampled tokens are high-variance and can destabilize RL fine-tuning, especially for long responses and large/sparse models.
- GSPO moves importance sampling and clipping to the sequence level, length-normalizes ratios, and aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward.
- The result is counterintuitive but clear in experiments: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens overall but learns faster and more stably because it preserves higher-quality gradients.
- Additional practical benefits: it stabilizes MoE training without Routing Replay and relaxes some infrastructure constraints.

If you are training large LMs with RL (long responses, MoE layers, or split training/inference engines), GSPO is worth considering: better-behaved importance sampling at the sequence level can beat more aggressive token-level utilization that appears efficient on the surface but suffers from noisy, unstable gradients underneath.



# Simpler RL Stacks for LLMs: How GSPO Streamlines Infrastructure

Reinforcement learning at LLM scale brings not only algorithmic challenges but also practical engineering burdens. One recurring pain point is the need to reconcile differences between the engines used for inference (sampling responses) and the engines used for training (recomputing likelihoods and optimizing). Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), introduced in “Group Sequence Policy Optimization”, provides a simple algorithmic change—move from token-level to sequence-level importance ratios—that materially eases several real-world engineering needs. This post highlights those practical wins and explains why they hold, grounded in the GSPO paper.

## The pain: mismatched precisions and costly recomputation

In large-scale RL workflows it is common to separate inference and training:

- An inference engine (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) is used to sample responses at high throughput. These engines may use different numeric precisions, kernels, or optimized code paths compared to the training engine.
- The training engine (e.g., Megatron-style implementations) is used to recompute likelihoods under the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) and to run gradient updates.

Because many RL algorithms (PPO, GRPO, etc.) operate with token-level importance ratios of the form
\[
w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})},
\]
practitioners typically recompute token likelihoods on the training engine to avoid precision mismatches and numerical instability. That recomputation is expensive: it adds latency, doubles compute for likelihoods, complicates distributed pipelines, and especially burdens partial-rollout or multi-turn scenarios where the set of tokens to re-evaluate is dynamic.

Moreover, token-level ratios are sensitive: tiny numerical differences at many token positions can accumulate, increasing variance and destabilizing training.

## GSPO’s core change: sequence-level importance ratios

GSPO replaces token-level weights with a sequence-level importance ratio (length-normalized):
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Two practical consequences follow directly from this choice:

1. The optimization and clipping are performed at the whole-response level, matching the fact that rewards in RL for generation are typically given per sequence.
2. Sequence-level likelihoods are intrinsically more tolerant to small numerical or implementation differences at the token level, because they aggregate (and length-normalize) log-likelihood contributions across the entire response.

These observations are central to the infrastructure benefits described below.

## Engineering wins: fewer recomputations, simpler pipelines

1. Use inference-engine likelihoods directly
   - Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, the likelihood values returned by the inference engine are typically sufficient for computing \(s_i(\theta)\).
   - The paper argues—and empirically demonstrates—that sequence-level likelihoods tolerate precision mismatches better than token-level ratios. This means you can avoid recomputing \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)\) on the training engine in many settings, reducing duplicate compute and data movement.

2. Easier partial-rollout and multi-turn RL
   - In partial-rollout or multi-turn scenarios, token recomputation becomes messy: which turns do you recompute, how do you stitch likelihoods, etc. Sequence-level likelihoods collapse this complexity into a single scalar per sampled response.
   - GSPO also offers a token-level variant (GSPO-token) that is numerically equivalent when all tokens share the same advantage but which keeps the sequence-level importance scalar detached from per-token gradients. This design provides flexibility for multi-turn advantage shaping without reintroducing token-level sensitivity.

3. Simpler disaggregated training–inference architectures
   - Disaggregated systems (separate inference clusters and training clusters) benefit from not having to shuttle token-level traces back to training nodes. With GSPO you can sample and compute sequence likelihoods on the inference side and send those compact scalars to the trainer.
   - This reduces communication volume and avoids tight coupling between inference and training engines, simplifying deployment and scaling.

## Additional practical benefits for MoE and sparse models

The paper also highlights an orthogonal but important engineering burden for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: expert-activation volatility. Under token-level importance ratios, small changes in routing can make \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid\cdot)\) fluctuate dramatically and invalidate token-level corrections. Prior work introduced Routing Replay (cache and replay of routing decisions) to stabilize token-level ratios, but that adds memory, communication, and reduces effective capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this: it relies on sequence likelihoods, which are far less sensitive to which experts were active for particular tokens. The result is that GSPO removes the dependency on Routing Replay, simplifying MoE training infrastructure and avoiding the overheads that routing replay incurs.

## What changes in practice

Adopting GSPO in your RL stack implies these concrete operational changes:

- You can often avoid recomputing token-level likelihoods on the training engine. Instead, collect sequence likelihoods from the inference engine and use them to compute \(s_i(\theta)\).
- Your rollout pipeline becomes simpler: store sampled response text plus the sequence log-likelihood (and reward). Trainers only need these scalars and the responses for gradient steps.
- For MoE models you can drop Routing Replay in many cases, reducing memory and communication complexity and enabling the model to use its full capacity.

Caveats and operational notes from the paper:
- GSPO uses length-normalized sequence ratios \(s_i(\theta)\), and its clipping ranges are of a different magnitude than token-level clipping (you will tune these ranges differently).
- The empirical results in the paper show stable training, higher sample efficiency than GRPO, and successful application to large-scale Qwen3 models, but adoption still requires standard engineering validation on your tasks and deployments.

## Bottom line

GSPO’s shift to sequence-level importance ratios is a small algorithmic change with outsized engineering payoff. By making the optimization unit match the reward unit and aggregating likelihoods at the sequence level, GSPO reduces sensitivity to token-level precision mismatches, eliminates many recomputation demands, simplifies multi-turn and partial-rollout workflows, and relaxes engineering workarounds for MoE models. For teams building disaggregated or high-throughput RL systems for LLMs, GSPO is worth considering as a way to simplify the stack while improving stability and efficiency—precisely the kinds of practical wins that scale-aware RL needs.



# GSPO vs. GRPO: What Changes in the Math—and Why It Matters

## TL;DR
GRPO applies importance weights at each token; GSPO aggregates importance at the sequence level and applies a single, length-normalized ratio per response. Mathematically this replaces many noisy, per-token reweights with one stable multiplier per sequence. The result: lower variance gradient estimates for long responses, better behavior for sparse MoE models, and more robust RL updates when rewards arrive at the sequence level.

## Setup — the problem we care about
We are in the standard RL-for-language setup: an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$ generates a response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ to a query $x$, and a verifier gives a scalar reward $r(x,y)$. In practice we generate groups of $G$ responses per query and compute advantages within each group to avoid a separate value network.

Two algorithms considered here differ in how they (1) compute importance ratios that correct for off-policy sampling and (2) distribute those ratios across tokens when computing gradients.

- GRPO: an importance ratio per token, applied to each token's log-probability gradient.
- GSPO: an importance ratio per sequence (length-normalized), applied uniformly to all tokens of that sequence.

Below I walk through the two gradient forms and give intuition for why GSPO is less noisy for long responses.

## Quick reminder: importance sampling in one line
If you want the expectation of a function $f(z)$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ but samples come from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, importance sampling gives
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
Crucially, the weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ should be attached to the same unit $z$ that carries the function $f(z)$. If $f$ is a function of the whole sequence, the weight should be the sequence-level likelihood ratio, not a per-token ratio computed from one token sample.

## The two gradients (cleaned up)
Omitting clipping and constants, the paper derives:

- GRPO gradient (per-token importance ratios):
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

- GSPO gradient (sequence-level ratio, length-normalized):
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
where
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Two observations stand out from these forms.

## Observation 1 — where the weight attaches matters
- In GRPO, each token's gradient is multiplied by a token-level ratio that depends on that token's single-sample likelihood under the old policy. This ratio is noisy because it is formed from a single sample from the next-token distribution; it does not approximate a reweighting over that entire next-token distribution.
- In GSPO, each token's gradient within a response is multiplied by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$. That scalar is derived from the full-sequence likelihood ratio and thus aligns with the fact that the reward (and advantage estimate) is computed for the whole sequence.

Intuition: importance sampling is correcting a distribution mismatch. If the thing you're correcting (the reward) is assigned to the whole sequence, attaching the correction to the whole sequence is the principled choice.

## Observation 2 — length normalization stabilizes the multiplier
Without length normalization one would use the raw sequence ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}
= \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)},
\]
which can be tiny or huge for long sequences simply because it multiplies many factors. GSPO takes the geometric mean by raising the ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$; equivalently, it averages log-ratios across tokens:
\[
s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot)}\right).
\]

Analogy: think of each token as a multiplicative "stretch" factor. Multiplying 100 stretches produces an extreme overall stretch. Taking the geometric mean turns that into a per-token average stretch, which is numerically much better behaved and keeps the clip threshold in a consistent scale across different lengths. For optimization this means updates for a 50-token response are comparable in scale to those for a 10-token response, rather than wildly different.

## Why this reduces variance and instability
- GRPO: unequal per-token multipliers (each noisy) accumulate across tokens. The gradient is a sum of many small noisy terms, each reweighted by a noisy ratio. For long responses this accumulation inflates variance, and clipping those token ratios interacts poorly with that noise (clipping can amplify bias and make training dynamics unpredictable).
- GSPO: a single, smoother multiplier per response modulates a sum of token gradients. Effectively you "average first, multiply later": you average token log-gradients inside the response and then scale by the sequence-level ratio. Averaging before scaling reduces variance; scaling by a length-normalized ratio keeps the multiplier in a reasonable range independent of sequence length.

Put differently, GSPO enforces uniform weighting across tokens inside the same response, which removes one source of per-token sensitivity that caused instability in GRPO.

## Practical consequences mentioned in the paper
- GSPO stabilized RL training at large scale and on long-response tasks where GRPO could experience catastrophic collapse.
- For Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, token-level likelihoods can fluctuate because different updates can change which experts activate for a token. GRPO's token-level ratios become volatile as a result. GSPO relies only on sequence likelihoods (aggregated) and is less sensitive to per-token routing changes, removing the need for special replay tricks that pin expert activations.
- GSPO’s length-normalized ratios also allow clipping thresholds that are numerically much smaller (because $s_i(\theta)$ lives in a tighter range), and GSPO still achieves better sample efficiency even though sequence-level clipping may discard more tokens numerically. This indicates that GRPO’s per-token signal is noisier and less useful.

## A short concrete analogy
Imagine you want to correct for a biased camera that samples frames differently from reality. If your reward is measured per video, it is better to correct by the ratio of whole-video probabilities (the chance the camera would have produced that whole video) than by correcting each frame individually using a single observed frame sample from the camera. Correcting per-frame with only one observed frame per frame-distribution is noisy; correcting per-video aggregates information across frames and gives a more reliable correction for the video-level reward.

## Takeaway
When rewards and advantages are defined at the sequence (response) level, align the importance sampling correction with that same unit. GSPO does this by using a length-normalized sequence likelihood ratio as a single multiplier per response; mathematically this moves the multiplier outside the token-sum and reduces variance and instability that arise when noisy, per-token ratios are used. For long responses, sparse models (MoE), and large-scale RL runs, this alignment matters — it stabilizes training and simplifies practical engineering (fewer tricks needed to make importance ratios well behaved).



# When You Need Finer Control: Introducing GSPO-token

In Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) we replaced noisy token-level importance weights with a sequence-level importance ratio, clipped and applied at the response level. That change fixes a core instability in GRPO and PPO-style objectives for long responses and sparse (MoE) models. But sometimes you still want finer-grained control over which parts of a response should get credit or blame — for example, in multi-turn RL where different tokens correspond to different turns, or when you want to shape learning at a token or segment granularity. GSPO-token is the variant that gives you that granular control while retaining GSPO’s stable, sequence-level importance weighting.

## The idea in one sentence

GSPO-token keeps the sequence-level importance ratio from GSPO as the off-policy correction and clipping anchor, but allows advantages to be specified (and applied) at the token level. Numerically, if all token advantages in a response are identical, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO; when token advantages differ, GSPO-token provides a principled way to adjust training signals per token without reintroducing the high-variance, invalid token-level importance ratios that caused instability in GRPO.

## Recap: why sequence-level weighting matters

GSPO defines the sequence importance ratio as a length-normalized ratio of sequence likelihoods:

s_i(θ) = (π_θ(y_i | x) / π_{θ_old}(y_i | x))^{1/|y_i|}
       = exp( (1/|y_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|} log ( π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) / π_{θ_old}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) ) ).

This single scalar s_i(θ) correctly measures how far the whole sampled response y_i (drawn from π_{θ_old}) deviates from the current policy π_θ, which matches the fact that rewards r(x,y) are given at sequence level. Using s_i(θ) for clipping and weighting avoids the invalid application of token-level importance weights computed from single next-token samples — the source of large variance and collapse in GRPO.

## GSPO-token: formulation

GSPO-token uses the same sequence-level correction s_i(θ) but allows a per-token advantage \widehat{A}_{i,t}:

J_{GSPO-token}(θ) = E_{x, {y_i}∼π_{θ_old}} [
  (1/G) ∑_{i=1}^G (1/|y_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    min( s_{i,t}(θ) · \widehat{A}_{i,t}, clip( s_{i,t}(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε ) · \widehat{A}_{i,t} )
].

GSPO-token defines s_{i,t}(θ) so it is numerically equal to s_i(θ) but constructed to stop gradients through the per-token scaling:

s_{i,t}(θ) = sg[s_i(θ)] · ( π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) / sg[ π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) ] ).

Because the ratio ( π_θ(y_{i,t}|...) / sg[π_θ(y_{i,t}|...)] ) evaluates to 1 numerically, s_{i,t}(θ) is numerically s_i(θ). The sg[·] denotes the stop-gradient (detach) operator.

## Why this is principled and stable

- Off-policy correction remains sequence-level. The single scalar s_i(θ) is the meaningful importance sampling correction for a whole-sequence reward. That preserves the theoretical grounding and the low-variance behavior that makes GSPO stable for long responses and MoE models.
- Token-level advantages are simply multipliers of the same sequence-level correction. You can upweight or downweight specific tokens (or turns) without reintroducing token-level importance ratios computed from single-token samples.
- Gradient flow is controlled: the construction of s_{i,t}(θ) detaches the per-token likelihood numerators so that you do not obtain independent importance-weight gradients per token. The effective gradient (see the paper) becomes
  E[ (1/G) ∑_i s_i(θ) · (1/|y_i|) ∑_t \widehat{A}_{i,t} ∇_θ log π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) ],
  i.e., the same s_i(θ) multiplies the average token log-gradient for that response.

This avoids the problematic behavior of GRPO, whose gradient is weighted by token-specific ratios w_{i,t}(θ) = π_θ(y_{i,t}|...)/π_{θ_old}(y_{i,t}|...), which vary independently across tokens and introduce large, cumulative variance and clipping interactions.

## When GSPO-token is the right tool

- Multi-turn RL: different turns or utterances within the same response may deserve different credit assignments. You can set token/turn-level advantages to reflect that structure (for example, higher advantage for tokens generated in later turns or for tokens in a particular segment).
- Mixed supervision: you may combine a sequence-level reward with localized token-level signals (e.g., token-level error indicators or heuristics). GSPO-token lets you apply these localized signals while keeping a stable global correction.
- Fine-grained curriculum or shaping: you might want to encourage a model to be better at certain token spans (e.g., factual statements, code correctness markers) without destabilizing whole-response optimization.

## When GSPO and GSPO-token are identical

If you assign the same advantage to every token in a response, i.e. \widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i for all t, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO. Both the numerical objective and the theoretical gradient coincide. Thus GSPO-token is a strict generalization: it adds flexibility without sacrificing GSPO’s stability when you do not need per-token granularity.

## Practical notes for implementation

- Compute s_i(θ) as in GSPO (length-normalized sequence likelihood ratio). Use that scalar both for sequence clipping and as the multiplier across tokens.
- Normalize token-level advantages within each group to keep scales stable (the paper uses group mean/std normalization for sequence advantages; you can analogously standardize \{\widehat{A}_{i,t}\} across tokens in the group).
- Ensure the detach/stop-gradient usage matches s_{i,t}(θ) construction so that per-token likelihood numerators do not introduce additional gradient paths for importance weights.
- Clipping ranges for s_i(θ) are different in magnitude from token-level PPO clipping: because s_i(θ) is length-normalized, choose ε consistent with the length-normalized scale. The paper reports much smaller numerical clipping ranges for sequence-normalized ratios versus token-level ratios.

## Takeaway

GSPO-token gives you per-token (or per-turn) advantage control while preserving the sequence-level importance correction that makes GSPO stable and efficient for long responses and MoE models. Use plain GSPO when a single sequence advantage suffices; switch to GSPO-token when you need principled, low-variance, per-token shaping of the learning signal.



# Scaling Gains in the Real World: What GSPO Delivers for Qwen3

## TL;DR
Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a simple algorithmic change with outsized practical effects. By moving importance weighting and clipping from the token level to the sequence level, GSPO stabilizes long-horizon RL training, improves sample efficiency under the same compute, and unlocks reliable RL for large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. These properties were instrumental in the sustained RL improvements seen in the Qwen3 line on difficult math and coding benchmarks.

## The practical problem GSPO targets
When you run RL at scale on modern language models you face three intertwined challenges:

- Long responses and large rollout batches force partitioning into mini-batches for optimization, producing an effectively off-policy setup.
- Token-level importance weights (as used in GRPO/PPO variants) are computed from single next-token samples and therefore inject high variance that accumulates over long sequences.
- Sparse models (MoE) add another instability source: expert-activation volatility — the set of activated experts for the same sample can change across updates, making token-level likelihoods fluctuate and invalidating token-level importance ratios.

These effects cause noisy gradients and, in the worst case, catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse during RL.

## The core idea of GSPO
GSPO fixes the mismatch by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward: treat the entire response as the optimization unit.

Concretely, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|},
i.e., a length-normalized ratio of sequence likelihoods, and applies clipping and advantage weighting at the sequence level:
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right)\right].

Key practical choices:
- Use group-based advantage normalization: advantages \widehat{A}_i are computed as standardized rewards across G responses to the same query.
- Length-normalize the sequence ratio (the 1/|y_i| exponent) to control variance and unify scaling across response lengths.

Why this matters: the sequence-level ratio has a clear importance-sampling interpretation and is far less sensitive to single-token sampling noise. GSPO weights token gradients uniformly within a response (through the sequence ratio), removing the unstable, per-token multiplicative factors that GRPO carries.

## What this changes in practice

Stable long-horizon training
- GSPO prevents the accumulation of high-variance noise across long sequences by avoiding noisy token-level importance weights. Empirically, training with GSPO proceeds stably when increasing generation length, updating query sets, and consuming more compute—conditions that previously triggered collapse with GRPO.

Better sample efficiency under the same compute
- Despite clipping entire responses (which can mean clipping a larger fraction of tokens compared to token-level clipping), GSPO yields higher training efficiency. The paper reports that under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO achieves better training rewards and stronger benchmark performance than a carefully tuned GRPO baseline.

Robust MoE RL without special hacks
- MoE models suffer expert-activation volatility: after parameter updates, the activated experts for the same sample can change, making token likelihoods unstable. GRPO required a Routing Replay strategy (replaying cached routing decisions) to stabilize training of MoE models. GSPO obviates Routing Replay: because it operates on sequence likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods, the sequence ratio is much less sensitive to expert-routing changes and therefore naturally stabilizes MoE RL without extra engineering, reducing memory and communication overhead.

Cleaner RL infrastructure
- Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it is more tolerant of precision mismatches between inference and training engines. This opens the door to using likelihoods directly from fast inference engines (e.g., vLLM-style backends) rather than recomputing token-level likelihoods on the training engine, simplifying pipelines for partial-rollout and disaggregated training-inference setups.

## Empirical story in the paper (high level)
The authors fine-tuned a cold-start model derived from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and compared GSPO to GRPO across training reward and downstream benchmarks: AIME'24 (math, Pass@1), LiveCodeBench (coding, Pass@1), and CodeForces (Elo rating). Highlights:

- GSPO runs stably throughout longer RL training and continues to improve when training compute, query updates, or generation length increase.
- Under the same compute and query consumption, GSPO achieves higher training reward and better benchmark results than tuned GRPO.
- GSPO enabled RL scaling for Qwen3 models in practice and removed the need for Routing Replay in MoE RL runs.

The paper also reports that the clipping ranges used are quite different in magnitude between sequence-level and token-level schemes (e.g., very small numeric clipping for GSPO after length-normalization vs. larger token-level clipping for GRPO), so raw clipping numbers are not directly comparable.

## Takeaways and implications for future RL scaling
GSPO is a minimal, theoretically grounded modification with outsized engineering and empirical benefits. Its main implications:

- Algorithmic alignment matters: match the importance-sampling unit to the reward unit. When rewards are sequence-level, do off-policy correction at the sequence level.
- Stability unlocks scale: removing token-level noise lets you push generation length, batch sizes, and compute without triggering collapse.
- Less engineering overhead: by stabilizing MoE RL intrinsically, GSPO reduces or eliminates the need for complex remedies (Routing Replay), lowering memory/comm costs and simplifying pipelines.
- Better infrastructure flexibility: sequence-only likelihoods are more robust to precision and engine differences, enabling simpler, faster rollout-inference approaches.

For projects that want to scale RL on large language models — especially sparse/MoE models and long-horizon tasks such as mathematical reasoning and coding — GSPO provides a practical path to safer, more efficient, and higher-performing training. The Qwen3 improvements reported in the paper illustrate that this is not just theoretical: the algorithmic change translated into real-world gains on challenging benchmarks.
