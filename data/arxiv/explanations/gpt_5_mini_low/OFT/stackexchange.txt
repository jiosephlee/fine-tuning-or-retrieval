\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does preserving hyperspherical energy actually guarantee preservation of pairwise angles?
Question:
In Section 3 (General Framework), hyperspherical energy for a layer with columns $\{\hat{\mathbf w}_i\}$ is defined as $\text{HE}(\mathbf W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$. The paper then argues that applying a shared orthogonal transform $\mathbf R$ to columns preserves HE and thus pairwise relationships. Since $\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta_{ij}$ on the unit sphere, preserving HE seems equivalent to preserving each $\cos\theta_{ij}$ (i.e., all pairwise angles). Is the implication strictly bidirectional? Could two different angle configurations yield the same sum $\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$ but different individual angles, or does orthogonality uniquely enforce angle-wise preservation beyond just matching the summed energy?
Answer:
Short answer: No — preserving the scalar hyperspherical energy value does not by itself guarantee preservation of every pairwise angle. Orthogonal transforms do preserve all pairwise angles, but equality of the single number $HE(W)$ for two configurations is far weaker than equality of the full matrix of pairwise cosines.

Why, intuitively and technically: hyperspherical energy as defined in the paper is a single scalar
$HE(W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},$
and on the unit sphere $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^2 = 2-2\cos\theta_{ij}$, so HE aggregates the pairwise angles via the map $\theta_{ij}\mapsto (2-2\cos\theta_{ij})^{-1/2}$ and then sums them. A single scalar sum cannot uniquely determine the full set of many pairwise angles except in degenerate, highly constrained cases. Counting degrees of freedom already shows this: for $n$ unit vectors in $d$ dimensions the number of independent pairwise angles (or cosines) is roughly $O(n^2)$, whereas HE is one scalar. Many different angle/geometry configurations can produce the same sum of reciprocals of chord lengths.

Consequence: two different configurations $\{\hat w_i\}$ and $\{\hat w'_i\}$ can have the same HE value but different individual $\cos\theta_{ij}$. Thus minimizing the difference $|HE(W)-HE(W^0)|$ to zero does not force $W$ to be equal to $W^0$ up to an orthogonal matrix; it only matches that one global statistic. Without an additional constraint, there is an enormous family of possible configurations with the same HE.

What the paper actually uses and proves: an orthogonal transformation $R$ applied to all columns, $W = R W^0$, preserves every pairwise inner product (and thus every $\cos\theta_{ij}$) exactly, and therefore preserves HE. So orthogonality implies preservation of the full pairwise-angle structure. But the converse—HE equality implying the existence of an orthogonal $R$ relating the two sets—is not true in general. That is why the paper does not just regularize HE to be equal, but parameterizes the update as a shared orthogonal matrix $R$ (OFT). That choice gives a provable, exact preservation of all pairwise angles (and of HE), whereas merely matching the scalar HE would not guarantee angle-wise preservation.

Practical upshot tied to the paper: optimizing HE alone is underconstrained and could allow destructive changes that keep the total energy the same. OFT enforces the stronger structural constraint (shared orthogonal $R$, optionally with a small deviation constraint in COFT) so that pairwise angles are truly preserved during finetuning, which explains the paper’s claim and why they prefer the orthogonal parameterization over an HE regularizer alone.

### Which vectors are considered “neurons” for a weight matrix and why is $\mathbf W = [\mathbf w_1,\dots,\mathbf w_n]$ left-multiplied by $\mathbf R$?
Question:
The paper treats the $i$-th neuron as column $\mathbf w_i \in \mathbb R^d$ and applies OFT as $\mathbf W \leftarrow \mathbf R\mathbf W^0$. Conceptually, in many deep learning libraries, a linear layer uses weight of shape $(\text{out},\text{in})$ and multiplies inputs on the right. Can you clarify the exact convention: are neurons outputs (columns) in your notation? Why is the orthogonal transform applied on the input dimension (left multiplication) rather than on the output dimension (right multiplication)? Would right-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve the relevant pairwise relations if the neuron definition were transposed?
Answer:
Short answer: in the paper each neuron in a layer is the column vector $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, those columns are the per-output weight vectors (so the layer computes $z = W^\top x$). An orthogonal transform $R$ is left-multiplied ($W \leftarrow R W^0$) because $R$ acts on the input coordinate system and therefore rotations/reflections of the columns exactly preserve all pairwise angles/cosines between neurons. Right-multiplying $W$ by an orthogonal matrix would instead mix the output coordinates (linear combinations of columns) and in general would not preserve the pairwise relations among the original column neurons unless you also change your neuron definition.

Longer explanation and intuition grounded in the paper

The paper defines a fully connected layer as $W = [w_1, \ldots, w_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with each $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ being the $i$-th neuron, and the forward pass $z = W^\top x$ where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $z \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Under that convention the neurons are the column vectors (each column is the linear functional producing one scalar output). The hyperspherical energy the authors want to preserve is a function of the pairwise angles/inner products between these column vectors: it depends on quantities like $\hat w_i^\top \hat w_j$ (or equivalently $\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|$).

Orthogonal matrices $R \in O(d)$ preserve inner products and norms in $\mathbb{R}^d$: for any $u,v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ we have $(R u)^\top (R v) = u^\top v$. Therefore when you replace $W^0$ by $R W^0$, each column is mapped to $R w_i^0$ and every pairwise inner product is preserved:
$$(R w_i^0)^\top (R w_j^0) = (w_i^0)^\top w_j^0.$$
Hence the hyperspherical energy (which is a function of normalized pairwise distances or cosines) is exactly invariant under a common left multiplication by any orthogonal $R$. This is the key mathematical reason the paper left-multiplies by $R$: it rotates the input/basis in which all neurons live while leaving the geometric relations (angles) among the neurons unchanged.

What would right-multiplication do? If you form $W' = W S$ with $S \in O(n)$ (an orthogonal on the output dimension), the new columns are linear combinations of the original columns: the $i$-th column of $W'$ is $W s_i$ where $s_i$ is the $i$-th column of $S$. Those new columns need not preserve inner products between original columns; in general $(W S)_i^\top (W S)_j = s_i^\top (W^\top W) s_j$, which equals the old inner products only in special cases (for example if $W^\top W$ is a scalar multiple of the identity, or if $S$ just permutes/sign-flips columns). So right-multiplication by an orthogonal on the output side does not guarantee invariance of pairwise neuron angles defined as columns.

There is a symmetry if you change the neuron definition. If instead you treated neurons as the rows of $W^\top$ (i.e. each neuron is a row vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$), then to preserve pairwise relations among those row-vectors you would apply orthogonals on the output axis (right-multiplication by an orthogonal in that alternative representation). In other words the action that preserves the geometry depends on which space (input space or output-index space) your “neurons” live in. The paper chooses the conventional neural-network reading where a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ maps $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to $n$ scalar outputs and treats the per-output weight vectors (columns) as neurons; therefore the natural orthogonal degrees of freedom that preserve column angles act on the input space and are implemented as left-multiplication by $R$.

How this matches common deep learning shapes (practical note)
Many libraries store a linear weight as shape (out, in) and compute $y = x W^\top$ (or $y = x\ \texttt{@}\ W^\top$), which is algebraically identical to $z = W^\top x$ in the paper. The paper’s $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ corresponds to (in, out) in that framework; its columns are the per-output weight vectors. The left-multiplication $R W^0$ in the paper is therefore an orthogonal transform on the input dimension (the same axis that $x$ lives in), which is exactly what you want to preserve the hyperspherical geometry of the per-output weight vectors.

Summary
Neurons in the paper are columns (output-weight vectors). Left-multiplication by $R$ acts on the input coordinates and preserves pairwise angles among those columns because orthogonals preserve inner products. Right-multiplying by an orthogonal would instead mix outputs (columns) and generally does not preserve the column-wise pairwise relations unless you redefine neurons to be the other axis or restrict $S$ to trivial permutations/sign flips.

### How is hyperspherical energy computed for convolutional layers?
Question:
Sections 3 and 6 mainly discuss fully connected layers. For a convolution with weight tensor of shape $(C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k_h, k_w)$, what exactly are the "neurons" whose directions and pairwise angles are preserved? Do you flatten each output filter into a length-$d$ vector with $d=C_{\text{in}}k_hk_w$ and treat those as columns? If OFT uses a block-diagonal $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$, how are blocks aligned with channels or spatial positions, and does this preserve HE across filters in a way comparable to the fully-connected case?
Answer:
Short answer: treat each output filter (convolution kernel) as a $d$-dimensional neuron by flattening its $C_{\text{in}}\times k_h\times k_w$ weights into a vector of length $d = C_{\text{in}}\cdot k_h\cdot k_w$, and apply the same orthogonal transform (or a block‑diagonal orthogonal transform) to those vectors. Any full orthogonal matrix $R$ acting on those $d$-vectors preserves all pairwise angles (and therefore hyperspherical energy) exactly; a block‑diagonal orthogonal $R$ is still an orthogonal matrix in the full $d$ space, so it likewise preserves hyperspherical energy (while trading off parameter cost and flexibility).

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper
The paper’s hyperspherical energy (HE) is defined on a set of neurons in a layer after normalization, e.g. for a fully connected layer $W = [w_1, \dots, w_n]$ with $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ the HE is $\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat w_i = w_i/\|w_i\|$. The OFT idea is: rather than changing those neuron directions arbitrarily, learn a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix $R$ and replace $W^0$ by $W = R W^0$. For fully connected layers this is stated explicitly in the paper; for conv layers the same principle applies if you view each output channel/filter as a neuron.

Concretely for a conv weight tensor of shape $(C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k_h, k_w)$ you form $n = C_{\text{out}}$ neurons by flattening each output filter into a column vector $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $d = C_{\text{in}}\cdot k_h\cdot k_w$. The forward pass still computes $z = W^\top x$ (with $x$ being a flattened input patch), and OFT replaces $W^0$ by $R W^0$ so that every filter is rotated by the same orthogonal $R$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and norms, they preserve all pairwise cosines/angles and hence HE exactly.

What about block‑diagonal $R$?
The paper uses a block‑diagonal parameterization $R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1, \dots, R_r)$ for efficiency. Two points to note: (1) any block‑diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks $R_i$ are orthogonal is itself an orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. As an orthogonal operator on the full flattened filter space it still preserves pairwise angles and therefore HE exactly. (2) the choice of how blocks align with channels / spatial positions is a design choice that controls inductive bias and parameter cost. The paper points out two natural interpretations:

- If you set $r = C_{\text{in}}$ (number of input channels), each block covers one input channel’s spatial kernel (block size = $k_h\cdot k_w$). Then $R_i$ rotates the spatial weights inside each input channel independently — analogous to learning depthwise transformations, and parameter‑efficient.
- If you instead set $r$ so that each block groups spatial positions across channels (or use shared blocks across channels), you get different coupling patterns: sharing the same block across channels applies the same orthogonal transform to every channel’s subvector (more coupling across channels), while more/smaller blocks give stronger regularization and less flexibility.

Trade‑offs and practical effect
Using a full $R$ ($r = 1$) gives maximum flexibility (but $O(d^2)$ parameters). Block‑diagonal $R$ reduces parameters (paper gives complexity reductions) and biases the transformation to act independently on subspaces you choose (channels, spatial locations, or other groupings). Importantly, any of these $R$ choices that are orthogonal preserve HE across filters in the same provable way as the fully connected case, because HE depends only on pairwise angles and orthogonal mappings preserve angles globally.

A small caveat: if you were to apply different orthogonal transforms to different filters (i.e., not layer‑shared), or apply non‑orthogonal blocks, then HE would not be globally preserved; the paper’s guarantee comes from using the same orthogonal $R$ (or a single orthogonal block‑diagonal $R$) shared across the neurons in the layer.

Extra note
Rescaling per‑neuron (the re‑scaled OFT variant) does not change HE because HE uses normalized directions; scaling factors alter magnitudes but not the normalized directions used in HE.

### Cayley transform only yields det=1 matrices—does excluding reflections matter for HE preservation?
Question:
Section 3.3 uses the Cayley parameterization $\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ with $\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$, which restricts $\mathbf R\in\text{SO}(d)$ (determinant $+1$). Since HE is invariant under any orthogonal transform (including reflections with determinant $-1$), is there any scenario where needing a reflection would materially improve optimization or expressivity? If not, can you provide intuition or empirical evidence that restricting to $\text{SO}(d)$ is sufficient for finetuning quality in these diffusion models?
Answer:
Short answer: Practically no — excluding reflections (determinant $-1$) via the Cayley parameterization does not meaningfully hurt OFT’s optimization or expressivity for the finetuning tasks in this paper. The authors explicitly note this limitation (Sec. 3.3) and report that it “does not affect the performance in practice.” Intuitively, the continuous rotations parameterized by $SO(d)$ are flexible enough to achieve the necessary neuron-direction changes for subject-driven and controllable generation, and the experiments (DreamBooth-style subject finetuning, multiple control tasks) show faster, more stable convergence and better final metrics than baselines even when Cayley ($SO(d)$) is used.

Why this is reasonable (intuition + technical points)
1. Hyperspherical energy (HE) invariance depends only on orthogonality: any orthogonal $R$ (in $O(d)$) preserves pairwise angles. The paper’s OFT objective is achieved whenever $W = R W^{0}$ for some orthogonal $R$ — whether $\det(R)=+1$ or $\det(R)=-1$ is irrelevant to the HE-preservation property itself. So reflections are not required by the mathematical invariant the method relies on.

2. $SO(d)$ is the identity-connected component of $O(d)$. Cayley gives continuous paths from the identity to any rotation. Finetuning initialized at identity benefits from smooth, small updates on that connected manifold. Many practical finetuning objectives are solved by continuous adjustments of neuron directions; rotations supply a rich family of continuous transformations that can approximate the needed directional changes. In other words, the subspace of rotations already provides sufficient continuous degrees of freedom to adjust semantic directions without needing a discrete sign‑flip (reflection).

3. Reflections are “discrete” relative to identity (they cannot be reached by a continuous curve entirely inside $SO(d)$), so if an optimal transform truly required an exact reflection relative to the pretrained weights, Cayley cannot reach it by continuous small steps. But in high-dimensional weight spaces, an exact reflection is rarely necessary: a rotation can typically achieve the same relative angle relationships among neurons (the quantities that matter for HE and for downstream behavior) or at least an arbitrarily close approximation, and the re-scaled OFT variant (which learns per-neuron magnitudes) further increases representational flexibility.

Empirical grounding from the paper
The paper states in Section 3.3 that Cayley only produces matrices with $\det=+1$ but that “such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice.” All main experiments (subject-driven generation, several controllable-generation tasks) use the Cayley parameterization (and block-diagonal variants) and demonstrate consistent empirical advantages: faster and more stable convergence, better subject and control fidelity, and improved quantitative metrics relative to baselines (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter). Those results are the direct empirical evidence that restricting to $SO(d)$ via Cayley was adequate for the studied finetuning problems.

When could reflections matter?
There is a conceptual caveat: if a downstream task truly required a global transform that is an exact reflection of the pretrained neuron basis (for example, flipping sign on an odd-dimensional invariant subspace where every small-rotation approximation would necessarily change some critical pairwise angles), then Cayley-parameterized $SO(d)$ would miss that exact transform. Such cases would be corner-case and discrete (you’d need the transform to differ from identity by an odd number of sign flips that cannot be approximated by rotations without altering important angle relations). The paper does not find such cases in their image-generation finetuning tasks; if you suspect such pathology in a different application, you could augment parameterization (e.g., allow one learned diagonal sign flip, or parametrically multiply by a fixed reflection) to add that discrete degree of freedom.

Practical takeaway
For text-to-image diffusion finetuning as studied in the paper, restricting to $SO(d)$ via the Cayley transform is empirically sufficient and has advantages: it yields a smooth manifold for optimization (helping stability and convergence), preserves HE as intended, and reduces implementation complexity (Cayley gives an efficient differentiable orthogonalization). If you encounter a task where you have reason to believe an exact reflection is necessary, you can test adding a single learned reflection factor (or allow $O(d)$ parameterizations), but for the paper’s domains the $SO(d)$ choice proved adequate.

### What matrix norm does COFT use for the constraint $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\epsilon$, and how does it control angle change?
Question:
COFT introduces $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\epsilon$ but does not state whether this is the spectral norm, Frobenius norm, or something else. Different norms bound different geometric quantities. For small $\epsilon$, can you relate this norm bound to a bound on the maximum principal angle between $\mathbf R$-rotated and original neuron directions? If the implementation constrains $\|\mathbf Q\|\le\epsilon'$ (post Neumann approximation), which norm is used for $\mathbf Q$, and how does it translate into a concrete bound on per-neuron angular deviations?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s math and the Neumann-series argument strongly point to the operator (spectral) norm being the intended meaning of "$\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon$". Interpreting the constraint that way gives a very simple and tight control on how much any single neuron direction (unit vector) can rotate: for any unit neuron vector $u$ the angle $\theta$ between $u$ and $Ru$ satisfies
$$
\|Ru-u\| \le \|R-I\|_{2} \le \epsilon
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\theta \le 2\arcsin\!\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big),
$$
and for small $\epsilon$ this is approximately $\theta \approx \epsilon$.

Why the operator norm is the natural reading here
- In Section 3.3 the authors write the Neumann expansion and explicitly say it converges “in the operator norm”; that is a direct clue that they are thinking in terms of the spectral/operator norm (largest singular value) when talking about matrix perturbation size.  
- The operator norm gives the tight uniform bound $\| (R-I)u\| \le \|R-I\|_{2}$ valid for every unit vector $u$, which directly controls worst-case per-neuron displacement and therefore the angle between a neuron and its rotated version. Frobenius or other norms do not give that per-vector worst-case bound without an extra dimension-dependent factor.

Derivation and intuition (concise)
For any two unit vectors $x$ and $y$, the chord length is related to the angle between them: $\|x-y\|=2\sin(\theta/2)$, where $\theta=\angle(x,y)$. Take $x=u$ and $y=Ru$ (both unit length because $R$ is orthogonal). Then
$$
2\sin\!\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)=\|Ru-u\|\le \|R-I\|_{2}\le \epsilon,
$$
so
$$
\theta \le 2\arcsin\!\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big).
$$
For small $\epsilon$, $\arcsin(\epsilon/2)\approx \epsilon/2$, hence $\theta\approx\epsilon$. This gives a direct and interpretable per-neuron angular bound.

What about the Cayley/Neumann approximation and the constraint on Q?
The paper parameterizes $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew-symmetric and then uses the Neumann-series approximation $R\approx I+2Q + O(Q^2)$ (they discuss convergence in operator norm). Moving the constraint inside the Cayley map they replace $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ by $\|Q\|\le\epsilon'$. Interpreting “$\|\cdot\|$” consistently as the operator norm again yields the simplest correspondence. Under the first-order approximation $R\approx I+2Q$ one gets $\|R-I\|_{2}\approx 2\|Q\|_{2}$, so the per-neuron angle bound becomes
$$
\theta \lesssim 2\arcsin\!\Big(\frac{2\|Q\|_{2}}{2}\Big)=2\arcsin(\|Q\|_{2}),
$$
and for small $\|Q\|_{2}$ this is $\theta\approx 2\|Q\|_{2}$. Thus constraining $\|Q\|_{2}\le\epsilon'$ roughly enforces per-neuron angular deviations $\lesssim 2\epsilon'$ (in radians) to first order.

If instead someone used Frobenius norm in the projection step
The paper does not explicitly say which norm the projected gradient step uses. If an implementation projects $Q$ by Frobenius norm (common in practice because projection is simple), you only have $\|Q\|_{2}\le\|Q\|_{F}$, so $\|Q\|_{F}\le\epsilon'$ implies $\|Q\|_{2}\le\epsilon'$ as well (so the same angle bounds still hold), but the reverse is not true: a spectral-norm ball is a strictly smaller (stronger) constraint than a Frobenius ball of the same radius. If the implementation instead enforces spectral-norm projection, then the geometric per-neuron angle guarantees above are exactly what you get.

Practical takeaway
Read the paper’s “operator norm / Neumann series” comment as evidence they intended the spectral/operator norm. With that choice you obtain the clean worst-case per-unit-vector angle bound $\theta\le 2\arcsin(\epsilon/2)$ for $\|R-I\|_{2}\le\epsilon$, and under the Cayley/Neumann approximation $\|Q\|_{2}\le\epsilon'$ gives roughly $\theta\lesssim 2\epsilon'$ for small $\epsilon'$. If you want a tight, interpretable per-neuron angular control in code, project $Q$ in the spectral norm (or pick $\epsilon'$ conservatively if you project in Frobenius norm).

### Validity of the Neumann series approximation used in COFT
Question:
Section 3.4 approximates $\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\approx \mathbf I + 2\mathbf Q + \mathcal O(\mathbf Q^2)$ using a Neumann series, then moves the constraint onto $\mathbf Q$. Under what conditions on $\|\mathbf Q\|$ (and which operator norm) does this approximation hold with a controlled error? During training, how do you ensure $\|\mathbf Q\|<1$ (or the relevant radius) so that the Neumann series converges and the constraint on $\mathbf Q$ is a faithful proxy for the original constraint on $\mathbf R$?
Answer:
Short answer: the Cayley map
$R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$
has a convergent Neumann expansion for $(I - Q)^{-1}$ whenever the spectral radius of $Q$ is $< 1$; in practice you should control the operator (matrix) norm of $Q$ so that $\|Q\| < 1$ (and preferably much smaller than 1) in a consistent induced norm such as the spectral norm (operator 2‑norm). Under that condition the expansion gives
$R = I + 2Q + 2 \sum_{k=2}^\infty Q^k$,
so truncating at $I + 2Q$ produces a remainder with the explicit bound
$\|R - (I + 2Q)\| \le 2 \sum_{k=2}^\infty \|Q\|^k = \dfrac{2 \|Q\|^2}{1 - \|Q\|}$,
valid for any submultiplicative operator norm and in particular for the spectral norm when $\|Q\|_2 < 1$.

Why the spectral norm is natural here: $Q$ is skew‑symmetric in the paper ($Q = -Q^T$), so its eigenvalues are pure imaginary and the spectral radius equals the largest magnitude of those imaginary parts; the spectral norm (operator 2‑norm) tightly controls powers $Q^k$ because $\|Q^k\|_2 = \|Q\|_2^k$, which makes the Neumann-series error bound simple and sharp.

How to ensure this during training so the Neumann approximation is a faithful proxy for the constraint on $R$: initialize $Q = 0$ (so $R = I$ initially), keep $Q$ skew‑symmetric by parameterizing $Q = A - A^T$ ($A$ unconstrained), and explicitly enforce a norm bound on $Q$ after each gradient step. Practical enforcement options are simple and cheap:

1) Projected gradient step: after the gradient update form $Q := A - A^T$, compute its spectral norm $\|Q\|_2$ (or an upper bound via power iteration) and, if $\|Q\|_2 > \delta$, rescale $Q \leftarrow (\delta / \|Q\|_2) Q$ with chosen $\delta < 1$. This keeps $Q$ inside the spectral‑norm ball where the Neumann series converges and gives a controlled remainder by the formula above.

2) Spectral normalization style: parameterize a scaling factor $s$ and normalize $Q$ by $Q \leftarrow s Q / \max(1, \|Q\|_2 / \delta)$, which is the same effect as projection but can be implemented as a differentiable layer.

3) Penalty: add a soft penalty $\lambda \max(0, \|Q\|_2 - \delta)^2$ to the training loss to discourage leaving the ball (less strict, but simpler if exact projection is inconvenient).

Choose $\delta$ based on the desired error bound. For example, $\delta = 0.1$ gives $\|R - (I + 2Q)\| \le 2(0.1)^2/(1 - 0.1) \approx 0.022$; $\delta = 0.3$ yields remainder $\le 2(0.3)^2/(0.7) \approx 0.257$. If you want the linear approximation $R \approx I + 2Q$ to be accurate to, say, $10^{-2}$ in spectral norm, pick $\delta$ so that $2 \delta^2 /(1 - \delta) \le 10^{-2}$.

Two practical notes tied to the paper. First, because the COFT constraint is expressed as $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$ but the Cayley parameterization is in $Q$, using the Neumann expansion to move the radius constraint inside implicitly assumes the regime above; so enforcing a spectral‑norm bound on $Q$ (as the paper suggests) is the correct way to make the proxy faithful. Second, the block‑diagonal parametrization used in the paper makes computing (or approximating) $\|Q\|_2$ cheaper (compute per‑block norms and combine), and keeping per‑block bounds can be even more stable.

In summary: require $\rho(Q) < 1$ (operationally enforce $\|Q\|_2 < \delta < 1$), use the explicit remainder bound $2\|Q\|^2/(1-\|Q\|)$ to choose $\delta$ for the desired approximation accuracy, and enforce the constraint by projection/spectral normalization or a penalty during training. This makes the linearized Cayley approximation controlled and makes the constraint on $Q$ a faithful proxy for the original constraint on $R$.

### Does preserving HE imply preserving cosine similarities, or only a weighted function of them?
Question:
HE is defined as $\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$ with $\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|=\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}$. An orthogonal transform preserves each $\cos\theta_{ij}$, but the OFT principle is motivated via preserving HE as a scalar. Is the theoretical guarantee you rely on “orthogonality preserves all pairwise angles” (strong) or “minimizing HE difference drives small average angular distortions” (weaker)? If the latter, why does minimizing only the summed inverse distance suffice, given it might hide compensating changes across pairs?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper relies on the strong guarantee that an orthogonal layerwise transform exactly preserves every pairwise angle. Minimizing the scalar hyperspherical energy (HE) alone is a strictly weaker objective — many different angular configurations can give the same HE — so HE-minimization by itself does not guarantee small per-pair angular distortions. OFT uses orthogonality (and optionally the small-deviation COFT constraint) to obtain the strong per-pair preservation.

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper. HE is defined (for normalized neurons) as
$$
\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1},\qquad
\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|=\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}.
$$
Because each pairwise Euclidean chord length on the unit sphere is a deterministic function of the cosine of the angle, an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ (i.e. $\mathbf R^\top\mathbf R=\mathbf I$) maps normalized neurons to normalized neurons while preserving every angle: for every pair $(i,j)$,
$$
\cos\theta_{ij}\;=\;\hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top\hat{\mathbf w}_j
\;=\;(\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_i)^\top(\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_j).
$$
Thus an orthogonal transform gives an exact, elementwise invariance of the full pairwise cosine matrix and therefore of HE as a scalar. This is the strong theoretical guarantee OFT exploits: by parameterizing finetuning as a layer-shared orthogonal transform (Eq. (3) in the paper), the method provably preserves the entire pattern of pairwise angles.

Why then introduce HE in the first place? The authors use HE as a structural measure to motivate the desideratum of preserving pairwise neuron relations, and to argue that the attainable minimizer of the HE-difference objective (Eq. (1)) is achieved when the finetuned weights differ from pretrained ones by an orthogonal transform. But note the distinction: minimizing the scalar difference
$$
\big\|\text{HE}(\mathbf W)-\text{HE}(\mathbf W^0)\big\|
$$
is only a global constraint on that one number. Many different angle matrices can produce the same HE value (because HE sums a nonlinear function of all pairwise angles); thus equality of HE as a scalar does not imply equality of each $\cos\theta_{ij}$. In other words, HE equality is necessary (for orthogonality it happens), but not sufficient to conclude pairwise angle equality in general. You can easily imagine compensating changes across pairs (some angles increase, others decrease) that leave the sum of inverse chord lengths unchanged.

This is exactly why OFT does not rely on unconstrained scalar HE-minimization as the finetuning rule. Instead, the paper enforces the orthogonality structure directly (via Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal approximations) so that the strong, elementwise angle-preservation property holds by construction. COFT further constrains the orthogonal transform to be near the identity ($\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\epsilon$), which explicitly prevents large collective rotations and reduces degrees of freedom, improving stability and ensuring the finetuned model remains close in Euclidean sense as well.

A few concrete implications to keep in mind. First, re-scaling of neuron magnitudes does not change HE (normalization removes magnitudes), so a method allowed to rescale neurons could preserve HE while strongly changing magnitudes — OFT addresses this by operating multiplicatively via orthogonal matrices (and optionally adding a learnable diagonal scaling only when desired, see re-scaled OFT). Second, a pure optimization that only minimizes the HE scalar could converge to a configuration that preserves the HE but distorts many individual angles; that is why the paper chooses the orthogonality parameterization rather than solving the scalar HE-matching optimization directly.

In summary: the motivating quantity is HE, but the theoretical guarantee used in OFT is the strong fact that orthogonal transforms preserve all pairwise cosines. Minimizing only the summed inverse-distance HE is a weaker statement and can hide compensating changes; OFT avoids that weakness by enforcing orthogonality (and optionally a small-deviation constraint) so per-pair angular structure is preserved.

### How does OFT interact with attention: which matrices get transformed and why left-only?
Question:
For Transformer attention layers, LoRA often targets $\mathbf W_Q,\mathbf W_K,\mathbf W_V$ (and sometimes $\mathbf W_O$). In OFT, do you apply a separate $\mathbf R$ to each of these weight matrices, and are they all left-multiplied as $\mathbf R\mathbf W^0$? Since left-multiplication rotates the input feature subspace, is there any mismatch when $\mathbf W_Q$ and $\mathbf W_K$ are expected to maintain compatible geometries (e.g., for dot-product attention stability)? Would using the same $\mathbf R$ across $Q$ and $K$ be beneficial or harmful?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT is implemented as a left-multiplicative, layer-shared orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weight matrix, i.e. the finetuned weight is $W = R W^0$ (with $R$ initialized to $I$). In practice you apply an $R$ separately to each weight matrix you finetune (e.g. $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$, $W_O$) unless you deliberately tie them. The reason OFT is left-only is that OFT is designed to rotate neuron direction (the row / input-feature basis of the weight matrix) so that the pairwise angles among neurons (columns of $W$, as the paper frames neurons) are preserved; left-multiplication is the natural way to realize that while keeping the hyperspherical-energy invariance proved in the paper.

Why left-multiplication, what it preserves, and the Q/K concern (intuitive + math). OFT’s invariance statement concerns pairwise angles among neurons in the same weight matrix: if $W = R W^0$ with orthogonal $R$, then the normalized columns of $W$ have the same pairwise cosines as those of $W^0$, because orthogonal $R$ preserves angles. This is exactly the property the paper uses to argue that semantic structure is preserved.

For attention, dot-product scores use both Q and K. Let $W_Q$ and $W_K$ be the pretrained query and key weight matrices. With OFT applied you get $W_Q = R_Q W_Q^0$ and $W_K = R_K W_K^0$. A canonical attention score between input vectors $x$ and $x'$ (ignoring scaling and heads) is
$\mathrm{score} = (W_Q^T x) \cdot (W_K^T x') = x^T W_Q W_K^T x'$.
If you apply the same orthogonal $R$ to both Q and K ($R_Q = R_K = R$), then
$W_Q W_K^T = (R W_Q^0)(R W_K^0)^T = R W_Q^0 (W_K^0)^T R^T$.
So attention scores become $x^T R [W_Q^0 (W_K^0)^T] R^T x'$. Because $R$ is orthogonal, $R^T$ is its inverse, but note the inputs $x,x'$ have not been rotated. Thus the score is not invariant in general: left-multiplying both Q and K by the same $R$ conjugates the cross-term $W_Q^0 (W_K^0)^T$ by $R$ and $R^T$ and will change scores unless $x$ and $x'$ are also transformed. What OFT guarantees is preservation of intramatrix neuron-angle structure (columns within $W_Q$ and within $W_K$), not necessarily preservation of pairwise geometry across two different matrices.

Practical implications and recommended choices. Because attention relies on a compatible geometry between queries and keys, two failure modes are plausible: (1) applying very different orthogonal transforms to $W_Q$ and $W_K$ can alter the relative geometry between the query and key subspaces and destabilize dot-product magnitudes or their alignment; (2) applying the same $R$ to both reduces that risk because you are changing the input-basis in a shared way, so the relative orientation between query- and key-neurons is more likely to be preserved (intuitively you are rotating the common input basis both heads “see”). Therefore, tying $R$ across Q and K in the same attention block is a safe, often beneficial choice when you care about attention stability. Tying $R$ reduces degrees of freedom (stronger regularization) and helps preserve Q–K compatibility; tying to a single $R$ for $\{Q,K,V,O\}$ is also possible but more restrictive and may reduce flexibility where different heads or projections legitimately need different directional changes.

On the other hand, allowing separate $R$ for Q and K gives more expressiveness: if the downstream task truly requires changing query geometry independently from key geometry, separate $R$'s might help. The paper’s philosophy emphasizes preserving hyperspherical energy and limiting deviation from pretrained structure (COFT), so it favors regularity over unconstrained expressiveness. In the experiments the paper says OFT is applied to the attention weights in order to compare fairly to LoRA, but does not state an explicit per-matrix tying pattern; the framework supports per-layer (i.e. per-weight-matrix) $R$ and also supports block-diagonal sharing to reduce parameters. Given the paper’s stated goals (stability, preservation of pretrained generative ability), the conservative recommendation is to share $R$ across Q and K within the same attention layer (or at least to experiment with tying Q and K first), and to use COFT’s $\epsilon$ constraint or small block sizes if you need extra stability. If you need additional flexibility, use separate $R$’s or re-scaled OFT ($R$ plus diagonal $D$) to permit controlled magnitude changes without breaking hyperspherical invariance.

Summary recommendation: implement OFT as left-multiplication $W = R W^0$ for each finetuned weight matrix; to preserve attention geometry and stability, tie $R$ for $W_Q$ and $W_K$ in the same attention block (or at minimum try that first). Separate $R$’s can be used if task requires more flexibility, but expect to monitor attention score statistics and use COFT ($\epsilon$) to limit deviation if instability appears.

### Reconciling parameter complexity claims for OFT vs LoRA when $n\neq d$
Question:
Section 3.3 compares trainable parameter counts, stating LoRA has $r'(d+n)$ while OFT with $r$ blocks has roughly $\mathcal O(d^2/r)$. The later claim that OFT becomes $\mathcal O(d)$ when $r=\alpha d$ seems to assume $r$ scales with $d$. In practice for attention layers, $n$ (number of neurons/heads/out-dim) may differ from $d$ (in-dim). Could you provide a more explicit comparison for a typical SD v1.5 attention layer shape (e.g., $d=768$, $n=768$ per head projection or similar), and clarify regimes (values of $r, r'$) where OFT is strictly more parameter-efficient than LoRA?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT’s parameter count falls as you increase the number of orthogonal blocks $r$, while LoRA’s grows linearly with the chosen low-rank $r'$. For the concrete Stable Diffusion–style example you asked ($d = 768$, $n = 768$) the crossover point is modest: with LoRA rank $r' = 8$, OFT becomes smaller once $r \gtrsim 24$ blocks. Below I show the algebra, explicit numbers, and the intuitive regime interpretation so you can decide which method is more parameter-efficient in practice.

Derivation and formulae (grounded in the paper)
LoRA (as stated in the paper) uses $r'$ and has trainable parameters
  $P_{\text{LoRA}} = r' (d + n)$.

OFT parameterizes a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks of size $d/r$ each. The paper gives the number of free parameters for that $R$ as
  $P_{\text{OFT}} = \dfrac{d\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2}$,
which is $\approx d^2/(2 r)$ for large $d$ (dominant term).

To compare, solve $P_{\text{OFT}} \le P_{\text{LoRA}}$:
  $\dfrac{d\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2} \le r' (d + n)$
which we can approximate by the dominant term
  $\dfrac{d^2}{2 r} \le r'(d + n)$
so roughly
  $r \ge \dfrac{d^2}{2 r' (d + n)}$.

Concrete Stable Diffusion example: $d = 768$, $n = 768$
Plugging numbers into the exact formulas:

LoRA:
- $P_{\text{LoRA}} = r' (768 + 768) = r' \times 1536$.
  Examples: $r' = 4 \to 6{,}144$; $r' = 8 \to 12{,}288$; $r' = 16 \to 24{,}576$; $r' = 32 \to 49{,}152$.

OFT:
- $P_{\text{OFT}} = \dfrac{768\bigl(768/r - 1\bigr)}{2}$.
  Compute common $r$ choices:
   $r = 1  \to 294{,}528$
   $r = 2  \to 147{,}072$
   $r = 4  \to 73{,}344$
   $r = 8  \to 36{,}480$
   $r = 16 \to 18{,}048$
   $r = 32 \to 8{,}832$
   $r = 64 \to 4{,}224$
   $r = 128\to 1{,}920$
   $r = 256\to 768$

Crossover (numerical)
Using the approximate inequality above for $d = n = 768$:
  $r \gtrsim \dfrac{768^2}{2 r' \times 1536} = \dfrac{589{,}824}{3072\, r'} = \dfrac{192}{r'}$.

Thus:
- If $r' = 4 \to$ need $r \gtrsim 48$ for OFT to be smaller than LoRA.
- If $r' = 8 \to$ need $r \gtrsim 24$.
- If $r' = 16 \to$ need $r \gtrsim 12$.
- If $r' = 32 \to$ need $r \gtrsim 6$.

Check with numbers: for $r' = 8$, LoRA uses $12{,}288$ params; OFT at $r = 24$ (interpolating between $r=16$ and $r=32$) is $\approx$ between $18{,}048$ and $8{,}832$ so indeed $r \approx 24$ is about the break-even.

Practical interpretation and trade-offs
Three facts you should keep in mind when deciding which is more parameter-efficient in practice:

1) Dependence on $n$ matters. LoRA’s cost includes $(d + n)$. If the output dimension $n$ is larger than $d$ (e.g., some MLP layers or expanded projection sizes), LoRA becomes relatively more expensive and OFT’s $d^2/(2r)$ scaling becomes comparatively better. Conversely, if $n \ll d$, LoRA is cheaper for the same rank choices.

2) OFT’s $r$ is the number of orthogonal blocks (higher $r = $ smaller block size = fewer parameters). Increasing $r$ reduces OFT’s parameter cost as $O(d^2/r)$. But larger $r$ also reduces the expressive power of $R$ (more, smaller independent rotations), so there is a flexibility vs regularization trade-off. The paper demonstrates good results with moderate $r$ choices — not necessarily $r \propto d$.

3) LoRA’s $r'$ controls rank of an additive update. Small $r'$ (4–16) is common and gives very small parameter counts. For typical LoRA ranks, OFT must use a fairly large number of blocks to beat LoRA in raw parameter count (see table above). For example with LoRA $r' = 8$, OFT must use $r \gtrsim 24$ blocks to be strictly smaller in parameters.

Other practical knobs mentioned in the paper
The paper also notes two ways OFT can further reduce parameters (at the cost of architectural bias): share the block matrix across blocks (they claim parameter complexity can become $O(d^2 / r^2)$ in that shared-block extreme) or increase $r$ (more blocks). Shared blocks can dramatically lower $P_{\text{OFT}}$ — for $d = 768$ and $r = 32$, sharing a single $24\times 24$ orthogonal block would make parameters $\approx (24\cdot 23)/2 = 276$, which is tiny — but that is a much stronger inductive bias and may or may not work for your layer.

Bottom-line guidance
If you are comparing typical LoRA ranks used in practice ($r' = 4$–$16$) and you want OFT to be strictly more parameter-efficient, pick $r$ (number of OFT blocks) so that
  $r \gtrsim \dfrac{d^2}{2 r' (d + n)}$,
which for $d = n = 768$ becomes $r \gtrsim 192/r'$. Concretely, for LoRA $r' = 8$ you need about $r \ge 24$. If you can afford that many blocks (i.e., accept the blockwise constraint), OFT will beat LoRA in parameter count; if you insist on few blocks (small $r$) then LoRA at modest $r'$ will usually be smaller.

Finally, remember that parameter count is only one axis: OFT and LoRA impose different structural biases (orthogonal multiplicative transforms vs low-rank additive updates). The paper’s experiments show OFT/COFT can produce better convergence and stability even when parameter counts are comparable, so also consider empirical performance, not just raw parameter efficiency.

### Practical stability of the Cayley inversion: how is $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ computed and regularized?
Question:
The Cayley transform requires inverting $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)$ at every update. Numerically, near-singularities can occur if eigenvalues of $\mathbf Q$ approach $1$ in magnitude on the imaginary axis. Do you enforce explicit bounds on $\|\mathbf Q\|$ or use damping (e.g., $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q+\delta\mathbf I)^{-1}$) to stabilize gradients? How do you implement this efficiently on GPU within large models without introducing significant training-time overhead?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper computes the Cayley inverse $(I - Q)^{-1}$ by working with a skew‑symmetric $Q$ that is block‑diagonal, and stabilizes finetuning by constraining the norm of $Q$ (COFT) and using projected gradient steps on $Q$. Practically this means (1) $Q$ is kept skew‑symmetric by construction, (2) $Q$ is restricted to be small (so the Neumann series for $(I - Q)$ converges and $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$), and (3) the block‑diagonal structure lets you invert many small matrices on the GPU cheaply (batched linear solves). The paper flags the matrix inverse as a scalability/efficiency limitation and uses the block trick and projection to make it practical.

Here is a compact, implementation‑oriented explanation (intuitive + technical) grounded in the paper:

1) Parameterization and skew‑symmetry
The Cayley map used is
$R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$, with $Q = -Q^T$.
A convenient and numerically safe way to implement this is to parametrize an unconstrained matrix $A$ and set $Q = (A - A^T)/2$. That guarantees skew‑symmetry exactly (no extra constraints needed).

2) Avoiding near‑singularity: keep Q small
The danger you mentioned (eigenvalues of $Q$ approaching $1$ on the imaginary axis so $I - Q$ becomes near‑singular) is addressed in the paper by explicitly constraining the size of $Q$ in COFT. They note the Neumann expansion $R \approx I + 2Q + O(Q^2)$ (which assumes $\|Q\| < 1$ in operator norm), and therefore enforce a constraint of the form $\|Q\| \le \varepsilon'$ by projected gradient descent on $Q$. In practice you can implement that projection in a few ways that are inexpensive:
- Project the Frobenius norm: if $\|Q\|_F > \tau$, scale $Q \leftarrow \tau Q / \|Q\|_F$. This is inexpensive and preserves skew‑symmetry.
- Project the operator norm approximately by scaling via power iteration estimate of the largest singular value (a couple of iterations), then scale $Q$ by $\min(1, \tau/\sigma_{\max})$.
Either projection keeps $Q$ away from the dangerous regime and is exactly what COFT advocates (they convert the $R - I$ constraint to a $Q$ constraint via the Neumann expansion).

3) Efficient computation of $(I - Q)^{-1}$ on GPU
Two practical hardware‑friendly choices are suggested by the paper:
- Block‑diagonal $Q$: represent $Q$ as $\operatorname{diag}(Q_1,\dots,Q_r)$. Then $(I - Q)$ becomes block‑diagonal and you only invert small blocks of size $(d/r) \times (d/r)$. On modern GPUs you can perform these inverses/linear solves in a batched way (e.g., torch.linalg.solve or torch.linalg.inv on a batch of small matrices). Batched small solves are highly optimized and much cheaper than inverting one large $d\times d$ matrix.
- Batched linear solve vs explicit inverse: prefer solving $(I - Q) X = (I + Q)$ (or solving $(I - Q)Y = I$ to obtain $(I - Q)^{-1}$) via a batched linear solver; solving is numerically more stable than forming an explicit inverse and is fully supported on GPU with little overhead.

4) Extra practical stabilizers (consistent with paper’s spirit)
The paper does not recommend changing the Cayley formula itself, but in practice people sometimes add a tiny damping $(I - Q + \delta I)^{-1}$ to improve conditioning. Note such a damping means $R$ is no longer strictly orthogonal; COFT prefers to keep orthogonality and instead control $\|Q\|$. If you need further robustness in practice, use a very small $\delta$ and be aware it breaks exact orthogonality (tradeoff between numerical stability and strict group membership). The paper’s preferred approach is to keep $Q$ small via projection (so $\delta$ is not necessary).

5) Cheap orthogonality enforcement and GPU cost
- Construct $Q$ from $A$ ($Q = (A - A^T)/2$) — cheap elementwise ops.
- Keep $Q$ block‑diagonal — this reduces parameter count and makes batched solves small and fast.
- Batched solves for many small blocks cost $O\big(r \times (d/r)^3\big)$ total (i.e., $O(d^3 / r^2)$ for naive dense solve), but for typical block sizes used in the paper these are small matrices (e.g., $8\times 8$, $16\times 16$, $32\times 32$) and are very fast on GPU; in experiments the authors report this is practical and the main scaling concern is why they list the matrix inverse as an open problem.
- The projection step and forming $Q$ are negligible compared to the rest of a large diffusion model (attention, convs, etc.), so training‑time overhead is modest if you keep blocks small.

6) Summary recipe you can implement quickly
Parametrize $A$ (trainable); set $Q = (A - A^T)/2$; split $Q$ into blocks (or share blocks); after each gradient step project $Q$ to satisfy $\|Q\| \le \tau$ (Frobenius or approximate operator norm); compute $R$ via solving $(I - Q)X = I + Q$ with a batched linear solver (or directly solve $Z = (I - Q)^{-1}(\text{something})$ in the forward pass without materializing the inverse). Use small blocks so the batched solve is cheap, and avoid adding large $\delta$ unless you accept slight loss of orthogonality.

Connection to the paper’s statements: this procedure is exactly what the paper proposes for COFT (constrain $\|R - I\|$ by working through $Q$ and using projected gradient descent), and the paper explicitly calls out that the Cayley inverse is the main scalability bottleneck and that block‑diagonalization is their practical fix. They also note the Neumann series view ($R \approx I + 2Q$) which justifies constraining $Q$ directly.

If you want, I can sketch minimal PyTorch pseudocode (A→Q projection→batched solve) showing exactly how to implement the batched block inverses and projection with low overhead.

### If OFT preserves angles, why introduce re-scaled OFT with a diagonal $\mathbf D$?
Question:
Section 3.5 adds a learnable diagonal scaling $\mathbf D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ in $\mathbf z=(\mathbf R\mathbf W^0\mathbf D)^\top\mathbf x$, arguing that re-scaling neurons does not change HE. If directions (angles) carry most semantic information, what empirical phenomena require magnitude changes for better finetuning? Also, is constraining $s_i>0$ important (to avoid sign flips that could mimic reflections not in $\text{SO}(d)$)?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (hence hyperspherical energy), but real networks are not purely “angle-only” systems in practice. Adding a small per-neuron magnitude factor gives a cheap, targeted degree of freedom that fixes mismatch in activation scale / calibration / downstream interactions and empirically improves finetuning. Constraining each scaling scalar to be positive is important: a negative scalar flips the sign ($180^\circ$ rotation) of a neuron, which is not a harmless scaling and would reintroduce reflection-like degrees of freedom that break the $SO(d)$ / angle-preservation intent and can destabilize finetuning — so the paper enforces $s_i > 0$.

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper
The paper’s motivation for OFT is that directions (angles) among neurons capture much of the semantic information, and orthogonal transforms preserve those angles (hence HE). However, real model behavior depends not only on normalized directions but also on magnitudes and the interaction of those magnitudes with layer statistics, normalization layers, attention scaling, residual paths, and the next layers’ parameterizations. Multiplying a neuron by a positive scalar does not change hyperspherical energy (because HE uses normalized neurons), but it does change the actual forward activations $z = W^\top x$ and therefore can alter: the absolute activation scale in subsequent nonlinearities, relative contribution of neurons when later weights are not re-normalized, and optimizer dynamics during finetuning.

Concretely, small per-neuron scales allow the finetuned model to (1) adjust activation magnitudes to match pretrained activation distributions after applying $R$, (2) compensate for layernorm/scale interactions or for attention query/key/value magnitude needs, and (3) give a tiny amount of extra flexibility so OFT can better fit downstream signals (prompt fidelity or control supervision) without changing neuron angles. The authors report that “re-scaled OFT is generally better” (see Appendix) while keeping the parameter cost negligible, so empirically those magnitude knobs improve convergence and final performance beyond pure orthogonal rotation.

Why positivity ($s_i > 0$) matters
Allowing negative $s_i$ changes more than magnitude: it flips the vector direction (multiplication by a negative scalar gives the opposite unit vector), which changes pairwise angles in a substantive way (a $180^\circ$ flip is not a small continuous rotation) and can mimic reflections. The paper notes that Cayley parameterization they use restricts $R$ to $\det(R)=+1$ ($SO(d)$), i.e. rotations (no reflections). If you let $D$ include negative entries, you inject sign-flips that can emulate reflections or large angular flips that the OFT orthogonal part was explicitly chosen not to introduce. That undermines the guarantee that HE is preserved up to orthogonal transforms in the intended sense and can reintroduce instability or overly large semantic changes. So constraining $s_i > 0$ keeps $D$ as a pure magnitude/scaling layer that leaves normalized directions unchanged, preserves the spirit of OFT/COFT, and prevents sign-flip reflections from sneaking back in.

Connection to broader concepts
This is the same practical trade-off that appears in other “direction-preserving” ideas: normalized representations carry much semantic content, but absolute scales matter for calibration, optimization, and interactions with non-homogeneous downstream layers. The rescaled OFT implements a minimal, controlled relaxation of the strict angle-only hypothesis that keeps the desirable invariants while letting the finetuning correct scale mismatches. Requiring $s_i > 0$ enforces that the relaxation remains a magnitude tweak rather than a qualitatively different transform.

Empirical summary (as stated in the paper)
The paper keeps the main experiments with plain OFT to demonstrate angle-preserving finetuning, but reports that the re-scaled OFT (with diagonal $D$ and $s_i > 0$) “is generally better” in Appendix, indicating that the tiny extra flexibility typically helps finetuning quality and stability without sacrificing the HE-preservation objective.

Bottom line: re-scaling is a light-weight, empirically helpful degree of freedom that adjusts activation magnitudes / calibration without changing the HE objective; enforcing $s_i > 0$ is important to avoid sign flips that would defeat the intended rotation-only / angle-preserving constraints.

### How does OFT’s layer-shared $\mathbf R$ compose across multiple finetuning tasks?
Question:
The conclusion mentions potential compositionality: products of orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal. Suppose we finetune for task A to get $\mathbf R_A$ and for task B to get $\mathbf R_B$. If we compose as $\mathbf R_B\mathbf R_A\mathbf W^0$, what conditions ensure we preserve performance on both A and B? Are there empirical results on order sensitivity (non-commutativity), interference between tasks, or methods (e.g., commuting block structures) to mitigate conflicts?
Answer:
Short answer: multiplying layer-shared orthogonal transforms preserves orthogonality (so hyperspherical energy is still preserved), but that algebraic fact alone does not guarantee that the composed model will retain the behaviors learned for both tasks. To preserve performance on both A and B when you apply the composed weights
$$
W^{AB} = R_B R_A W^0,
$$
you need additional structure/compatibility between $R_A$ and $R_B$. The paper notes compositionality as an open question and does not report experiments that confirm composition preserves multiple task behaviors, so the following characterizes what must hold and practical ways to mitigate conflicts.

Why orthogonality is necessary but not sufficient
Orthogonality means each $R$ is in $O(d)$ (or $SO(d)$ with Cayley), and products of orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal, so $R_B R_A$ still preserves hyperspherical energy. Preserving hyperspherical energy is the paper’s proxy for not destroying pretrained semantics. But task-specific behavior arises because $R_A W^0$ changes neuron directions in a way that makes the model perform A; similarly $R_B W^0$ makes it perform B. When you apply $R_B R_A$ you are not “superposing” two behaviors in a guaranteed way — you are applying the rotation for B to the already-rotated-for-A weights. Unless $R_B$ maps the $R_A$-modified representation back into a representation that still encodes task A, performance on A can degrade.

Formal/intuition conditions for preserving both tasks
A sufficient (but strong) condition is that $R_A$ and $R_B$ commute on the relevant subspace of $W^0$:
$$
R_A R_B = R_B R_A.
$$
If they commute, ordering does not matter, so $R_B R_A W^0 = R_A R_B W^0$. In particular, if there exists a common orthonormal basis that diagonalizes both (simultaneous block-diagonalization / same invariant subspaces), then each rotation acts independently on disjoint subspaces and composition tends to preserve the per-task effects. Intuitively, if the two finetunings modify disjoint neuron directions (or disjoint channels/blocks), their effects do not interfere.

A weaker but still useful condition is subspace separation: if each $R$ acts (approximately) only on a different subspace of the input neuron space (for example block-diagonal with non-overlapping blocks), then
$$
R_B R_A W^0 \approx (R_A W^0) \; \text{on A-subspace}\quad\text{and}\quad (R_B W^0)\; \text{on B-subspace},
$$
so both behaviors can be preserved. Another sufficient viewpoint: if the task-specific maps can be written as multiplicative transforms on orthogonal factors of $W^0$ (i.e., each task’s rotation lies in a subgroup that fixes the other task’s transformed weights), composition will preserve both.

What usually goes wrong (sources of interference)
Because general rotations mix coordinates, $R_B$ typically re-orients directions that $R_A$ set for task A, so the composed model can lose A’s behavior. Orthogonality prevents explosion of hyperspherical energy but does not prevent semantic interference. Non-commutativity is therefore the norm, not the exception, and order matters in general.

Empirical results in the paper
The paper explicitly lists compositionality of orthogonal matrices as an intriguing open problem and does not provide empirical tests of composing $R$ matrices across different finetuning tasks, nor measures of order sensitivity or interference. So there are no reported empirical results on composition in the paper.

Practical ways to reduce interference / encourage composability
1) Enforce or encourage commuting / shared eigenspaces. Constrain or regularize $R$ so multiple task rotations are simultaneously block-diagonal in the same basis (e.g., fix a shared block partition and allow per-task orthogonal blocks). If each task’s $R$ is block-diagonal with the same block partition and blocks operate on disjoint channels, they commute when blocks are diagonalizable in the same basis.

2) Learn orthogonal rotations on disjoint subspaces. Design the block structure so tasks focus on different blocks (e.g., task-specific blocks or channel groups). This is the paper’s block-diagonal idea taken further: allocate different blocks to different tasks.

3) Constrain rotation magnitude (COFT-style). Keep each $R$ close to identity ($\|R-I\|\le \epsilon$). Small rotations are less likely to destroy previously-learned behavior. COFT is explicitly designed to limit deviation and so will reduce interference when composing.

4) Parameterize rotations with a shared basis plus task-specific commuting factors. For example, pick a fixed orthonormal basis $U$ and let each task learn a block-diagonal rotation in that basis: $R_t = U \, \tilde R_t \, U^\top$ with all $\tilde R_t$ block-diagonal in the same block structure — this encourages commutativity.

5) Use gating or selector mechanisms at inference. Rather than composing, store $R_A$ and $R_B$ and apply the appropriate one (or an interpolated mixture) depending on desired behavior. This avoids destructive composition altogether.

6) Explicit multi-task training with compositional objective. Finetune with loss terms that require the composed model to retain per-task performance (e.g., jointly optimize tasks and include regularizers that encourage the composition to match both task outputs). That is more computationally heavy but directly optimizes for composability.

Diagnostics and experiments to run
Because the paper doesn’t report composition experiments, you can empirically probe composability by measuring: task metrics (e.g., CLIP, DINO, control-consistency) on models $R_A W^0$, $R_B W^0$, $R_B R_A W^0$, and $R_A R_B W^0$. Also compute the commutator norm $\|[R_A,R_B]\| = \|R_A R_B - R_B R_A\|$ and correlate it with performance drop on task A when applying $R_B$ after $R_A$. Visualize how $R_B$ re-orients neurons important for A (e.g., track principal components of neuron directions). Try varying block size and COFT $\epsilon$ to see trade-offs between flexibility and composition robustness.

Takeaway
OFT gives an appealing algebraic property (products stay orthogonal), but that alone does not guarantee multi-task preservation. To compose multiple task finetunings successfully you need structural compatibility between learned rotations (commutation, shared invariant subspaces, or disjoint blocks) or explicit constraints (COFT small $\epsilon$, shared block partitions) or alternative deployment strategies (store and select per-task $R$ or jointly train for composability). The paper flags this as an open problem and does not provide empirical answers, so the recommendations above are the natural next steps to study in follow-up work.

### Why does OFT not increase inference-time cost even though it introduces extra parameters?
Question:
The paper claims “no additional inference overhead,” since one can fold the learned $\mathbf R$ into $\mathbf W^0$ as $\mathbf W=\mathbf R\mathbf W^0$. For block-diagonal $\mathbf R$, this is just a pre-multiplication done once before deployment. Are there any caveats for layers that are dynamically re-parameterized at inference (e.g., tied weights, KV-caching, or weight quantization)? Does folding $\mathbf R$ into quantized weights require re-quantization, and if so, does it affect accuracy or latency compared to baseline models?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT itself does not add run-time arithmetic because the learned orthogonal transform $R$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ once at deployment to produce an equivalent single weight $W = R W^0$. After that $W$ is used exactly like the original $W^0$, so per-inference FLOPs and activation shapes are unchanged. That is the basis of the paper’s “no additional inference overhead” claim.

Caveats and practical points you should watch for in dynamic/optimized inference setups (tied weights, KV-caching, weight quantization):

Tied/shared weights
If the original model shares a single parameter tensor $W^0$ across multiple layers/locations, folding different $R$'s into each usage will break that sharing. To preserve the behavior and keep one folded matrix per shared usage you must either (a) use the same $R$ wherever $W^0$ is shared, or (b) avoid folding and apply the same $R$ at runtime (which re-introduces runtime cost). In short: respect the original tying when you build $W = R W^0$, or accept that you change the sharing semantics.

KV-caching and attention projections
KV-caching stores the projected key/value tensors produced by applying the attention projection matrices to past hidden states. Folding $R$ into the attention projection weights (or into upstream linear layers) changes nothing about the projection operation itself: the projection matrix shape is unchanged, so the cached key/value tensors are computed the same way from inputs and can be cached identically. Therefore folding $R$ offline is compatible with existing caching mechanisms and does not change the cache format or add runtime work. Only caveat: if you applied OFT to some layers but not the ones used to compute cached tensors, make sure you fold the specific projection matrices that are actually used for key/value computation.

Weight quantization
This is the area that needs the most care. Typical pipelines quantize stored weights (e.g., 8-bit or 4-bit) for faster inference and smaller memory. Folding $R$ into $W^0$ produces a new floating-point matrix $W = R W^0$; if you want to run inference on quantized weights you will normally need to re-quantize $W$, because quantization is not linear with respect to an arbitrary matrix product. Two consequences follow:

1) Offline cost: re-quantizing $W$ is an offline deployment step. It adds no per-inference arithmetic cost, only a one-time conversion cost before deployment.

2) Accuracy: re-quantizing the new $W$ can change the model’s numerical behavior compared to quantizing $W^0$ directly. The magnitude and distribution of entries in $W$ typically change after left-multiplication by $R$, and that can affect the quantization error and thus final quality. In practice, because $R$ is orthogonal and preserves vector norms, the change to $W$'s singular values is limited (orthogonal left-multiplication does not amplify norms), so quantization degradation is often small, but you must validate it empirically. If you use post-training quantization, run calibration or per-channel quantization on $W$ after folding; if you use quantization-aware training (QAT), you may need to fine-tune with the folded/quantized weights to fully recover any small accuracy loss.

If you want to avoid re-quantization entirely you have two alternatives, each with trade-offs: (a) keep $R$ as a small float adapter and apply it at runtime (this introduces extra ops and memory reads and defeats the “no inference overhead” promise), or (b) constrain your $R$ during finetuning so that the folded $W$ quantizes similarly to $W^0$ (e.g., limit scale shifts, enforce structure), but that is extra engineering and not what the paper assumes.

Practical recommendations
Fold $R$ into $W^0$ as a deployment step and then quantize the folded weights using your normal quantization pipeline; validate generation quality on a representative set of prompts. If the quantized folded model shows degradation, try per-channel quantization and/or a small calibration/QAT pass with the folded weights. For tied/shared weights, either share $R$ to preserve tying semantics (so you only need one folded $W$) or accept that you will replace the single shared tensor with separate folded tensors (same inference cost, but different model semantics). For KV-caching, you can fold safely—no change to cache behavior.

Bottom line: the “no additional inference overhead” statement in the paper is correct under the usual assumption that you fold $R$ into the base weights once prior to deployment. The main practical issue is deployment-time re-quantization (if you quantize): that is an offline cost and can slightly change accuracy, so you should re-quantize/corroborate and, if necessary, run a light calibration or QAT step on the folded weights.

### Does minimizing hyperspherical energy (as in prior work) empirically harm diffusion finetuning?
Question:
Section 6 argues that minimizing HE (pushing neurons to be uniformly spread) is counterproductive for finetuning diffusion models. Is there an ablation where you replace OFT with an HE-regularized objective that explicitly reduces HE and measure controllability/quality? How does performance degrade (e.g., prompt alignment, identity preservation)? This would help disentangle whether angle preservation per se is crucial versus simply constraining updates in a geometric manifold.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report an ablation that replaces OFT with an objective that explicitly minimizes hyperspherical energy (HE) during finetuning. The authors explicitly argue (Section 4, “Why not minimize hyperspherical energy”) that minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ is the wrong objective for finetuning diffusion models and give indirect empirical support (e.g., the contrast to orthogonal vs non‑orthogonal OFT in Section 3.1), but they do not show a direct $\mathrm{HE}$-regularized finetuning experiment measuring controllability or identity/prompt alignment.

Why that matters and what to expect if you ran the ablation
Intuitively, minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ pushes neurons to be uniformly spread on the unit hypersphere (low pairwise similarities). That is useful in some classification settings because it reduces redundancy among neurons, but it also removes structure in the pretrained weight geometry that encodes semantic priors. The paper’s central claim is that the pretrained pairwise angular relations among neurons are part of the model’s semantic “knowledge.” Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves those pairwise angles; COFT further constrains deviation from the pretrained coordinates. If instead you aggressively minimize $\mathrm{HE}$ you should expect two main failure modes: (1) loss of prompt alignment (the model will no longer map textual prompts to visual content as reliably because the pretraining feature geometry was altered), and (2) degraded identity/subject preservation (subject‑driven tasks will lose instance details), plus likely poorer control consistency in signal‑conditioned tasks. Mechanistically, uniformizing the neuron directions breaks the angular “phase” relationships the paper argues carry semantic information (the Fourier/phase analogy in Section 3.1).

Paper evidence relevant to this expectation
The paper contrasts OFT (angle‑preserving) with methods that do not enforce orthogonality and shows empirically that removing the orthogonality constraint “fails to generate any realistic image and achieve no control effect” (Section 3.1). While that is not the same as adding an explicit $\mathrm{HE}$ minimizer, it supports the claim that changing neuron angles without the right structural constraint destroys generative and control performance. The paper also cites Liu et al. (2021) which used orthogonal transformations to keep $\mathrm{HE}$ small in classification; the authors explicitly state that minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ is desirable for classification but “is not a meaningful objective for finetuning, as it may destroy the pretraining information” (Section 4). In short, the paper gives principled and circumstantial empirical reasons to expect $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization to hurt finetuning, but does not present a direct $\mathrm{HE}$-minimization ablation.

If you want to run the ablation (how to design it and what to measure)
A clean experiment would compare three variants on the same finetuning tasks (subject‑driven and controllable generation): (A) OFT (angle preserving), (B) OFT + explicit $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization term (e.g., add $\lambda \cdot \mathrm{HE}(W)$ to the finetuning loss and tune $\lambda$ to push $\mathrm{HE}$ downward), and (C) a pure $\mathrm{HE}$-minimization finetune (no orthogonal parameterization, optimize weights directly with an $\mathrm{HE}$ regularizer). Measure the same metrics used in the paper: CLIP‑T (prompt fidelity), CLIP‑I and DINO (identity/subject fidelity), LPIPS (diversity), and the control consistency metrics ($\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$, mean $\mathrm{IoU}$/accuracy, landmark $L_2$). Also track $\mathrm{HE}$ during training and qualitative sample quality. My expectation, grounded on the paper’s arguments, is that (B) and especially (C) will show lower $\mathrm{HE}$ but at the cost of worse CLIP‑T/CLIP‑I/DINO and worse control consistency: prompt alignment and identity preservation should degrade as $\mathrm{HE}$ decreases past the pretrained level. You may also observe unstable or collapsed generations for aggressive $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization (similar to the collapse when orthogonality is removed in the paper).

Concluding remark
The paper’s main empirical lesson is that preserving pretrained hyperspherical energy (or at least the pairwise angular structure) is beneficial for preserving semantic generation and controllability; it intentionally avoids minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ and does not report the direct ablation you asked about. Running that ablation would be informative: it would likely confirm the paper’s hypothesis that $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization harms prompt alignment and identity preservation, and it would quantify how much uniformization is tolerable before controllability and quality collapse.

### Details of the control consistency metrics: which estimators and how robust are they?
Question:
For controllable generation, you define consistency by extracting control signals from generated images (edges, segmentation maps, landmarks) and comparing them to inputs. Which specific off-the-shelf models are used for each task (C2I IoU/F1, S2I mIoU/accuracy, L2F $\ell_2$ landmark error)? How sensitive are the metrics to the choice and accuracy of these estimators? For example, could different edge detectors or face landmark models change relative rankings among OFT, COFT, ControlNet, and LoRA?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not list the exact third‑party networks in the main text — it only states that control signals are re‑extracted from generated images and compared to the inputs (C2I: $\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$, S2I: $\mathrm{mIoU}/\mathrm{Accuracy}$, L2F: $\mathrm{L2F}$: mean $\ell_2$ landmark error) and that these comparisons are performed “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model” (see paper, controllable generation section and appendix reference). The authors note this dependency explicitly and use the extracted signals as a proxy metric for control consistency. 

Why that matters (intuitive + technical): these control‑consistency metrics are not intrinsic properties of the generator; they measure how well a downstream estimator (edge detector, semantic segmenter, landmark detector) recovers the input control signal from the generated image. Thus the metric is a composition of two things: (a) how well the generator obeys the control, and (b) how well the estimator recovers the control from the particular mode of images produced by each method. If the estimator has systematic failure modes (bias, class imbalance, domain gap), those failures will contaminate the metric. For example, a segmentation model trained on natural photos may underperform on stylized or overly smooth outputs; a landmark detector may fail on extreme poses or occlusions; an edge metric is sensitive to thresholding and to which edge operator is used (Canny vs learned HED gives different edge thinness/noise).

How sensitive rankings can be: in practice, relative rankings between methods (OFT / COFT vs ControlNet / LoRA) are fairly robust when all methods’ outputs are evaluated with the same, reasonably accurate estimator and when the estimator’s failure modes affect methods similarly. The paper claims and uses that common‑estimator setup. However, rankings can change if an estimator is poor or biased in a way that differentially affects methods. Concrete examples: (1) a segmentation model that struggles with fine textures might penalize a generator that uses texture to encode objects while favoring a generator that produces flatter regions; (2) a landmark detector with lower recall on occluded faces could hurt methods that produce more realistic occlusions; (3) different edge extractors (Canny with different thresholds, learned edge detectors) will change $\mathrm{F1}/\mathrm{IoU}$ numbers because they produce different binary maps — coarse detectors can make a method look better or worse depending on how the generator renders strokes. So yes, in principle different estimators can change quantitative scores and, in some cases, change method ordering if the estimator’s biases align with particular generator artifacts.

Practical recommendations (grounded in the paper’s discussion about estimator dependence and best practice):
- Always evaluate all methods with the exact same off‑the‑shelf estimator(s). That controls one major source of variance. The paper does this and explicitly cautions the reader that results are “up to the accuracy of the off‑the‑shelf segmentation/detection model.”
- Use well‑benchmarked, high‑quality estimators (modern semantic segmenter, robust landmark detector). Higher estimator accuracy reduces spurious ranking changes.
- Report multiple complementary estimators or metric variants if possible (e.g., $\mathrm{IoU}$ and $\mathrm{F1}$ for edges; $\mathrm{mIoU}$ plus overall $\mathrm{Accuracy}$ for segmentation; use two landmark detectors or supplement with Procrustes‑aligned error). The paper already reports multiple numbers per task ($\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$, $\mathrm{mIoU}/\mathrm{Accuracy}$, mean $\ell_2$) which helps mitigate single‑estimator artifacts.
- Do sanity checks: visualize estimator outputs alongside inputs for representative samples to ensure failure modes are visible; run human evaluation (the paper also reports human evaluation for subject fidelity) to confirm metric trends.
- For edge tasks, use a deterministic classical edge filter (e.g., Canny with fixed thresholds) as a low‑variance reference and optionally a learned edge detector for perceptual coverage; for segmentation/landmarks, use standard state‑of‑the‑art models trained on similar domains to the dataset (ADE20K for scene segmentation, face landmark networks trained on CelebA/HQ or 300W for faces).

Bottom line: the paper’s control‑consistency metrics rely on off‑the‑shelf estimators and the authors acknowledge this dependency. If you keep the estimator fixed and high quality, the reported relative improvements (OFT / COFT outperforming baselines) are credible and stable; but if you switch to a different, biased or low‑accuracy estimator, absolute numbers and—less commonly—rankings can change. The safest evaluation practice is multiple estimators, visual inspection, and complementary human studies (all of which the paper uses or recommends implicitly).
