# From OFT to COFT: Adding Just Enough Constraint for Rock-Solid Finetuning

## Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled way to adapt large pretrained text-to-image diffusion models by learning per-layer orthogonal transforms that rotate the pretrained neurons without changing their pairwise angular relationships. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds a small, explicit radius constraint around the pretrained model—i.e., it enforces that the learned orthogonal transform stays close to the identity—to further improve finetuning stability, reduce collapse, and make tuning simple hyperparameters (like learning rate and number of iterations) far less painful. This post explains the why and how of COFT, the intuition behind the radius constraint, and what it buys you in practice.

## Quick recap: why orthogonal finetuning (OFT)

Two empirical observations motivate OFT:

- Direction (angle) of neurons carries most semantic content. Prior work and simple toy experiments show that angular information often reconstructs semantic structure far better than raw magnitude.
- Hyperspherical energy—a sum over pairwise angular similarities of normalized neurons—captures relational structure among neurons. Preserving that structure helps keep the pretrained model’s generative ability intact.

OFT enforces preservation of hyperspherical energy by constraining the finetuned weights in each layer to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights. For a fully connected layer with pretrained weights W^0, OFT parameterizes finetuned weights as

z = W^\top x = (R W^0)^\top x,  where R^\top R = I.

Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles on the unit hypersphere, OFT provably keeps the hyperspherical energy of a layer unchanged. Practically, OFT initializes R = I so finetuning starts from the pretrained model, and learns a (block-diagonal, for parameter efficiency) orthogonal matrix R per layer.

Why is this a good middle ground? Rotating all neurons jointly provides enough expressive power to change semantics (you are changing directions) while strongly regularizing the update so you don’t destroy the pretrained generative structure. Compared to additive low-rank updates (LoRA), OFT changes weights multiplicatively with a full-rank, angle-preserving transform.

## Why add a radius constraint? Enter COFT

OFT already reduces catastrophic shifts compared to unconstrained finetuning, but it still allows an arbitrary orthogonal transform. In practice, that can be more flexibility than needed for many downstream tasks and can make finetuning dynamics sensitive to hyperparameters. COFT adds a simple, explicit additional constraint:

||R − I|| ≤ ε

so the orthogonal transform must stay within an ε-neighborhood of the identity. In other words, COFT restricts the finetuned model to lie on the same hypersphere (angle-preserving) and also inside a spherical shell centered on the pretrained weights (magnitude/Euclidean proximity). Formally the forward pass is the same as OFT:

z = (R W^0)^\top x,

subject to R orthogonal and ||R − I|| ≤ ε.

Intuition: preserving angles keeps semantics; limiting deviation from identity prevents drifting too far in parameter space. Combining both yields a finetuning regime that is both semantically conservative and explicitly proximity-regularized.

## Practical parameterization and enforcing the radius

Two practical points from the paper:

- Efficient orthogonal parameterization: OFT/COFT use the Cayley transform to build R from a skew-symmetric Q: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1}. Cayley gives a differentiable way to enforce orthogonality (it yields det = 1, i.e., rotations only).
- Enforcing the radius constraint: expand R via the Neumann series R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q^2) when Q is small. Practically COFT constrains Q with projected gradient steps (project Q back into a ball of radius ε′), which effectively constrains R to be within a small distance of I. Initialization is Q = 0 so R = I at start.

Block-diagonal R is used to reduce parameter counts: R = diag(R1, R2, ..., Rr). This keeps R orthogonal while lowering the cost from O(d^2) to O(d^2 / r), and block-sharing can further reduce parameters.

## What COFT buys you

Based on experiments and analysis in the paper, COFT brings several practical advantages over unconstrained finetuning, LoRA, and even OFT alone:

- Better finetuning stability: COFT reduces the risk of collapse when finetuning diffusion models for subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. The explicit ε constraint makes training dynamics smoother.
- Faster, more predictable convergence: OFT already converges faster than additive adapters in many cases because rotating directions is an effective way to change semantics. COFT keeps this benefit while making the convergence curve much steadier, avoiding sudden jumps or late collapses.
- Easier hyperparameter setting: with COFT you can often set a larger number of iterations or a less finicky learning rate without needing careful early stopping. The explicit proximity bound reduces sensitivity to iteration count.
- No inference overhead: R can be multiplied into W^0 once finetuning finishes, so the deployed model has the same runtime cost as the original pretrained model.
- Maintains semantic generation ability: by preserving hyperspherical energy (angle structure) COFT keeps pretrained semantic generation capabilities while enabling the desired control or personalization.

The paper shows these benefits concretely on two application families: subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style personalization) and controllable generation (ControlNet / adapter-style conditioning). COFT produced more stable finetuning and better subject fidelity and control consistency, while being parameter-efficient.

## Choosing ε — the flexibility/stability trade-off

ε controls the permitted angular/euclidean deviation:

- Small ε → behavior close to the pretrained model; highly conservative finetuning, maximum stability but limited flexibility.
- Large ε → approaches OFT (no radius constraint); more flexible, possibly more expressive but also potentially less stable.

The authors report that COFT with an appropriately chosen ε tends to be the easiest to use: training becomes robust to iteration count and learning rate. In practice, initialize Q = 0, choose ε′ small enough that R starts near identity but large enough to allow the intended editing/personalization effect; projected gradient descent on Q enforces the constraint.

Note: because the Cayley transform is approximated by a Neumann-series expansion when reasoning about the constraint, you should monitor the effective deviation ||R − I|| during training to ensure the approximation remains valid if ε is set larger.

## Other practical notes

- Re-scaled OFT: you can augment OFT/COFT by learning a per-neuron positive scaling diagonal D so weights become R W^0 D. Scaling does not change hyperspherical energy (angles are normalized out) but adds useful flexibility with few parameters.
- Architecture-agnostic: OFT/COFT apply to attention matrices, fully connected layers, and convolution kernels (block-diagonal structure maps intuitively to channel-wise transforms in convs).
- Parameter efficiency: block-diagonal parameterization and block sharing let you balance parameter budget vs transform expressivity. COFT typically uses a similar parameter budget to LoRA but often converges faster and is more stable.

## Limitations and open problems

COFT improves stability at the cost of an extra constraint that must be enforced via Cayley parameterization and projected updates on Q. The Cayley transform involves matrix inverse operations that can be expensive for very large blocks, which is addressed by block-diagonalization but remains an area for efficiency improvement. Also, the block structure introduces bias: choosing block size trades parameter efficiency against flexibility. Finally, while orthogonal matrices compose, it is an open question how sequences or combinations of OFT/COFT transforms (e.g., for multi-task personalization) interact and whether they preserve all downstream knowledge.

## Takeaway

COFT is a small but powerful modification on top of OFT: keep the angle-preserving orthogonal update that protects pretrained semantic structure, and add a simple radius constraint around the identity to explicitly limit deviation. The result is much more robust finetuning—less collapse, faster and steadier convergence, and hyperparameters that are far easier to set—without any added cost at inference time. If you finetune large diffusion models for subject personalization or conditional control, adding “just enough” constraint in the orthogonal space is an effective and practical recipe.
