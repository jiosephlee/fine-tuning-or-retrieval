\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Two Big Wins: Personalizing Subjects and Following Controls with One Finetuning Recipe

Large text-to-image diffusion models are impressive, but getting them to reliably do two practical things at once is still hard: (1) personalize to a new subject from a few images (DreamBooth-style), and (2) obey additional control signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a single, principled finetuning recipe that improves both tasks—faster, more stably, and often with better final quality—by changing how we adapt the pretrained weights.

## The core idea in one sentence
Instead of adding arbitrary small updates to pretrained weights, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal transform R so the finetuned weights become W = R W^0. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles between neurons (and thus hyperspherical energy), OFT changes neuron directions while keeping the relational structure learned during pretraining.

## Why preserve angles (and what hyperspherical energy is)
A growing body of work shows angular relationships between neurons/feature vectors encode semantic information better than raw magnitudes. Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a measure of pairwise neuron similarity on the unit hypersphere; roughly, HE = sum_{i != j} ||\hat w_i - \hat w_j||^{-1} where \hat w_i are normalized neurons. OFT enforces that HE(W) = HE(W^0) by construction: any orthogonal R preserves all pairwise angles, so HE(R W^0) = HE(W^0). The paper argues (and empirically supports) that keeping HE close to the pretrained value is key to preserving the model's general semantic generation ability while still allowing task-specific changes.

## Why operate by orthogonal transforms, intuitively
Two intuitions make orthogonal transforms attractive:

- Angular semantics: Analogous to phase information in Fourier transforms, the directions of neurons encode semantics more than magnitudes. Changing directions is effective for altering semantics (e.g., making the model represent a specific person or obey a structural control).
- Limited but expressive freedom: Rotating/reflection of the neuron basis (the same transform across a layer) is flexible enough to adapt behaviors while regularizing away destructive drift that breaks pretrained capabilities. Empirically, full unconstrained directional updates often destabilize training; orthogonality stabilizes it.

## Practical parameterizations
Fully unconstrained orthogonal matrices cost O(d^2) parameters and are expensive when d is large. OFT uses two practical tricks:

- Cayley parameterization to produce orthogonal matrices from a skew-symmetric Q: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}. This produces R with det = 1 (a rotation).
- Block-diagonal structure for R with r blocks, reducing parameter cost to roughly O(d^2 / r) and allowing channel-wise interpretations for convolutions. Block sharing can reduce parameters further.

There is also COFT (Constrained OFT) that explicitly constrains ||R - I|| ≤ ε so the orthogonal transform stays close to identity; this improves finetuning stability. A small re-scaled OFT variant learns diagonal magnitudes D as well so W = R W^0 D, allowing per-neuron scaling without changing HE.

## What OFT was applied to (the two flagship tasks)

1. Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style personalization)
   - Task: Given a few images of a subject, finetune the text-to-image model so that prompts containing a custom token produce images of that subject in new contexts.
   - Baselines: DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA-style adaptation.
   - Findings: OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than DreamBooth and LoRA. They preserve subject identity better (measured by DINO and CLIP image-based similarity metrics) while maintaining or slightly improving prompt fidelity (CLIP text-image similarity) and diversity (LPIPS). OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations—so you can finetune longer without collapsing, unlike naive finetuning.

2. Controllable generation (adding edge/segmentation/landmark conditioning)
   - Task: Enable a pretrained diffusion model to follow additional structure inputs—Canny edges (C2I), segmentation maps (S2I), and facial landmarks (L2F)—so generated images match the control signal and input prompt.
   - Baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA (all finetuning-based approaches).
   - Findings: OFT and COFT achieve stronger and more accurate control with far fewer training epochs and often fewer trainable parameters than the adapter methods. Convergence is smoother and earlier: e.g., COFT/OFT reach the accuracy that LoRA requires many more epochs to match. The paper also introduces a control-consistency evaluation: recover the control signal from generated images and compare with the input control (IoU/F1 for edges, mIoU/accuracy for segmentation, landmark L2 for faces). On these consistency measures, OFT/COFT outperform baselines.

## Examples that make the difference concrete
- Personalization: Given five photos of a person, DreamBooth and LoRA can either miss identity details or collapse after many iterations. OFT keeps identity features (color/shape/texture) faithful while still letting the subject appear in new scenes specified by the prompt.
- Control (edges/segmentation/landmarks): When given a rough Canny edge map, OFT consistently hallucinates plausible images aligned to that structure. For segmentation maps, OFT produces images whose object layout and semantics follow the map more faithfully than LoRA. For facial landmarks, OFT produces correct poses even for challenging poses, converging faster and with lower landmark distance.

## Why OFT works well in practice
- Preserving HE keeps the pretrained model's semantic priors intact, so adaptation does not overwrite the general capability that produces photorealistic, diverse images.
- Learning a shared orthogonal transform per layer concentrates the update on changing neuron directions—what matters most for semantics—while preventing destructive drift.
- COFT's explicit proximity constraint (||R - I|| ≤ ε) gives a safe “trust region” so you can finetune with less hyperparameter fiddling.
- No extra inference cost: after training you can multiply R into W^0 to get final weights; there is no adapter or extra module at runtime.

## Takeaways and limitations
OFT is a simple, theoretically motivated, and practically useful adaptation method that gives two wins with the same recipe: better subject personalization and stronger controllability. It is parameter-efficient compared to some adapters and converges faster and more stably than naive finetuning or LoRA in the evaluated settings.

Limitations and open issues noted by the authors include the cost of Cayley transforms (matrix inverse) at scale, the reliance on block-diagonal structure for parameter efficiency (which imposes bias), and open questions about composing multiple orthogonal transforms for multiple downstream tasks.

## Bottom line
If you want a principled way to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model that preserves pretrained semantics while enabling strong, stable control over content—whether it’s learning to depict a new person from a few images or obeying edges/segmentation/landmarks—Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained variant COFT) provides a practical and effective toolset.
