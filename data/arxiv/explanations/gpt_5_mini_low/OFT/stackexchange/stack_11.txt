\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### If OFT preserves angles, why introduce re-scaled OFT with a diagonal $\mathbf D$?
Question:
Section 3.5 adds a learnable diagonal scaling $\mathbf D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ in $\mathbf z=(\mathbf R\mathbf W^0\mathbf D)^\top\mathbf x$, arguing that re-scaling neurons does not change HE. If directions (angles) carry most semantic information, what empirical phenomena require magnitude changes for better finetuning? Also, is constraining $s_i>0$ important (to avoid sign flips that could mimic reflections not in $\text{SO}(d)$)?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (hence hyperspherical energy), but real networks are not purely “angle-only” systems in practice. Adding a small per-neuron magnitude factor gives a cheap, targeted degree of freedom that fixes mismatch in activation scale / calibration / downstream interactions and empirically improves finetuning. Constraining each scaling scalar to be positive is important: a negative scalar flips the sign ($180^\circ$ rotation) of a neuron, which is not a harmless scaling and would reintroduce reflection-like degrees of freedom that break the $SO(d)$ / angle-preservation intent and can destabilize finetuning — so the paper enforces $s_i > 0$.

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper
The paper’s motivation for OFT is that directions (angles) among neurons capture much of the semantic information, and orthogonal transforms preserve those angles (hence HE). However, real model behavior depends not only on normalized directions but also on magnitudes and the interaction of those magnitudes with layer statistics, normalization layers, attention scaling, residual paths, and the next layers’ parameterizations. Multiplying a neuron by a positive scalar does not change hyperspherical energy (because HE uses normalized neurons), but it does change the actual forward activations $z = W^\top x$ and therefore can alter: the absolute activation scale in subsequent nonlinearities, relative contribution of neurons when later weights are not re-normalized, and optimizer dynamics during finetuning.

Concretely, small per-neuron scales allow the finetuned model to (1) adjust activation magnitudes to match pretrained activation distributions after applying $R$, (2) compensate for layernorm/scale interactions or for attention query/key/value magnitude needs, and (3) give a tiny amount of extra flexibility so OFT can better fit downstream signals (prompt fidelity or control supervision) without changing neuron angles. The authors report that “re-scaled OFT is generally better” (see Appendix) while keeping the parameter cost negligible, so empirically those magnitude knobs improve convergence and final performance beyond pure orthogonal rotation.

Why positivity ($s_i > 0$) matters
Allowing negative $s_i$ changes more than magnitude: it flips the vector direction (multiplication by a negative scalar gives the opposite unit vector), which changes pairwise angles in a substantive way (a $180^\circ$ flip is not a small continuous rotation) and can mimic reflections. The paper notes that Cayley parameterization they use restricts $R$ to $\det(R)=+1$ ($SO(d)$), i.e. rotations (no reflections). If you let $D$ include negative entries, you inject sign-flips that can emulate reflections or large angular flips that the OFT orthogonal part was explicitly chosen not to introduce. That undermines the guarantee that HE is preserved up to orthogonal transforms in the intended sense and can reintroduce instability or overly large semantic changes. So constraining $s_i > 0$ keeps $D$ as a pure magnitude/scaling layer that leaves normalized directions unchanged, preserves the spirit of OFT/COFT, and prevents sign-flip reflections from sneaking back in.

Connection to broader concepts
This is the same practical trade-off that appears in other “direction-preserving” ideas: normalized representations carry much semantic content, but absolute scales matter for calibration, optimization, and interactions with non-homogeneous downstream layers. The rescaled OFT implements a minimal, controlled relaxation of the strict angle-only hypothesis that keeps the desirable invariants while letting the finetuning correct scale mismatches. Requiring $s_i > 0$ enforces that the relaxation remains a magnitude tweak rather than a qualitatively different transform.

Empirical summary (as stated in the paper)
The paper keeps the main experiments with plain OFT to demonstrate angle-preserving finetuning, but reports that the re-scaled OFT (with diagonal $D$ and $s_i > 0$) “is generally better” in Appendix, indicating that the tiny extra flexibility typically helps finetuning quality and stability without sacrificing the HE-preservation objective.

Bottom line: re-scaling is a light-weight, empirically helpful degree of freedom that adjusts activation magnitudes / calibration without changing the HE objective; enforcing $s_i > 0$ is important to avoid sign flips that would defeat the intended rotation-only / angle-preserving constraints.
