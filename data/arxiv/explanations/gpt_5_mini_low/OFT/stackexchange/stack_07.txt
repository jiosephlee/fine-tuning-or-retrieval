\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does preserving HE imply preserving cosine similarities, or only a weighted function of them?
Question:
HE is defined as $\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$ with $\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|=\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}$. An orthogonal transform preserves each $\cos\theta_{ij}$, but the OFT principle is motivated via preserving HE as a scalar. Is the theoretical guarantee you rely on “orthogonality preserves all pairwise angles” (strong) or “minimizing HE difference drives small average angular distortions” (weaker)? If the latter, why does minimizing only the summed inverse distance suffice, given it might hide compensating changes across pairs?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper relies on the strong guarantee that an orthogonal layerwise transform exactly preserves every pairwise angle. Minimizing the scalar hyperspherical energy (HE) alone is a strictly weaker objective — many different angular configurations can give the same HE — so HE-minimization by itself does not guarantee small per-pair angular distortions. OFT uses orthogonality (and optionally the small-deviation COFT constraint) to obtain the strong per-pair preservation.

Explanation and intuition grounded in the paper. HE is defined (for normalized neurons) as
$$
\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1},\qquad
\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|=\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}.
$$
Because each pairwise Euclidean chord length on the unit sphere is a deterministic function of the cosine of the angle, an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ (i.e. $\mathbf R^\top\mathbf R=\mathbf I$) maps normalized neurons to normalized neurons while preserving every angle: for every pair $(i,j)$,
$$
\cos\theta_{ij}\;=\;\hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top\hat{\mathbf w}_j
\;=\;(\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_i)^\top(\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_j).
$$
Thus an orthogonal transform gives an exact, elementwise invariance of the full pairwise cosine matrix and therefore of HE as a scalar. This is the strong theoretical guarantee OFT exploits: by parameterizing finetuning as a layer-shared orthogonal transform (Eq. (3) in the paper), the method provably preserves the entire pattern of pairwise angles.

Why then introduce HE in the first place? The authors use HE as a structural measure to motivate the desideratum of preserving pairwise neuron relations, and to argue that the attainable minimizer of the HE-difference objective (Eq. (1)) is achieved when the finetuned weights differ from pretrained ones by an orthogonal transform. But note the distinction: minimizing the scalar difference
$$
\big\|\text{HE}(\mathbf W)-\text{HE}(\mathbf W^0)\big\|
$$
is only a global constraint on that one number. Many different angle matrices can produce the same HE value (because HE sums a nonlinear function of all pairwise angles); thus equality of HE as a scalar does not imply equality of each $\cos\theta_{ij}$. In other words, HE equality is necessary (for orthogonality it happens), but not sufficient to conclude pairwise angle equality in general. You can easily imagine compensating changes across pairs (some angles increase, others decrease) that leave the sum of inverse chord lengths unchanged.

This is exactly why OFT does not rely on unconstrained scalar HE-minimization as the finetuning rule. Instead, the paper enforces the orthogonality structure directly (via Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal approximations) so that the strong, elementwise angle-preservation property holds by construction. COFT further constrains the orthogonal transform to be near the identity ($\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\epsilon$), which explicitly prevents large collective rotations and reduces degrees of freedom, improving stability and ensuring the finetuned model remains close in Euclidean sense as well.

A few concrete implications to keep in mind. First, re-scaling of neuron magnitudes does not change HE (normalization removes magnitudes), so a method allowed to rescale neurons could preserve HE while strongly changing magnitudes — OFT addresses this by operating multiplicatively via orthogonal matrices (and optionally adding a learnable diagonal scaling only when desired, see re-scaled OFT). Second, a pure optimization that only minimizes the HE scalar could converge to a configuration that preserves the HE but distorts many individual angles; that is why the paper chooses the orthogonality parameterization rather than solving the scalar HE-matching optimization directly.

In summary: the motivating quantity is HE, but the theoretical guarantee used in OFT is the strong fact that orthogonal transforms preserve all pairwise cosines. Minimizing only the summed inverse-distance HE is a weaker statement and can hide compensating changes; OFT avoids that weakness by enforcing orthogonality (and optionally a small-deviation constraint) so per-pair angular structure is preserved.
