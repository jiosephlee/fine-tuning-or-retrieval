\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does minimizing hyperspherical energy (as in prior work) empirically harm diffusion finetuning?
Question:
Section 6 argues that minimizing HE (pushing neurons to be uniformly spread) is counterproductive for finetuning diffusion models. Is there an ablation where you replace OFT with an HE-regularized objective that explicitly reduces HE and measure controllability/quality? How does performance degrade (e.g., prompt alignment, identity preservation)? This would help disentangle whether angle preservation per se is crucial versus simply constraining updates in a geometric manifold.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report an ablation that replaces OFT with an objective that explicitly minimizes hyperspherical energy (HE) during finetuning. The authors explicitly argue (Section 4, “Why not minimize hyperspherical energy”) that minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ is the wrong objective for finetuning diffusion models and give indirect empirical support (e.g., the contrast to orthogonal vs non‑orthogonal OFT in Section 3.1), but they do not show a direct $\mathrm{HE}$-regularized finetuning experiment measuring controllability or identity/prompt alignment.

Why that matters and what to expect if you ran the ablation
Intuitively, minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ pushes neurons to be uniformly spread on the unit hypersphere (low pairwise similarities). That is useful in some classification settings because it reduces redundancy among neurons, but it also removes structure in the pretrained weight geometry that encodes semantic priors. The paper’s central claim is that the pretrained pairwise angular relations among neurons are part of the model’s semantic “knowledge.” Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves those pairwise angles; COFT further constrains deviation from the pretrained coordinates. If instead you aggressively minimize $\mathrm{HE}$ you should expect two main failure modes: (1) loss of prompt alignment (the model will no longer map textual prompts to visual content as reliably because the pretraining feature geometry was altered), and (2) degraded identity/subject preservation (subject‑driven tasks will lose instance details), plus likely poorer control consistency in signal‑conditioned tasks. Mechanistically, uniformizing the neuron directions breaks the angular “phase” relationships the paper argues carry semantic information (the Fourier/phase analogy in Section 3.1).

Paper evidence relevant to this expectation
The paper contrasts OFT (angle‑preserving) with methods that do not enforce orthogonality and shows empirically that removing the orthogonality constraint “fails to generate any realistic image and achieve no control effect” (Section 3.1). While that is not the same as adding an explicit $\mathrm{HE}$ minimizer, it supports the claim that changing neuron angles without the right structural constraint destroys generative and control performance. The paper also cites Liu et al. (2021) which used orthogonal transformations to keep $\mathrm{HE}$ small in classification; the authors explicitly state that minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ is desirable for classification but “is not a meaningful objective for finetuning, as it may destroy the pretraining information” (Section 4). In short, the paper gives principled and circumstantial empirical reasons to expect $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization to hurt finetuning, but does not present a direct $\mathrm{HE}$-minimization ablation.

If you want to run the ablation (how to design it and what to measure)
A clean experiment would compare three variants on the same finetuning tasks (subject‑driven and controllable generation): (A) OFT (angle preserving), (B) OFT + explicit $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization term (e.g., add $\lambda \cdot \mathrm{HE}(W)$ to the finetuning loss and tune $\lambda$ to push $\mathrm{HE}$ downward), and (C) a pure $\mathrm{HE}$-minimization finetune (no orthogonal parameterization, optimize weights directly with an $\mathrm{HE}$ regularizer). Measure the same metrics used in the paper: CLIP‑T (prompt fidelity), CLIP‑I and DINO (identity/subject fidelity), LPIPS (diversity), and the control consistency metrics ($\mathrm{IoU}/\mathrm{F1}$, mean $\mathrm{IoU}$/accuracy, landmark $L_2$). Also track $\mathrm{HE}$ during training and qualitative sample quality. My expectation, grounded on the paper’s arguments, is that (B) and especially (C) will show lower $\mathrm{HE}$ but at the cost of worse CLIP‑T/CLIP‑I/DINO and worse control consistency: prompt alignment and identity preservation should degrade as $\mathrm{HE}$ decreases past the pretrained level. You may also observe unstable or collapsed generations for aggressive $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization (similar to the collapse when orthogonality is removed in the paper).

Concluding remark
The paper’s main empirical lesson is that preserving pretrained hyperspherical energy (or at least the pairwise angular structure) is beneficial for preserving semantic generation and controllability; it intentionally avoids minimizing $\mathrm{HE}$ and does not report the direct ablation you asked about. Running that ablation would be informative: it would likely confirm the paper’s hypothesis that $\mathrm{HE}$ minimization harms prompt alignment and identity preservation, and it would quantify how much uniformization is tolerable before controllability and quality collapse.
