\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does preserving hyperspherical energy actually guarantee preservation of pairwise angles?
Question:
In Section 3 (General Framework), hyperspherical energy for a layer with columns $\{\hat{\mathbf w}_i\}$ is defined as $\text{HE}(\mathbf W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$. The paper then argues that applying a shared orthogonal transform $\mathbf R$ to columns preserves HE and thus pairwise relationships. Since $\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta_{ij}$ on the unit sphere, preserving HE seems equivalent to preserving each $\cos\theta_{ij}$ (i.e., all pairwise angles). Is the implication strictly bidirectional? Could two different angle configurations yield the same sum $\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$ but different individual angles, or does orthogonality uniquely enforce angle-wise preservation beyond just matching the summed energy?
Answer:
Short answer: No — preserving the scalar hyperspherical energy value does not by itself guarantee preservation of every pairwise angle. Orthogonal transforms do preserve all pairwise angles, but equality of the single number $HE(W)$ for two configurations is far weaker than equality of the full matrix of pairwise cosines.

Why, intuitively and technically: hyperspherical energy as defined in the paper is a single scalar
$HE(W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},$
and on the unit sphere $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^2 = 2-2\cos\theta_{ij}$, so HE aggregates the pairwise angles via the map $\theta_{ij}\mapsto (2-2\cos\theta_{ij})^{-1/2}$ and then sums them. A single scalar sum cannot uniquely determine the full set of many pairwise angles except in degenerate, highly constrained cases. Counting degrees of freedom already shows this: for $n$ unit vectors in $d$ dimensions the number of independent pairwise angles (or cosines) is roughly $O(n^2)$, whereas HE is one scalar. Many different angle/geometry configurations can produce the same sum of reciprocals of chord lengths.

Consequence: two different configurations $\{\hat w_i\}$ and $\{\hat w'_i\}$ can have the same HE value but different individual $\cos\theta_{ij}$. Thus minimizing the difference $|HE(W)-HE(W^0)|$ to zero does not force $W$ to be equal to $W^0$ up to an orthogonal matrix; it only matches that one global statistic. Without an additional constraint, there is an enormous family of possible configurations with the same HE.

What the paper actually uses and proves: an orthogonal transformation $R$ applied to all columns, $W = R W^0$, preserves every pairwise inner product (and thus every $\cos\theta_{ij}$) exactly, and therefore preserves HE. So orthogonality implies preservation of the full pairwise-angle structure. But the converse—HE equality implying the existence of an orthogonal $R$ relating the two sets—is not true in general. That is why the paper does not just regularize HE to be equal, but parameterizes the update as a shared orthogonal matrix $R$ (OFT). That choice gives a provable, exact preservation of all pairwise angles (and of HE), whereas merely matching the scalar HE would not guarantee angle-wise preservation.

Practical upshot tied to the paper: optimizing HE alone is underconstrained and could allow destructive changes that keep the total energy the same. OFT enforces the stronger structural constraint (shared orthogonal $R$, optionally with a small deviation constraint in COFT) so that pairwise angles are truly preserved during finetuning, which explains the paper’s claim and why they prefer the orthogonal parameterization over an HE regularizer alone.
