\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: Introduction and Motivation

# Limits of text-only control in diffusion models

Large text-to-image diffusion models have recently achieved striking success at producing photorealistic and semantically rich images from textual descriptions. Examples include models like Imagen, GLIDE and Stable Diffusion, which couple powerful denoising diffusion generative processes with language-conditioned guidance. Despite this progress, relying on text alone to specify desired images has intrinsic limitations. Natural language is inherently ambiguous and often underspecified. A prompt like "a portrait of Alex in a café" leaves many crucial image attributes underspecified: the precise subject identity, pose, viewpoint, lighting, fine-grained clothing details, or the geometric layout of the scene. Even when a text prompt attempts to be more specific, some kinds of control (for example, exact pose, precise object contours, or the appearance of a specific subject pictured in a few photos) are difficult to communicate compactly in words.

Practically, this ambiguity shows up as model outputs that match the high-level semantics of the prompt but fail to capture particular details users care about. This is especially problematic in applications that require subject fidelity (recreating a specific person or object) or precise adherence to spatial structure (matching an input edge map or segmentation). Consequently, researchers and practitioners have sought ways to augment text-only conditioning with other signals or with mechanisms that steer the pretrained generative model toward a downstream objective. But augmenting or altering a large pretrained model raises a second difficulty: adapting the model to new control modalities or to represent a particular subject must be done without destroying the model's broad pretraining capabilities (its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images across prompts).

# Downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

Two concrete downstream tasks exemplify the need for more precise control beyond text alone.

Subject-driven generation targets the setting where a user provides a small set of images of a particular subject (for example, a few photos of a person, a pet, or a custom object) and then desires images that place that subject into new contexts specified by text prompts. The challenge is to incorporate the subject-specific appearance—identity, texture, distinct marks—so that the generated images genuinely depict the same subject across varied backgrounds and scenes. The task is difficult because it must generalize the subject to new poses and contexts while maintaining fidelity of identity and appearance; naive finetuning can overfit or corrupt the pretrained model's generalization.

Controllable generation refers to leveraging additional structured control signals along with text, such as edge maps (Canny), segmentation maps, landmark/pose keypoints, depth maps, or other spatial constraints. The goal is to make the diffusion model respect these structured signals so that generated images match the desired geometry or semantics as well as the text. Approaches in this family include methods that add adapter modules or finetune the original model to accept the control signal. The challenge here is similar: gain reliable, accurate control while keeping the high image quality and diversity that the pretrained model already possesses.

Both tasks share a central tension: they require modifying or augmenting a pretrained model to incorporate new, stronger control, but this adaptation must preserve the pretrained model's broad generation abilities. Achieving this without excessive compute cost, without needing large amounts of task-specific data, and without compromising model stability during training are the core practical demands.

# Desiderata for finetuning: efficiency and preservation of generalization

When we think about how to adapt massive pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks, two desiderata naturally arise.

First, training (or finetuning) efficiency. Large diffusion models have many weights, so standard full finetuning is computationally expensive and often unnecessary when the downstream task is narrow. Efficient finetuning aims to update far fewer parameters, reduce GPU hours, require fewer training images, and converge in fewer iterations. Model- and parameter-efficient methods (adapters, low-rank updates) have become popular because they allow rapid adaptation and make it practical to deploy many specialized variants.

Second, preservation of the model's pretrained generalization and high-fidelity generation. The pretrained model encodes vast visual knowledge and a careful balance of parameters that produce diverse realistic images across prompts. An effective finetuning procedure should not inadvertently erase or corrupt that knowledge. In other words, we want to adapt the model so it gains the new control behaviors while retaining the creative and semantic abilities it learned during pretraining.

These two desiderata together create a trade-off. A highly flexible finetuning method can fit the downstream objective but risks destroying pretrained capabilities; a strongly constrained method may preserve pretraining but lack the flexibility to learn the new control. A principled finetuning approach must find a sweet spot in this trade-off: sufficient flexibility to learn the downstream constraint, but enough structural regularity that the model's semantic generation properties are preserved.

# Shortcomings of conventional finetuning and adapter-style approaches

Typical finetuning strategies fall into two broad categories.

The first is conventional gradient-based finetuning: updating some or all of the pretrained weights using a small learning rate. The small step sizes are intended to keep the final weights close in Euclidean norm to the pretrained weights, under the implicit belief that small Euclidean changes preserve the model's prior capabilities. While simple, this approach offers no principled guarantee that semantic behaviors (the kinds of images the model generates, or how it composes semantics) are preserved. Small Euclidean distance does not directly constrain higher-level structural properties of weight matrices that encode semantic relationships. Moreover, even with small learning rates, finetuning large generators can be unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters like number of iterations; finetuning runs can collapse or overfit, especially for small datasets typical in subject-driven tasks.

The second class includes adapter-style or reparameterization methods such as LoRA and other parameter-efficient adaptation techniques. These add a small learnable module—often modeled as an additive low-rank update to certain weight matrices—or insert small adapter blocks that are trained while the bulk of the model remains frozen. Adapter methods reduce the number of trainable parameters and have shown practical success, but they also lack principled constraints that directly capture the structural relations among neurons. For instance, LoRA assumes a low-rank structure for the additive weight update, which controls capacity but does not provide guarantees about the pairwise geometric relationships among neurons. Consequently, adapter approaches can still change the geometry of neuron representations in ways that degrade the pretrained semantic behavior.

Both approaches implicitly rely on proximity in Euclidean norm (small steps, small additive updates) as a proxy for preserving pretrained behavior. The paper argues that Euclidean proximity alone is inadequate: it does not fully capture the relational or angular structure among neurons that underlies how the model represents semantics. Thus a finetuning strategy that directly preserves more meaningful structural invariants of the pretrained model could yield better behavior: retaining pretraining semantics while allowing targeted, efficient adaptation.

# High-level idea: preserving pairwise neuron geometry on the hypersphere

The central insight of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in each layer during finetuning. These angular relations are naturally captured by treating neuron vectors as points on a unit hypersphere and measuring pairwise similarity via normalized vectors, typically using cosine or angular distances. The hyperspherical energy is a scalar quantity that aggregates these pairwise relations across a layer: formally, for a weight matrix with neurons \(\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\) and normalized neurons \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\), the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
which is a sum of inverse chordal distances and depends only on the pairwise angles among normalized neurons. Empirical and theoretical work cited in the paper suggests that angular relationships among features carry much of the semantic information learned by neural networks; SphereNet-like observations show direction can be more informative than magnitude in many representation settings. A toy experiment in the paper's introduction illustrates that angular (phase-like) information of convolutional kernels retains most semantic content, while magnitudes contain little.

Because hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise angular structure, preserving it between a finetuned model and the pretrained model is a stronger and more semantically meaningful constraint than preserving Euclidean distance alone. However, directly regularizing the hyperspherical energy with a penalty during optimization can be difficult to enforce exactly and may not reach zero difference.

OFT leverages a key invariance: if all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) (a rotation/reflection), then their pairwise angles are preserved exactly. That is, replacing \(\bm{W}^0\) by \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) leaves \(\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\). Orthogonal transformations are therefore a natural family of parameter updates that guarantee hyperspherical energy preservation. Rather than adding arbitrary additive updates or applying unconstrained gradient steps, OFT constrains finetuning to learn layer-shared orthogonal transformations \(\bm{R}\) (initialized as identity so training starts from the pretrained weights). This multiplicative, orthogonal re-parameterization effectively rotates the neuron directions without altering their pairwise angular geometry.

This design achieves several objectives simultaneously. Because the angular geometry is preserved, many of the pretrained model's semantic generation properties persist. At the same time, orthogonal transforms change neuron directions collectively in a structured way, giving the model meaningful ways to alter semantics where needed: changing global direction bases can effect controlled modifications (for example, adapting identity features for a subject). OFT therefore finds a middle ground: it restricts finetuning to transformations that keep hyperspherical energy invariant—providing strong regularity—while allowing targeted, efficient adaptation by learning the orthogonal matrices.

The paper further refines this idea with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which additionally constrains the learned orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) to remain near the identity. This explicitly limits the deviation from the pretrained model in operator norm (a radius constraint), giving a tunable trade-off between flexibility and stability. COFT can help prevent over-adjustment and yields even more stable finetuning dynamics in practice. Other practical design choices discussed include efficient orthogonal parameterizations (Cayley transform, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices) and a rescaled variant that also learns magnitudes per neuron while preserving angular invariants.

Taken together, these ideas motivate OFT as a principled finetuning strategy: instead of using Euclidean proximity as the proxy for semantic preservation, OFT preserves pairwise neuron geometry on the hypersphere, which the paper argues (and empirically supports) is a more faithful structural invariant for retaining pretrained generative semantics while enabling efficient adaptation to subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.
