\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Background: Diffusion Models and Finetuning Landscape

# Text-to-image diffusion models: pixel-space vs latent-space (GLIDE, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2)

Text-to-image diffusion models are generative systems trained to reverse a gradual noising process and thereby synthesize images from conditioning signals such as text prompts. Within the family of diffusion-based image generators, two main engineering choices have emerged in the literature discussed in the paper: training the diffusion model in pixel space, or training it in a latent space. The distinction matters because it affects model capacity, computational cost, and how finetuning or control mechanisms are attached.

Pixel-space models operate directly on image pixels: the diffusion process adds noise to images in the pixel domain and the denoising model is trained to predict the clean image (or score) from noisy pixels and conditioning. The paper cites GLIDE and Imagen as representative pixel-space text-to-image diffusion models. GLIDE trains a text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data. Imagen trains diffusion models in pixel space while using a frozen pretrained text encoder. Training in pixel space typically yields very high-fidelity output because the model directly models the image distribution, but it can be computationally heavy due to the high dimensionality of pixel space.

Latent-space models perform diffusion in a lower-dimensional learned latent representation of images. A separate encoder/decoder (a learned visual codebook or other mapping) maps images to and from that latent. Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 are cited as prominent latent-space approaches. Stable Diffusion uses a VQ-GAN learned visual codebook as its latent space and trains diffusion models in that latent; DALL·E 2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space for representing images and text. Operating in latent space reduces the dimensionality the diffusion model must handle, improving computational and memory efficiency while still allowing high-quality outputs after decoding. The choice between pixel-space and latent-space setups interacts with finetuning and control strategies discussed later: some control or adapter modules attach to the diffusion U-Net or to components that map between text and image latents, and whether the model is pixel- or latent-based constrains where and how such modules are integrated.

# Text and vision-language backbones (CLIP, VQ-GAN, text encoders)

Text-to-image diffusion systems leverage separate components to represent text and images. The paper highlights a few backbone technologies used in contemporary models. CLIP is mentioned as the joint vision-language model used by DALL·E 2 to produce a shared embedding space for images and text; CLIP embeddings are also used as evaluation features (CLIP-I and CLIP-T metrics) in experiments described by the paper. VQ-GAN is referenced as the visual codebook approach used by Stable Diffusion to build a compact latent representation in which the diffusion model operates.

More generically, the architectures combine three functional blocks: an image encoder/decoder or codebook for mapping between pixels and latents (VQ-GAN style), a diffusion U-Net that performs the denoising conditioned on text and possibly other signals, and a text encoder that maps prompts to conditioning vectors. Implementation choices—whether the text encoder is trained jointly (as in GLIDE) or frozen (as in Imagen), and whether the image representation is a learned codebook (Stable Diffusion) or handled in pixel space—shape how finetuning is performed. For example, DreamBooth-style finetuning is typically applied to the diffusion model weights (the U-Net) and uses the text encoder and image encoder/decoder as part of the fixed pipeline; ControlNet and adapter methods attach additional learned modules to the diffusion backbone so they can incorporate extra control signals without entirely changing the pretrained weights.

# Standard finetuning with small learning rates

A straightforward approach to adapt a pretrained large text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream task is to continue training (finetuning) its weights on task-specific data, typically with a much smaller learning rate than used during pretraining. The intuition behind using a small learning rate is that the pretrained model already captures general generative knowledge and we want to preserve that knowledge while nudging the model to handle task-specific signals. In this style of finetuning, the objective minimized during adaptation is the same as the pretraining objective (e.g., the denoising or reconstruction loss used in diffusion models), but over the new task dataset. Because the updates are small, this implicitly keeps the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the original pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$ small, i.e., the optimization implicitly limits $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$.

The paper notes that while this small-step finetuning is simple and often effective, it provides no principled guarantee for preserving the pretrained model’s generative behavior beyond the small Euclidean change. In practice this leads to several shortcomings: finetuning can still destroy desirable generic generative abilities if training runs too long or the learning rate is not carefully tuned, and choosing hyperparameters such as iteration count or learning rate becomes delicate. In other words, Euclidean proximity to the pretrained parameters is an intuitive but incomplete proxy for preserving the model’s semantic generation capability.

# Adapter-based finetuning (Adapters, LoRA): mechanisms and trade-offs

Adapter-based finetuning methods take a different route: instead of modifying the original weights directly, they introduce a small additional module or parameterization whose output modifies the model’s computation. Two examples discussed in the paper are Adapters in the NLP literature and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). The shared motivation is parameter efficiency and safer adaptation: by keeping most pretrained weights frozen and only learning a compact set of extra parameters, these methods reduce storage and computation for task-specific adaptations and aim to avoid catastrophic changes to the pretrained model.

LoRA is highlighted as particularly relevant. It models the additive update to a weight matrix during finetuning as a low-rank decomposition: the weight change is expressed as a product of two small matrices (rank-$r'$ decomposition). This low-rank update yields a parameter count proportional to $r'(d+n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight matrix, which can be far smaller than the full matrix. LoRA’s implicit regularization keeps finetuning updates in a low-dimensional subspace, which reduces the risk of destabilizing the pretrained model while still allowing expressive changes.

The trade-offs are explicit. Adapter/LoRA approaches reduce the number of tunable parameters and the storage burden of per-task weights, and they can make finetuning more stable than unconstrained full-weight finetuning. However, they do not guarantee preservation of structural properties of the pretrained weights beyond being a small or low-rank update. The paper positions OFT as an alternative that updates weights multiplicatively with an orthogonal transform; unlike additive low-rank updates, OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) exactly and thus aims to better retain the pretrained model’s semantic generation behavior.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter for controllable generation

Controllable generation addresses the limitation that text prompts alone can be ambiguous or insufficient to convey detailed structural constraints (for example, precise shape, pose, or layouts). Two adapter-style approaches cited in the paper, ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, explicitly finetune a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional per-sample control signals (e.g., Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, landmarks). They add new modules into the diffusion network that take the extra control input and produce features that guide the denoising process. Empirically these methods achieved impressive controllability: the added modules enable the pretrained generator to respect the new conditioning signals at inference time.

However, the paper points out practical limitations. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter introduce additional parameters and computation during inference: the extra adapter modules are present and invoked for each generation. They also typically require substantial dataset-specific finetuning and may converge slowly. The experiments in the paper directly compare these adapter-based methods to OFT (and COFT), noting that OFT can achieve strong controllability while introducing no additional inference overhead because its learned orthogonal matrices can be folded into the existing pretrained weights at inference time. In other words, unlike adapter modules that remain separate during generation, OFT yields a modified weight matrix that replaces the original without runtime cost increase.

# Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth) and its challenges

Subject-driven generation is the setting where a user provides a few images of a specific subject (for example, a particular person, object, or pet), and the goal is to generate new images of that same subject under novel contexts described by text prompts. DreamBooth is the canonical method referenced in the paper for this problem. DreamBooth finetunes the text-to-image diffusion model using a combination of a reconstruction (or likelihood) loss on the few subject images and a class-specific prior preservation loss. The latter is intended to prevent the model from overfitting to the small subject set in a way that collapses general generation ability.

Despite DreamBooth’s success, the paper highlights persistent challenges. First, finetuning the full model risks degrading the pretrained model’s general semantic generation capability if the fine-tuning process is not carefully constrained (for example, if the learning rate or iteration count is too large). Second, choosing finetuning hyperparameters and the number of training iterations is delicate: too little training will not capture subject identity, too much can cause model collapse or undesired drift. LoRA has been applied as a parameter-efficient variant in this context, but, as shown in the paper’s experiments, LoRA can still fail to preserve subject-specific identity reliably under the same iteration counts that OFT handles stably.

The key conceptual difficulty centers on what property of the pretrained network best correlates with preserved semantic generation. Existing finetuning methods implicitly treat small Euclidean weight differences as the desideratum. The paper proposes a different structural measure—hyperspherical energy, which encodes pairwise angular relationships among neurons after normalization—and argues that preserving this angular structure is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) operationalizes this idea by restricting the allowable adaptation to layer-shared orthogonal transformations of neuron directions, which by construction keep pairwise angles (and hence hyperspherical energy) unchanged. Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity to also limit Euclidean deviation, combining angular preservation and proximity to the original weights to improve stability.

By situating OFT relative to DreamBooth and adapter approaches, the paper frames finetuning as a trade-off: we want sufficient flexibility to achieve new behaviors (subject identity or control adherence) while imposing structural regularity that prevents destroying pretrained generative knowledge. The subsequent chapters in the paper develop the formalism of hyperspherical energy, the orthogonal parameterizations used to realize OFT efficiently, and empirical evaluations that demonstrate faster convergence, better stability, and stronger controllability compared to DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter across subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.
