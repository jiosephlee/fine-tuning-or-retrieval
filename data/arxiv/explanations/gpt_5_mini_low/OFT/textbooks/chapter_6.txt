\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 6: Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

# Challenges of differentiable orthogonalization (Gram–Schmidt costs)

When we design Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for large text-to-image diffusion models, a central practical requirement is to maintain the orthogonality constraint on the transformation matrices while keeping the computation tractable and differentiable. A straightforward mathematically-correct way to enforce orthogonality is to orthonormalize a parameter matrix during training, for example by applying Gram–Schmidt or QR decomposition to a raw parameter matrix and using the orthonormal factor as the actual transform. While such methods are differentiable in principle, they carry two major practical costs when applied to modern large models.

First, the computational cost is high. Gram–Schmidt and QR require $O(d^3)$ operations for an unconstrained $d\times d$ matrix when implemented naively, and repeatedly performing these operations for many layers and many gradient steps rapidly becomes a bottleneck. For very large $d$ (typical in transformer or convolution-channel dimensions) the repeated orthonormalization is expensive both in time and in memory, which reduces training throughput and increases GPU requirements.

Second, numerical stability and gradient complexity can become problematic. Although QR and related factorizations are differentiable, backpropagating through them introduces additional matrix operations and dense Jacobians. In practice this leads to added implementation complexity and sometimes unstable training dynamics unless carefully handled. Because OFT is intended to be an efficient, parameter‑efficient finetuning method that can be applied across many layers, these practical drawbacks motivate alternative parameterizations of orthogonal matrices that are both cheaper to compute and easier to differentiate.

# Cayley transform: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1}, Q skew-symmetric

To address the cost and differentiation issues, OFT adopts the Cayley transform as an efficient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices. The Cayley parameterization expresses an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
with the constraint that $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. This construction produces a matrix $\bm{R}$ that satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, i.e., $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, and it does so using a relatively compact and well-structured parameter $\bm{Q}$.

There are practical advantages to this parameterization. Computing $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{Q}$ requires only forming $\bm{I}\pm\bm{Q}$ and solving a linear system or computing an inverse of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$, an operation that can be implemented with standard batched linear algebra kernels. Compared with running a full Gram–Schmidt on a dense matrix every update, forming and inverting $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is typically cheaper and more amenable to modern GPU-accelerated linear solvers, especially when $\bm{Q}$ has exploitable structure (see block-diagonal below). Differentiation through this transformation is straightforward because matrix inversion and linear solves are supported by automatic differentiation frameworks; gradients can be computed with standard matrix‑calculus rules and efficiently implemented adjoint operations.

A practical point for training initialization: to start finetuning exactly from the pretrained weights, OFT initializes the orthogonal transformation to the identity. Under the Cayley parameterization this corresponds to initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$. This identity initialization aligns with the desideratum that the finetuned model starts from the pretrained model and then gradually adapts.

# Determinant constraint and practical implications

The Cayley transform has one structural limitation: it produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e., matrices in the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (rotations), but cannot represent orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ (improper rotations or reflections). In formula form, Cayley-parameterized $\bm{R}$ satisfy $\det(\bm{R})=1$.

In practical OFT usage this limitation is noted but found not to be harmful. The paper reports that restricting to $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ via the Cayley transform does not affect empirical performance in finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. Intuitively, the family of rotation-only orthogonal matrices is rich enough to provide the necessary angular adjustments of neuron directions that OFT requires for downstream adaptation. Because OFT's main goal is to preserve hyperspherical energy (pairwise angles among neurons) while providing a flexible yet constrained family of transformations, the inability to represent reflections (determinant $-1$) is a minor restriction in practice.

Nevertheless, this determinant constraint is worth keeping in mind. If a specific application demanded transformations that include reflections, an alternative orthogonal parameterization (e.g., using Householder sequences or Cayley combined with a fixed reflection) would be required; however, the paper's empirical findings justify using Cayley for the OFT setting due to its computational and implementation benefits.

# Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices: structure and intuition

Even with Cayley parameterization, naively parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix is expensive when $d$ is large, because the number of free parameters in a general orthogonal matrix scales as $d(d-1)/2$. To make OFT scalable and parameter-efficient, the method introduces block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. Concretely, the orthogonal transform is represented as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks:
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathrm{O}(d/r)$ is an orthogonal matrix acting on a subspace of dimension $d/r$. Each block can itself be Cayley-parameterized via a skew-symmetric block $\bm{Q}_i$, and the whole transform is orthogonal because the direct sum of orthogonal blocks is orthogonal.

There are several intuitive motivations for this structure. The block-diagonal form dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters and the computational cost of forming and applying $\bm{R}$, because each block is smaller. Functionally, block-diagonal orthogonality imposes structured regularization: different groups of neuron coordinates are rotated together, while interactions across blocks are not introduced. This is a beneficial bias in finetuning because it limits the degrees of freedom to a sweet spot between complete rigidity (identity) and full, unconstrained rotation. Empirically, the paper finds that this trade-off yields stable and effective finetuning: the block structure provides enough flexibility to adapt neuron directions for semantic control while preventing excessive disruption of pretrained generative capabilities.

When OFT is applied to convolutional layers, the block-diagonal interpretation has further natural meaning. Setting the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels makes each block operate as a per-channel orthogonal transform, analogous to depthwise operations; sharing blocks across channels corresponds to channel-shared rotations. This maps the mathematical block structure to intuitive architectural priors in convolutional networks.

# Parameter complexity: full vs r-block diagonal; optional block sharing

To quantify the parameter savings, consider a full orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$. The number of free parameters (degrees of freedom) in a general orthogonal matrix is $d(d-1)/2$, because skew-symmetric parameterization yields that many independent entries. Thus a full-$d$ Cayley parameterization would naively require on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

By contrast, for an $r$‑block diagonal orthogonal matrix where each block has size $d/r \times d/r$, the number of parameters becomes
\[
r \cdot \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2} = \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2},
\]
which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Intuitively, increasing $r$ (i.e., using more but smaller blocks) reduces parameter count and computational cost roughly proportionally to $1/r$.

An additional parameter-saving strategy is block sharing: one can choose to reuse the same block matrix across multiple block positions, i.e., set $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for many or all $i\neq j$. If all blocks are identical and shared, the number of parameters reduces further to the degrees of freedom of a single block, yielding parameter complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ when expressed in terms of $d$ and $r$. Block sharing introduces stronger regularization but can be appropriate when similar transformations across subspaces are desirable.

These structured parameterizations permit practical trade-offs: choose small $r$ (larger blocks) when more flexibility is needed and computing resources allow, or choose larger $r$ (smaller blocks and optional sharing) when parameter-efficiency and speed are paramount.

# Comparative parameter counts with LoRA on concrete sizes

It is useful to contrast OFT's parameter scaling to another popular finetuning method, LoRA, to see where OFT offers savings. LoRA models weight updates as low-rank additive terms: if the base weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is of size $d\times n$, LoRA parameterizes the update with two low-rank factors of rank $r'$, leading to $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters. This yields complexity roughly $\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))$.

For OFT with an $r$-block diagonal Cayley parameterization applied to the same layer, the parameter complexity is on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d(d/r-1)/2)$ (or lower with block sharing). To provide a concrete numeric comparison drawn from the paper: suppose we have a weight matrix of size $128\times 128$ and choose $r'=8$ for LoRA and $r=8$ blocks for OFT. LoRA with $r'=8$ produces $r'(d+n)=8(128+128)=8\cdot 256=2048$ trainable parameters. For OFT with $r=8$, the paper reports 960 trainable parameters. Thus in this example OFT uses fewer parameters than LoRA while preserving orthogonality and hyperspherical energy invariance. The general takeaway is that, for comparable expressivity choices (i.e., choices of $r$ vs $r'$ that grow with $d$), OFT can be more parameter-efficient in practice, especially when block-diagonal structure and block sharing are used.

Finally, while LoRA reduces parameter counts by imposing a low-rank additive structure, OFT reduces parameter counts by exploiting sparsity and structured orthogonality. These are complementary design philosophies: LoRA constrains the update space additively, whereas OFT constrains the multiplicative transformation applied to pretrained weights. The empirical evaluation in the paper shows that OFT's structured orthogonal parameterizations yield a favorable balance of parameter efficiency, stability, and finetuning performance for text-to-image diffusion models.
