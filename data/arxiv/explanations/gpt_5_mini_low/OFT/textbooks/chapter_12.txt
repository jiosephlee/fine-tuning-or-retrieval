\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 12: Extensions, Insights, and Open Problems

# Architecture-agnostic nature: transformers and convolutions (depth-wise view)

One of the key properties emphasized in the paper is that Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is fundamentally architecture-agnostic: in principle OFT can be applied to any neural network layer where neuron weight vectors are meaningful objects to transform. The paper discusses OFT in the context of fully connected layers and shows how the same idea carries over to attention matrices in Transformers and to convolutional layers. The central mechanism is the same across architectures: learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (or a block-diagonal composition of such matrices) and apply it multiplicatively to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, yielding finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles between normalized neuron vectors, OFT guarantees preservation of hyperspherical energy and the neuron relational structure that the paper argues is important for semantic generation.

For Transformers, the natural place to apply OFT is to the attention weight matrices, which are analogous to fully connected layers in their linear mapping behavior. The paper explicitly compares OFT to LoRA in this setting by applying OFT to the same attention weights that LoRA modifies, ensuring a fair experimental comparison. The identity initialization of $\bm{R}$ means the pretrained model is recovered at the start of finetuning, and the Cayley-based orthogonal parameterization makes it straightforward to impose orthogonality while optimizing in the same gradient-based training loop.

For convolutional layers the paper highlights two useful interpretations of a block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization. If the block partitioning aligns with input channels (e.g., one block per input channel), each block acts on a single channel's neuron vectors and the overall transform behaves similarly to depth-wise operations: each channel's filters are rotated independently. If the same orthogonal block is shared across channels, the transform becomes an orthogonal mapping applied uniformly across channels. These two extremes illustrate how block-diagonal OFT can interpolate between per-channel flexibility and global regularity, providing a convenient, interpretable lever to control parameter overhead and inductive bias specific to convolutional architectures.

# Compositionality potential: chaining orthogonal matrices across tasks

A notable conceptual insight discussed in the paper is the compositionality potential of OFT. Orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. That algebraic property immediately suggests an appealing operational idea: if we finetune the same pretrained model for multiple distinct downstream tasks, each finetuning could learn its own orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots$; composing those tasks by multiplying the corresponding matrices yields another orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}} = \bm{R}_k\cdots\bm{R}_2\bm{R}_1$, and thus a single orthogonal transform that is still guaranteed to preserve hyperspherical energy.

The paper frames this as an open direction: whether such multiplicative composition of task-specific orthogonal matrices preserves the knowledge required for all downstream tasks is an intriguing question. The algebra guarantees preservation of pairwise neuron angles (hence hyperspherical energy), but semantics for multiple tasks may interact nontrivially. Composition could, in principle, allow a compact mechanism for incremental adaptation: store per-task orthogonal factors and multiply them at inference time to assemble composite behaviors without modifying the original pretrained weights. However, the paper cautions that whether composed orthogonal matrices retain performance for all constituent tasks remains empirical and theoretical territory to explore. The idea is promising because it leverages a small, structured parameter (an orthogonal factor per task) and keeps the global property (angle preservation) intact by construction.

# Scalability: differentiable matrix inverse bottlenecks and faster parameterizations

A practical limitation acknowledged and discussed in the paper is the computational cost associated with enforcing orthogonality via Cayley parameterization, which requires computing matrix inverses of the form $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. While Cayley gives a differentiable and stable way to obtain orthogonal matrices (with determinant $+1$), matrix inversion is computationally heavier than the simple low-rank additive updates used by other methods. This cost motivates two of the paper's pragmatic countermeasures.

First, the block-diagonal parameterization reduces the effective dimensionality of each inverse by partitioning $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks of size $d/r$, so the inversion cost and parameter count scale with the block size rather than the full $d$. The number of parameters for an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix is $d(d/r-1)/2$, which reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ complexity. Sharing blocks across subdivisions can reduce parameter cost further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These structural reductions make Cayley viable even when $d$ is large.

Second, the paper recognizes this matrix inverse as a bottleneck and mentions it as an open problem: finding faster, differentiable parameterizations or approximations that retain orthogonality guarantees but avoid heavy inversion. The paper uses the Neumann series to reason about small deviations from identity: when $\bm{Q}$ is small, $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k$ and one can approximate $\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This expansion is used in COFT to move the radius constraint onto $\bm{Q}$ for easier enforcement via projection. More generally, such series-based approximations or other structured orthogonal parameterizations (e.g., products of Givens rotations, Householder reflections, or parameterizations that exploit sparsity) are pragmatic avenues suggested by the paper to improve scalability while preserving the desired mathematical properties.

# Bias–flexibility trade-offs in block-diagonal designs

A recurring theme in the paper is the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. OFT constrains finetuning to orthogonal transforms, thereby preserving hyperspherical energy; the block-diagonal parameterization imposes a stronger constraint that reduces parameter count and computational cost. This design introduces an inductive bias: the model cannot express arbitrary global rotations of the neuron space if blocks are small or shared. The paper presents this bias as a feature, not solely a drawback, because it stabilizes finetuning and prevents collapse: smaller blocks limit the degrees of freedom and therefore the risk of destroying pretrained generative structures while still allowing meaningful directional updates that modify semantics.

However, the paper also recognizes that block-diagonal structure limits flexibility and may introduce undesirable biases for certain layers or tasks that require cross-block interactions. Choosing the number of blocks $r$ (and whether to share blocks) becomes a practical hyperparameter balancing expressivity and parameter efficiency. Empirically, the paper reports that OFT performs well with modest blockization and that increasing the size or number of trainable parameters can further improve performance; this suggests the block design should be tuned according to resource budgets and the needed degree of adaptation. The paper highlights this bias–flexibility trade-off explicitly as an area for further research: can we devise parameter-efficient orthogonal structures that are less biased than naive block-diagonal splitting, or can adaptive blockization schemes be learned per layer or per task?

# Human evaluation considerations and additional control modalities

The paper reports quantitative evaluation metrics for both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS, IoU/F1, mean IoU, accuracy, landmark L2) and also mentions human evaluation and qualitative comparisons in appendices. It emphasizes that OFT not only improves automated metrics but also yields qualitatively better preservation of subject identity and stronger adherence to control signals. The paper calls out human evaluation in the appendices as corroborating evidence of OFT’s advantages.

Beyond the evaluated control modalities (Canny edges, segmentation maps, facial landmarks), the OFT framework is architecturally agnostic and therefore readily applicable to additional control signals that are commonly used in controllable generation research (the paper demonstrates further applicability in appendices to sketches, depth maps, dense pose, etc.). Because OFT works by rotating neuron directions and optionally rescaling magnitudes (in the re-scaled OFT variant), it is compatible with any control mechanism that is realized via finetuning weights to condition on auxiliary inputs. The paper emphasizes that OFT introduces no extra inference-time overhead, which is an important practical advantage when deploying models that support many control modalities: stored orthogonal matrices (or their multiplied equivalents) can be applied directly to pretrained weights, keeping inference latency identical to the base model.

For human evaluation, the paper indicates that user studies were performed and reported in the appendix; these studies further validate OFT’s strengths. The broad implication is that OFT’s angle-preserving constraint aligns with human judgments about semantic preservation and faithful control, but the paper also acknowledges that the design of human studies and the particular tasks/modalities chosen can influence conclusions, and that more expansive human-evaluation protocols across modalities and demographic axes remain valuable future work.

# Open questions: improved parameter efficiency, theory of semantic preservation via HE, multi-task orthogonal composition

The paper closes by enumerating several open problems and directions that naturally follow from OFT’s contributions. A first and practical open question is how to improve parameter and computational efficiency while retaining provable orthogonality and hyperspherical energy preservation. The block-diagonal Cayley approach is effective, but exploring parameterizations that reduce inversion cost or that distribute adaptivity more economically (e.g., sparse orthogonal factors, learned block partitions, products of structured low-cost rotations) are promising research directions mentioned in the paper.

A second, more theoretical open problem is understanding precisely why preserving hyperspherical energy (HE) corresponds to preserving pretrained generative semantics. The paper motivates HE as a measure of pairwise neuron relationships on the unit hypersphere and empirically finds that HE preservation correlates with maintenance of semantic generation ability. However, a formal theory that links HE or related angular statistics of neuron populations to generative semantics, diversity, and fidelity across architectures and training regimes is not developed in the paper and remains an important avenue for future work. Such theory could inform principled hyperparameter choices (e.g., block sizes, $\epsilon$ in COFT) and illuminate the limits of angular invariants in preserving task-general knowledge.

A third open question concerns multi-task composition and incremental adaptation. The algebraic compositionality of orthogonal matrices invites the hypothesis that chains of per-task orthogonal transforms can be composed to achieve multiple task behaviors without interference. The paper points out that whether composed orthogonal matrices preserve knowledge of all downstream tasks is unknown: composition preserves the hyperspherical energy property, but interactions among tasks might require non-orthogonal corrections or additional mechanisms (e.g., small diagonal rescalings in re-scaled OFT). Studying interference, catastrophic forgetting, and capacity limits for chains of orthogonal factors is a concrete multi-task research agenda suggested by the authors.

Finally, the paper mentions the constrained variant COFT, which imposes an explicit radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ (implemented approximately via constraining $\bm{Q}$ in the Cayley parameterization). Tuning and theoretically characterizing how such a radius trades off adaptability versus preservation—and how it interacts with data size, task difficulty, and model depth—are open empirical and theoretical questions. Taken together, these open problems chart a roadmap spanning engineering (faster parameterizations), empirical (multi-task and modality evaluations), and theoretical (why HE matters) research endeavors arising from OFT.

# Closing remarks

The OFT framework forwards a simple but powerful idea: constrain finetuning to layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that pairwise neuron angles—and the hyperspherical energy that summarizes them—are preserved. This produces a principled balance between the flexibility needed to adapt neuron directions (which the paper argues are central to semantic changes) and the regularity needed to avoid destroying pretrained generative capabilities. The architectural generality, the compositional algebraic properties, and the explicit open problems identified in the paper together form a fertile set of directions for future work: making orthogonal finetuning more scalable, more parameter-efficient, theoretically grounded, and broadly applicable across tasks and modalities.
