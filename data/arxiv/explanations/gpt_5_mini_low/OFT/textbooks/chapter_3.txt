\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: Hyperspherical Geometry and Energy

# Neuron direction vs magnitude: why angles matter
A central empirical observation motivating Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is that the directional component of neurons — their angles or orientations in weight space — encodes semantic information more strongly than their magnitudes. In other words, when a neuron (a filter, a weight vector, or a feature vector) is represented as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the unit-normalized direction of that vector often carries the bulk of the useful signal for representing input semantics. The paper motivates this claim through an intuitive experiment: a convolutional autoencoder trained with the usual inner-product activations can reconstruct input images almost perfectly if one replaces the internal activations by their angular (normalized) components alone, whereas using only magnitudes yields little semantic content. This empirical fact suggests that changing neuron directions is an effective way to modify semantic aspects of a model's behavior.

Why does this matter for finetuning large pretrained models? When adapting a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream control task (for example, subject-driven generation or controllable generation), we want to change the model's behavior in targeted semantic ways without destroying the pretrained model's broad generative capabilities. Because directions encode semantics, small, structured changes in directions can produce meaningful semantic adjustments (such as making a generated image depict a particular subject or conform to a control signal). In contrast, arbitrary changes to magnitudes or to a mix of magnitude and direction tend to be less semantically interpretable and can more easily disrupt the pretrained model. Thus, focusing finetuning on neuron directions provides a natural mechanism for achieving targeted semantic control with better preservation of the pretrained model's capabilities.

# Cosine similarity and angular representations in deep learning
A natural mathematical proxy for neuron direction is cosine similarity. Given two vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, their cosine similarity is
\[
\cos\theta(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|},
\]
which depends only on the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors. When we normalize each neuron to unit length, inner products reduce to cosine similarities and comparisons among neurons become purely angular. Several lines of empirical work referenced in the paper (e.g., SphereNet and angular-margin observations) indicate that angular relationships between neurons or features capture semantic similarity well: models that constrain or emphasize directions on the unit hypersphere can match or exceed performance compared to unconstrained models, highlighting that much of the representational power resides in directions rather than magnitudes.

Working with angular representations thus shifts analysis and optimization onto the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1} = \{ \mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d: \|\mathbf{w}\|=1\}$. On this manifold, the natural similarity measure is angular (cosine) similarity and distances are related to angles: for unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$, the Euclidean distance satisfies
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\| = 2\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta_{ij}}{2}\right),
\]
so angle and chord distance are tightly linked. Emphasizing cosine similarity therefore provides a direct way to reason about pairwise neuron relationships in a normalized, geometry-aware manner that is invariant to scalings of individual neurons.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy (HE) for a layer
Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar summary statistic that captures the aggregate pairwise angular relationships among all neurons in a layer, after normalizing each neuron to lie on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ represent neurons, define the normalized neurons
\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},\quad i=1,\dots,n.
\]
The hyperspherical energy of the layer is then defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Equivalently, since the chord distance $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is a monotone function of the angle between the two unit vectors, HE is a sum of pairwise inverse-chord distances and thus is sensitive to how clustered or uniformly spread the neuron directions are on the hypersphere.

HE is a single-layer statistic: it aggregates over all unordered pairs $(i,j)$ of neurons in that layer. Because normalization removes magnitudes, HE focuses purely on angular relationships. Small values of HE correspond to neurons that are more uniformly distributed on the sphere (large inter-neuron angular separation), while large HE arises when neurons are more clustered (small angular separations) so that inverse distances are large.

# HE as a proxy for pairwise neuron uniformity and semantic structure
Hyperspherical energy has an interpretation as measuring uniformity versus clustering of neuron directions. If neurons are uniformly spread over the sphere, pairwise chord distances are, on average, large, making each inverse distance small; summing over pairs yields a smaller HE. Conversely, if many neurons are pointed in similar directions, some pairwise distances become small, and their inverses blow up, increasing HE. Thus HE quantifies the degree of redundancy or concentration among normalized neurons.

Why is this useful as a proxy for semantic structure preservation? The paper argues (and empirically observes) that the pretrained generative capabilities of large text-to-image diffusion models are reflected in the angular arrangement of their neurons. In other words, the set of pairwise angles among neurons in pretrained weights encodes relationships that underpin the model's broad semantic performance. When finetuning, preserving these pairwise angular relationships — and therefore preserving HE — helps maintain the pretrained model's generalization and image-generation quality while allowing controlled, semantically meaningful adjustments. In practice, the authors found that methods which implicitly or explicitly keep HE close to the pretrained model perform better at keeping the pretrained semantic generation ability intact during adaptation to downstream tasks.

The paper contrasts HE preservation with naive Euclidean closeness (minimizing $\|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^0\|$), pointing out that a small Euclidean change is not a sufficient condition to preserve semantic structure: two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm yet have substantially different angular configurations once neurons are normalized. HE provides a structural measure that directly targets angular relationships among neurons and is thus more aligned with preserving semantic generation behavior.

# Invariance of pairwise angular relationships under orthogonal transforms
A key mathematical property used by OFT is the invariance of pairwise angles (and thus HE) under orthogonal transformations. An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, and multiplication by $\mathbf{R}$ performs a rotation and/or reflection of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$. For any two neurons $\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j$, applying the same orthogonal transform yields
\[
\cos\theta(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j) = \frac{(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)\cdot(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{w}_j\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{w}_j\|},
\]
so the cosine similarity — hence the angle — is preserved. Because HE depends only on normalized pairwise distances (which in turn depend only on pairwise angles), HE is invariant under a shared orthogonal transform applied to all neurons in a layer:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

This invariance is the theoretical linchpin of OFT. Instead of updating each neuron independently (which can change the entire angular relationship structure), OFT restricts finetuning updates to learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$:
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0,\quad\text{with}\ \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}.
\]
Because such an update preserves all pairwise angles between neurons in the layer, HE is provably preserved. Thus OFT directly enforces the principle of keeping HE unchanged while allowing a controlled degree of directional change (a global rotation) that adapts the model's behavior.

Preserving HE through orthogonal transforms therefore provides a principled way to maintain the pretrained model's semantic structure: the relative angular geometry among neurons — hypothesized to encode important semantic priors — remains intact, reducing the risk of catastrophic drift in the model's generative ability during finetuning.

# Intuition from Fourier magnitude/phase separation
The paper draws an analogy between neuron direction/magnitude separation and the decomposition of an image into magnitude and phase in the Fourier domain. In a 2D Fourier transform of an image, the transform yields complex coefficients with both magnitude and phase. It is a well-known empirical fact (cited as motivating intuition) that the phase spectrum contains most of the structural and semantic content of an image: if one keeps the phase spectrum and replaces the magnitude spectrum with random values, the reconstructed image still retains much of its recognizable structure and semantic content. Conversely, preserving magnitude while randomizing phase destroys the recognizable structure.

This observation maps naturally to the neuron vector picture. If phase corresponds to angular relationships and magnitude corresponds to scalar scales, then the analogy suggests that angular relationships among neurons encode the semantics of what the network represents. As with images, altering magnitudes while preserving phases tends to preserve semantic structure, whereas tampering with phases (angles) in an uncontrolled manner can abolish semantics. The paper uses this analogy to justify focusing finetuning effort on controlled updates to angles: by applying orthogonal transforms, OFT changes neuron orientations in a constrained, coherent way that preserves the overall angular geometry (akin to preserving phase relationships) while enabling the model to adapt to new tasks.

This Fourier intuition supports the design choice of OFT: orthogonal, layer-shared transformations provide degrees of freedom to steer the network's outputs in semantically meaningful ways, but because they preserve pairwise angles (phase-like relationships), they avoid destroying the pretrained semantic priors. Coupling this with additional constraints (e.g., Constrained OFT) that limit the magnitude of the orthogonal deviation further balances flexibility with stability.

# Summary
Hyperspherical energy offers a geometric, angle-centered statistic that captures the internal angular structure among neurons in a layer after normalization. Empirically and intuitively, neuron directions (angles) carry substantial semantic information, and cosine similarity is the natural measure for comparing these directions. HE quantifies layer-wide pairwise angular relationships and serves as a proxy for the pretrained semantic organization that one wishes to preserve during finetuning. Orthogonal transformations are the natural class of updates that leave HE unchanged, and the phase/magnitude intuition from Fourier analysis helps explain why preserving angular relationships is crucial for maintaining semantics while permitting controlled adaptations. Orthogonal Finetuning leverages these insights: it adapts pretrained networks by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that pairwise angular geometry — and thus hyperspherical energy — is preserved, striking a principled compromise between adaptability and semantic preservation.
