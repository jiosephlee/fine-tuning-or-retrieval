\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Introduction and Motivation

# Limits of text-only control in diffusion models

Large text-to-image diffusion models have recently achieved striking success at producing photorealistic and semantically rich images from textual descriptions. Examples include models like Imagen, GLIDE and Stable Diffusion, which couple powerful denoising diffusion generative processes with language-conditioned guidance. Despite this progress, relying on text alone to specify desired images has intrinsic limitations. Natural language is inherently ambiguous and often underspecified. A prompt like "a portrait of Alex in a café" leaves many crucial image attributes underspecified: the precise subject identity, pose, viewpoint, lighting, fine-grained clothing details, or the geometric layout of the scene. Even when a text prompt attempts to be more specific, some kinds of control (for example, exact pose, precise object contours, or the appearance of a specific subject pictured in a few photos) are difficult to communicate compactly in words.

Practically, this ambiguity shows up as model outputs that match the high-level semantics of the prompt but fail to capture particular details users care about. This is especially problematic in applications that require subject fidelity (recreating a specific person or object) or precise adherence to spatial structure (matching an input edge map or segmentation). Consequently, researchers and practitioners have sought ways to augment text-only conditioning with other signals or with mechanisms that steer the pretrained generative model toward a downstream objective. But augmenting or altering a large pretrained model raises a second difficulty: adapting the model to new control modalities or to represent a particular subject must be done without destroying the model's broad pretraining capabilities (its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images across prompts).

# Downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

Two concrete downstream tasks exemplify the need for more precise control beyond text alone.

Subject-driven generation targets the setting where a user provides a small set of images of a particular subject (for example, a few photos of a person, a pet, or a custom object) and then desires images that place that subject into new contexts specified by text prompts. The challenge is to incorporate the subject-specific appearance—identity, texture, distinct marks—so that the generated images genuinely depict the same subject across varied backgrounds and scenes. The task is difficult because it must generalize the subject to new poses and contexts while maintaining fidelity of identity and appearance; naive finetuning can overfit or corrupt the pretrained model's generalization.

Controllable generation refers to leveraging additional structured control signals along with text, such as edge maps (Canny), segmentation maps, landmark/pose keypoints, depth maps, or other spatial constraints. The goal is to make the diffusion model respect these structured signals so that generated images match the desired geometry or semantics as well as the text. Approaches in this family include methods that add adapter modules or finetune the original model to accept the control signal. The challenge here is similar: gain reliable, accurate control while keeping the high image quality and diversity that the pretrained model already possesses.

Both tasks share a central tension: they require modifying or augmenting a pretrained model to incorporate new, stronger control, but this adaptation must preserve the pretrained model's broad generation abilities. Achieving this without excessive compute cost, without needing large amounts of task-specific data, and without compromising model stability during training are the core practical demands.

# Desiderata for finetuning: efficiency and preservation of generalization

When we think about how to adapt massive pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks, two desiderata naturally arise.

First, training (or finetuning) efficiency. Large diffusion models have many weights, so standard full finetuning is computationally expensive and often unnecessary when the downstream task is narrow. Efficient finetuning aims to update far fewer parameters, reduce GPU hours, require fewer training images, and converge in fewer iterations. Model- and parameter-efficient methods (adapters, low-rank updates) have become popular because they allow rapid adaptation and make it practical to deploy many specialized variants.

Second, preservation of the model's pretrained generalization and high-fidelity generation. The pretrained model encodes vast visual knowledge and a careful balance of parameters that produce diverse realistic images across prompts. An effective finetuning procedure should not inadvertently erase or corrupt that knowledge. In other words, we want to adapt the model so it gains the new control behaviors while retaining the creative and semantic abilities it learned during pretraining.

These two desiderata together create a trade-off. A highly flexible finetuning method can fit the downstream objective but risks destroying pretrained capabilities; a strongly constrained method may preserve pretraining but lack the flexibility to learn the new control. A principled finetuning approach must find a sweet spot in this trade-off: sufficient flexibility to learn the downstream constraint, but enough structural regularity that the model's semantic generation properties are preserved.

# Shortcomings of conventional finetuning and adapter-style approaches

Typical finetuning strategies fall into two broad categories.

The first is conventional gradient-based finetuning: updating some or all of the pretrained weights using a small learning rate. The small step sizes are intended to keep the final weights close in Euclidean norm to the pretrained weights, under the implicit belief that small Euclidean changes preserve the model's prior capabilities. While simple, this approach offers no principled guarantee that semantic behaviors (the kinds of images the model generates, or how it composes semantics) are preserved. Small Euclidean distance does not directly constrain higher-level structural properties of weight matrices that encode semantic relationships. Moreover, even with small learning rates, finetuning large generators can be unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters like number of iterations; finetuning runs can collapse or overfit, especially for small datasets typical in subject-driven tasks.

The second class includes adapter-style or reparameterization methods such as LoRA and other parameter-efficient adaptation techniques. These add a small learnable module—often modeled as an additive low-rank update to certain weight matrices—or insert small adapter blocks that are trained while the bulk of the model remains frozen. Adapter methods reduce the number of trainable parameters and have shown practical success, but they also lack principled constraints that directly capture the structural relations among neurons. For instance, LoRA assumes a low-rank structure for the additive weight update, which controls capacity but does not provide guarantees about the pairwise geometric relationships among neurons. Consequently, adapter approaches can still change the geometry of neuron representations in ways that degrade the pretrained semantic behavior.

Both approaches implicitly rely on proximity in Euclidean norm (small steps, small additive updates) as a proxy for preserving pretrained behavior. The paper argues that Euclidean proximity alone is inadequate: it does not fully capture the relational or angular structure among neurons that underlies how the model represents semantics. Thus a finetuning strategy that directly preserves more meaningful structural invariants of the pretrained model could yield better behavior: retaining pretraining semantics while allowing targeted, efficient adaptation.

# High-level idea: preserving pairwise neuron geometry on the hypersphere

The central insight of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in each layer during finetuning. These angular relations are naturally captured by treating neuron vectors as points on a unit hypersphere and measuring pairwise similarity via normalized vectors, typically using cosine or angular distances. The hyperspherical energy is a scalar quantity that aggregates these pairwise relations across a layer: formally, for a weight matrix with neurons \(\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\) and normalized neurons \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\), the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
which is a sum of inverse chordal distances and depends only on the pairwise angles among normalized neurons. Empirical and theoretical work cited in the paper suggests that angular relationships among features carry much of the semantic information learned by neural networks; SphereNet-like observations show direction can be more informative than magnitude in many representation settings. A toy experiment in the paper's introduction illustrates that angular (phase-like) information of convolutional kernels retains most semantic content, while magnitudes contain little.

Because hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise angular structure, preserving it between a finetuned model and the pretrained model is a stronger and more semantically meaningful constraint than preserving Euclidean distance alone. However, directly regularizing the hyperspherical energy with a penalty during optimization can be difficult to enforce exactly and may not reach zero difference.

OFT leverages a key invariance: if all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) (a rotation/reflection), then their pairwise angles are preserved exactly. That is, replacing \(\bm{W}^0\) by \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) leaves \(\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\). Orthogonal transformations are therefore a natural family of parameter updates that guarantee hyperspherical energy preservation. Rather than adding arbitrary additive updates or applying unconstrained gradient steps, OFT constrains finetuning to learn layer-shared orthogonal transformations \(\bm{R}\) (initialized as identity so training starts from the pretrained weights). This multiplicative, orthogonal re-parameterization effectively rotates the neuron directions without altering their pairwise angular geometry.

This design achieves several objectives simultaneously. Because the angular geometry is preserved, many of the pretrained model's semantic generation properties persist. At the same time, orthogonal transforms change neuron directions collectively in a structured way, giving the model meaningful ways to alter semantics where needed: changing global direction bases can effect controlled modifications (for example, adapting identity features for a subject). OFT therefore finds a middle ground: it restricts finetuning to transformations that keep hyperspherical energy invariant—providing strong regularity—while allowing targeted, efficient adaptation by learning the orthogonal matrices.

The paper further refines this idea with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which additionally constrains the learned orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) to remain near the identity. This explicitly limits the deviation from the pretrained model in operator norm (a radius constraint), giving a tunable trade-off between flexibility and stability. COFT can help prevent over-adjustment and yields even more stable finetuning dynamics in practice. Other practical design choices discussed include efficient orthogonal parameterizations (Cayley transform, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices) and a rescaled variant that also learns magnitudes per neuron while preserving angular invariants.

Taken together, these ideas motivate OFT as a principled finetuning strategy: instead of using Euclidean proximity as the proxy for semantic preservation, OFT preserves pairwise neuron geometry on the hypersphere, which the paper argues (and empirically supports) is a more faithful structural invariant for retaining pretrained generative semantics while enabling efficient adaptation to subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.

Background: Diffusion Models and Finetuning Landscape

# Text-to-image diffusion models: pixel-space vs latent-space (GLIDE, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2)

Text-to-image diffusion models are generative systems trained to reverse a gradual noising process and thereby synthesize images from conditioning signals such as text prompts. Within the family of diffusion-based image generators, two main engineering choices have emerged in the literature discussed in the paper: training the diffusion model in pixel space, or training it in a latent space. The distinction matters because it affects model capacity, computational cost, and how finetuning or control mechanisms are attached.

Pixel-space models operate directly on image pixels: the diffusion process adds noise to images in the pixel domain and the denoising model is trained to predict the clean image (or score) from noisy pixels and conditioning. The paper cites GLIDE and Imagen as representative pixel-space text-to-image diffusion models. GLIDE trains a text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data. Imagen trains diffusion models in pixel space while using a frozen pretrained text encoder. Training in pixel space typically yields very high-fidelity output because the model directly models the image distribution, but it can be computationally heavy due to the high dimensionality of pixel space.

Latent-space models perform diffusion in a lower-dimensional learned latent representation of images. A separate encoder/decoder (a learned visual codebook or other mapping) maps images to and from that latent. Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 are cited as prominent latent-space approaches. Stable Diffusion uses a VQ-GAN learned visual codebook as its latent space and trains diffusion models in that latent; DALL·E 2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space for representing images and text. Operating in latent space reduces the dimensionality the diffusion model must handle, improving computational and memory efficiency while still allowing high-quality outputs after decoding. The choice between pixel-space and latent-space setups interacts with finetuning and control strategies discussed later: some control or adapter modules attach to the diffusion U-Net or to components that map between text and image latents, and whether the model is pixel- or latent-based constrains where and how such modules are integrated.

# Text and vision-language backbones (CLIP, VQ-GAN, text encoders)

Text-to-image diffusion systems leverage separate components to represent text and images. The paper highlights a few backbone technologies used in contemporary models. CLIP is mentioned as the joint vision-language model used by DALL·E 2 to produce a shared embedding space for images and text; CLIP embeddings are also used as evaluation features (CLIP-I and CLIP-T metrics) in experiments described by the paper. VQ-GAN is referenced as the visual codebook approach used by Stable Diffusion to build a compact latent representation in which the diffusion model operates.

More generically, the architectures combine three functional blocks: an image encoder/decoder or codebook for mapping between pixels and latents (VQ-GAN style), a diffusion U-Net that performs the denoising conditioned on text and possibly other signals, and a text encoder that maps prompts to conditioning vectors. Implementation choices—whether the text encoder is trained jointly (as in GLIDE) or frozen (as in Imagen), and whether the image representation is a learned codebook (Stable Diffusion) or handled in pixel space—shape how finetuning is performed. For example, DreamBooth-style finetuning is typically applied to the diffusion model weights (the U-Net) and uses the text encoder and image encoder/decoder as part of the fixed pipeline; ControlNet and adapter methods attach additional learned modules to the diffusion backbone so they can incorporate extra control signals without entirely changing the pretrained weights.

# Standard finetuning with small learning rates

A straightforward approach to adapt a pretrained large text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream task is to continue training (finetuning) its weights on task-specific data, typically with a much smaller learning rate than used during pretraining. The intuition behind using a small learning rate is that the pretrained model already captures general generative knowledge and we want to preserve that knowledge while nudging the model to handle task-specific signals. In this style of finetuning, the objective minimized during adaptation is the same as the pretraining objective (e.g., the denoising or reconstruction loss used in diffusion models), but over the new task dataset. Because the updates are small, this implicitly keeps the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the original pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$ small, i.e., the optimization implicitly limits $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$.

The paper notes that while this small-step finetuning is simple and often effective, it provides no principled guarantee for preserving the pretrained model’s generative behavior beyond the small Euclidean change. In practice this leads to several shortcomings: finetuning can still destroy desirable generic generative abilities if training runs too long or the learning rate is not carefully tuned, and choosing hyperparameters such as iteration count or learning rate becomes delicate. In other words, Euclidean proximity to the pretrained parameters is an intuitive but incomplete proxy for preserving the model’s semantic generation capability.

# Adapter-based finetuning (Adapters, LoRA): mechanisms and trade-offs

Adapter-based finetuning methods take a different route: instead of modifying the original weights directly, they introduce a small additional module or parameterization whose output modifies the model’s computation. Two examples discussed in the paper are Adapters in the NLP literature and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). The shared motivation is parameter efficiency and safer adaptation: by keeping most pretrained weights frozen and only learning a compact set of extra parameters, these methods reduce storage and computation for task-specific adaptations and aim to avoid catastrophic changes to the pretrained model.

LoRA is highlighted as particularly relevant. It models the additive update to a weight matrix during finetuning as a low-rank decomposition: the weight change is expressed as a product of two small matrices (rank-$r'$ decomposition). This low-rank update yields a parameter count proportional to $r'(d+n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight matrix, which can be far smaller than the full matrix. LoRA’s implicit regularization keeps finetuning updates in a low-dimensional subspace, which reduces the risk of destabilizing the pretrained model while still allowing expressive changes.

The trade-offs are explicit. Adapter/LoRA approaches reduce the number of tunable parameters and the storage burden of per-task weights, and they can make finetuning more stable than unconstrained full-weight finetuning. However, they do not guarantee preservation of structural properties of the pretrained weights beyond being a small or low-rank update. The paper positions OFT as an alternative that updates weights multiplicatively with an orthogonal transform; unlike additive low-rank updates, OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) exactly and thus aims to better retain the pretrained model’s semantic generation behavior.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter for controllable generation

Controllable generation addresses the limitation that text prompts alone can be ambiguous or insufficient to convey detailed structural constraints (for example, precise shape, pose, or layouts). Two adapter-style approaches cited in the paper, ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, explicitly finetune a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional per-sample control signals (e.g., Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, landmarks). They add new modules into the diffusion network that take the extra control input and produce features that guide the denoising process. Empirically these methods achieved impressive controllability: the added modules enable the pretrained generator to respect the new conditioning signals at inference time.

However, the paper points out practical limitations. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter introduce additional parameters and computation during inference: the extra adapter modules are present and invoked for each generation. They also typically require substantial dataset-specific finetuning and may converge slowly. The experiments in the paper directly compare these adapter-based methods to OFT (and COFT), noting that OFT can achieve strong controllability while introducing no additional inference overhead because its learned orthogonal matrices can be folded into the existing pretrained weights at inference time. In other words, unlike adapter modules that remain separate during generation, OFT yields a modified weight matrix that replaces the original without runtime cost increase.

# Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth) and its challenges

Subject-driven generation is the setting where a user provides a few images of a specific subject (for example, a particular person, object, or pet), and the goal is to generate new images of that same subject under novel contexts described by text prompts. DreamBooth is the canonical method referenced in the paper for this problem. DreamBooth finetunes the text-to-image diffusion model using a combination of a reconstruction (or likelihood) loss on the few subject images and a class-specific prior preservation loss. The latter is intended to prevent the model from overfitting to the small subject set in a way that collapses general generation ability.

Despite DreamBooth’s success, the paper highlights persistent challenges. First, finetuning the full model risks degrading the pretrained model’s general semantic generation capability if the fine-tuning process is not carefully constrained (for example, if the learning rate or iteration count is too large). Second, choosing finetuning hyperparameters and the number of training iterations is delicate: too little training will not capture subject identity, too much can cause model collapse or undesired drift. LoRA has been applied as a parameter-efficient variant in this context, but, as shown in the paper’s experiments, LoRA can still fail to preserve subject-specific identity reliably under the same iteration counts that OFT handles stably.

The key conceptual difficulty centers on what property of the pretrained network best correlates with preserved semantic generation. Existing finetuning methods implicitly treat small Euclidean weight differences as the desideratum. The paper proposes a different structural measure—hyperspherical energy, which encodes pairwise angular relationships among neurons after normalization—and argues that preserving this angular structure is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) operationalizes this idea by restricting the allowable adaptation to layer-shared orthogonal transformations of neuron directions, which by construction keep pairwise angles (and hence hyperspherical energy) unchanged. Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity to also limit Euclidean deviation, combining angular preservation and proximity to the original weights to improve stability.

By situating OFT relative to DreamBooth and adapter approaches, the paper frames finetuning as a trade-off: we want sufficient flexibility to achieve new behaviors (subject identity or control adherence) while imposing structural regularity that prevents destroying pretrained generative knowledge. The subsequent chapters in the paper develop the formalism of hyperspherical energy, the orthogonal parameterizations used to realize OFT efficiently, and empirical evaluations that demonstrate faster convergence, better stability, and stronger controllability compared to DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter across subject-driven and controllable generation tasks.

Hyperspherical Geometry and Energy

# Neuron direction vs magnitude: why angles matter
A central empirical observation motivating Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is that the directional component of neurons — their angles or orientations in weight space — encodes semantic information more strongly than their magnitudes. In other words, when a neuron (a filter, a weight vector, or a feature vector) is represented as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the unit-normalized direction of that vector often carries the bulk of the useful signal for representing input semantics. The paper motivates this claim through an intuitive experiment: a convolutional autoencoder trained with the usual inner-product activations can reconstruct input images almost perfectly if one replaces the internal activations by their angular (normalized) components alone, whereas using only magnitudes yields little semantic content. This empirical fact suggests that changing neuron directions is an effective way to modify semantic aspects of a model's behavior.

Why does this matter for finetuning large pretrained models? When adapting a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream control task (for example, subject-driven generation or controllable generation), we want to change the model's behavior in targeted semantic ways without destroying the pretrained model's broad generative capabilities. Because directions encode semantics, small, structured changes in directions can produce meaningful semantic adjustments (such as making a generated image depict a particular subject or conform to a control signal). In contrast, arbitrary changes to magnitudes or to a mix of magnitude and direction tend to be less semantically interpretable and can more easily disrupt the pretrained model. Thus, focusing finetuning on neuron directions provides a natural mechanism for achieving targeted semantic control with better preservation of the pretrained model's capabilities.

# Cosine similarity and angular representations in deep learning
A natural mathematical proxy for neuron direction is cosine similarity. Given two vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, their cosine similarity is
\[
\cos\theta(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|},
\]
which depends only on the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors. When we normalize each neuron to unit length, inner products reduce to cosine similarities and comparisons among neurons become purely angular. Several lines of empirical work referenced in the paper (e.g., SphereNet and angular-margin observations) indicate that angular relationships between neurons or features capture semantic similarity well: models that constrain or emphasize directions on the unit hypersphere can match or exceed performance compared to unconstrained models, highlighting that much of the representational power resides in directions rather than magnitudes.

Working with angular representations thus shifts analysis and optimization onto the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1} = \{ \mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d: \|\mathbf{w}\|=1\}$. On this manifold, the natural similarity measure is angular (cosine) similarity and distances are related to angles: for unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$, the Euclidean distance satisfies
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\| = 2\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta_{ij}}{2}\right),
\]
so angle and chord distance are tightly linked. Emphasizing cosine similarity therefore provides a direct way to reason about pairwise neuron relationships in a normalized, geometry-aware manner that is invariant to scalings of individual neurons.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy (HE) for a layer
Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a scalar summary statistic that captures the aggregate pairwise angular relationships among all neurons in a layer, after normalizing each neuron to lie on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ represent neurons, define the normalized neurons
\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},\quad i=1,\dots,n.
\]
The hyperspherical energy of the layer is then defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Equivalently, since the chord distance $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is a monotone function of the angle between the two unit vectors, HE is a sum of pairwise inverse-chord distances and thus is sensitive to how clustered or uniformly spread the neuron directions are on the hypersphere.

HE is a single-layer statistic: it aggregates over all unordered pairs $(i,j)$ of neurons in that layer. Because normalization removes magnitudes, HE focuses purely on angular relationships. Small values of HE correspond to neurons that are more uniformly distributed on the sphere (large inter-neuron angular separation), while large HE arises when neurons are more clustered (small angular separations) so that inverse distances are large.

# HE as a proxy for pairwise neuron uniformity and semantic structure
Hyperspherical energy has an interpretation as measuring uniformity versus clustering of neuron directions. If neurons are uniformly spread over the sphere, pairwise chord distances are, on average, large, making each inverse distance small; summing over pairs yields a smaller HE. Conversely, if many neurons are pointed in similar directions, some pairwise distances become small, and their inverses blow up, increasing HE. Thus HE quantifies the degree of redundancy or concentration among normalized neurons.

Why is this useful as a proxy for semantic structure preservation? The paper argues (and empirically observes) that the pretrained generative capabilities of large text-to-image diffusion models are reflected in the angular arrangement of their neurons. In other words, the set of pairwise angles among neurons in pretrained weights encodes relationships that underpin the model's broad semantic performance. When finetuning, preserving these pairwise angular relationships — and therefore preserving HE — helps maintain the pretrained model's generalization and image-generation quality while allowing controlled, semantically meaningful adjustments. In practice, the authors found that methods which implicitly or explicitly keep HE close to the pretrained model perform better at keeping the pretrained semantic generation ability intact during adaptation to downstream tasks.

The paper contrasts HE preservation with naive Euclidean closeness (minimizing $\|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^0\|$), pointing out that a small Euclidean change is not a sufficient condition to preserve semantic structure: two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean norm yet have substantially different angular configurations once neurons are normalized. HE provides a structural measure that directly targets angular relationships among neurons and is thus more aligned with preserving semantic generation behavior.

# Invariance of pairwise angular relationships under orthogonal transforms
A key mathematical property used by OFT is the invariance of pairwise angles (and thus HE) under orthogonal transformations. An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, and multiplication by $\mathbf{R}$ performs a rotation and/or reflection of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$. For any two neurons $\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j$, applying the same orthogonal transform yields
\[
\cos\theta(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j) = \frac{(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)\cdot(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{w}_j\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\|\mathbf{w}_j\|},
\]
so the cosine similarity — hence the angle — is preserved. Because HE depends only on normalized pairwise distances (which in turn depend only on pairwise angles), HE is invariant under a shared orthogonal transform applied to all neurons in a layer:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

This invariance is the theoretical linchpin of OFT. Instead of updating each neuron independently (which can change the entire angular relationship structure), OFT restricts finetuning updates to learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$:
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0,\quad\text{with}\ \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}.
\]
Because such an update preserves all pairwise angles between neurons in the layer, HE is provably preserved. Thus OFT directly enforces the principle of keeping HE unchanged while allowing a controlled degree of directional change (a global rotation) that adapts the model's behavior.

Preserving HE through orthogonal transforms therefore provides a principled way to maintain the pretrained model's semantic structure: the relative angular geometry among neurons — hypothesized to encode important semantic priors — remains intact, reducing the risk of catastrophic drift in the model's generative ability during finetuning.

# Intuition from Fourier magnitude/phase separation
The paper draws an analogy between neuron direction/magnitude separation and the decomposition of an image into magnitude and phase in the Fourier domain. In a 2D Fourier transform of an image, the transform yields complex coefficients with both magnitude and phase. It is a well-known empirical fact (cited as motivating intuition) that the phase spectrum contains most of the structural and semantic content of an image: if one keeps the phase spectrum and replaces the magnitude spectrum with random values, the reconstructed image still retains much of its recognizable structure and semantic content. Conversely, preserving magnitude while randomizing phase destroys the recognizable structure.

This observation maps naturally to the neuron vector picture. If phase corresponds to angular relationships and magnitude corresponds to scalar scales, then the analogy suggests that angular relationships among neurons encode the semantics of what the network represents. As with images, altering magnitudes while preserving phases tends to preserve semantic structure, whereas tampering with phases (angles) in an uncontrolled manner can abolish semantics. The paper uses this analogy to justify focusing finetuning effort on controlled updates to angles: by applying orthogonal transforms, OFT changes neuron orientations in a constrained, coherent way that preserves the overall angular geometry (akin to preserving phase relationships) while enabling the model to adapt to new tasks.

This Fourier intuition supports the design choice of OFT: orthogonal, layer-shared transformations provide degrees of freedom to steer the network's outputs in semantically meaningful ways, but because they preserve pairwise angles (phase-like relationships), they avoid destroying the pretrained semantic priors. Coupling this with additional constraints (e.g., Constrained OFT) that limit the magnitude of the orthogonal deviation further balances flexibility with stability.

# Summary
Hyperspherical energy offers a geometric, angle-centered statistic that captures the internal angular structure among neurons in a layer after normalization. Empirically and intuitively, neuron directions (angles) carry substantial semantic information, and cosine similarity is the natural measure for comparing these directions. HE quantifies layer-wide pairwise angular relationships and serves as a proxy for the pretrained semantic organization that one wishes to preserve during finetuning. Orthogonal transformations are the natural class of updates that leave HE unchanged, and the phase/magnitude intuition from Fourier analysis helps explain why preserving angular relationships is crucial for maintaining semantics while permitting controlled adaptations. Orthogonal Finetuning leverages these insights: it adapts pretrained networks by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that pairwise angular geometry — and thus hyperspherical energy — is preserved, striking a principled compromise between adaptability and semantic preservation.

Why Orthogonal Transformations for Finetuning?

# Rotations/reflections as the simplest global direction-only updates

When we think about adapting a large pretrained neural network to a new task, a central question is what degrees of freedom we should allow during finetuning. One very natural set of operations are rotations and reflections of the neuron coordinate system within a layer. Concretely, consider a weight matrix for a fully connected layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\{\bm{w}^0_i\}_{i=1}^n$ are the neurons (or filters flattened) in that layer. Applying a global rotation or reflection corresponds to multiplying the pretrained weights on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ so that the finetuned weights become $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The orthogonality condition is $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, and depending on $\det(\bm{R})$ the transform is a rotation ($+1$) or a reflection ($-1$).

This family of transforms has a simple and attractive interpretation: it changes only neuron directions (their orientation in $\mathbb{R}^d$) while leaving relative angular relationships — the angles between neurons — intact up to a global change of basis. Because many semantic properties of neural representations appear to be encoded in directions rather than magnitudes, such direction-only updates offer a minimalist, structurally constrained way to steer the network towards new behavior while touching the pretrained geometry as little as possible. In practice, initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ gives a zero-change starting point so finetuning proceeds by learning how to rotate the representation basis layer by layer.

The appeal of rotations and reflections is thus dual: they are conceptually the simplest global updates that alter direction but not pairwise angles, and they are computationally implementable as multiplicative updates on existing weight matrices. The paper frames this choice as a "sweet spot" between flexibility (we can change directions to modify semantics) and regularity (we do not arbitrarily distort neuron relationships). Compared to unconstrained, elementwise weight updates, orthogonal (rotation/reflection) updates greatly restrict the search space, which in turn controls the risk of catastrophic deviations from pretrained behavior during finetuning.

# Preserving HE to retain pretrained semantics

To formalize what it means to “touch the pretrained geometry as little as possible,” the paper adopts hyperspherical energy (HE) as a measure of the pairwise angular structure among neurons. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ let the normalized neurons be $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is then defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

HE aggregates pairwise hyperspherical similarities (inverse distances on the unit sphere) across all neuron pairs in a layer; it is a scalar summary of neuron uniformity and mutual angular relationships. The central hypothesis in the paper is that the semantic generation abilities encoded by a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model are tightly linked to these angular relationships among neurons, so finetuning that preserves HE will better retain pretrained semantics.

An important mathematical property motivates the orthogonal approach: if two sets of unit vectors are related by a common orthogonal transform, their pairwise angles (and therefore their HE) are exactly preserved. In symbols, if $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal, then

\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i \quad\text{and}\quad \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|
\]

for all $i,j$, hence $\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. This invariance provides a provable guarantee: by constraining finetuning to layer-shared orthogonal transformations, we explicitly control and preserve the hyperspherical energy of each layer. The paper argues that preserving HE is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model's diverse and high-fidelity generative behavior while still enabling semantic changes through direction adjustments.

The proposed Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) principle can be stated as seeking finetuned weights that minimize the HE difference:

\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|,
\]

and the attainable minimum of zero is achieved whenever $\bm{W}$ differs from $\bm{W}^0$ only by an orthogonal factor $\bm{R}$. Thus orthogonal layer-shared transforms are a natural parameterization to realize the HE-preservation objective exactly, rather than approximately via regularization.

# Trade-off: controlling degrees of freedom to prevent collapse

Preservation of HE alone does not fully determine finetuning behavior. A pure orthogonal transform leaves neuron angles intact but can still provide substantial re-orientation freedom. This raises a classic trade-off: flexibility versus regularity. Too much flexibility risks destabilizing the pretraining benefits or enabling degenerate solutions; too much regularity may prevent the model from adapting to the downstream task. The paper proposes two concrete mechanisms to manage this trade-off.

First, OFT already imposes a strong structural regularization: the entire left-multiplicative update must be orthogonal and, in practice, is often parameterized as block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. Using a block-diagonal $\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ reduces the effective parameter count and reduces the degrees of freedom proportionally. This is a practical knob — decreasing block size increases flexibility, increasing block size (more blocks) enforces more regularity.

Second, the paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) which adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity:

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \qquad \text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon.
\]

This constraint directly limits how far $\bm{R}$ can deviate from identity, explicitly controlling angular deviation from the pretrained coordinate system. Operationally, COFT is implemented by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ via a Cayley transform $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, and bounding $\bm{Q}$ (via projected gradient steps) to enforce the neighborhood constraint. The paper shows that COFT improves finetuning stability: small $\epsilon$ pulls the finetuned model close to the pretrained one and yields predictable, smooth training dynamics, while larger $\epsilon$ allows more adaptation and approaches OFT performance.

Thus, orthogonal finetuning provides a structured family of updates that preserves HE, and by tuning block structure and radius constraints we can navigate the flexibility–stability trade-off to avoid collapse (unrealistic outputs, loss of identity or control) while allowing the necessary semantic changes.

# Toy evidence: angle-only information reconstructs semantics in autoencoders

A central empirical motivation for focusing on angles (directions) rather than magnitudes comes from a toy autoencoder experiment described in the paper. The experiment trains a standard convolutional autoencoder on images of flowers using the usual inner-product activations during training: each convolutional kernel $\bm{w}$ produces an element output $z=\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}$ for a local input patch $\bm{x}$. After training, the authors probe which part of the learned neuron outputs carries semantics by reconstructing images in three ways at test time: (a) using the same inner product outputs as during training (baseline), (b) using only the magnitude of neuron responses (discarding sign/direction), and (c) using only angular information (i.e., replacing the inner product with its angular component).

The striking observation reported is that the angular-only reconstruction (c) almost perfectly recovers the input images, whereas the magnitude-only reconstruction (b) provides little useful content. Importantly, the network was not trained with an explicit angular activation; training used ordinary inner products. The fact that angles alone suffice to reconstruct semantic content suggests that neuron directions are the principal carriers of semantic information in the learned filters. This toy evidence motivates the decision to concentrate finetuning power on rotating neuron directions, which is exactly what OFT does.

Viewing this through an intuitive lens: if neuron directions encode structure (edges, object parts, identity cues), then finetuning that modifies directions can change semantics without disrupting the pretrained mapping encoded in relative angles. Magnitudes, in contrast, may primarily scale activations but not carry the same semantic load, so allowing large unconstrained magnitude shifts can be less effective or more harmful for controlled adaptation.

# Failure without orthogonality: instability and collapse case study

The paper includes a clear experimental contrast showing what happens when similar multiplicative updates are allowed but orthogonality is removed. In a controllable generation experiment following the ControlNet setup, the authors compare standard OFT (with orthogonality constraint) versus an otherwise identical method that drops the orthogonality requirement. The outcome is stark: OFT converges stably and produces images that respect the control signal after training, whereas the non-orthogonal variant fails to generate realistic images and exhibits no control effect.

This empirical failure illustrates two key points. First, the orthogonality constraint is doing more than enforcing a mathematical invariant — it is a practical stabilizer that prevents the finetuned weights from drifting into regions of parameter space that break the pretrained generative prior. Without orthogonality, the multiplicative update can arbitrarily alter pairwise neuron angles and therefore the HE, leading to loss of the pretrained semantic structure and, ultimately, collapse or hallucinated artifacts. Second, the stability advantage is not marginal: OFT is reported to converge faster and to be less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations. In subject-driven generation experiments (DreamBooth-style), OFT and COFT reach good control in far fewer iterations than baselines; LoRA and naive finetuning either converge slower or produce degraded outputs (e.g., changing shirt texture to fur or losing subject identity) and are sensitive to the iteration count.

Taken together, these experiments provide an existence proof that an apparently modest constraint — enforcing orthogonality on a learned layer-shared transform — can be the difference between successful, stable finetuning and failure. The results support the paper's thesis that preserving angular relational structure (HE) during adaptation is a principled and practically effective safeguard for retaining pretrained semantics while enabling task-specific control.

# Synthesis: why orthogonal finetuning is a principled practical choice

Bringing the threads together, orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is motivated both theoretically and empirically. Theoretically, orthogonal left-multiplicative transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles and hence the hyperspherical energy, a compact statistic hypothesized to capture important semantic structure in pretrained models. Practically, concentrating adaptation on direction-only changes is supported by toy evidence showing angles encode semantic content, and by experiments showing orthogonality stabilizes training and avoids collapse where unconstrained updates fail.

OFT also admits natural extensions to tune the flexibility–stability trade-off: block-diagonal orthogonal parameterizations reduce parameter count and bias the transform, and COFT's identity-neighborhood constraint explicitly limits deviation magnitude. A lightweight re-scaled variant adds per-neuron magnitude scaling without changing HE, further increasing adaptability when needed. Crucially, OFT achieves these benefits without adding inference-time cost: the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into pretrained weights offline so model deployment is as fast as the original pretrained model.

For a student encountering this approach for the first time, the key conceptual takeaways are: (1) representation geometry matters—angles between neurons encode semantics; (2) invariances (orthogonal transforms) provide provable preservation guarantees (on HE) that translate into empirical robustness; and (3) structured, multiplicative finetuning can provide a better trade-off than standard additive low-rate updates. These ideas open follow-up questions about parameter efficiency, scalability of orthogonal parameterizations, and compositionality of orthogonal transforms across tasks—questions the paper highlights as open problems.

OFT: Core Formulation and Layer-Wise Application

# Objective: minimize HE difference; solution via shared orthogonal matrices

The central optimization objective behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to preserve the pretrained network's pairwise geometric relationships among neurons while allowing targeted adaptation to a downstream task. The pairwise relationships are captured by hyperspherical energy (HE), a scalar that summarizes how neurons (weight vectors) are arranged on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix \(\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), where each \(\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d\) denotes a neuron, the normalized neurons are \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|\). The hyperspherical energy of the layer is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Intuitively, \(\text{HE}(\bm{W})\) aggregates inverse distances (or, equivalently, functions of pairwise cosines) between all pairs of normalized neurons; it therefore encodes the angular configuration of neurons and the degree of uniformity or clustering on the unit hypersphere.

OFT formalizes finetuning as the constrained optimization problem of keeping the HE of the finetuned weights equal (or close) to that of the pretrained weights \(\bm{W}^0\). Concretely, the desideratum is to minimize the HE difference:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|.
\]
The attainable global minimum of zero is reached whenever the finetuned neuron configuration differs from the pretrained one only by an orthogonal transformation (a rotation or reflection), since orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles and thus HE. Therefore OFT restricts the class of allowable finetuning transformations to layer-shared orthogonal matrices \(\bm{R}\) so that the finetuned weights are of the form
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0
\]
with \(\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}\). With this restriction, the HE objective is satisfied exactly (HE is invariant under orthogonal maps) and the finetuning problem reduces to learning the orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) that optimally adapts the pretrained layer to the downstream loss while keeping the hyperspherical structure intact.

This formulation provides a principled compromise between two competing goals: retain the pretrained model's semantic and generative priors (by preserving HE) and provide sufficient degrees of freedom to adapt to new control signals or subject-specific data. By optimizing over orthogonal matrices that are shared across the neurons of a layer, OFT enforces a structured, global angular update rather than arbitrary per-neuron angle perturbations that could destroy pretrained semantics.

# Forward pass: W = R W0 with R orthogonal; identity initialization

In the forward pass OFT replaces the original pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) by an orthogonally transformed version \(\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\). For a layer receiving input \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the layer output \(\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]
The orthogonality constraint on \(\bm{R}\),
\[
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I},
\]
guarantees that \(\bm{R}\) is a pure rotation or reflection in the input coordinate space of that layer. Because such transforms preserve inner products and angles between vectors, they leave the hyperspherical relationships among columns of \(\bm{W}^0\) unchanged after normalization.

A practical and important implementation detail is how finetuning is initialized. OFT initializes \(\bm{R}\) as the identity matrix \(\bm{I}\), i.e., \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) at the start of adaptation. This ensures the finetuned model exactly matches the pretrained model pre-update, avoiding any sudden change in behavior at the first optimization step and enabling stable, incremental adaptation. Optimization then proceeds to update the parameterization of \(\bm{R}\) (subject to orthogonality) using gradients from the downstream task loss. Because \(\bm{R}\) starts at identity, OFT inherits the pretrained model's predictions as the starting point and only departs from it as the downstream loss dictates.

Maintaining strict orthogonality during gradient-based updates requires a differentiable parameterization or orthogonalization step. The paper recommends computationally efficient parameterizations (discussed below) so that training can proceed while preserving \(\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) throughout.

# Interpretation: change of neuron coordinate system; preserving pairwise angles

A useful way to conceptualize OFT is as a change of the coordinate system used to describe neurons in a layer. The columns of \(\bm{W}^0\) are the neuron vectors expressed in the original coordinate basis of that layer. Multiplying by \(\bm{R}\) on the left corresponds to rotating (or reflecting) that coordinate basis, thereby re-expressing each neuron vector in a new orthonormal basis. Because orthogonal transforms are isometries, they preserve lengths and angles: for any two neurons \(\bm{w}_i^0,\bm{w}_j^0\), their cosine similarity
\[
\frac{(\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{w}_j^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\|\|\bm{w}_j^0\|}
\]
is identical to that of the transformed neurons \(\bm{w}_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\) and \(\bm{w}_j = \bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0\). Consequently, all pairwise angles are preserved and the hyperspherical energy, which depends only on normalized pairwise distances/angles, remains exactly the same.

This interpretation clarifies why OFT focuses on orthogonal, multiplicative updates rather than additive ones. Additive updates such as \(\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\) can change magnitudes and angles arbitrarily, risking disruption of the pretrained layer's learned geometry. By contrast, OFT's multiplicative change \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) keeps the angular relationships intact while allowing the layer to react to new objectives by a structured global change of coordinates. From a representational perspective, OFT modifies how incoming activations are projected onto the neuron basis without changing the relative geometry of that neuron basis. This is a logically conservative but expressive form of adaptation: the model's internal feature geometry (which encodes semantic priors) is retained while the orientation of the input-to-feature mapping can be tuned to realize task-specific behaviors.

Because OFT modifies directions rather than magnitudes, it aligns with empirical observations that angular information often captures semantic content in deep networks. The Cayley parameterization and block-structured orthogonal variants (described in the original paper) further trade off flexibility and parameter efficiency, but in all variants the preserved invariants remain pairwise angles and thus HE.

# Applying OFT to different layers: fully connected, attention weights, convolutions

OFT is conceptually architecture-agnostic because the core operation—left-multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix—is applicable wherever neurons are represented as columns of a weight tensor or matrix. The practical realization varies by layer type.

For fully connected (dense) layers, the weight matrix is naturally \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) where \(d\) is the input dimension and \(n\) the number of output neurons. OFT inserts a learned orthogonal \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and uses \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) in the forward pass. This is the canonical setting used to introduce the HE-preserving objective because the columns are explicit neuron vectors and orthogonality directly preserves column-pair angles.

In Transformer attention layers, the most impactful weights are the projection matrices for queries, keys, values, and the output projection. LoRA-style baselines typically adapt the attention projection matrices by low-rank additive updates; to compare fairly, OFT is applied in the experiments to the same attention-weight matrices. Practically, OFT can learn an orthogonal transform \(\bm{R}\) that multiplies the input-side of these projection matrices (i.e., left-multiplying the projection matrix). Because attention projections convert input token embeddings into query/key/value spaces, an orthogonal transform on that input side reorients how embeddings are mapped into those spaces while preserving the pretrained geometry among projection vectors, which keeps downstream attention behaviors stable but adaptable.

Convolutional layers require mapping OFT's matrix view to tensor structure. A convolutional kernel can be interpreted as a set of channelwise filters; if we flatten the spatial kernel and stack filters for all output channels, we obtain a matrix with neuron columns representing filters. Applying a block-diagonal orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) that respects channel groupings yields a computationally efficient OFT for convolutions. The paper highlights two useful parameterizations: (1) when the orthogonal matrix is block-diagonal with one block per input channel, each block transforms a single channel's filter weights (akin to learning depthwise orthonormal transforms across spatial filter weights); (2) when all blocks share the same orthogonal block, OFT uses a single orthogonal transform shared across channels. These block structures reduce the parameter count from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) or further if blocks are shared, while retaining orthogonality and HE invariance. Thus, OFT applies naturally to convolutional kernels with interpretable architectural choices that trade flexibility and parameter efficiency.

Across all these layer types, the same core principle applies: left-multiply pretrained weights by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix (possibly block-structured) that is learned during finetuning. That design guarantees HE preservation at the layer level while enabling targeted adaptation where it matters.

# No inference overhead: folding R into W0 post-training

A practical advantage of OFT is that it does not increase inference-time computation relative to the original pretrained model. The forward-pass during finetuning uses \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) explicitly, but this multiplicative relation can be collapsed after training. Once finetuning converges, one can compute the equivalent finetuned weight matrix
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0
\]
and store \(\bm{W}\) as the deployed weights. In other words, the learned orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) is folded into the original weight tensor, yielding a single weight matrix that produces the finetuned behavior without any extra matrix multiplication or auxiliary modules at inference. This contrasts with adapter-style methods or auxiliary control networks that require additional compute and memory during generation.

Because orthogonal multiplication is associative with the linear layer operation, there is no change in the computational graph complexity after fusion: replacing \(\bm{W}^0\) by \(\bm{W}\) keeps the layer's arithmetic identical to the pretrained setting. Thus OFT offers a deployable way to adapt large diffusion models without runtime overhead, enabling practical use in latency- or resource-constrained scenarios.

The folding step also obviates the need to ship extra adapter weights for inference; only the final finetuned weights need to be stored. If parameter storage is a concern, one could also keep only \(\bm{R}\) (or a low-parameter block-diagonal representation of it) and compute \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) on the device at load time, but this is an engineering choice rather than a conceptual requirement.

# Closing remarks

OFT reframes finetuning as a constrained, multiplicative adaptation that preserves the hyperspherical geometry of pretrained layers. By optimizing layer-shared orthogonal matrices initialized to identity, OFT achieves HE invariance while providing meaningful angular degrees of freedom to adapt models for subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. Its architectural agnosticism lets it be applied to dense, attention, and convolutional layers via appropriate parameterizations (full or block-diagonal orthogonal matrices), and its ability to fold learned transforms into base weights eliminates any inference cost. The formulation thus offers a principled, practical mechanism to balance adaptation flexibility with preservation of pretrained generative semantics.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

# Challenges of differentiable orthogonalization (Gram–Schmidt costs)

When we design Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for large text-to-image diffusion models, a central practical requirement is to maintain the orthogonality constraint on the transformation matrices while keeping the computation tractable and differentiable. A straightforward mathematically-correct way to enforce orthogonality is to orthonormalize a parameter matrix during training, for example by applying Gram–Schmidt or QR decomposition to a raw parameter matrix and using the orthonormal factor as the actual transform. While such methods are differentiable in principle, they carry two major practical costs when applied to modern large models.

First, the computational cost is high. Gram–Schmidt and QR require $O(d^3)$ operations for an unconstrained $d\times d$ matrix when implemented naively, and repeatedly performing these operations for many layers and many gradient steps rapidly becomes a bottleneck. For very large $d$ (typical in transformer or convolution-channel dimensions) the repeated orthonormalization is expensive both in time and in memory, which reduces training throughput and increases GPU requirements.

Second, numerical stability and gradient complexity can become problematic. Although QR and related factorizations are differentiable, backpropagating through them introduces additional matrix operations and dense Jacobians. In practice this leads to added implementation complexity and sometimes unstable training dynamics unless carefully handled. Because OFT is intended to be an efficient, parameter‑efficient finetuning method that can be applied across many layers, these practical drawbacks motivate alternative parameterizations of orthogonal matrices that are both cheaper to compute and easier to differentiate.

# Cayley transform: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1}, Q skew-symmetric

To address the cost and differentiation issues, OFT adopts the Cayley transform as an efficient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices. The Cayley parameterization expresses an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
with the constraint that $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. This construction produces a matrix $\bm{R}$ that satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, i.e., $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, and it does so using a relatively compact and well-structured parameter $\bm{Q}$.

There are practical advantages to this parameterization. Computing $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{Q}$ requires only forming $\bm{I}\pm\bm{Q}$ and solving a linear system or computing an inverse of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$, an operation that can be implemented with standard batched linear algebra kernels. Compared with running a full Gram–Schmidt on a dense matrix every update, forming and inverting $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is typically cheaper and more amenable to modern GPU-accelerated linear solvers, especially when $\bm{Q}$ has exploitable structure (see block-diagonal below). Differentiation through this transformation is straightforward because matrix inversion and linear solves are supported by automatic differentiation frameworks; gradients can be computed with standard matrix‑calculus rules and efficiently implemented adjoint operations.

A practical point for training initialization: to start finetuning exactly from the pretrained weights, OFT initializes the orthogonal transformation to the identity. Under the Cayley parameterization this corresponds to initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$, which yields $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$. This identity initialization aligns with the desideratum that the finetuned model starts from the pretrained model and then gradually adapts.

# Determinant constraint and practical implications

The Cayley transform has one structural limitation: it produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e., matrices in the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (rotations), but cannot represent orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ (improper rotations or reflections). In formula form, Cayley-parameterized $\bm{R}$ satisfy $\det(\bm{R})=1$.

In practical OFT usage this limitation is noted but found not to be harmful. The paper reports that restricting to $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ via the Cayley transform does not affect empirical performance in finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. Intuitively, the family of rotation-only orthogonal matrices is rich enough to provide the necessary angular adjustments of neuron directions that OFT requires for downstream adaptation. Because OFT's main goal is to preserve hyperspherical energy (pairwise angles among neurons) while providing a flexible yet constrained family of transformations, the inability to represent reflections (determinant $-1$) is a minor restriction in practice.

Nevertheless, this determinant constraint is worth keeping in mind. If a specific application demanded transformations that include reflections, an alternative orthogonal parameterization (e.g., using Householder sequences or Cayley combined with a fixed reflection) would be required; however, the paper's empirical findings justify using Cayley for the OFT setting due to its computational and implementation benefits.

# Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices: structure and intuition

Even with Cayley parameterization, naively parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix is expensive when $d$ is large, because the number of free parameters in a general orthogonal matrix scales as $d(d-1)/2$. To make OFT scalable and parameter-efficient, the method introduces block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. Concretely, the orthogonal transform is represented as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks:
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathrm{O}(d/r)$ is an orthogonal matrix acting on a subspace of dimension $d/r$. Each block can itself be Cayley-parameterized via a skew-symmetric block $\bm{Q}_i$, and the whole transform is orthogonal because the direct sum of orthogonal blocks is orthogonal.

There are several intuitive motivations for this structure. The block-diagonal form dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters and the computational cost of forming and applying $\bm{R}$, because each block is smaller. Functionally, block-diagonal orthogonality imposes structured regularization: different groups of neuron coordinates are rotated together, while interactions across blocks are not introduced. This is a beneficial bias in finetuning because it limits the degrees of freedom to a sweet spot between complete rigidity (identity) and full, unconstrained rotation. Empirically, the paper finds that this trade-off yields stable and effective finetuning: the block structure provides enough flexibility to adapt neuron directions for semantic control while preventing excessive disruption of pretrained generative capabilities.

When OFT is applied to convolutional layers, the block-diagonal interpretation has further natural meaning. Setting the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels makes each block operate as a per-channel orthogonal transform, analogous to depthwise operations; sharing blocks across channels corresponds to channel-shared rotations. This maps the mathematical block structure to intuitive architectural priors in convolutional networks.

# Parameter complexity: full vs r-block diagonal; optional block sharing

To quantify the parameter savings, consider a full orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$. The number of free parameters (degrees of freedom) in a general orthogonal matrix is $d(d-1)/2$, because skew-symmetric parameterization yields that many independent entries. Thus a full-$d$ Cayley parameterization would naively require on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

By contrast, for an $r$‑block diagonal orthogonal matrix where each block has size $d/r \times d/r$, the number of parameters becomes
\[
r \cdot \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r - 1\bigr)}{2} = \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2},
\]
which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Intuitively, increasing $r$ (i.e., using more but smaller blocks) reduces parameter count and computational cost roughly proportionally to $1/r$.

An additional parameter-saving strategy is block sharing: one can choose to reuse the same block matrix across multiple block positions, i.e., set $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for many or all $i\neq j$. If all blocks are identical and shared, the number of parameters reduces further to the degrees of freedom of a single block, yielding parameter complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ when expressed in terms of $d$ and $r$. Block sharing introduces stronger regularization but can be appropriate when similar transformations across subspaces are desirable.

These structured parameterizations permit practical trade-offs: choose small $r$ (larger blocks) when more flexibility is needed and computing resources allow, or choose larger $r$ (smaller blocks and optional sharing) when parameter-efficiency and speed are paramount.

# Comparative parameter counts with LoRA on concrete sizes

It is useful to contrast OFT's parameter scaling to another popular finetuning method, LoRA, to see where OFT offers savings. LoRA models weight updates as low-rank additive terms: if the base weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is of size $d\times n$, LoRA parameterizes the update with two low-rank factors of rank $r'$, leading to $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters. This yields complexity roughly $\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))$.

For OFT with an $r$-block diagonal Cayley parameterization applied to the same layer, the parameter complexity is on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d(d/r-1)/2)$ (or lower with block sharing). To provide a concrete numeric comparison drawn from the paper: suppose we have a weight matrix of size $128\times 128$ and choose $r'=8$ for LoRA and $r=8$ blocks for OFT. LoRA with $r'=8$ produces $r'(d+n)=8(128+128)=8\cdot 256=2048$ trainable parameters. For OFT with $r=8$, the paper reports 960 trainable parameters. Thus in this example OFT uses fewer parameters than LoRA while preserving orthogonality and hyperspherical energy invariance. The general takeaway is that, for comparable expressivity choices (i.e., choices of $r$ vs $r'$ that grow with $d$), OFT can be more parameter-efficient in practice, especially when block-diagonal structure and block sharing are used.

Finally, while LoRA reduces parameter counts by imposing a low-rank additive structure, OFT reduces parameter counts by exploiting sparsity and structured orthogonality. These are complementary design philosophies: LoRA constrains the update space additively, whereas OFT constrains the multiplicative transformation applied to pretrained weights. The empirical evaluation in the paper shows that OFT's structured orthogonal parameterizations yield a favorable balance of parameter efficiency, stability, and finetuning performance for text-to-image diffusion models.

Constrained and Re-Scaled Variants: COFT and Re-Scaled OFT

# COFT: constraint $\|R - I\| \leq \epsilon$ for controlled deviation

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) extends the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea by adding an explicit constraint that limits how far the learned orthogonal transform $R$ can deviate from the identity matrix $I$. The forward pass in COFT for a pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ becomes
\[
z = W^\top x = (R\,W^0)^\top x,
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad R^\top R = RR^\top = I,\quad \|R - I\| \le \epsilon,
\]
where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a matrix norm (the paper treats this as an operator-norm style deviation constraint) and $\epsilon$ is a user-chosen radius. The orthogonality constraint preserves pairwise angles among normalized neurons (hence hyperspherical energy), while the deviation constraint explicitly enforces that the finetuned model remains within an $\epsilon$-neighborhood of the pretrained weights in the transformed parameter space.

The primary motivation for COFT is stability. Standard finetuning or even unconstrained OFT can permit transformations that, while angle-preserving, change the overall coordinates enough to harm pretraining generalization or produce unstable optimization dynamics. Many existing finetuning methods implicitly rely on small learning rates to keep models near their initial point; COFT makes that proximity explicit. By combining hyperspherical-energy preservation (via orthogonality) with a hard bounded deviation from $I$, COFT gives two simultaneous regularizers: structural preservation of neuron relationships and explicit closeness to the original coordinate basis. Empirically in the paper, this combination yields improved finetuning stability compared to both naive finetuning and OFT without the deviation bound, reducing sensitivity to the number of iterations and learning rate choices.

Choosing $\epsilon$ trades off flexibility and preservation. Small $\epsilon$ forces $R$ to stay nearly identity, so the finetuned network remains very close to the pretrained model and will change behavior only minimally; this is useful when one wants very conservative adaptation that avoids catastrophic drift. Larger $\epsilon$ allows $R$ to rotate neuron directions more substantially and therefore provides more capacity to fit the downstream task; in the limit of large $\epsilon$ (or no deviation constraint), COFT behaves like unconstrained OFT. The paper demonstrates that intermediate $\epsilon$ values give a sweet spot: enough flexibility to learn controllability or subject-specific changes, but still constrained enough to avoid collapse or sudden, brittle changes in generation. COFT therefore makes the implicit small-step finetuning prior explicit and tunable.

# Implementing COFT with Cayley parameters via Neumann approximation and projection on $Q$

COFT must satisfy two constraints simultaneously: orthogonality of $R$ and the bounded deviation $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$. The paper adopts the Cayley parameterization to enforce orthogonality efficiently. The Cayley transform parameterizes an orthogonal matrix $R$ (with determinant $+1$) via a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$:
\[
R = (\,I + Q\,)(\,I - Q\,)^{-1},\qquad Q^\top = -Q.
\]
This construction yields a differentiable map from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices in the special orthogonal group. Using $Q$ as the trainable parameter therefore guarantees $R$ is orthogonal by construction and conveniently lets the optimizer act in the (skew-symmetric) tangent-like space.

Imposing the explicit bound on $\|R-I\|$ is not directly trivial in the Cayley parameterization because $R$ is a nonlinear rational function of $Q$, involving a matrix inverse. To make the constraint tractable, the paper uses a Neumann series approximation to linearize the relationship between $R$ and $Q$ for small $Q$. Under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in operator norm, one can expand:
\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2).
\]
To first order, $R - I \approx 2Q$, so requiring $\|R - I\|\le \epsilon$ translates approximately to $\|Q\| \le \epsilon'$ for some $\epsilon'$ (roughly $\epsilon'/2 \approx \epsilon$ up to higher-order terms). The implementation uses this insight: it constrains $Q$ directly during optimization by projecting $Q$ back into a ball $\{Q:\|Q\|\le\epsilon'\}$ after gradient steps. Projected gradient descent on $Q$ is straightforward because $Q$ lives in a linear space (linear constraints such as skew-symmetry are enforced by parameterizing only skew entries), and the projection onto a norm-ball is standard.

This two-step practical scheme—Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality and projection on $Q$ to enforce the deviation bound via the Neumann approximation—gives a computationally efficient way to train COFT. Initialization is simple: set $Q=0$ so that $R=I$ initially, ensuring the finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained model. During training, $Q$ is updated, clipped/projection ensures the norm bound, and $R$ is recomputed via the Cayley transform for the forward pass. The paper notes that because the Cayley map only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, reflections (determinant $-1$) are excluded, but this does not affect empirical performance.

# Effect of $\epsilon$ on flexibility vs preservation; empirical stability gains

The radius parameter $\epsilon$ in COFT controls the degree of permitted coordinate change and thereby directly mediates the trade-off between flexibility and preservation. The paper reports empirical behavior where small $\epsilon$ leads to finetuned models that remain very similar to the pretrained generator: changes are minimal and the pretrained generalization is almost entirely preserved, but the ability to adapt to challenging downstream control tasks is limited. As $\epsilon$ increases, the model gains capacity to represent more substantial directional changes in neuron representations and therefore fits downstream signals (subject identity or external controls) better. Past a certain $\epsilon$, however, the model can again become unstable—the freedom to implement large orthogonal transforms may permit modes that, while angle-preserving, change coordinates enough to reduce generation quality or introduce collapse if combined with other training dynamics.

Practically, COFT improves finetuning stability compared to both naive finetuning and unconstrained OFT. The paper shows that COFT with a suitable $\epsilon$ achieves smoother training curves, avoids the sudden emergence behavior seen in some adapter approaches, and is less sensitive to the exact number of training iterations. This stability simplifies hyperparameter choices: with COFT, users can select a reasonable iteration budget and a moderate learning rate without careful tuning, because the deviation constraint prevents runaway adaptation. The paper presents qualitative and quantitative evidence that COFT reaches good performance faster and more robustly across tasks (subject-driven and controllable generation), and that the additional explicit constraint is particularly helpful in preventing model collapse for long finetuning runs.

Choosing $\epsilon$ in practice is therefore a calibration problem: start small to preserve pretrained behavior, increase until the downstream task objective improves, and avoid overshooting into instability. The paper shows empirically that medium $\epsilon$ values often give the best balance for the tested text-to-image tasks.

# Re-scaled OFT: $W = R\,W^0\,D$ with diagonal $D \!>\! 0$; preserves HE while adding magnitude freedom

Re-scaled OFT augments the OFT parameterization by allowing per-neuron magnitude adjustments while still preserving the hyperspherical energy (HE). Original OFT changes neural directions via an orthogonal matrix $R$ and keeps magnitudes implicit in $W^0$. Re-scaled OFT inserts a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ with strictly positive diagonal entries, producing the forward pass:
\[
z = (R\,W^0\,D)^\top x.
\]
Because HE depends only on normalized neuron directions (it is computed from unit-normalized rows or columns of $W$), multiplying neurons by positive scalars does not change the pairwise angles among normalized neurons; normalization cancels magnitude. Thus, introducing $D$ preserves the hyperspherical energy invariance guaranteed by orthogonality, while adding an extra degree of freedom: each neuron can be rescaled in magnitude independently.

This extension is motivated by the observation that some downstream adaptations may benefit from slight magnitude adjustments in addition to angular rotations. Learning per-neuron scalings allows the finetuning process to adapt activation magnitudes without sacrificing the angle-preserving property that protects pretrained semantic structure. Importantly, $D$ has only $n$ additional parameters for a layer with $n$ output neurons, so it is parameter-efficient.

In practice, re-scaled OFT is implemented by jointly optimizing the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter (for $R$) and the diagonal entries $s_i$ (for $D$), with $s_i>0$ enforced (e.g., parameterize $s_i=\exp(t_i)$ or clamp). The initialization typically sets $D=I$ to begin at the pretrained model. Empirically in the paper, re-scaled OFT generally improves flexibility and final performance relative to plain OFT with negligible parameter cost; nevertheless, the main experiments in the paper focus on showing orthogonal transformation alone is powerful, and the re-scaled variant is presented as a straightforward, often beneficial extension.

# When to use each variant in practice

Selecting between OFT, COFT, and re-scaled OFT depends on the priorities of the finetuning scenario: stability/preservation, flexibility, parameter budget, and simplicity.

- Use COFT when finetuning stability and preservation of pretrained generative properties are paramount. If you want a method that is robust to larger iteration budgets and less sensitive to learning-rate tuning, COFT’s explicit deviation bound $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ provides an extra safeguard. Start with a small-to-moderate $\epsilon$ and increase it only until downstream performance improves without instability.

- Use OFT (unconstrained) when you need more flexibility than COFT and when the downstream task benefits from larger angular changes. OFT preserves hyperspherical energy and often converges faster than baseline finetuning strategies, but it requires a bit more care in hyperparameter selection since there is no hard radius bound.

- Use re-scaled OFT when you suspect that per-neuron magnitude adjustments will help the task. This is a low-cost augmentation: it preserves the HE invariance of orthogonal transforms and gives additional expressive power by letting the model adapt magnitudes. Initialize $D=I$ and keep its parameter count minimal (one scalar per neuron), and consider enforcing positivity via an exponential or similar transform.

Across the experiments reported in the paper, COFT yields the best empirical stability while OFT (and re-scaled OFT) often reach the strongest task adaptation if tuned well. A practical workflow recommended by the results in the paper is to begin with COFT to find a stable regime and then, if necessary, relax $\epsilon$ or switch to re-scaled OFT to squeeze out additional task performance once stability is established. All variants remain parameter-efficient compared to full finetuning and introduce no inference-time overhead: the learned $R$ and $D$ can be folded into the pretrained $W^0$ to produce an equivalent $W$ for deployment.

# Summary

COFT and re-scaled OFT are principled variants of Orthogonal Finetuning designed to balance preservation of pretrained generative capabilities with the flexibility required for downstream control tasks. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint around identity to limit deviation, implemented practically by Cayley parameterization plus a Neumann-based approximation that lets one project the skew-symmetric parameter $Q$ into a norm ball. This yields improved stability and simpler hyperparameter behavior. Re-scaled OFT augments OFT with a diagonal magnitude matrix $D$, preserving hyperspherical energy while allowing per-neuron scaling for additional flexibility. Together these variants give practitioners a small but powerful toolkit to control the trade-offs between stability and adaptability when finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models.

Connections, Trade-offs, and Convergence

# Additive low-rank (LoRA) vs multiplicative full-rank constrained (OFT)
When practitioners finetune large pretrained models, two broad algorithmic philosophies have emerged in the literature and in practice: additive low-rank updates (exemplified by LoRA) and multiplicative constrained updates (exemplified by Orthogonal Finetuning, OFT). LoRA parameterizes weight updates as an additive low-rank matrix: it replaces a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$ where $\Delta\bm{W}$ is constrained to be low-rank (typically factorized as $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ with small rank). This additive view directly limits the expressivity of how much the network can change: the update lives in a low-dimensional subspace, so only those directions are available during finetuning. This constraint reduces the number of trainable parameters and implicitly regularizes the update, which stabilizes finetuning compared to full unconstrained updates.

OFT takes a different approach: instead of adding a low-rank correction, OFT multiplies the pretrained weights by a learned orthogonal transformation, so the finetuned weights take the form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This is a multiplicative, full-rank re-parameterization of the layer. The orthogonality constraint is a structural constraint on the transformation rather than an explicit rank constraint on the update. Because orthogonal matrices preserve pairwise angles among column vectors (the neuron directions when columns are treated as neurons), OFT enforces a geometric invariance — the hyperspherical relationships between neurons remain unchanged. This invariance is the core mechanism by which OFT preserves pretrained semantic generation ability while allowing meaningful directional adjustments to neuron representations.

# Rewriting OFT as $W^0 + (R - I)W^0$: implicit low-rank when $(R - I)$ is low-rank
Although OFT is multiplicative by construction, it can be algebraically rewritten in an additive form to build connections with additive methods. Expanding the multiplicative transform yields
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
\]
This shows that OFT is equivalent to an additive update with $\Delta\bm{W} = (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. If the matrix $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$ happens to be low-rank, then $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ will be low-rank as well (or at least lie in a low-dimensional manifold determined by $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ and $\bm{W}^0$). Thus OFT can implicitly realize a low-rank additive update without explicitly parameterizing $\Delta\bm{W}$ as low-rank. But OFT differs crucially: the structure of the additive term is tied to an orthogonal transform applied before multiplying with $\bm{W}^0$, and the orthogonality enforces angle-preserving constraints that are not enforced by a generic low-rank additive factorization.

This rewriting clarifies two important points. First, OFT and LoRA are not orthogonal in capability — OFT can represent additive low-rank updates in certain regimes. Second, the constraints on where the update lives differ: LoRA restricts the column-space of the additive update directly via a low-rank factorization, while OFT restricts the update indirectly by constraining the multiplicative transform to be orthogonal. The two constraints yield different inductive biases and different effects on the pretrained geometry of neuron directions.

# Two routes to parameter efficiency: low-rank vs block-sparse orthogonality
Model efficiency is central to practical finetuning. The two families of methods realize parameter efficiency along distinct axes. LoRA reduces parameters by forcing the additive update to be low-rank; the number of trainable parameters scales with the chosen rank $r'$ and is proportional to $r'(d + n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight matrix. OFT attains efficiency differently: by constraining the multiplicative matrix $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal and further parameterizing it with a block-diagonal structure, the effective number of free parameters is reduced while keeping orthogonality intact.

Concretely, a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which is expensive for large $d$. OFT therefore uses a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks, each of size $(d/r)\times(d/r)$. This reduces parameters to $d(d/r-1)/2 = \mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$; sharing blocks across channels reduces parameters further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. In other words, OFT leverages sparsity in the block structure (a block-sparse orthogonality) to trade off flexibility and parameter count. Thus there are two complementary routes: low-rank additive updates (LoRA) and block-sparse orthogonal multiplicative updates (OFT). Each route imposes a different structural bias on the permissible weight modifications.

# Optimization on smooth hypersphere manifolds: convergence intuition
An important practical observation in the paper is that OFT converges faster and more stably than many baselines. One conceptual explanation lies in the geometry of optimization induced by OFT. The orthogonality constraint means we are optimizing over orthogonal matrices, which can be seen as a manifold (the orthogonal group) embedded in Euclidean space. When OFT acts by rotating neuron directions collectively, the effective optimization moves parameters along a smooth manifold of rotations rather than arbitrarily in Euclidean space. This constrains the search space to transformations that preserve pairwise angles, which removes many pathological directions that would quickly break pretrained semantics.

Optimizing on such smooth manifolds often yields nicer landscapes: gradients restricted to the manifold avoid directions that cause rapid destruction of representational geometry, and the constrained updates tend to be more stable (less prone to sudden collapse). Additionally, because OFT directly targets neuron directions — the component of weights that carries semantic structure according to the paper's motivating empirical observations — it focuses optimization on the most relevant degrees of freedom for altering semantics. Combining a targeted search space (angles) with smooth manifold structure explains why OFT empirically converges faster and more reliably than both unconstrained finetuning and some adapter methods.

# Why minimizing hyperspherical energy is inappropriate for finetuning
Hyperspherical energy (HE) measures the pairwise closeness of normalized neuron vectors on the unit sphere: heuristically, lower HE corresponds to more uniformly distributed neuron directions. Prior work used minimization of HE as a regularizer when training classification networks from random initialization because a uniform spread of neurons can improve generalization. However, in the finetuning setting for pretrained generative models, minimizing HE is not a desirable objective. The pretrained model's neuron geometry already encodes learned semantic structure; pushing the neurons toward a new uniformly low-HE configuration would likely erase those encoded semantics and damage generation quality.

OFT does not minimize HE. Instead, it enforces invariance of HE by restricting updates to orthogonal transformations. Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles and thus preserve HE exactly. This is precisely the intended behavior during finetuning a generator: we want to adjust how the network uses its semantic components (by rotating them collectively) without altering the internal geometric relationships among neurons that the pretrained model had already learned. In short, minimizing HE is helpful during from-scratch training for certain objectives, but in finetuning a generative model the goal is preservation of pretrained relational structure, not its collapse to some newly minimized energy.

# Flexibility-regularity spectrum: standard finetuning, LoRA, OFT, COFT
Finetuning methods can be organized along a spectrum from maximum flexibility to maximum regularity, and each point on the spectrum reflects a different trade-off between expressivity and stability. At the most flexible end is standard finetuning: unconstrained update of weights. This gives the optimizer full freedom but risks destroying pretrained capabilities, and it often requires careful tuning of small learning rates and early stopping to avoid collapse.

LoRA sits closer to the regularity side than vanilla finetuning by constraining the additive update to a low-rank subspace. This reduces some instability because many possible destructive directions are simply unavailable; however, LoRA still changes neuron angles arbitrarily (subject to the low-rank span), and therefore can fail to preserve certain pretrained geometric relationships, which may lead to degraded semantic preservation in some tasks.

OFT occupies a distinct region of the spectrum: it allows full-rank multiplicative changes but constrains them to be orthogonal (and often block-diagonal). This enforces strong geometric regularity — pairwise neuron angles are preserved exactly — while still enabling effective directional changes that alter semantics in controlled ways. OFT thus finds a middle path: it is flexible enough to effect semantic changes by rotating the coordinate system but regular enough to avoid destructive updates.

Constrained OFT (COFT) moves further toward regularity by adding an explicit deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$. COFT keeps the orthogonality invariance while explicitly bounding how far the orthogonal transform can deviate from identity, limiting the magnitude of change and improving finetuning stability. This extra constraint makes COFT particularly robust: it combines the angle-preserving invariance of OFT with an explicit radius constraint around the pretrained parameters, thereby making COFT one of the most conservative (yet effective) finetuning options in the family.

# Summary: trade-offs and practical implications
The core insight tying these comparisons together is that finetuning success hinges on choosing the right inductive bias for how weights may change. LoRA biases updates to a low-dimensional additive subspace; OFT biases updates to angle-preserving multiplicative transforms (often with block structure); COFT further biases toward small deviations. These choices yield different parameter-efficiency and optimization behaviors. OFT's angle-preserving multiplicative parameterization targets the part of weights that empirical evidence suggests carries semantic information (directions), and by optimizing on the orthogonal manifold it attains faster, more stable convergence while preserving pretrained generative capabilities. Recognizing these trade-offs helps explain when each method will be preferable: when the priority is strict preservation of pretrained semantics with stable convergence, OFT/COFT are attractive; when a specific low-dimensional additive capacity is sufficient and very few parameters are desired, LoRA remains competitive.

Application I: Subject-Driven Generation

# Task setup: few-shot subject images + text prompts; preserving identity and prompt fidelity

Subject-driven generation is the task of taking a small number of images of a particular subject (a person, pet, object, etc.) and producing new images that depict that same subject in novel contexts specified by text prompts. In practice, this is a few-shot problem: the finetuning procedure has access to just a handful of reference images of the subject and must adapt a large pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that at sampling time the model can render that subject consistently while following arbitrary text guidance (for example, “a photo of {S} riding a bicycle in Paris”, where {S} is a learned token that denotes the subject).

Two requirements compete in this setup. First, identity (or subject) fidelity: generated images must retain the distinctive, instance-specific visual attributes present in the reference images (shape, color patterns, facial identity, clothing details, etc.). Second, prompt fidelity: the generated images must follow the new context or attributes specified by the text prompt (pose, background, objects, artistic style). A successful method must maintain both; changing the model so much that identity is lost (e.g., producing different animals or faces) or so little that prompts are ignored both fail the objective.

In the paper, subject-driven generation is framed within the DreamBooth setting. DreamBooth introduces a learned special token (a “customized token”) that represents the subject in text prompts. The finetuning objective used in DreamBooth—and adopted unchanged by OFT/COFT in the experiments—is a combination of reconstruction objectives on subject images and a class-specific prior-preservation loss that prevents overfitting the subject to degenerate outputs. The core practical question addressed by this work is: how to finetune the diffusion model so it acquires the subject-specific behavior while not destroying the pretrained model’s broad generative capabilities that are necessary to satisfy diverse prompts?

# Training protocol alignment with DreamBooth; layers finetuned with OFT/COFT

The OFT framework integrates directly into the DreamBooth finetuning pipeline by replacing the usual parameter update mechanism with an orthogonal layerwise transform. Concretely, rather than updating the pretrained weights \(\bm{W}^0\) of chosen layers by standard additive gradient steps, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) and applies the transformed weights \(\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) during forward passes. COFT (Constrained OFT) further constrains \(\bm{R}\) so that it remains close to the identity, enforcing \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon\) in addition to orthogonality. Both OFT and COFT initialize \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) (or equivalently initialize the Cayley parameter \(\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\) used to produce \(\bm{R}\)), so training starts from exactly the pretrained weights and gradually adapts by rotating neuron directions.

To stay comparable to other adaptation methods (notably LoRA), OFT is applied to the same subset of network layers where LoRA would normally be used. In the experiments reported in the paper, OFT/COFT are applied to the attention weights (as LoRA commonly targets attention matrices in transformer-based diffusion U-Nets) so that comparisons are fair in both parameter count and layer scope. The Cayley parameterization is used to keep \(\bm{R}\) orthogonal efficiently; block-diagonal structure for \(\bm{R}\) (with \(r\) blocks) is employed to reduce parameter cost and to provide an additional regularization bias that reflects natural channel- or block-structure in convolutional/attention layers. This block-diagonal parameterization does not change the orthogonality property that underpins the method’s theoretical guarantees.

The training objective itself—losses, reconstruction + prior-preservation—is the same as DreamBooth’s. The only change is the optimization variables (orthogonal matrices \(\bm{R}\), optionally with a diagonal rescaling \(\bm{D}\) in the re-scaled OFT variant). COFT enforces the extra small-radius constraint via projection on the Cayley parameter (approximating \(\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}\) and constraining \(\|\bm{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'\)), enabling explicit control over how far the finetuned weights may deviate from \(\bm{W}^0\).

# Evaluation metrics: DINO/CLIP-I (subject fidelity), CLIP-T (prompt fidelity), LPIPS diversity

To quantitatively evaluate subject-driven finetuning, the paper uses a set of metrics that separately probe subject fidelity, prompt fidelity, and sample diversity.

Subject fidelity is assessed by two feature-based similarity metrics: DINO and CLIP-I. DINO computes the similarity of ViT-S/16 self-supervised embeddings (DINO embeddings) between generated images and real reference images; CLIP-I uses CLIP image embeddings to compute average pairwise cosine similarity between generated and real images. High scores on DINO and CLIP-I indicate that the generated images match the subject appearance in the reference set.

Prompt fidelity is measured by CLIP-T: the average cosine similarity between the CLIP text embedding of the prompt and the CLIP image embedding of the generated images. This captures whether generated images actually reflect the textual instruction provided at generation time. Good prompt fidelity means the model changed the context or attributes according to the text while preserving the subject identity.

Diversity is important because a degenerate finetuned model might repeatedly output trivial near-identical reconstructions of the subject. The paper uses LPIPS, a perceptual distance measure, to assess diversity: average LPIPS cosine similarity among generated images for the same subject and prompt. Reasonable diversity indicates the model can produce different plausible variations rather than memorizing a single output.

These metrics together help determine whether OFT/COFT strike the desired balance: preserving the instance identity while remaining responsive to prompts and still producing varied outputs.

# Convergence and stability comparisons with DreamBooth and LoRA

One of the principal empirical claims of the paper is that OFT and COFT provide faster and more stable finetuning dynamics compared to DreamBooth’s vanilla finetuning and to LoRA. Several behaviors are emphasized.

First, convergence speed: OFT/COFT reach effective control (good subject and prompt fidelity) in far fewer training iterations. The paper reports that after 400 iterations OFT and COFT already produce good control results, whereas other methods require many more iterations. This faster convergence means fewer epochs and less compute are needed to get acceptable subject-driven generation.

Second, stability over longer training: standard DreamBooth finetuning can become unstable and eventually collapse if training runs too long; LoRA can fail to preserve subject identity under some settings. By contrast, both OFT and COFT maintain stable behavior even after many more iterations. In particular, COFT with a properly chosen \(\epsilon\) makes both the learning rate and the number of iterations much less sensitive hyperparameters because the model is explicitly constrained to remain within a small radius around the pretrained weights.

Third, failure modes: the paper highlights examples where LoRA begins to generate incorrect semantics (e.g., transforming clothing colors into fur textures) as training progresses, while OFT/COFT continue to preserve identity and correct prompt conditioning. This empirical stability is explained by OFT’s preservation of hyperspherical energy (pairwise neuron-angle structure): by applying layerwise orthogonal transforms at finetuning, the pairwise angular relationships among neurons are unchanged, which the authors hypothesize (and empirically observe) is crucial to maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic generation capability.

# Quantitative outcomes: fidelity/diversity improvements

In the DreamBooth-style subject-driven experiments using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model, the paper reports that both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject-fidelity metrics while maintaining comparable or better prompt fidelity and diversity.

Specifically, on the DINO and CLIP-I metrics—both designed to measure how closely generated images match the subject reference images—OFT and COFT achieve a considerable margin over DreamBooth and LoRA. This indicates stronger preservation of subject-specific visual features. On CLIP-T, which measures text prompt adherence, OFT and COFT perform slightly better or comparably to baselines, demonstrating that improved subject fidelity does not come at the cost of ignoring prompts. For LPIPS diversity, OFT and COFT match or slightly improve over baselines, which indicates that OFT’s orthogonal constraint does not force the generator into narrow memorization modes.

The experimental protocol included repeating finetuning runs with 30 different random seeds for each method to reduce randomness; the reported improvements are said to be consistent across these runs. These quantitative gains are thus not isolated to a single lucky initialization but reflect more stable optimization behavior.

# Qualitative analyses: identity preservation and prompt adherence; robustness to longer training

Beyond the numerical metrics, the paper presents qualitative comparisons showing visual examples across methods. These qualitative observations align with the quantitative findings.

Identity preservation: OFT and COFT-produced images consistently retain distinctive subject attributes—face features, clothing patterns, or unique object marks—from the few-shot reference images, even when the generated images place the subject in markedly different contexts or poses. LoRA often fails to preserve subject identity in several showcased examples (for instance, replacing clothing texture with fur or altering facial traits), while DreamBooth sometimes preserves identity but struggles to follow the text prompt accurately.

Prompt adherence: OFT and COFT demonstrate accurate compliance with the text prompt. Where DreamBooth might preserve identity but ignore some prompt details, OFT/COFT show both identity preservation and alignment with prompt semantics. This confirms that the orthogonal adaptation mechanism is effective at altering neuron directions in a way that encodes new context instructions without breaking pretrained semantic structure.

Robustness to longer training: A particularly practical advantage of OFT/COFT is reduced sensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations. Because these methods initialize at the pretrained weights and only rotate neuron directions (with COFT additionally bounding how far those rotations can move from the identity), they avoid the common need in DreamBooth-style training to carefully stop finetuning at exactly the right iteration. The paper reports that DreamBooth and LoRA may start producing collapsed or semantically incorrect images when run long, whereas OFT/COFT remain stable and continue to produce high-quality outputs. This robustness eases hyperparameter selection (learning rate and epoch count) in practice and makes the finetuning process safer to overrun.

# Summary

In the subject-driven generation application, OFT and its constrained variant COFT fit into the DreamBooth finetuning framework by replacing standard additive weight updates with orthogonal, layer-shared multiplicative transforms applied to the pretrained weights. This preserves hyperspherical energy (pairwise neuron-angle relationships) by construction, a property the authors identify as empirically important for preserving the pretrained model’s semantic generation capabilities. Evaluated with DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for prompt fidelity, and LPIPS for diversity, OFT/COFT show consistent quantitative improvements over DreamBooth and LoRA, converge faster, and yield much more stable behavior across longer training. Qualitatively, OFT/COFT better maintain identity details while responding accurately to prompts and are less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations, making them practical and reliable choices for few-shot subject-driven generation.

Application II: Controllable Generation

# Tasks and datasets: C2I (COCO), S2I (ADE20K), L2F (CelebA-HQ)

Controllable generation in this chapter refers to finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that it respects an additional structural control signal at generation time. The work discussed uses three concrete tasks that are representative of common control modalities and diverse visual domains.

The first task is Canny edge to image (C2I) on the COCO dataset. Here the control signal consists of Canny edge maps extracted from COCO images. The model must synthesize photorealistic images that match a given text prompt while conforming to the edge layout. COCO provides complex scenes and object compositions, making this task a test of both geometric adherence and semantic hallucination from sparse edge information.

The second task is segmentation map to image (S2I) on ADE20K. The control signal is a semantic segmentation map indicating object and region categories. ADE20K contains diverse scene layouts and many semantic classes; therefore S2I tests the model’s ability to produce images whose semantic content (object presence and placement) aligns with the supplied segmentation while preserving photorealism and plausibility under an accompanying text prompt.

The third task is landmark to face (L2F) on CelebA-HQ. The control signal is a set of facial landmarks indicating keypoint positions that determine pose and relative facial geometry. This task emphasizes fine-grained spatial control: the generated face should exhibit a pose and configuration matching the landmark input while maintaining identity-agnostic realism and compatibility with the text prompt.

These three tasks cover sparse structural cues (C2I), dense semantic guidance (S2I), and precise geometric constraints (L2F). Using COCO, ADE20K, and CelebA-HQ as datasets anchors evaluation in widely-studied benchmarks with varying complexity and semantic content, enabling assessment of both control fidelity and image quality across domains.

# Training setup: finetuning SD v1.5; parameter counts vs adapters

All experiments in this chapter finetune Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD v1.5) as the pretrained text-to-image diffusion backbone. The finetuning objective follows the same task formulations used by prior adapter-based methods: ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are representative baselines that add external modules and finetune those modules, while LoRA is a parameter-efficient adapter that adds low-rank additive updates to certain transformer weights. To ensure fair comparison, OFT/COFT are applied to the same layers targeted by LoRA when comparing to LoRA, and to the same overall evaluation protocol used for ControlNet and T2I-Adapter when comparing to those methods.

OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) and COFT (Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning) differ in how many trainable parameters they introduce and in the nature of those parameters. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter typically add substantial module(s) with many parameters: these adapter modules increase inference-time complexity (ControlNet in particular). LoRA introduces additive low-rank matrices; the parameter count grows with the chosen low-rank dimension \(r'\) and with the matrix sizes it adapts. OFT parameterizes a layer-shared orthogonal transformation \(\bm{R}\) (often in a block-diagonal form) and may optionally add a small rescaling diagonal \(\bm{D}\); COFT adds an additional constraint \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon\) to limit deviation from identity. In practice, OFT and COFT use far fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet and achieve a parameter count comparable to or smaller than LoRA for typical choices of rank and block settings. Importantly, unlike ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, OFT/COFT do not add inference-time overhead: the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights, yielding identical inference speed to the original SD v1.5.

Training schedule and data: for the controllable generation experiments reported here, all methods finetune SD v1.5 on the respective dataset for a fixed number of epochs (20 epochs). OFT/COFT typically converge faster (see later sections) and are effective even with fewer trainable parameters and less training data (the chapter reports experiments where OFT converges well with only 5% of ADE20K), highlighting improved sample efficiency.

# Control consistency metrics: IoU/F1 (edges), mIoU/acc (segmentation), landmark L2 (faces)

To evaluate whether the generated images follow the input control signals, this work proposes a control consistency metric for each control modality. The core idea is straightforward: take a generated image, estimate the control signal from that image using an off-the-shelf detector or estimator, and compare the estimated control signal to the original input control. This yields a quantitative measure of how faithfully the model implements the control.

For C2I (edges): the control map is binary edges. After generating an image, an edge detector is applied to the generated image to produce a predicted edge map. The predicted map is compared to the input Canny edges using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score. IoU measures the ratio of the intersection area of predicted and ground-truth edges over their union, and F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall computed from edge pixels. Higher IoU and F1 indicate stronger adherence to the edge layout.

For S2I (segmentation): the control map is a semantic segmentation. A pretrained semantic segmentation model is applied to the generated image to produce predicted class maps. These are compared to the input segmentation using mean Intersection over Union (mIoU), mean accuracy, and overall accuracy—standard segmentation evaluation metrics. Mean IoU averages per-class IoUs; mean accuracy averages per-class accuracies; overall accuracy counts total correct pixels divided by total pixels. Higher values indicate better semantic fidelity to the supplied segmentation.

For L2F (landmarks): the control signal is a set of facial landmark coordinates. A landmark detector is run on the generated face to produce predicted landmark positions. The average Euclidean distance (mean \( \ell_2 \) distance) between predicted landmarks and input landmark coordinates is computed. Formally, if the input landmark set is \( \{p_i\}_{i=1}^K \) and the predicted set is \( \{\hat p_i\}_{i=1}^K \), the metric is
\[
\text{mean-} \ell_2 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^K \| \hat p_i - p_i \|_2.
\]
Smaller mean-\( \ell_2 \) indicates more accurate pose and geometry alignment.

Using these metrics allows a quantitative assessment of control fidelity that is interpretable and comparable across methods. The chapter makes clear that the metric’s accuracy depends on the performance of the off-the-shelf estimator, but it provides an objective proxy for controllability.

# Convergence speed and stability: epoch-wise curves and qualitative progression

One of the salient empirical findings is that OFT and COFT converge substantially faster and more stably than alternative finetuning approaches. Convergence is evaluated both quantitatively with the control consistency metrics (e.g., landmark \( \ell_2 \) for L2F) plotted against training epochs and qualitatively by visualizing generated images across epochs to observe progressive adherence to controls.

For the L2F task, the mean-\( \ell_2 \) error between target and predicted landmarks is plotted as a function of finetuning epochs. OFT and COFT reach low landmark errors in far fewer epochs than LoRA, ControlNet, or T2I-Adapter. Concretely, the experiments show that LoRA requires about 20 epochs to reach the error level that OFT achieves around epoch 8. This faster convergence demonstrates that OFT’s search space—orthogonal rotations of neuron directions (possibly with small rescaling or constrained deviation)—is well-aligned with the kinds of semantic changes needed for controlling geometry and structure.

Stability refers to whether training exhibits sudden collapses, spikes, or abrupt emergence of behavior. OFT and COFT provide smooth and predictable training dynamics: control quality improves gradually and consistently across epochs. In contrast, methods like ControlNet sometimes display sudden emergence of control capability after several epochs—a brittle dynamic that can make hyperparameter selection and early stopping difficult. Similarly, LoRA may fail to preserve structure or converge slowly, with poorer stability in some control tasks.

Qualitative progression supports the quantitative curves: for L2F, generated faces gradually align pose and landmark positions with the control map epoch by epoch when using OFT/COFT. For C2I and S2I, OFT/COFT show steadily improved adherence to edge layouts or semantic boundaries over training. The experiments also report that OFT can achieve effective control even when trained with a reduced fraction of dataset images (e.g., 5% of ADE20K), indicating strong data efficiency.

Two complementary reasons explain the speed and stability advantages. First, OFT directly manipulates neuron directions (angles), which empirical analyses indicate are the dominant carrier of semantic information; rotating neuron directions is therefore an efficient way to change semantics in a controlled manner. Second, optimizing orthogonal transforms constrains the optimization to a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), which reduces ill-conditioning and yields more predictable optimization trajectories than unconstrained large-dimensional additive updates.

# Quantitative results: stronger control and quality with fewer parameters

Across the three control tasks, OFT and COFT consistently outperform competing methods according to the control consistency metrics and also often produce higher-fidelity images. Quantitatively:

- On the C2I task evaluated on COCO with IoU and F1, OFT/COFT achieve higher IoU and F1 scores than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, indicating better alignment of generated edges to the input edge maps.
- On S2I (ADE20K), OFT/COFT obtain superior mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy, showing that generated images better match the provided segmentation maps in terms of pixel-level class agreement.
- On L2F (CelebA-HQ), the mean-\( \ell_2 \) landmark error for OFT/COFT is lower than for the baselines, demonstrating more precise pose and facial geometry control.

The chapter emphasizes that these gains occur while using fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet and with parameter counts comparable to or smaller than LoRA. Moreover, OFT/COFT require fewer training epochs to reach competitive performance, which reduces total compute and data requirements. In particular, the COFT variant, which constrains orthogonal deviation by \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon\), often yields improved finetuning stability while maintaining or improving control metrics.

The experiments also show that adapter-based approaches (T2I-Adapter, ControlNet) tend to converge more slowly and often need more data or epochs to approach OFT’s final performance. LoRA exhibits variability: it may do relatively well on some tasks (e.g., S2I in some settings) but struggles on others (e.g., C2I and L2F), and its performance ceiling appears lower than OFT’s given typical hyperparameter choices.

# Qualitative comparisons: structure adherence, fidelity, and diversity

Qualitatively, OFT and COFT generate images that show better structural adherence to control inputs, higher photorealistic fidelity, and often more plausible geometric and semantic details than baselines. The reported visual comparisons illustrate several consistent patterns.

Structure adherence: In C2I, OFT/COFT produce images whose salient contours, object boundaries, and major shapes follow the input Canny edges closely, while baselines sometimes ignore or misinterpret edge structure, producing mismatched object placements or spurious content. In S2I, OFT/COFT produce images whose object regions and class boundaries align with the segmentation map; LoRA sometimes fails to generate images consistent with segmentation layouts, while ControlNet and T2I-Adapter perform reasonably but with less fine-grained texture quality or geometric reasoning.

Fidelity: OFT/COFT often produce more vivid textures, clearer object details, and overall more realistic images than adapter baselines. This is attributed to OFT preserving the pretrained model’s generalization ability via orthogonal transformations that keep hyperspherical neuron relationships intact, avoiding degradation of photorealistic priors during finetuning.

Diversity: OFT/COFT maintain diversity of outputs while respecting control signals. For subject-driven generation and controllable tasks alike, the method yields diverse variations consistent with prompts and controls. Even under strong geometric constraints (L2F), OFT/COFT manage to produce high-quality variations that follow the landmarks without collapsing to a single mode.

Stability and failure modes: The chapter reports that OFT/COFT finetuning trajectories are robust across different datasets and hyperparameter settings. ControlNet sometimes shows sudden onset of control capability (abrupt changes across epochs), making qualitative behavior less predictable. LoRA can fail qualitatively by losing identity-related details or by producing inconsistent structures (for example, converting clothing textures into unrelated patterns). OFT/COFT avoid such failures more often, which makes them easier to use in practice.

In summary, the qualitative comparisons corroborate the quantitative control metrics: OFT and COFT produce images that adhere more closely to control signals while preserving or improving image fidelity and maintaining diversity, all with fewer or comparable additional parameters and without adding inference overhead.

# Final remarks

Controllable generation via orthogonal finetuning demonstrates that constraining finetuning updates to orthogonal transformations (possibly with constrained deviation or small rescaling) is an effective strategy for integrating structural control signals into large pretrained text-to-image diffusion models. By preserving neuron pairwise angular relationships (hyperspherical energy), OFT/COFT maintain pretrained generative capability while providing a compact and stable mechanism to adapt models to edge, segmentation, and landmark controls. Empirical evaluation across COCO (C2I), ADE20K (S2I), and CelebA-HQ (L2F) shows faster convergence, stronger control consistency (IoU/F1, mIoU/accuracy, mean-\( \ell_2 \)), and better qualitative fidelity compared to ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, all while keeping inference cost unchanged.

Practical Implementation Guide

# Where to apply OFT
The OFT framework is model-agnostic in principle, but in practice the most effective and parameter-efficient places to apply OFT in text-to-image diffusion pipelines are the projection matrices inside attention modules and (optionally) selected convolutional layers.

- Attention projections in U-Net / transformer blocks. To make a fair comparison with LoRA and to follow the experiments in the paper, apply OFT to the same weight matrices where LoRA would be applied: the query, key, value and/or output projection matrices inside multi-head attention. These projection matrices are dense fully-connected weight matrices of shape $d\times n$ (or $n\times d$ depending on implementation); they map between token/channel and projector spaces and are highly influential on semantic behaviour. Learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to premultiply a pretrained attention weight $\bm{W}^0$ (so the forward pass becomes $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$) targets the neuron directions that control semantics while limiting destructive updates.

- Optional convolution layers. OFT can be applied to convolutional kernels as well. When you arrange convolutional kernels in the usual shape (e.g. flatten the kernel spatial + input-channel axes so each output channel corresponds to a neuron vector of dimension $d$), the same block-diagonal orthogonalization applies. Two practical interpretations arise depending on block choices (see next section): (1) if you set one block per input channel, the OFT transform acts like a depthwise per-channel orthogonal transform; (2) if you share blocks across channels you effect a shared orthogonal transform across channels. These variants trade flexibility for parameter efficiency and are described more below.

# Initialization and parameterization
OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$, and replaces the forward pass
z = $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with
z = $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$,
subject to $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

Efficient and stable orthogonal parameterization used in the paper
- Cayley parameterization. Represent $\bm{R}$ via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ and the Cayley transform
  $\displaystyle \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$.
  This produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (special orthogonal group). The paper finds this restriction does not harm practical performance.

- Identity initialization. Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$. With Cayley transform this yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ at the start of finetuning, so the finetuned network exactly equals the pretrained network before any training steps. This zero initialization is analogous to LoRA’s zero init and greatly improves stability.

- Block-diagonal structure for parameter efficiency. For large neuron dimension $d$, parameterizing a full $\bm{R}$ is expensive. The paper uses a block-diagonal $\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with $r$ blocks, each of size $d/r$. Each $\bm{R}_i$ is itself orthogonal. This reduces trainable parameter count from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to approximately $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$; sharing the same block across positions/channels can reduce it further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Use the Cayley parameterization independently for each block (i.e., parameterize each block by its skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}_i$).

- Re-scaled OFT (optional). If you need slightly more flexibility, add a learnable positive diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ after the pretrained weights and before the output: the forward pass becomes
  z = $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$.
  This does not change the hyperspherical energy (directions remain the same) and adds only $n$ trainable scalar parameters. The paper reports this variant generally improves flexibility with minimal cost.

# Selecting number of blocks r and sharing strategy
Choosing $r$ controls the trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency:

- Small $r$ (e.g., $r=1$). A single block yields a full orthogonal matrix on the entire neuron dimension: most expressive, most parameters. Use this if you have budget for parameters and want maximum flexibility.

- Moderate $r$. Common practical strategy: set $r$ so each block size (dimension $d/r$) is comparable to a natural channel group in your model (e.g., a multiple of the attention head size or convolution channel grouping). This gives a balance — expressive per-group transforms but fewer parameters than full $\bm{R}$.

- Large $r$ (many small blocks, even one-per-channel). This is the most parameter-efficient and introduces a stronger inductive bias (per-channel orthogonal transforms). It often helps stability and can be particularly natural for convolutional kernels where channel-wise transforms are meaningful.

- Block sharing. You may set all $\bm{R}_i$ equal (shared block) to further reduce parameters. The paper notes shared blocks reduce parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Sharing tends to increase regularity (less overfitting) but reduces per-layer expressiveness.

Practical rule-of-thumb (as used implicitly in the experiments): match the blocks to the internal structure of the layer (attention head size, channel grouping) and pick the smallest number of blocks that still gives the control you need. If you are uncertain, start with moderate $r$ and increase if you see underfitting of controllability.

# COFT hyperparameters: choosing ε and projecting Q
COFT adds an explicit constraint keeping the orthogonal transform close to identity:
$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$,
so the finetuned weight stays in an $\epsilon$-neighborhood around the pretrained weight.

Practical implementation details and approximations:
- Neumann approximation to move the constraint to $\bm{Q}$. For small $\bm{Q}$ the Cayley transform satisfies
  $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$,
  so constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ can be approximated by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ for some $\epsilon'$ (mapped from $\epsilon$ via the approximation or set empirically).

- Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$. After each gradient update to the skew-symmetric parameters $\bm{Q}$ (or to each block $\bm{Q}_i$), project back to the feasible set by scaling/clipping if the operator norm is above the chosen threshold. A simple practical projector is:
  if $\|\bm{Q}\|_F > \epsilon'$, replace $\bm{Q} \leftarrow \bm{Q}\cdot (\epsilon'/\|\bm{Q}\|_F)$,
  and then re-symmetrize (enforce skew-symmetry) by $\bm{Q}\leftarrow (\bm{Q}-\bm{Q}^\top)/2$. For block-diagonal parameterization apply the projection per block.

- Choosing $\epsilon$. The paper shows that $\epsilon$ controls COFT’s flexibility: smaller $\epsilon$ makes the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained model (more conservative), larger $\epsilon$ approaches OFT’s flexibility. The paper does not prescribe a single universal $\epsilon$; instead pick a value that yields stable finetuning for your task. Empirically in the paper COFT makes learning rate and iteration count less sensitive, so if you want safety pick a modest $\epsilon$ (a small nonzero value) and increase only if the model underfits control.

- Practical tip: start with $\bm{Q}$ initialized to zero (so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$). Run short experiments sweeping $\epsilon'$ (or the equivalent $\epsilon$) on a validation set and look for the smallest $\epsilon$ that achieves your control metric target. Because COFT explicitly constrains deviation, you can confidently set larger iteration budgets without catastrophic model drift.

# Optimizers, learning rates, and iteration budgets
The paper highlights that OFT/COFT tend to converge faster and are more stable than unconstrained finetuning and adapter methods. From the experimental regimen and observed dynamics in the paper:

- Optimizer. Any gradient-based optimizer used for finetuning large diffusion models is compatible. The paper does not require specialized optimizers for OFT/COFT. Use the same optimizer family you use for other finetuning (e.g., Adam-style optimizers) so that comparisons are controlled.

- Learning rates. OFT and COFT are less sensitive to very small learning rates because the identity initialization and orthogonality constraint regularize updates. In practice, you can initially use learning rates comparable to other adapter/LoRA-style finetuning; COFT’s explicit radius constraint further reduces the need for ultra-small learning rates. If you observe instability reduce the learning rate or tighten the COFT radius.

- Iteration / epoch budgets. The paper’s experiments provide useful reference points:
  - Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-like): OFT/COFT reached good control after about 400 iterations and remained stable at 2000 iterations, while naive methods could collapse with long training. This suggests OFT permits safe use of larger iteration budgets.
  - Controllable generation: experiments finetuned for 20 epochs on control datasets. The OFT family typically converged substantially faster than baseline adapters: in the face landmark task COFT/OFT reached the final baseline-level performance in ≈8 epochs while LoRA took ≈20 epochs.
Use these as starting points, but remember dataset size and task complexity will influence required epochs. COFT in particular allows you to set a higher epoch budget with less risk of overfitting or collapse.

# Integrating with DreamBooth losses and control-conditioning pipelines
OFT/COFT are a drop-in mechanism for parameterizing the finetuned weights. They do not change the loss function or training objective; instead they constrain how weights are updated. Integration patterns used in the paper:

- Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth framework). Keep the DreamBooth reconstruction loss and the prior-preservation loss exactly as in DreamBooth. Replace the finetunable matrices in the diffusion U-Net (typically attention projection matrices) with the OFT parameterization $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$. Initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and train $\bm{Q}$ (and optionally scaling $\bm{D}$) under the OFT/COFT constraints. The rest of the DreamBooth training loop (prompt construction, data augmentations, prior-preservation sampling) remains unchanged.

- Controllable generation pipelines (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter style). If you follow ControlNet or T2I-Adapter, finetune the same attention projection weights where LoRA or the adapter would have been applied, but use the OFT parameterization instead. Because OFT does not add extra model branches at inference it integrates cleanly: at inference you either (a) fold $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ to produce an updated weight matrix, or (b) keep separate and compute $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$ during forward. The training loss and conditioning signal (additional control inputs like Canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks) and their conditioning heads remain unchanged.

- Keep existing conditioning heads / adapters unchanged. OFT is not a replacement for control encoders (the networks that transform a control signal into a conditioning embedding). It simply modifies how the base diffusion model’s neurons are updated. If your control pipeline uses a separate lightweight adapter head, you can finetune that as usual while also applying OFT to the base projection matrices.

# Exporting folded weights to avoid inference overhead
OFT’s transforms are applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weight. There is no extra inference-time cost if you fold the learned orthogonal transform into the pretrained weights after training:

- Folding operation. After training, compute the folded weight
  $\displaystyle \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$
  (for re-scaled OFT use $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$). Replace $\bm{W}^0$ in your model with this new $\bm{W}$. This yields a standard weight matrix with the same inference-time compute and memory as the original pretrained model.

- Implementation notes. Because Cayley parameterization uses matrix inverses during training, make sure the final folded $\bm{W}$ is computed with the numeric $\bm{R}$ value obtained after training. If you used block-diagonal blocks, fold each block similarly. Once folded, you can discard the OFT parameters entirely in inference deployment.

# Practical checklist and recommended workflow
1. Select target layers: typically attention projections in the U-Net / transformer blocks. Optionally add selected conv layers if needed.
2. Decide block strategy: pick $r$ so block sizes match head/channel grouping; consider block sharing to reduce parameters.
3. Parameterize each block $\bm{R}_i$ via Cayley transform from skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}_i$. Initialize all $\bm{Q}_i=\bm{0}$.
4. If using COFT, choose an initial radius $\epsilon$ and map to a practical $\epsilon'$ for the $\bm{Q}$ projector. Start conservatively (small $\epsilon$) and relax if underfitting.
5. Use same loss as your target pipeline (DreamBooth or control conditioning). Do not change the objective — OFT is orthogonal reparameterization only.
6. Train with your usual optimizer; learning rates comparable to adapter-based finetuning are a safe starting point. Monitor control-consistency metrics on a validation set.
7. If training is unstable: decrease learning rate, decrease $\epsilon$ (for COFT), or increase block granularity (more blocks / more sharing) to strengthen regularization.
8. After training, fold $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ to produce final weights and drop OFT parameters for inference.

# Final notes and cautions
- The Cayley parameterization produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ only (no reflections). The paper found this restriction acceptable in practice; if your task needs broader transforms you would need an alternative orthogonal parameterization (not covered by the paper).

- COFT’s projection onto $\|\bm{Q}\|\le \epsilon'$ is an approximation of $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$. For large $\bm{Q}$ the approximation degrades; therefore COFT works best when the desired deviation from identity is moderate and when training keeps updates relatively local.

- OFT/COFT preserve hyperspherical energy by construction (orthogonal multiplies preserve pairwise angles of normalized neurons). This property is central to the approach and is what gives OFT its stability and semantic-preservation advantages compared to unconstrained finetuning.

This guide summarizes the implementation and practical choices demonstrated in the paper. Following the recommendations above reproduces the behaviours observed in the experiments: faster, more stable finetuning, strong subject-preservation and controllability, and the ability to export folded weights for zero-overhead inference.

Extensions, Insights, and Open Problems

# Architecture-agnostic nature: transformers and convolutions (depth-wise view)

One of the key properties emphasized in the paper is that Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is fundamentally architecture-agnostic: in principle OFT can be applied to any neural network layer where neuron weight vectors are meaningful objects to transform. The paper discusses OFT in the context of fully connected layers and shows how the same idea carries over to attention matrices in Transformers and to convolutional layers. The central mechanism is the same across architectures: learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (or a block-diagonal composition of such matrices) and apply it multiplicatively to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, yielding finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles between normalized neuron vectors, OFT guarantees preservation of hyperspherical energy and the neuron relational structure that the paper argues is important for semantic generation.

For Transformers, the natural place to apply OFT is to the attention weight matrices, which are analogous to fully connected layers in their linear mapping behavior. The paper explicitly compares OFT to LoRA in this setting by applying OFT to the same attention weights that LoRA modifies, ensuring a fair experimental comparison. The identity initialization of $\bm{R}$ means the pretrained model is recovered at the start of finetuning, and the Cayley-based orthogonal parameterization makes it straightforward to impose orthogonality while optimizing in the same gradient-based training loop.

For convolutional layers the paper highlights two useful interpretations of a block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization. If the block partitioning aligns with input channels (e.g., one block per input channel), each block acts on a single channel's neuron vectors and the overall transform behaves similarly to depth-wise operations: each channel's filters are rotated independently. If the same orthogonal block is shared across channels, the transform becomes an orthogonal mapping applied uniformly across channels. These two extremes illustrate how block-diagonal OFT can interpolate between per-channel flexibility and global regularity, providing a convenient, interpretable lever to control parameter overhead and inductive bias specific to convolutional architectures.

# Compositionality potential: chaining orthogonal matrices across tasks

A notable conceptual insight discussed in the paper is the compositionality potential of OFT. Orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. That algebraic property immediately suggests an appealing operational idea: if we finetune the same pretrained model for multiple distinct downstream tasks, each finetuning could learn its own orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots$; composing those tasks by multiplying the corresponding matrices yields another orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}} = \bm{R}_k\cdots\bm{R}_2\bm{R}_1$, and thus a single orthogonal transform that is still guaranteed to preserve hyperspherical energy.

The paper frames this as an open direction: whether such multiplicative composition of task-specific orthogonal matrices preserves the knowledge required for all downstream tasks is an intriguing question. The algebra guarantees preservation of pairwise neuron angles (hence hyperspherical energy), but semantics for multiple tasks may interact nontrivially. Composition could, in principle, allow a compact mechanism for incremental adaptation: store per-task orthogonal factors and multiply them at inference time to assemble composite behaviors without modifying the original pretrained weights. However, the paper cautions that whether composed orthogonal matrices retain performance for all constituent tasks remains empirical and theoretical territory to explore. The idea is promising because it leverages a small, structured parameter (an orthogonal factor per task) and keeps the global property (angle preservation) intact by construction.

# Scalability: differentiable matrix inverse bottlenecks and faster parameterizations

A practical limitation acknowledged and discussed in the paper is the computational cost associated with enforcing orthogonality via Cayley parameterization, which requires computing matrix inverses of the form $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. While Cayley gives a differentiable and stable way to obtain orthogonal matrices (with determinant $+1$), matrix inversion is computationally heavier than the simple low-rank additive updates used by other methods. This cost motivates two of the paper's pragmatic countermeasures.

First, the block-diagonal parameterization reduces the effective dimensionality of each inverse by partitioning $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks of size $d/r$, so the inversion cost and parameter count scale with the block size rather than the full $d$. The number of parameters for an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix is $d(d/r-1)/2$, which reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ complexity. Sharing blocks across subdivisions can reduce parameter cost further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These structural reductions make Cayley viable even when $d$ is large.

Second, the paper recognizes this matrix inverse as a bottleneck and mentions it as an open problem: finding faster, differentiable parameterizations or approximations that retain orthogonality guarantees but avoid heavy inversion. The paper uses the Neumann series to reason about small deviations from identity: when $\bm{Q}$ is small, $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k$ and one can approximate $\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This expansion is used in COFT to move the radius constraint onto $\bm{Q}$ for easier enforcement via projection. More generally, such series-based approximations or other structured orthogonal parameterizations (e.g., products of Givens rotations, Householder reflections, or parameterizations that exploit sparsity) are pragmatic avenues suggested by the paper to improve scalability while preserving the desired mathematical properties.

# Bias–flexibility trade-offs in block-diagonal designs

A recurring theme in the paper is the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. OFT constrains finetuning to orthogonal transforms, thereby preserving hyperspherical energy; the block-diagonal parameterization imposes a stronger constraint that reduces parameter count and computational cost. This design introduces an inductive bias: the model cannot express arbitrary global rotations of the neuron space if blocks are small or shared. The paper presents this bias as a feature, not solely a drawback, because it stabilizes finetuning and prevents collapse: smaller blocks limit the degrees of freedom and therefore the risk of destroying pretrained generative structures while still allowing meaningful directional updates that modify semantics.

However, the paper also recognizes that block-diagonal structure limits flexibility and may introduce undesirable biases for certain layers or tasks that require cross-block interactions. Choosing the number of blocks $r$ (and whether to share blocks) becomes a practical hyperparameter balancing expressivity and parameter efficiency. Empirically, the paper reports that OFT performs well with modest blockization and that increasing the size or number of trainable parameters can further improve performance; this suggests the block design should be tuned according to resource budgets and the needed degree of adaptation. The paper highlights this bias–flexibility trade-off explicitly as an area for further research: can we devise parameter-efficient orthogonal structures that are less biased than naive block-diagonal splitting, or can adaptive blockization schemes be learned per layer or per task?

# Human evaluation considerations and additional control modalities

The paper reports quantitative evaluation metrics for both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS, IoU/F1, mean IoU, accuracy, landmark L2) and also mentions human evaluation and qualitative comparisons in appendices. It emphasizes that OFT not only improves automated metrics but also yields qualitatively better preservation of subject identity and stronger adherence to control signals. The paper calls out human evaluation in the appendices as corroborating evidence of OFT’s advantages.

Beyond the evaluated control modalities (Canny edges, segmentation maps, facial landmarks), the OFT framework is architecturally agnostic and therefore readily applicable to additional control signals that are commonly used in controllable generation research (the paper demonstrates further applicability in appendices to sketches, depth maps, dense pose, etc.). Because OFT works by rotating neuron directions and optionally rescaling magnitudes (in the re-scaled OFT variant), it is compatible with any control mechanism that is realized via finetuning weights to condition on auxiliary inputs. The paper emphasizes that OFT introduces no extra inference-time overhead, which is an important practical advantage when deploying models that support many control modalities: stored orthogonal matrices (or their multiplied equivalents) can be applied directly to pretrained weights, keeping inference latency identical to the base model.

For human evaluation, the paper indicates that user studies were performed and reported in the appendix; these studies further validate OFT’s strengths. The broad implication is that OFT’s angle-preserving constraint aligns with human judgments about semantic preservation and faithful control, but the paper also acknowledges that the design of human studies and the particular tasks/modalities chosen can influence conclusions, and that more expansive human-evaluation protocols across modalities and demographic axes remain valuable future work.

# Open questions: improved parameter efficiency, theory of semantic preservation via HE, multi-task orthogonal composition

The paper closes by enumerating several open problems and directions that naturally follow from OFT’s contributions. A first and practical open question is how to improve parameter and computational efficiency while retaining provable orthogonality and hyperspherical energy preservation. The block-diagonal Cayley approach is effective, but exploring parameterizations that reduce inversion cost or that distribute adaptivity more economically (e.g., sparse orthogonal factors, learned block partitions, products of structured low-cost rotations) are promising research directions mentioned in the paper.

A second, more theoretical open problem is understanding precisely why preserving hyperspherical energy (HE) corresponds to preserving pretrained generative semantics. The paper motivates HE as a measure of pairwise neuron relationships on the unit hypersphere and empirically finds that HE preservation correlates with maintenance of semantic generation ability. However, a formal theory that links HE or related angular statistics of neuron populations to generative semantics, diversity, and fidelity across architectures and training regimes is not developed in the paper and remains an important avenue for future work. Such theory could inform principled hyperparameter choices (e.g., block sizes, $\epsilon$ in COFT) and illuminate the limits of angular invariants in preserving task-general knowledge.

A third open question concerns multi-task composition and incremental adaptation. The algebraic compositionality of orthogonal matrices invites the hypothesis that chains of per-task orthogonal transforms can be composed to achieve multiple task behaviors without interference. The paper points out that whether composed orthogonal matrices preserve knowledge of all downstream tasks is unknown: composition preserves the hyperspherical energy property, but interactions among tasks might require non-orthogonal corrections or additional mechanisms (e.g., small diagonal rescalings in re-scaled OFT). Studying interference, catastrophic forgetting, and capacity limits for chains of orthogonal factors is a concrete multi-task research agenda suggested by the authors.

Finally, the paper mentions the constrained variant COFT, which imposes an explicit radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ (implemented approximately via constraining $\bm{Q}$ in the Cayley parameterization). Tuning and theoretically characterizing how such a radius trades off adaptability versus preservation—and how it interacts with data size, task difficulty, and model depth—are open empirical and theoretical questions. Taken together, these open problems chart a roadmap spanning engineering (faster parameterizations), empirical (multi-task and modality evaluations), and theoretical (why HE matters) research endeavors arising from OFT.

# Closing remarks

The OFT framework forwards a simple but powerful idea: constrain finetuning to layer-shared orthogonal transforms so that pairwise neuron angles—and the hyperspherical energy that summarizes them—are preserved. This produces a principled balance between the flexibility needed to adapt neuron directions (which the paper argues are central to semantic changes) and the regularity needed to avoid destroying pretrained generative capabilities. The architectural generality, the compositional algebraic properties, and the explicit open problems identified in the paper together form a fertile set of directions for future work: making orthogonal finetuning more scalable, more parameter-efficient, theoretically grounded, and broadly applicable across tasks and modalities.
