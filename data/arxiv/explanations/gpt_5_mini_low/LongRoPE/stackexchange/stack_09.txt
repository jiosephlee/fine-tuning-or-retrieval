\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the progressive extension avoid catastrophic drift when re-searching on a fine-tuned 256k model?
Question:
The method fine-tunes to 256k and then runs a second search to reach 2048k without further training. Why does this not undo the benefits of the 256k fine-tuning? Intuitively, the weights adapted to a certain geometry of RoPE angles at 256k. After you change the angle scaling again for 2048k, what keeps the representations coherent? Is there evidence that the 256k-adapted weights land in a basin that is robust to subsequent frequency adjustments?
Answer:
Short answer: because LongRoPE does two things that together preserve the geometry learned by the model during $256\text{k}$ fine‑tuning: (1) the non‑uniform rescaling found by the search deliberately leaves the “important” RoPE frequencies and the early token positions mostly unchanged, so the fine‑tuned weights still see familiar angle patterns; and (2) the second search is performed on the already fine‑tuned model and optimizes rescale factors by perplexity, so it chooses a new rescaling that minimally perturbs the model’s operating point (i.e., it finds a rescaling that stays inside a basin where the fine‑tuned weights still work). The paper’s experiments and ablations are the evidence for this.

Why the $256\text{k}$ fine‑tuned weights don’t “drift” catastrophically when you rescale again for $2048\text{k}$
Intuitively, the problem with naïve global interpolation is that it compresses or shifts all rotary angles uniformly, which can make previously well‑separated position encodings collapse into the same small angular region and thus break attention geometry. LongRoPE’s searches are explicitly non‑uniform across RoPE dimensions and across token positions: lower (high‑frequency) dimensions and the initial token positions are often left with much smaller rescaling (sometimes none). In other words, the search preserves the parts of the positional encoding that the model relies on most for short‑ and medium‑range structure. Because those “anchor” components change little, the downstream weight patterns (attention heads, MLPs) remain coherent after a secondary rescale.

Mechanistically, two factors in LongRoPE make the secondary rescale gentle and robust:

1) The search finds per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ (with a monotonicity constraint) so important low‑frequency/high‑information dimensions do not get large $\lambda$. This keeps the core angular geometry that the model’s weights adapted to during $256\text{k}$ fine‑tuning.  
2) The second search is applied to the already fine‑tuned checkpoint and evaluated by perplexity on actual long sequences; it therefore prefers rescale patterns that preserve model performance rather than arbitrary angle changes. So the algorithm effectively searches for a rescaling that the current weights can accommodate (staying in a good basin), rather than forcing the weights to adapt to a radically different geometry.

Empirical evidence in the paper
The paper provides multiple experimental signals supporting this explanation. The “Effectiveness of the second positional interpolation” ablation shows that taking a $256\text{k}$ fine‑tuned model and applying LongRoPE’s second search to reach $512\text{k}/1024\text{k}/2048\text{k}$ keeps perplexity at a consistent level, whereas PI and YaRN applied at the same step cause perplexity to rise quickly. That directly demonstrates the secondary search finds rescalings compatible with the $256\text{k}$ weights.

Further supporting observations: (a) models fine‑tuned at $256\text{k}$ perform better after the second extension than models fine‑tuned at $128\text{k}$ because the secondary extension ratio is smaller ($8\times$ vs $16\times$), which indicates that smaller rescale deltas are easier for the fine‑tuned weights to tolerate — consistent with the “stay in basin” idea; (b) the paper’s findings that preserving initial tokens (a non‑uniformity in token positions) helps performance show the model relies on those early positions as anchors, so leaving them unchanged makes later rescaling less disruptive; (c) the RoPE‑dimension non‑uniformity ablation shows most gains come from per‑dimension rescaling rather than crude global scaling, implying that selectively preserving some dimensions is key to robustness.

Caveats and limits
There is no formal theorem in the paper proving that fine‑tuned weights necessarily land in a basin robust to arbitrary subsequent frequency changes. The evidence is empirical: the evolutionary search chooses rescale factors that minimize perplexity on the (fine‑tuned) model, and the ablations show those choices work far better than naïve alternatives. Also, the paper notes that token‑position non‑uniformity helps more at moderate extension lengths than at the extreme $2048\text{k}$ case (initial‑token preservation becomes less effective at the largest scales), so robustness is not infinite — it depends on the secondary extension ratio and on how well the search can preserve the critical angular components.

Takeaway
Progressive extension works because LongRoPE’s searches are designed to (a) preserve the critical parts of the original RoPE encoding that the model’s weights learned to use, and (b) re‑search rescale factors on the fine‑tuned checkpoint to find rescalings that the weights can already handle. The paper’s ablations (second interpolation effectiveness, better results for ft=$256\text{k}$ vs ft=$128\text{k}$, and per‑dimension/token experiments) are the primary empirical evidence that the $256\text{k}$-adapted weights remain coherent under the subsequent $2048\text{k}$ rescaling.
