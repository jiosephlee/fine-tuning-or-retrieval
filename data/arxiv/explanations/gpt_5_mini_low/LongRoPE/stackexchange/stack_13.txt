\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the relationship between per-dimension $\lambda_i$ and an effective change of RoPE base $\theta$?
Question:
Standard RoPE uses $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$, equivalently angles $n/\beta^i$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$. If LongRoPE applies different $\lambda_i$ per dimension, is this equivalent to using a different base $\theta_i'$ per $i$ (i.e., changing $\beta$ per dimension)? If so, could this be interpreted as learning a non-geometric frequency ladder instead of a single-base geometric progression, and does that viewpoint suggest constraints or regularization that might improve search stability?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ are equivalent to changing the effective frequency for each RoPE dimension, but in general they do not correspond to a single new base $\theta'$ unless the $\lambda_i$ follow a geometric law. That means LongRoPE is effectively learning a non-geometric frequency ladder (one frequency per RoPE dimension). That viewpoint suggests a few simple and practical constraints/regularizers (many of which the paper already uses) to improve stability and reduce search complexity.

Explanation and math grounded in the paper
RoPE originally uses rotation angles
$\cos(n \theta_0), \sin(n \theta_0), ..., \cos(n \theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n \theta_{d/2-1})$
with $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$. The paper rewrites angles as $n/\beta^i$ where $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$, so the i-th dimension’s angle is
$n / \beta^i$.

LongRoPE applies a per-dimension rescale factor (in the paper’s notation the factor applied to the angle is $\mathbb{I}$ — for $n\ge\hat n$ they multiply the original angle by $1/\lambda_i$). So the new angle becomes
$n / (\lambda_i \beta^i)$.

Ask whether that equals changing the base to some $\theta_i'$ (or equivalently $\beta'$ such that angle = $n / (\beta')^i$): to write
$n / (\lambda_i \beta^i) = n / (\beta'_{} )^{i}$
we would need
$(\beta')^{i} = \lambda_i \beta^{i}$
so
$\beta' = \beta \cdot (\lambda_i)^{1/i}$.

Because $(\lambda_i)^{1/i}$ depends on $i$ in general, $\beta'$ would be different for each $i$. Only if $\lambda_i = c^{i}$ (i.e., $\lambda_i$ grows geometrically with $i$) does $(\lambda_i)^{1/i}$ become the constant $c$ and you recover a single new base $\beta' = \beta c$. Thus: per-dimension $\lambda_i$ is a much more general modification than changing a single $\theta$ (single-base geometric progression). Equivalently, LongRoPE is learning a non-geometric frequency ladder: each RoPE dimension can shift independently.

Intuition and consequences
A single-base change rescales all frequencies multiplicatively with the same geometric progression; per-dimension $\lambda_i$ lets you preserve or selectively modify certain frequencies. That matches the paper’s empirical findings: lower-index (high-frequency) dimensions should be changed less and some mid/high dimensions more, and also initial token positions may require different treatment. Per-dimension freedom explains why non-uniform interpolation can preserve useful high-frequency structure and avoid “crowding” positions.

Practical regularization / constraints suggested by this viewpoint
Because per-dimension freedom is powerful but creates a huge search space and potential instability, the paper and the theory suggest effective constraints/regularizers:

1) Monotonicity in $\lambda_i$ (used in the paper): enforce $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$. This encodes the NTK/intuition that lower (higher-frequency) dimensions should be interpolated less. It reduces search space and avoids pathological oscillations across dimensions.

2) Smoothness / low-complexity prior on $\log \lambda_i$: penalize large second differences in $\log \lambda_i$ (e.g., $\sum_i (\log \lambda_{i+1}-2\log \lambda_i+\log \lambda_{i-1})^2$). This encourages a near-geometric ladder but permits gentle departures where data needs them. It combines the flexibility of per-dimension scaling with stability of a geometric prior.

3) Parametric bottleneck / low-rank basis: represent $\log \lambda_i$ as a small set of basis functions (affine + few sinusoids or low-degree polynomial): $\log \lambda_i = a + b i + \sum_k c_k \phi_k(i)$. Setting most $c_k$ to zero reduces degrees of freedom while allowing deviations from strict geometric scaling. This is useful to speed search and improve generalization.

4) Anchor to baseline (shrinkage): add an L2 penalty toward the PI/NTK baseline $\lambda^{(0)}_i$ (e.g., minimize $\sum_i (\log \lambda_i - \log \lambda^{(0)}_i)^2$ weighted by how important that dimension is). This prevents wild deviations, especially for very large extension ratios where extrapolation is risky.

5) Clip range / relative bounds (used implicitly in paper): bound $\lambda_i \in [1.0, s\times 1.25]$ and step-size. Limiting extremes prevents catastrophic crowding/extrapolation.

6) Grouping (coarse per-band search): when full per-dimension search is too expensive, search per-group (like YaRN’s groups) but allow adaptive grouping learned from data.

Initialization and optimization tips
Initialize $\lambda_i$ close to a geometric solution (PI or NTK) and search only residuals. The paper already seeds the population with PI/NTK/YaRN solutions — that matches the idea of starting near a geometric ladder and letting controlled deviations improve performance.

Connection to broader concepts
This is conceptually like learning a custom frequency basis for positional encoding rather than assuming a fixed exponential frequency ladder (analogy: learned Fourier features vs fixed Fourier frequencies). The regularizers above are analogous to priors used in spectral learning: prefer smooth, low-frequency corrections to a canonical spectrum, only allowing large localized changes when strongly supported by data.

Short takeaway
Per-dimension $\lambda_i$ is not generally equivalent to a single changed base $\theta'$ unless $\lambda_i$ itself is geometric. LongRoPE’s learned $\lambda_i$ therefore implements a non-geometric frequency ladder. To improve search stability and generalization, impose monotonicity, smoothness in $\log \lambda_i$, low-dimensional parameterizations, mild shrinkage towards geometric baselines, and bounded ranges — all of which keep flexibility but reduce overfitting and search instability (and several of which the paper already employs).
