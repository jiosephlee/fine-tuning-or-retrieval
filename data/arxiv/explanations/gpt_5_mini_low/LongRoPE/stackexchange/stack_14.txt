\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Does the hard threshold at $\hat n$ introduce artifacts around that boundary, and did you try a smooth schedule?
Question:
Eq. (3) applies $\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i, \hat n)$ such that angles for $n < \hat n$ are unscaled and for $n \ge \hat n$ are scaled by $1/\lambda_i$. That creates a discontinuity at $n = \hat n$. Did you observe any boundary artifacts (e.g., spikes in loss) near that position? If so, did you try a smooth transition (e.g., a cosine ramp for $n \in [\hat n - w, \hat n + w]$) and how did it compare?
Answer:
Short answer: We did not observe any obvious or systematic boundary artifacts (e.g., spikes in perplexity or loss) caused by the hard cutoff at $\hat n$ in our experiments, and we did not implement or evaluate an explicit smooth ramp in the paper. Instead, the evolutionary search over $\hat n$ plus the monotonic constraint on per-dimension $\lambda_i$ tends to find thresholds that avoid harmful behavior in practice. Below I explain why that is plausible, what we actually measured, and why a smooth schedule is a reasonable future experiment.

Why a hard threshold is not fatal (intuitively and practically)
RoPE encodes positions via pairs $\cos(n\theta_i),\sin(n\theta_i)$. Changing the scale for positions $n\ge\hat n$ replaces the argument of those trig functions from $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ to $\frac{n}{\beta^i\lambda_i}$. That is a discrete change in the angle sequence at index $\hat n$, so in principle there can be a jump in value (not just derivative) at the boundary. However, in practice two facts reduce the practical harm:

1. Perplexity / downstream metrics average over many positions and many examples; a single local mismatch at one index is diluted and usually does not dominate global loss unless it consistently breaks attention patterns.  
2. Our search explicitly optimizes $\hat n$ (over values $\{0,1,2,4,\dots,256\}$) together with the per-dimension $\lambda_i$. The search prefers $\hat n$ that preserves early token encodings that are most influential for attention, so it avoids thresholds that create large systematic mismatches at widely-attended positions. Empirically this produced stable perplexity curves and good passkey retrieval up to very long lengths.  
3. We also impose the monotonic constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, which prevents pathological oscillatory $\lambda$ patterns across dimensions that could amplify a boundary mismatch.

What we actually measured
The experiments reported in Sections 2–4 focus on perplexity traces across increasing evaluation lengths (sliding-window perplexity on PG19, Proof-pile, Books3) and the passkey retrieval accuracy. If the hard cutoff caused a strong, reproducible boundary artifact (for example a spike in loss concentrated around tokens at $\hat n$), it would have shown up as degraded perplexity or retrieval performance around lengths where that boundary is encountered. Instead:

- Retaining the first $\hat n$ tokens without interpolation consistently improved perplexity (Finding 2 and Table 2), and the search picked sensible $\hat n$ values for different extension ratios.
- We report no abrupt spikes in perplexity trace that would indicate a catastrophic boundary effect attributable to the indicator in Eq. (3). (By contrast we do point out other interpolation methods that show spikes—e.g., YaRN under certain settings—but those are different failure modes.)

Did we try a smooth transition (cosine ramp / ramp width $w$)?
No—there was no experiment in the paper that replaced the hard indicator $\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)$ with a continuous ramp (e.g., gradually changing $\lambda$ for $n\in[\hat n-w,\hat n+w]$). The paper focused on searching discrete $\hat n$ values and per-dimension $\lambda_i$ because that gave a tractable search space and strong empirical gains (including reliable $8\times$ non-fine-tuned extension and the progressive route to $2048\text{k}$). We therefore cannot report comparative results for smooth schedules.

Why smoothing is worth trying (and how to do it)
A smooth ramp is a natural idea: instead of a hard switch, set the effective per-position scale to $\lambda_i(n)$ that moves continuously from 1 to $\lambda_i$ over a window. For example a cosine ramp

$$
\lambda_i(n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat n - w\\[4pt]
1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\cos\bigl(\pi\frac{n-(\hat n-w)}{2w}\bigr)\bigr)(\lambda_i-1) & \hat n-w \le n \le \hat n+w\\[6pt]
\lambda_i & n>\hat n+w
\end{cases}
$$

makes the angle argument change continuously and smoothly across the boundary. This may reduce any local discontinuity and could help in cases where attention strongly focuses on tokens near the threshold. Implementation-wise it is cheap at inference (just compute a scalar per position) and straightforward to include in the evolution search by adding $w$ as an extra search dimension or by testing a small set of widths.

Why the paper authors likely chose the hard cutoff
A few practical reasons: (1) keeping the search space compact (discrete $\hat n$ choices) simplifies the evolutionary search and keeps evaluation costs down; (2) the empirical gains from per-dimension $\lambda_i$ + discrete $\hat n$ were already large and stable (including progressive fine-tuning to $256\text{k}$ then extension to $2048\text{k}$); (3) the authors found token-position non-uniformity to be helpful up to certain scales (see the ablation: useful at $16\text{k}/32\text{k}$ but less so at extreme $2048\text{k}$), so a more complex smoothing knob may not have been necessary to reach their main results.

Recommendation
If you are worried about subtle boundary artifacts in your use case (e.g., many tasks where content near $\hat n$ is repeatedly important), try a small experiment replacing the step with a ramp of width $w\in\{4,8,32,128\}$ and measure perplexity and any localized loss around the boundary. It is cheap to implement and could only help; if it shows no effect, you keep the simpler hard-threshold solution that the paper used.

Summary
Empirically, the hard threshold used in Eq. (3) did not produce observable catastrophic artifacts in our reported metrics; the evolutionary search over $\hat n$ and $\lambda_i$ together with monotonicity constraints appears sufficient to avoid problematic boundaries. The paper did not test an explicit smooth ramp, but a cosine/linear ramp is straightforward to try and may be a useful small extension for cases where local boundary stability matters.
