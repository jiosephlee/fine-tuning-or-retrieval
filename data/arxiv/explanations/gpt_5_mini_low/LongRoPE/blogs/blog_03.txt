# The Big Insight: Positional Information Isn’t Uniform

Large language models (LLMs) rely on positional encodings to know the order of tokens. RoPE (rotary positional embeddings) is the dominant choice: it encodes position by rotating query/key subspaces with angles proportional to position and a per-dimension frequency. Most positional-extension techniques (e.g., linear interpolation / PI, NTK-inspired scaling, YaRN) treat position interpolation in a fairly uniform way — either scaling all dimensions the same or grouping dimensions into a few frequency buckets. LongRoPE shows that this is missing a fundamental point: positional information is non-uniform in two separate ways, and exploiting both yields far better long-context behavior.

This post explains the two key discoveries from LongRoPE and the intuition behind why they matter.

## Quick primer: how RoPE represents positions

RoPE represents position \(n\) across half the embedding dimensions with sinusoidal-like rotary angles:
\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \sin(n\theta_1), \dots\right],
\]
where \(\theta_i\) are per-dimension frequencies (often parameterized so frequencies decrease across dimensions). When you extend a model to a longer context length \(L'\) from the original \(L\), common approaches rescale the rotation angles so that new positions fall into the pretrained angle range. The usual view is: pick a scale \(\lambda\) (often equal to the extension ratio \(s = L'/L\)) and multiply all angles by \(1/\lambda\). LongRoPE asks: what if we do not treat all dimensions or all token positions the same?

## Discovery 1 — RoPE dimensions are not equal: dimension-wise, non-uniform rescaling helps

Observation: different RoPE dimensions carry different "kinds" of positional information. Lower-index dimensions correspond to higher-frequency rotations and are more sensitive to small position shifts; higher-index dimensions are lower-frequency and can encode longer-range coarse position signals.

What LongRoPE finds empirically is that a single uniform rescale \(\lambda\) across all dimensions is sub-optimal. Instead, you should allow a per-dimension rescale \(\lambda_i\). Concretely, LongRoPE searches for \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and applies them so the rotary angle at dimension \(i\) becomes
\[
\text{angle}_{i}(n) = \frac{n}{\beta^i} \times \frac{1}{\lambda_i},
\]
(with \(\beta\) the usual frequency base). The search enforces monotonicity \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\), which matches the NTK intuition that higher-frequency dims need less scaling.

Why this helps: uniform downscaling crowds many original positions into a tight angle range, especially in the high-frequency dims, destroying the model’s ability to distinguish nearby tokens. By selectively scaling less in the dimensions that are responsible for fine-grained positional discrimination (lower-index, higher-frequency dims) and more in the dims that are safe to compress, you preserve the distinctions that matter while still making room for larger absolute positions. The result is lower perplexity and more stable behavior when you evaluate at extended context lengths — and it even enables significant extension without any fine-tuning in many cases.

## Discovery 2 — Early tokens matter more: preserve initial token positions

Observation: not all token positions are equally important for attention and representation. The earliest tokens in a sequence frequently receive strong attention and play outsized roles in the model's computations (e.g., prompts, left context, or early context anchors). LongRoPE finds that leaving the first \(\hat{n}\) token positions un-interpolated (i.e., using the original RoPE angles for those positions) improves performance.

Formally, LongRoPE uses a threshold \(\hat{n}\) and applies per-dimension rescaling only for positions \(n \ge \hat{n}\):
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n} \\
1/\lambda_i & n \ge \hat{n}
\end{cases}
\]
So the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens keep their original angular encoding, while later tokens use the rescaled angles.

Why this helps: the early tokens often dominate attention (they are the “hot spots” that many other tokens attend to). If you heavily compress their angles, you reduce the model’s ability to discriminate those positions, leading to greater downstream error. Preserving the original angles for initial tokens keeps those crucial attention targets readable and sharply separated in angle space, which reduces information loss.

LongRoPE also shows that the optimal \(\hat{n}\) depends on the target extended length: for modest extensions, a small \(\hat{n}\) may suffice; for larger extensions the best \(\hat{n}\) can vary. The key is that preserving a slice of early positions can materially improve perplexity and retrieval-like tasks.

## Putting the two discoveries together

Treating both per-dimension and per-position non-uniformities jointly gives a large space of possible rescalings, and LongRoPE tackles this by an evolutionary search that:

- Searches per-dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) (with monotonicity constraints).
- Searches an early-token threshold \(\hat{n}\) to avoid rescaling the most-attended positions.
- Initializes the search with existing interpolation strategies (PI, NTK, YaRN) to speed convergence.

That searched initialization preserves the most informative parts of the original RoPE and therefore makes subsequent fine-tuning far easier (or, in many cases, allows impressive extensions without fine-tuning at all). Using this approach LongRoPE demonstrates stable extensions up to 8× without fine-tuning, and—via a progressive strategy that fine-tunes first at 128k/256k and then re-searches—achieves practical 2,048k context windows while recovering short-context performance by re-adjusting rescale factors for small lengths.

## Intuition recap: why preserving sharpness matters

You can think of each RoPE dimension as a “lens” with a different focus: some lenses see tiny local offsets (high-frequency dims), others see coarse position trends (low-frequency dims). If you uniformly squash all lenses, you get blurry positional perception and the model struggles to tell adjacent tokens apart. By selectively preserving the high-resolution lenses (and protecting early, highly-attended positions), LongRoPE keeps positional distinctions sharp where they matter and compresses only the parts that can tolerate it. This minimizes information loss and keeps both long-range capability and short-range precision.

## Practical takeaways

- If you need to extend a RoPE-based model to longer contexts, don’t assume a single global scaling is best. Per-dimension and per-position choices matter.
- Preserve early token positions (tune a small \(\hat{n}\)) to keep attention targets distinct.
- Use a constrained search (monotonic \(\lambda_i\)) initialized from known methods to find practical rescale schedules that reduce perplexity and stabilize fine-tuning.

LongRoPE turns what was previously a blunt instrument — uniform interpolation — into a more surgical operation, and that surgical approach makes very large context windows both practical and performant.
