# From 4k to 2 Million: The Progressive Extension Playbook

LLMs with huge context windows unlock new workflows: long-form summarization, in-context learning with many examples, and agents that can maintain lengthy histories. LongRoPE shows how to push a RoPE-based model from a typical 4k window all the way to 2,048k (2 million+ tokens) without needing to train on million-token sequences. The trick is a two-stage, practical recipe: a targeted search that finds non-uniform rescale factors for RoPE, a small but focused fine-tuning to 256k, then a second search that yields a 2048k model — with only about 1k fine-tuning steps in total. Here’s the how and the why.

## The two ideas you must keep in mind

1. Positional interpolation is not “one-size-fits-all.” RoPE’s rotary angles have structure across dimensions and across token positions: low-index RoPE dims (high-frequency) and the first tokens are more sensitive. Treating all dimensions/positions uniformly (linear scaling) is suboptimal.

2. Good initialization dramatically reduces the need for long, expensive fine-tuning. If you preserve important dimensions/positions during interpolation, the model sees fewer “catastrophic” positional values and can adapt with far fewer gradient steps.

These two observations drive the LongRoPE recipe.

## Recipe overview (high level)

1. Search a non-uniform rescale schedule for RoPE to extend from 4k toward 128k/256k without changing model weights. This uses an evolutionary search that finds per-dimension scale factors and an optional prefix length of tokens that remain unscaled.

2. Fine-tune the model briefly under a much smaller long-context training length (256k) using the searched RoPE initialization. The paper uses about 400 steps for an intermediate 128k then 600 steps after swapping to 256k (≈1k steps total for LLaMA2).

3. Run a second search (again non-uniform RoPE rescale) on the fine-tuned 256k model to produce RoPE factors that cover 2048k. No further fine-tuning is required after this secondary interpolation.

4. Optionally, run a short recovery search for small context windows (4k/8k) so the model retains performance on the original shorter contexts.

Next I unpack each stage with the key practical details and the reasons they work.

## Stage 1 — Intelligent search: find a non-uniform RoPE initialization

What we’re searching for
- For each rotary dimension index \(i\) (RoPE has \(d/2\) rotation pairs), LongRoPE searches a rescale factor \(\lambda_i\). The effective rotary angle at token position \(n\) becomes
  \[
  \cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\times\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\quad
  \sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\times\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big)
  \]
  where \(\beta\) encodes RoPE’s frequency schedule, and \(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\) leaves the first \(\hat n\) token positions unchanged (value 1) and applies \(1/\lambda_i\) thereafter.

Why per-dimension and prefix-exempt matters
- Lower-index RoPE dims (high frequency) encode fine-grained positional differences; scaling them aggressively compresses useful distinctions. Letting \(\lambda_i\) vary preserves those important dimensions.
- The earliest tokens typically get high attention mass in transformers. Avoiding interpolation for a small prefix \(\hat n\) keeps the model’s high-attention slots stable.

How the search is done (practical points)
- Evolutionary search over the large space of \(\{\lambda_i\}\) plus \(\hat n\). The search is guided by perplexity on a small set of long validation samples.
- Two efficiency tricks reduce compute:
  - Seed the population with sensible baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) then mutate.
  - Enforce monotonicity \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) to respect frequency ordering and prune bad candidates early.
- This search is effective enough that the method can produce up to an 8× extension (e.g., 4k → 32k) without any fine-tuning on weights.

Result of Stage 1
- A RoPE rescaling that preserves critical positional information and yields a much better initialization for subsequent fine-tuning than naive linear scaling.

## Stage 2 — Short, focused fine-tuning to 256k

Why not fine-tune straight to 2,048k?
- Training on million-token sequences is scarce, slow, and expensive. Directly fine-tuning to a huge ratio produces many new positions (catastrophic values) and is hard to converge.

The progressive hack
- Fine-tune in two short phases: first with the RoPE found for 128k (400 steps), then swap to the 256k RoPE and continue fine-tuning (600 steps). Total ≈1k steps in the experiments for LLaMA2.
- Training length during fine-tuning uses manageable chunks (the paper used 128k segments for the 128k phase; 16k sequence training lengths are used in some Mistral setups). This keeps the GPU memory and compute requirements practical.

Why such a small number of steps is enough
- The search already minimized the positional-distribution mismatch by preserving key dimensions/positions. That shrinks the “distance” from the pretrained model to the extended-context regime.
- The progressive approach reduces each fine-tuning jump: 4k → 128k (32×), then 128k → 256k (2×) as opposed to a single huge jump; smaller jumps are easier to adapt to with few steps.
- The model’s weights only need to adapt to new attention patterns and the remaining positional shift; most of the work was handled by the non-uniform RoPE initialization.

Outcome
- A model fine-tuned to reliably handle 256k windows with minimal compute and steps.

## Stage 3 — Second search: leap to 2,048k without more weight updates

What happens after you have a 256k-finetuned model?
- LongRoPE runs the non-uniform RoPE evolutionary search again, now using the fine-tuned 256k model as the evaluation target, but searching for rescale factors that cover 2,048k. Because the model has already adapted to much longer positional indices during fine-tuning, the second search can safely find interpolation/extrapolation that scales the rotary angles for a final 8× non-fine-tuning extension (256k → 2,048k).

Why this works
- The fine-tuned model has already learned to interpret broadened positional encodings and attention spread. The second search is purely an inference-time reparameterization of position angles; it does not need gradient-based retraining to maintain good behavior.
- The non-uniform search is able to preserve crucial dimensions and early-token behavior, so the model avoids the catastrophic “new token positions” problem that plagues naive extensions.

Final result
- A working 2,048k context window model (paper demonstrates this for LLaMA2 and Mistral) without training on million-token sequences and without long fine-tuning runs.

## Recovery: keep short-context performance intact

Problem
- Large extensions can “crowd” the original short-range positions into a narrow region of angle space, degrading performance on tasks designed for small windows.

Fix
- Run a small additional search on the extended model, constrained to smaller maximum \(\lambda\) (so less interpolation). During inference, select the RoPE factors dynamically depending on the current sequence length (e.g., if length ≤ 8k, use the short-length RoPE found by the recovery search).

Effect
- The model recovers much of its original short-context performance while retaining the huge extended window.

## Practical takeaways and constraints

- This is compatible with any RoPE-based transformer; it only changes the positional scaling factors and does not require architectural changes.
- The heavy lifting is the evolutionary search cost (inference-perplexity evaluations), but that is far cheaper than training on million-token sequences.
- The paper’s fine-tuning recipe for LLaMA2 used ~1k steps split as 400 (128k) + 600 (256k). Exact numbers and training lengths can vary by model and dataset; the principle is to keep fine-tuning short and progressive.
- LongRoPE is complementary to other efficiency methods (LoRA-style adapters, attention-window tricks, retrieval-based memories).

## Why this matters

LongRoPE’s progressive playbook addresses three practical bottlenecks for huge contexts: catastrophic new positional values, scarcity of extremely long training sequences, and the compute cost of training. By combining a targeted, non-uniform RoPE search with a short, progressive fine-tuning schedule and a second non-fine-tuning extension step, you can produce models that handle million-token contexts with small fine-tuning budgets and without sacrificing short-context behavior.

If you want to try this yourself, the paper’s code release promises the search scripts, RoPE rescale formats, and example fine-tuning recipes — a pragmatic path from 4k models to multi-million-token contexts.
