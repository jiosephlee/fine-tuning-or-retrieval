\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Why Long Context Windows Matter

# Use cases: in-context learning with many examples, long-document understanding, software repos, multi-agent systems

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated powerful capabilities when given sufficient context. Many practical tasks, however, demand context lengths far beyond the typical few thousand tokens that most pre-trained models were trained on. One important use case is in-context learning with many examples. When you prompt a model with a large number of demonstrations or auxiliary examples, the model benefits from seeing more examples in the prompt, but that requires a much larger context window to place all examples and the query together. Without a long context window, either the number of examples must be limited or examples must be truncated, both of which degrade the benefits of in-context learning.

Long-document understanding is another obvious application. Tasks such as multi-chapter summarization, long-form question answering, document-level reasoning, legal or medical document analysis, and book-scale retrieval require models to attend over hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens to capture document-level structure, repeated themes, and cross-references. For example, reasoning about entities that appear thousands of tokens apart, or extracting a detail buried deep in a long transcript, mandates that the model be able to represent and attend to far earlier positions.

Code and software repositories constitute a further motivation. Repositories combine many files, long dependency graphs, and lengthy contexts such as full projects or history logs. Effective code synthesis, cross-file refactoring, or synthesizing patches based on a whole repository can require more context than conventional windows allow.

Finally, multi-agent systems and agent-based pipelines that use LLMs to act, plan, and keep long-running histories also benefit from longer contexts. Agent frameworks often require persistent state or extensive logs of past messages; when those histories grow, the ability to put a long run of past interactions into the LLM input enables more coherent and consistent behavior.

The LongRoPE paper positions these use cases as important motivations for pushing context windows from the typical 4k/8k scale toward much longer lengths, ultimately demonstrating a method to reach 2,048k (2 million+ tokens). The argument is that enabling such long contexts unlocks direct, single-pass solutions for tasks previously solved by retrieval or architectural changes.

# What degrades beyond the trained window: unseen positions and out-of-distribution issues

When an LLM is exposed to token positions that exceed the positional indices it saw during pretraining, the model encounters two related problems: unseen positional indices and the resulting out-of-distribution (OOD) behavior. Positional encodings—RoPE (rotary position embeddings) in the models examined in the paper—encode token order through rotation angles proportional to the token index. If the model has never seen rotation angles corresponding to very large position indices, those rotations can produce "catastrophic values" in internal computations, causing attention patterns and subsequent representations to behave unpredictably.

Concretely, suppose the model was trained up to length \(L\). A naive extension to \(L' > L\) introduces a large fraction of completely new position indices. In extreme extensions (e.g., 4k to over 1000k), more than 90% of positions are new, so the attention layers and downstream projections are effectively operating out of distribution. This mismatch leads to degraded perplexity and poor generation or retrieval performance. The paper emphasizes that the problem is not merely a matter of scaling; it is that the positional embedding values associated with new indices are simply not aligned with what the Transformer learned to expect.

Existing strategies try to mitigate this by modifying positional embeddings for the extended indices. One approach is linear positional interpolation (PI), which rescales rotation angles to compress the extended indices back into the trained range. Another approach uses NTK-inspired unequal scaling across RoPE dimensions so that some frequencies are compressed more than others. But these approaches still create distributional shifts, and overly aggressive compression ("crowding" positions into a narrower angular range) reduces the model's ability to distinguish nearby tokens. The LongRoPE paper shows that careful, non-uniform handling of both RoPE dimensions and token positions can reduce this OOD effect, providing better initializations for any fine-tuning that follows.

# Compute and data constraints for training on very long sequences

Extending a pre-trained LLM's context window by fine-tuning directly to an extremely long length faces two major practical constraints: data availability and compute cost. First, training requires long contiguous sequences of the target token length so that the model experiences the full range of positions during gradient updates. Text sources that naturally contain documents exceeding hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens are scarce. Even when long texts exist, corpora often must be chunked and bookended appropriately, and the total number of high-quality, very-long examples is limited.

Second, training on very long sequences is computationally expensive. Memory and runtime scale with sequence length in Transformers, even with memory-efficient mechanisms; fine-tuning to lengths like 256k or beyond increases GPU memory needs and substantially raises wall-clock training time. The paper emphasizes that directly fine-tuning to extreme lengths (e.g., 2048k) would be prohibitively expensive in practice, motivating a progressive strategy that keeps fine-tuning cost modest (e.g., about 1k fine-tuning steps within a 256k training length) while using clever positional interpolation searches to bridge to the million-token range.

LongRoPE's design therefore focuses on minimizing required fine-tuning: by searching for rescale factors that preserve essential positional information, it provides an initialization that both enables large non-fine-tuned extensions (up to 8×) and enables efficient progressive fine-tuning to reach much larger windows without directly training on the final extreme length.

# Tension between long-context performance and short-context retention

A core tension when extending context windows is keeping the model effective on both the new very-long contexts and the original short contexts. Positional interpolation strategies that compress or rescale long positions often change the positional encoding distribution for shorter positions too. When a model must represent both short and very long sequences, a single global rescaling can "crowd" the original range: values that used to be well separated across positions become concentrated in a narrower angular region, making it hard for the model to preserve the fine-grained distinctions needed for short-context tasks. This effect can degrade performance on standard benchmarks and conventional tasks that expect the original short context behavior.

The LongRoPE paper tackles this directly. First, it demonstrates that non-uniform rescaling across RoPE dimensions and the option to exempt early token positions from rescaling can preserve critical information used in short contexts. Second, it introduces a recovery step: after progressively extending the model to very large contexts (ultimately using a second interpolation step on a fine-tuned 256k model to reach 2048k), it re-searches rescale factors targeted at short windows (4k and 8k). At inference time, the model dynamically selects the rescale factors appropriate for the current sequence length; for sequences shorter than 8k, the recovered short-length rescale factors are applied. This dynamic adjustment mitigates the degradation that naive global rescaling tends to produce.

# Overview of the LongRoPE contribution: 2048k window, minimal fine-tuning, preserved short-length performance

LongRoPE is presented as a practical recipe that achieves an unprecedented expansion of the context window while balancing the challenges above. The method rests on three core innovations.

First, LongRoPE empirically identifies and exploits two explicit non-uniformities in positional interpolation: variation across RoPE dimensions (different frequency channels encode positional information unevenly) and variation across token positions (initial tokens are more critical and should often be interpolated less). Rather than using hand-designed grouping rules, LongRoPE formulates a search that assigns an independent rescale factor \(\lambda_i\) per RoPE dimension and a threshold \(\hat{n}\) up to which no rescaling is applied. The RoPE at token position \(n\) and dimension \(i\) thus becomes
\[
\text{RoPE}_i(n) = \begin{cases}
\cos\!\big(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big), \ \sin\!\big(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big) & n < \hat{n}\\[4pt]
\cos\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big), \ \sin\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big) & n \ge \hat{n}
\end{cases},
\]
where \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\) as in the RoPE formulation, and \(\lambda_i\) controls how much interpolation (or extrapolation) is applied per dimension.

Second, LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search to find these rescale factors efficiently. The search space is extremely large: for each of the \(d/2\) RoPE dimensions, \(\lambda_i\) is allowed to vary (in experiments the search grid considered values from \(1.0\) to \(s\times 1.25\) with step \(0.01\)), and the threshold \(\hat{n}\) is chosen from a small set. To make search tractable, the algorithm seeds the initial population with existing strategies (PI, NTK, YaRN), restricts candidates to satisfy a monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) (consistent with NTK intuition that lower-frequency dimensions can tolerate larger rescaling), and uses selection, mutation, and crossover to find rescale factors that minimize perplexity on held-out long-document samples.

Third, LongRoPE applies a progressive extension strategy to avoid direct fine-tuning at extreme lengths. The workflow demonstrated in the paper is: search for good rescale factors and extend the pretrained LLM to 128k and 256k; fine-tune the LLM at those intermediate lengths (the authors used a modest number of fine-tuning steps, e.g., 400 then 600 steps for LLaMA2 to reach 256k); then perform a second search on the fine-tuned 256k model to obtain new rescale factors that enable a non-fine-tuned extension up to 2048k. This approach avoids needing to fine-tune at the 2048k length itself and keeps the fine-tuning cost low (on the order of 1k steps within 256k training length).

Finally, LongRoPE includes a short-length recovery: after the progressive extension, it performs additional searches tailored to short windows (4k and 8k) to readjust rescale factors and recover the original short-context performance. During inference, the model chooses rescale factors appropriate to the runtime sequence length (e.g., use the short-length rescale factors for sequences below 8k), thus preserving performance across scales.

Empirically, LongRoPE extends LLaMA2 and Mistral models to a 2,048k context window, while maintaining strong perplexity across lengths and high accuracy on tasks such as passkey retrieval (>=90% up to 2,048k for LLaMA2 in the experiments). The method leaves the model architecture unchanged except for the positional embedding rescaling (so most pre-existing optimizations and deployment practices remain usable). The paper's results show that carefully exploiting non-uniformities in RoPE and using a practical search + progressive fine-tuning pipeline can produce very large context windows with modest additional training and without sacrificing short-context performance.

In summary, LongRoPE addresses the major obstacles to extremely long context windows: it reduces the distribution shift from unseen positions (by preserving important RoPE dimensions and early positions), it avoids the need for massive datasets of extremely long texts (by progressive fine-tuning at intermediate lengths), and it prevents degradation on short-context benchmarks (by readjusting and dynamically selecting rescale factors). The result is a practical path from conventional context windows to multi-million-token windows while maintaining usable performance across the entire range.

Background: Positional Information in Transformers

# Why Transformers need position information

Transformers process input tokens in parallel: the self-attention mechanism treats each token as a set of vector representations and computes interactions between every pair of tokens regardless of their original order. This permutation-invariance is a strength for modeling relationships, but it means that, by default, a Transformer has no built-in notion of token order. Without any positional signal, the model cannot distinguish between the sentence "Alice loves Bob" and "Bob loves Alice" because the same set of token embeddings would be present in both orders.

To recover sequence order, models augment token embeddings with explicit positional information so that the attention layers can condition on not only token identity but also where tokens occur in the sequence. In practice, positional information is critical for tasks that depend on ordering (language modeling, question answering over ordered context, code, etc.). The paper frames this need in the context of extending the context window: when we increase the range of positions the model must handle, the model must also extend or adapt its positional representation to cover those new positions in a way that preserves the distinctions learned during pretraining.

# Absolute vs relative vs rotary encodings

There are several families of positional encodings; the paper focuses on RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) and compares it implicitly to other approaches through discussion of positional interpolation and NTK-based views.

- Absolute position encodings assign each position an independent vector (or deterministic function of position) that is added to or concatenated with token embeddings. An absolute encoding directly indexes a vector for each position in the pretraining range. When the model sees a position outside that range, those positions are out-of-distribution for the encoder.

- Relative position encodings express positions in terms of pairwise relations (e.g., the distance between two tokens) rather than the absolute index. This can generalize better to unseen absolute indices because the model learns how the relative displacement affects attention patterns.

- Rotary position embeddings (RoPE), the focus of this chapter and the paper, encode position information by rotating parts of the token representations (specifically, query and key vectors) by position-dependent angles. Rather than adding a vector per position, RoPE modifies the algebra of dot-product attention so that the positional effect is integrated through rotations. This has the practical benefit that the same transform can be applied at arbitrary positions and, critically for the paper, can be rescaled/interpolated in principled ways to extend the context window.

The paper emphasizes RoPE because it is widely used by recent LLMs and because its structure admits controlled interpolation and extrapolation of positional angles—operations that underpin the LongRoPE method for extending the context window.

# RoPE mechanics: rotating query/key pairs and embedding dimensions as frequency bands

RoPE represents positional information by applying a rotation to components of query and key vectors. The core intuition is that each pair of query and key components is treated as a two-dimensional plane; applying a position-dependent rotation to those planes causes inner products between rotated queries and keys to change in a way that depends on token positions. This integrates positional dependence directly into attention scores.

The paper gives a compact formulation for the RoPE encoding of a token at position index $n$. If the rotary encoding spans $d$ dimensions (so $d/2$ rotation planes), the positional encoding can be written as the concatenation of cosine and sine of angle multiples:

\[
\left[ \cos(n \theta_0), \sin(n \theta_0), \cos(n \theta_1), \ldots, \cos(n \theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n \theta_{d/2-1}) \right],
\]

where each rotary angle for dimension (plane) $i$ is $n\theta_i$. The frequencies $\theta_i$ are chosen so that different dimensions correspond to different rotation frequencies. In RoPE the frequency sequence is typically geometric: the paper adopts the common parametrization

\[
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\]

with a base $\theta$ (often 10000 in common practice). Thus the $d/2$ rotation planes cover a range of angular frequencies: lower index planes rotate faster with position (higher frequency), while higher index planes rotate more slowly (lower frequency). The effect is analogous to representing positional information as a set of frequency bands: each pair of dimensions behaves like a sinusoid of a particular frequency, and together they can represent fine-grained (high-frequency dimensions) and coarse-grained (low-frequency dimensions) positional structure.

When queries and keys are rotated by these angle patterns, their dot products encode a positional modulation: attention between two tokens depends on both content and the rotation-induced phase difference determined by their positions. Because the rotations are multiplicative operations applied to continuous vectors, RoPE naturally supports compositional usage in attention and can be applied at inference to arbitrary positions provided the angular pattern is defined.

# RoPE parameters and base frequency (e.g., $\theta=10000$) and dimensional frequency scaling

Two parameters figure prominently in the RoPE design discussed in the paper: the base frequency $\theta$ (the default base is 10000) and the per-dimension scaling that yields $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$.

The base $\theta$ sets the overall envelope of rotation frequencies across dimensions. Larger base values spread frequencies across a wider numeric range; conversely, a smaller base compresses frequencies. The geometric scaling $\theta^{-2i/d}$ ensures that the sequence of angles spans multiple scales: dimensions with small $i$ (low index) correspond to relatively high angular rates with respect to $n$ (i.e., these dimensions produce rapidly changing phases across positions), while dimensions with large $i$ correspond to slowly varying phases. The paper abstracts part of this parametrization by defining a convenient quantity $\beta$:

\[
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\]

so that a rotary angle can be written as $\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}$ (the paper uses the equivalent representation $\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}$ to denote rotation angles in their interpolation equations). This representation makes explicit that each RoPE dimension $i$ effectively scales the position index $n$ by a frequency-dependent factor $\beta^{i}$.

Because different dimensions represent different effective frequencies, operations that rescale position indices or rotation angles can have unequal effects on different dimensions. This observation is central to the paper's concept of non-uniform positional interpolation: lower-index (higher-frequency) dimensions are more sensitive to changes in position scaling and thus often require less rescaling when extending the context window, whereas higher-index (lower-frequency) dimensions can tolerate stronger rescaling.

# Effects of scaling positions and frequencies on attention geometry

When extending the context window beyond positions seen during pretraining, a straightforward approach would be to directly apply the RoPE formula with larger $n$ values. In practice this can cause problems: the model's parameters were learned with rotation angles corresponding to a limited range of $n$, and feeding much larger angles can lead to out-of-distribution behavior and "catastrophic values" in the model's activations.

To avoid that, the paper (following prior work) considers rescaling of rotation angles so that the new, larger positions are mapped back into the angular range that the model saw during pretraining. If we plan to extend the maximum position by a factor $s$ (so that $L' = s L$), a simple linear positional interpolation (PI) rescales angles by a fixed factor $\lambda = s$ across all RoPE dimensions:

\[
\text{use angles } \quad \frac{n}{\lambda} = \frac{n}{s}.
\]

This linearly reduces the rotation angles attached to new positions so that they fall back into the pretrained envelope. However, such uniform downscaling tends to "crowd" many distinct new positions into a small angular range; as the paper explains, that makes it harder for the model to distinguish nearby tokens because their rotation-induced phase differences become smaller. In other words, uniformly scaling positions compresses the angular geometry of positions and can degrade attention discrimination.

Recognizing the heterogeneity across RoPE dimensions, NTK-based reasoning and related works advocate non-uniform rescaling: spread the interpolation pressure across dimensions so that high-frequency dimensions are scaled less and low-frequency dimensions more. Concretely, this can be viewed as applying a dimension-dependent rescaling $\lambda_i$ to dimension $i$, producing rotation angles of the form

\[
\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^{i}} \quad\text{(or equivalently)}\quad \frac{n}{\beta^i} \cdot \frac{1}{\lambda_i}.
\]

Because lower-index (high-frequency) dimensions represent fine positional distinctions, preserving their relative angular magnitudes (i.e., using smaller $\lambda_i$) helps the model keep local ordering information. Higher-index (low-frequency) dimensions can undergo more aggressive rescaling without losing as much discriminative power because they encode coarser position structure. This distribution of rescaling avoids crowding all dimensions equally and preserves the multi-scale geometry of positional encodings.

The paper makes two additional geometry-related observations tied to token positions and practical behavior of attention:

1. Initial tokens matter more and should be interpolated less. Empirically, the paper observed that keeping the early token positions (the first $\hat{n}$ tokens) closer to their original RoPE angles (i.e., no or less rescaling) improves performance. The intuition is that initial tokens often receive large attention weights and thus play a disproportionate role in model computations; preserving their positional representation reduces disturbance to important attention patterns. This introduces a token-position threshold $\hat{n}$ so that for $n<\hat{n}$ the original angle is used, and for $n\ge\hat{n}$ the rescaled angle is applied.

2. The optimal amount of rescaling depends on the target extension ratio and varies across both dimensions and positions. That is, there is a multi-dimensional, non-uniform structure to the best rescaling strategy: different RoPE dimensions (frequency bands) and different token positions benefit from different degrees of interpolation or extrapolation. The paper frames finding those per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and the initial cutoff $\hat{n}$ as a search problem and shows that exploiting this non-uniformity dramatically improves the model's ability to extend context windows, reduce perplexity, and retain short-context performance.

In summary, scaling positions or frequencies changes the angular geometry that RoPE imposes on token representations. Uniform scaling compresses the geometry and can crowd distinctions; dimension-aware and position-aware (non-uniform) scaling can preserve important multi-scale relationships, enabling larger context extensions with less loss in attention discriminability. The LongRoPE method builds directly on this understanding: it searches for per-dimension rescale factors and a position cutoff to produce RoPE angles that (a) keep critical dimensions and initial positions near their pretrained angles and (b) rescale other components enough to map new positions into the model's learned angular regime.

The Context-Extension Problem

# Definition of extension ratio $s = L'/L$

Extending the context window of a large language model (LLM) means enabling the model to accept and effectively condition on input sequences that are longer than what it was trained on. We denote the original trained context length by $L$ and the desired extended context length by $L'$. The extension ratio is the scalar
\[
s=\frac{L'}{L},
\]
which quantifies how many times longer the new window is relative to the original. This single scalar captures the multiplicative challenge: when $s=2$ we double the window; when $s=128$ we expand it by two orders of magnitude; in LongRoPE the ultimate goal is to reach $s=512$ when going from a typical $L=4\text{k}$ baseline to $L'=2048\text{k}$.

Expressing extension in terms of $s$ is useful because many of the positional-encoding transformations applied to rotary position embeddings (RoPE) are defined relative to $s$. For instance, simple linear positional interpolation (PI) rescales rotary angles by $\lambda=s$ across all RoPE dimensions. Other methods modulate interpolation differently across dimensions, but the target extension ratio $s$ remains the central parameter that determines how much change in positional encoding is needed.

# Catastrophic values from unseen position indices

A fundamental obstacle to extending context windows is that the model has never been trained on the new position indices beyond $L$. When you present token positions $n\ge L$, their positional encodings (and the resulting interaction patterns in attention) are out-of-distribution. This mismatch can introduce many catastrophic values—large, unanticipated activations or attention patterns—that degrade generation and likelihood performance.

In RoPE, a token at position $n$ uses rotary angles of the form $n\theta_i$, where $\theta_i$ are frequency parameters (the paper writes these angles compactly as factors such as $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$). Any abrupt change to those angles for positions $n\ge L$ (or simply the presence of previously unseen $n$) can produce representations that the rest of the Transformer layers are unprepared to process. The model's internal statistics and learned weights assume a particular geometry and spacing induced by the positional embedding up to $L$, so new positions can cause the model to attend inappropriately (for example, assigning unexpectedly high attention to certain remote tokens) or to misinterpret relative positions, which manifests as large increases in perplexity and failure modes in generation.

A common attempt to mitigate this is positional interpolation: downscaling new position indices so their rotary angles fall inside the pretrained range. Formally, many methods replace angles $n\theta_i$ with $n\theta_i/\lambda_i$ for some rescale factors $\lambda_i$. But if the rescaling is too aggressive or too uniform, important information encoded in particular RoPE dimensions or in early token positions can be lost, still producing catastrophic effects. Thus controlling the manner and degree of rescaling is critical to avoid these catastrophic values.

# Scarcity and cost of extremely long training sequences

A second major obstacle is the scarcity of training data and the computational cost associated with fine-tuning on extremely long sequences. Fine-tuning an LLM to handle positions up to $L'$ ideally requires abundant training examples with lengths comparable to $L'$, so the model can adapt its parameters to the new positional regime. However, documents that naturally exceed very large lengths (e.g., hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens) are rare in existing corpora. Moreover, processing such long sequences is expensive: each training step requires substantially more memory and compute, and naive training to convergence across millions of tokens quickly becomes infeasible.

The paper emphasizes a pragmatic consequence: direct fine-tuning to very large $L'$ (for example, 2048k) is prohibitively expensive in terms of GPU resources and training time. LongRoPE addresses this by using targeted fine-tuning at much smaller large lengths (e.g., 128k or 256k) and combining that with an informed positional-interpolation search strategy so that a later non-fine-tuning interpolation can reach $L'=2048\text{k}$. In other words, the approach recognizes that exhaustive fine-tuning at the target length is impractical and therefore uses a progressive strategy to minimize the expensive training burden while still achieving very large $s$ overall.

# Attention dispersion and short-context degradation at extreme scales

Expanding the context window by a large factor changes the distribution of attention weights the Transformer computes. When the attention mechanism must consider many more token positions, the same attention mass is spread over a much larger set of keys and queries. This dispersion can reduce the effective influence of nearby or important tokens, because the attention energy is diluted across many positions. Practically, that manifests as degraded performance even on tasks that fall within the original short context length: the model can lose precision or discriminability for short-range dependencies it previously handled well.

Positional interpolation techniques can exacerbate this problem for another reason. If the rescaling maps many new positions into a narrow rotary-angle region, then relative positional distinctions within the original window become compressed. In RoPE, different dimensions encode position at different frequencies; heavy interpolation that uniformly rescales all dimensions forces many original positions to lie in a small angular range in multiple dimensions, making nearby tokens harder to distinguish. Empirically, this harms perplexity and downstream task accuracy within the original window.

LongRoPE therefore identifies this as a critical failure mode: large extension ratios lead to both attention dispersion across many positions and crowding of positional encodings, which together degrade short-context performance. To address it the paper introduces two remedies: (1) non-uniform rescaling across RoPE dimensions (so that crucial high-frequency dimensions are preserved and not over-interpolated), and (2) recovery adjustments at short lengths after completing large extensions (searching for rescale factors that better preserve original short-window behavior). The latter allows the extended model to select different rescale factors during inference depending on the requested sequence length, mitigating short-context degradation.

# Success criteria: perplexity stability, retrieval accuracy, and short-context parity

When evaluating whether an extended-context method succeeds, the paper proposes three concrete success criteria that capture both long-range and original-window performance:

1. Perplexity stability across lengths. A primary criterion is how well the model preserves language-modeling performance (lower perplexity) as evaluation length grows. A successful extension shows low and ideally non-increasing perplexity as the evaluation window increases from $L$ up to $L'$. For example, LongRoPE demonstrates decreasing or stable perplexity trends from 4k up through 256k and maintains reasonable perplexity up to 2048k on large-document datasets. Perplexity computed with sliding windows (e.g., step sizes used in experiments) provides a quantitative measure of whether the model can use long context effectively.

2. Retrieval accuracy in long-context generation tasks. A complementary benchmark is the passkey retrieval task: hiding a short secret (a five-digit passkey) somewhere in a long document and asking the model to retrieve it. Retrieval accuracy as a function of the location and total length tests whether the model can find and attend to a target token far away amid irrelevant context. Existing baselines drop to near-zero accuracy beyond modest lengths; a successful method retains high retrieval accuracy (LongRoPE keeps ≥90% for LLaMA2 from 4k to 2048k in the reported experiments).

3. Short-context parity with the original model. Finally, success requires the extended model not to lose the abilities it had at the original context length. That is, performance on standard zero-shot and few-shot benchmarks designed for the original short context (e.g., 4k) should remain comparable. LongRoPE addresses this by an additional search step that re-tunes rescale factors for short lengths (e.g., 4k and 8k) after a large extension; during inference it dynamically applies the appropriate factors for the current sequence length. The aim is to achieve parity (or only minor degradation) on tasks such as MMLU, HellaSwag, and other benchmarks sampled in the paper.

Taken together, these three metrics balance long-range utility (perplexity and retrieval) with preservation of the model's existing competencies. A method that only lowers perplexity at huge scales but severely degrades short-context performance would be unsatisfactory; conversely, one that preserves short-context metrics but fails to utilize long context is not useful for long-context applications. LongRoPE uses non-uniform positional interpolation discovered by an efficient evolutionary search, progressive fine-tuning at feasible lengths, and a short-length recovery step to meet these success criteria while keeping training cost manageable.

# Concluding perspective

The context-extension problem is thus a multi-faceted engineering and statistical challenge. The extension ratio $s$ quantifies the scale of the change, but the practical difficulties include catastrophic values from unseen positions, lack of abundant ultra-long training sequences and the cost they impose, and attention and positional crowding that can erase original short-context capabilities. Effective solutions must therefore address both the encoding of new positions (without destroying critical existing structure) and the realities of limited data and compute. The evaluation of any proposed method should measure perplexity across window sizes, the ability to retrieve sparse signals from long contexts, and preservation of short-context performance; meeting all three simultaneously is the core objective that LongRoPE targets.

Prior Approaches to RoPE Interpolation

# Position Interpolation (PI)

Position Interpolation (PI) is the simplest and most widely used strategy for extending a model's context window by manipulating the RoPE (rotary positional encoding) angles. The basic idea is to downscale the rotation angles for every position uniformly so that positions beyond the pretrained maximum are mapped into the angular range the model has seen during pretraining. If the original context length is $L$ and the desired extended length is $L'$, the extension ratio is $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. PI accomplishes the extension by dividing the effective position index by the same scalar $\lambda = s$ across all RoPE dimensions and for all token positions, producing rotary angles of the form
\[
\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda}\right),\ \cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda}\right),\ \ldots\right]
\]
(as written in the unified RoPE formulation in the paper, PI sets a uniform $\lambda$ for every dimension).

The intuition behind PI is straightforward: by compressing the positional indices, new token positions are represented using angles within the same range as those seen during pretraining, thereby avoiding the catastrophic out-of-distribution rotation angles that would otherwise appear if you simply allowed positions to grow unbounded. This makes PI attractive because it is simple to implement and preserves the original model weights unchanged so long as we only change the positional mapping.

However, PI has an important and practically limiting drawback known as "crowding." Because all new positions are uniformly mapped into a compressed angular window, many distinct token indices end up having very similar (or nearly identical) rotary angles. That makes it harder for the model to distinguish nearby positions, especially at large extension ratios. The model's ability to discriminate relative order and local positional differences degrades as more tokens are squeezed into the same angular range. In practice, PI tends to work for modest extensions but degrades quickly at high extension ratios; under high $s$ the model's perplexity often spikes unless expensive fine-tuning is performed.

# NTK-based strategies

NTK-based strategies take a different perspective: rather than treating all dimensions of RoPE equally, these methods exploit the fact that the different dimensions correspond to different rotation frequencies. Lower-indexed RoPE dimensions (high frequency components) and higher-indexed dimensions (low frequency components) do not contribute identically to positional information. NTK-based methods aim to distribute the interpolation pressure across RoPE dimensions so that the important high-frequency dimensions are changed less and the low-frequency ones can be adjusted more.

Concretely, the NTK viewpoint motivates letting the rescale factor vary across dimensions. Instead of a single $\lambda$ for all dimensions, one might use $\lambda_i$ that depends on dimension index $i$, following an NTK-inspired schedule such as $\lambda_i = s^{\alpha(i)}$ for some monotonically increasing function $\alpha(i)$. This yields rotary angles
\[
\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_0}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_0}\right),\ \cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_1}\right),\ \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_1}\right),\ \ldots\right].
\]
Lower (high-frequency) dimensions therefore receive relatively less interpolation (smaller $\lambda_i$), preserving the fine-grained positional cues, while higher (low-frequency) dimensions are interpolated more to accommodate larger absolute positions.

The benefit of NTK-style non-uniform scaling is twofold. First, by protecting high-frequency dimensions from heavy compression, it helps the model maintain its ability to distinguish close-by tokens; this mitigates the crowding issue relative to PI. Second, because the rescaling is dimension-wise and informed by an information-theoretic intuition (via NTK), such strategies can often be applied without any fine-tuning and still yield reasonable behavior for modest extension ratios.

Dynamic NTK or other NTK-derived refinements further adapt the rescaling based on the current sequence length, attempting to match the theoretical encoding properties more closely. Empirically, NTK-based schemes extend the usable context window more than PI in non-fine-tuning scenarios, but their practical non-fine-tuned extension ratio is still limited: the paper reports that NTK-aware methods typically achieve up to about 4× extension without fine-tuning before perplexity becomes problematic.

# YaRN: three frequency groups with mixed strategies

YaRN builds on the NTK intuition but simplifies and partially automates the non-uniform treatment of RoPE by partitioning RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups and applying different interpolation strategies to each group. The three-group design is:

- High-frequency group: for the highest-frequency dimensions, YaRN applies extrapolation (effectively $\lambda = 1$), meaning no interpolation is performed at all on those dimensions. The rationale is that these dimensions encode fine-grained relative positional differences and should remain untouched to preserve discriminability of nearby tokens.

- Mid-frequency group: for intermediate frequencies, YaRN applies an NTK-like strategy, using dimension-dependent scaling that lies between identity and full interpolation. This is intended to balance the need to preserve some relative positional signal while also allowing absolute positions to extend.

- Low-frequency group: for the lowest-frequency dimensions, YaRN applies linear interpolation (similar to PI) and allows larger rescaling, because these dimensions encode very low-frequency, coarse-grained positional structure that can be compressed more without catastrophic loss of detailed ordering information.

This three-way partition gives a hybrid strategy: it is more selective than PI, and simpler and more directive than a fully continuous per-dimension NTK rescaling. The key advantage of YaRN is that it improves over purely uniform PI by giving protective priority to the most informative dimensions while permitting more aggressive compression where it is cheaper to do so.

However, there are trade-offs. YaRN's dimension grouping and the precise assignment of strategies are empirically determined and depend on human choices. That design choice makes YaRN effective on the models and settings for which those heuristics were tuned, but it may be suboptimal or brittle when applied to different LLM architectures or when the target extension ratio changes substantially. The paper notes that YaRN can underperform PI and NTK in some cases (for example, on PG19 it underperformed PI and NTK when used without fine-tuning for certain context lengths), and in particular it can fail to reach target context lengths reliably in non-finetuning regimes because its extrapolation choices cause angle behavior that degrades perplexity at specific lengths.

# Limits observed in practice

Across the approaches described above, the paper highlights practical limits encountered when extending context via RoPE manipulation.

First, non-fine-tuned extension typically stalls at small ratios. PI and NTK can work up to modest multiples of the original length, but the paper finds that non-fine-tuned NTK-style interpolation generally tops out around 2–4× in practice before perplexity rapidly degrades. The reason is that beyond these ratios the combined effects of angle compression and remaining unmodified dimensions cause the model to face inputs that differ too much from pretraining.

Second, fine-tuning can push the usable context much further, but at substantial cost. Recent works that fine-tune on longer texts achieve extended context windows on the order of 128k tokens, but doing so requires long sequences during training, adequate data of those lengths, and large computational resources. The paper identifies three concrete obstacles for scaling fine-tuned extensions much beyond 128k: (i) catastrophic values for new position indices cause out-of-distribution behavior that makes convergence difficult; (ii) long texts of lengths beyond 1000k are scarce, and training on artificially long sequences is expensive; (iii) when extending to extremely long windows, attention becomes distributed over many positions, which can degrade performance on the original, shorter contexts.

In other words, PI and NTK-based methods are simple and can help without fine-tuning, but only to a limited multiplier. Fine-tuning enables much larger windows but is expensive and runs into diminishing returns and instability as the fraction of totally new positions grows large.

# Why rule-based non-uniformity can be suboptimal

Rule-based non-uniform approaches like NTK-inspired formulas or YaRN's three-group heuristics are powerful because they recognize that RoPE components carry different information and should not be treated identically. Nevertheless, these rule-based strategies have intrinsic limitations that the paper emphasizes.

The central issue is that positional information in RoPE exhibits subtle, multidimensional, and dataset- or-model-specific non-uniformities. Two distinct forms of non-uniformity matter: variation across RoPE dimensions (different rotation frequencies carry different amounts and types of positional information) and variation across token positions (early tokens in a sequence often have outsized influence through attention and can tolerate less interpolation). Rule-based methods typically capture only one coarse aspect of this complexity: NTK focuses on dimension-dependent scaling derived from theory, and YaRN imposes three fixed frequency bands with pre-chosen strategies. Neither method searches jointly for a fine-grained per-dimension rescaling combined with position-threshold behavior that says, for example, "the first $\hat{n}$ tokens should be left nearly unchanged while later tokens are compressed in dimension-specific ways."

Because these non-uniformities interact in complicated ways and because optimal choices depend on the target extension ratio and on architecture-specific properties, hand-crafted grouping or analytic formulas can miss better solutions. The paper's empirical findings show that there exist non-uniform per-dimension rescale factors (and also position-dependent thresholds) that substantially reduce perplexity compared to PI, NTK, and YaRN, particularly in the non-fine-tuned regime where preserving original RoPE information is crucial. In other words, human-designed, coarse-grained rules fail to exploit the full structure of RoPE, resulting in unnecessary information loss and limiting the achievable extension ratio without fine-tuning.

The implication is clear: to extend context windows farther and with less fine-tuning, we need methods that can discover more detailed, joint patterns of non-uniformity across both RoPE dimensions and token positions. Rule-based methods provide a useful starting point but can be suboptimal because they do not adaptively preserve the most critical dimensions and early positions for the particular model and target length. The LongRoPE approach in the paper addresses this by framing the rescale selection as an optimization/search problem that jointly considers these two non-uniformities, yielding substantially better initializations for later fine-tuning and enabling larger non-fine-tuned extensions.

Empirical Findings: Non-Uniformities That Matter

# Finding 1: RoPE dimensions are not equal—optimal rescale factors differ by dimension

One of the central empirical discoveries in this work is that the dimensions of RoPE (rotary positional embeddings) do not contribute identically when extending a model's context window. RoPE represents a token at position $n$ by trigonometric components built from rotation angles $n\theta_i$, where typically $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ and different $i$ correspond to different frequencies. Prior interpolation schemes treated the dimensions uniformly (linear positional interpolation, PI) or grouped them coarsely (YaRN) or followed theory-driven formulas (NTK). The experiments reported in the paper, however, show that those approaches miss substantial performance improvements that are achievable when each RoPE dimension receives its own tailored rescale factor.

Concretely, the authors posed interpolation as replacing the original rotation angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ by $\frac{n}{\beta^i\lambda_i}$ (or equivalently multiplying the angle by $1/\lambda_i$) and used an evolutionary search to find a per-dimension set of rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$. Evaluated without any fine-tuning, the searched per-dimension solution yields significantly lower perplexity than PI, NTK-style formulas, and YaRN on datasets such as PG19 and Proof-pile (see paper Table comparisons). The improved perplexity demonstrates that preserving information in some RoPE dimensions while more aggressively rescaling others better maintains the positional information the model expects.

Intuitively, lower-indexed RoPE dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components and encode fine-grained relative position differences that are important for distinguishing close token positions. If those dimensions are down-scaled too much (i.e., large $\lambda_i$), the angle changes per-token shrink and adjacent positions become harder to distinguish. Conversely, higher-index (low-frequency) dimensions can tolerate or even benefit from stronger rescaling because they encode coarser long-range positional structure. The search finds per-dimension balances that reduce the information loss induced by interpolation, which directly reduces the model's perplexity when evaluated at longer contexts.

# Finding 2: Early token positions (the first \hat{n} tokens) benefit from less or no interpolation

The second empirical observation concerns non-uniformity across token positions rather than across RoPE dimensions. The paper shows that the initial segment of the input — the first $\hat{n}$ tokens — tends to be more critical for model performance and should receive less or no interpolation compared to later tokens. Formally, the authors introduce a token-threshold parameter $\hat{n}$ and use a piecewise rescaling: for positions $n < \hat{n}$ the original RoPE angles are preserved (scale factor effectively 1), and for $n \ge \hat{n}$ the searched rescale factors apply.

This choice is motivated both empirically and by attention dynamics: early tokens in a sequence commonly receive relatively large attention weights and therefore exert strong influence in attention layers. The experiments demonstrate that leaving a small prefix of tokens un-interpolated improves perplexity at extended context lengths. In practice the authors evaluated $\hat{n}$ values in a set like $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$ and observed that non-zero $\hat{n}$ often outperformed $\hat{n}=0$ (the uniform case) for medium extensions such as to 8k and 16k.

Importantly, the optimal value of $\hat{n}$ is not universal: it depends on the target extension length. The paper reports that as the target evaluation length grows, the best choice of $\hat{n}$ changes. For smaller extensions (e.g., up to 8k), preserving a modest number of initial tokens is helpful; for some larger extensions the benefit diminishes or the optimal $\hat{n}$ shifts. This dependence implies that token-position non-uniformity must be treated as a tunable component of an interpolation strategy, not as a fixed design choice.

# Finding 3: Incorporating both non-uniformities boosts both non-finetuned and finetuned performance

The third major empirical finding is that combining non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions with non-uniformity across token positions produces complementary gains. When the authors searched jointly for per-dimension rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ together with a threshold $\hat{n}$ (so that the first $\hat{n}$ tokens keep original RoPE), the resulting interpolation outperformed methods that considered only one type of non-uniformity or none at all. This holds in both non-fine-tuning settings (i.e., apply interpolation at inference without any additional training) and in fine-tuning scenarios where the model is subsequently trained on longer sequences.

In non-fine-tuning experiments the joint non-uniform solution yielded an impressive ability to extend the context window up to 8$\times$ (from 4k to 32k) while keeping perplexity low, a regime where PI, NTK, and YaRN typically fail and show large perplexity spikes. The joint approach preserves critical fine-grained positional information where it matters (specific dimensions and early token positions) and distributes interpolation where the model tolerates it, enabling practical non-finetuned extension beyond prior limits.

When fine-tuning is feasible (necessary for much larger extension ratios), the joint non-uniform initialization provides a much better starting point. Fine-tuning from that initialization (for example fine-tuning to 64k or to 256k in the progressive strategy) converges faster and reaches lower perplexity than initializing from uniform PI or other baselines. The experiments in the paper (see finetune comparison tables) show that models fine-tuned after non-uniform search significantly outperform those fine-tuned after PI or YaRN, both before and after the fine-tuning steps.

The empirical takeaway is that non-uniform interpolation reduces the information loss caused by mapping many unseen positions into the pretrained positional space. As a result, it both enables larger no-finetune extension ratios and improves fine-tuning effectiveness when larger extensions require gradient updates.

# Evidence: perplexity gains over PI/NTK/YaRN at 8k–16k without fine-tuning

The paper presents direct quantitative evidence that the searched non-uniform interpolation substantially reduces perplexity compared to PI, NTK, and YaRN in the 8k–16k range without any fine-tuning. Using LLaMA2-7B as an example and evaluating on validation splits of PG19 and Proof-pile, the evolutionary search discovered rescale factors that yield markedly lower perplexity at 8k and 16k evaluation lengths. The reported results show that PI and NTK cause the model's perplexity to rise quickly as the extension ratio increases; YaRN sometimes underperforms PI and NTK for certain datasets because its hand-designed grouping does not match the optimal non-uniform pattern for that model and dataset.

Beyond single-number summaries, the paper reports the qualitative behavior: PI tends to crowd positions and thereby impairs the model's ability to discriminate adjacent tokens, which manifests as large perplexities for modest extension ratios; NTK helps by redistributing scaling across dimensions, but its formulaic scaling is still suboptimal compared to the searched per-dimension profile; YaRN's discrete grouping improves over uniform PI in some regimes but can suffer abrupt failure near group boundary lengths. In contrast, the searched per-dimension plus prefix-preserving ($\hat{n}>0$) solutions avoid large perplexity spikes up to 8$\times$ extensions, demonstrating the practical advantage of exploiting both identified non-uniformities.

# Dependence of \hat{n} on target extension length

A careful empirical observation in the paper is that the optimal number of preserved initial tokens, $\hat{n}$, varies with the target extension ratio and evaluation length. The authors evaluated multiple $\hat{n}$ candidates when extending to 8k and 16k and found two consistent patterns. First, keeping some initial tokens un-interpolated (i.e., $\hat{n}>0$) improves performance relative to full interpolation ($\hat{n}=0$). Second, the value of $\hat{n}$ that yields the best perplexity depends on the extension target: a value that is best for 8k may not be best for 16k.

This dependence likely arises because attention distribution and the relative importance of the prefix change as the absolute sequence length changes. At moderate extension ratios, preserving a small prefix protects the tokens that attract the most attention and carry dense local information; at very large extension ratios, the attention landscape and positional scaling interactions change, and preserving a larger or smaller prefix may be appropriate. Methodologically, the paper addresses this by including $\hat{n}$ in the evolutionary search rather than fixing it, and by allowing the set of candidate $\hat{n}$ values to cover small to modest prefix sizes (for example up to 256 tokens). The empirical results indicate that this search over $\hat{n}$ is necessary to reach the best possible interpolation for a given target length.

Overall, the chapter's empirical findings emphasize that positional interpolation is not a one-dimensional problem. Both per-dimension frequency structure and position-specific importance matter, and explicitly searching for non-uniform rescaling patterns that respect these two axes leads to substantially better initialization for fine-tuning and significantly improved performance without fine-tuning up to an 8$\times$ extension. The dependence of the optimal prefix size $\hat{n}$ on the target length further underscores the need to treat position interpolation as a tunable, data- and target-aware procedure rather than a fixed formula.

LongRoPE: Problem Formulation

# Decision variables: $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension and $\hat{n}$ (early-position cutoff)

LongRoPE treats positional interpolation as a search problem in which the goal is to find per-dimension rescale factors and an early-position threshold that together define how RoPE (rotary position embedding) is adjusted for extended context windows. Concretely, the decision variables are:

- A set of rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$, one scalar $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE frequency dimension (the paper writes the rotation angle for the $i$-th pair of cosine/sine as proportional to $n/\beta^i$; the rescale factor modifies that frequency). Each $\lambda_i$ determines how strongly the positions beyond the initial region are compressed or left unchanged for that particular RoPE dimension.

- An early-position cutoff $\hat{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ that divides token indices into two zones: "initial" tokens $n<\hat{n}$ and "extended" tokens $n\ge\hat{n}$. The cutoff controls which token positions keep the original pretrained RoPE angles and which positions receive the rescaled angles.

These two types of variables allow LongRoPE to express non-uniformity both across RoPE dimensions (different interpolation per frequency) and across token positions (preserving early tokens differently from later ones). The combination produces a rich family of rescaling policies that can be searched to minimize next-token loss at the target extended length.

# Rescaling policy $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})$: identity for $n<\hat{n}$, scaled for $n\ge\hat{n}$

LongRoPE encodes the two non-uniformities via a simple piecewise rescaling policy for each RoPE dimension. Let the original rotary angle for token position $n$ and dimension index $i$ be $n/\beta^i$, where $\beta$ is the base step between frequencies (the paper uses $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$). The rescaled rotary angle is obtained by multiplying this original angle by a scalar factor that depends on both $i$ and $n$. The policy is:

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus, the RoPE vector at position $n$ becomes
\[
\left[ \ldots, \cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \times \tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \times \tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big), \ldots \right],
\]
for all RoPE dimensions $i$. Equivalently one can express the rescale factor applied to rotation angles for $n\ge\hat{n}$ as dividing the original angle by $\lambda_i$, so $\lambda_i=1$ corresponds to no interpolation (pure extrapolation), and $\lambda_i>1$ corresponds to compressing angles (interpolating positions into the pretrained angle range).

This piecewise rule explicitly implements the position-wise non-uniformity (through $\hat{n}$) and dimension-wise non-uniformity (through $\lambda_i$).

# Objective: minimize perplexity on long documents at target $L'$

The search objective in LongRoPE is to find the rescale factors and cutoff that minimize next-token prediction loss (negative log-likelihood, or equivalently perplexity) when the model is presented with sequences of the target extended length $L'$. Formally, given a collection of long documents $\mathbf{X}$ (each $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}$ with $|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'$), LongRoPE searches for the rescaling defined by $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ that minimizes the language modeling loss:

\[
\underset{\{\lambda_i\},\,\hat{n}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\;\; \mathcal{L}\big(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n}),\, \mathbf{X})\big),
\]

where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes next-token loss (perplexity) computed on the model using the modified RoPE for input samples of length $L'$. Expanding the modified RoPE according to the policy,

\[
\text{RoPE}_n = \big[\ldots,\; \cos(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}),\; \sin(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}),\ldots\big] \quad\text{for } n=0,\dots,|\mathbf{x}|-1.
\]

Because the objective is directly the model's next token loss on very long inputs, the search favors rescalings that preserve the model's ability to model local and global dependencies at the new context length.

# Why dimension-wise + position-wise non-uniformity reduces information loss

Intuitively, RoPE encodes position by rotating query and key vectors in different frequency components. The information contributed by different RoPE dimensions is non-uniform: lower-index dimensions correspond to higher-frequency rotations (capturing fine-grained, short-range positional distinctions), while higher-index dimensions correspond to lower-frequency rotations (capturing coarse, long-range structure). Likewise, token positions are not equally important: initial tokens often receive larger attention weights and play outsized roles in downstream computations.

Applying a single uniform scalar (as in linear positional interpolation, PI) to every RoPE dimension and every token position compresses all rotation angles equally. When the extension ratio $s=L'/L$ is large, this compresses high-frequency dimensions too much and “crowds” many distinct token positions into very similar rotation phases. The result is that the model loses its ability to distinguish nearby token positions and the pretrained positional signals are corrupted, causing perplexity to rise.

A dimension-wise rescaling $\{\lambda_i\}$ lets the method treat high-frequency and low-frequency dimensions differently: keep smaller $\lambda_i$ (less interpolation) for the low-index, high-frequency dimensions so that fine-grained local ordering signals remain distinct; allow larger $\lambda_i$ (more interpolation) for higher-index, low-frequency dimensions to accommodate long-range position changes without overflowing pretrained angle ranges. This follows the NTK intuition but generalizes it by learning many per-dimension scalars instead of a single analytic formula or coarse grouping.

A position-wise cutoff $\hat{n}$ protects the earliest tokens from any rescaling. Since early tokens tend to receive substantial downstream attention and are critical to preserving pretrained behavior (especially for in-context learning, prompts, and recently-attended content), leaving their RoPE angles unchanged avoids degrading those crucial local interactions. In practice the optimal $\hat{n}$ is found to depend on the target $L'$: for moderate extensions a modest $\hat{n}$ helps, while for extremely large $L'$ the benefit may change because most positions become far longer than the pretrained range.

Together, these two non-uniformities reduce information loss by selectively preserving the parts of RoPE that carry essential pretrained positional information (key low-dimension frequencies and the earliest token positions), while adapting other parts to permit representation of far larger absolute positions. By minimizing perturbation to the most informative components, the search yields initializations that require less fine-tuning and in many cases enable large extension ratios even without fine-tuning.

# Constraints to keep solutions plausible and learnable

Searching an enormous, unconstrained space of per-dimension scalars and position cutoffs would be computationally infeasible and could produce pathological solutions. LongRoPE therefore imposes several practical constraints and design choices that keep the search tractable and produce rescalings that are plausible for a pretrained model to use:

1. Range bounds on $\lambda_i$. Each $\lambda_i$ is searched in a bounded interval: the minimum allowed value is $1.0$ (no compression, equivalent to extrapolation), and the maximum is a reasonable multiple of the extension ratio $s$ (the paper uses up to $1.25\times s$). This prevents extreme compression or inversion of angles and keeps the modified angles within a sensible range relative to the pretrained scale.

2. Discretization step. To make the search finite and practical, $\lambda_i$ is discretized with a fixed step size (the paper uses step size $0.01$). This yields a large but enumerable set of candidate values across dimensions that evolutionary search can explore.

3. Candidate set for $\hat{n}$. The early-position cutoff $\hat{n}$ is not searched over every integer position; instead a small set of plausible cutoffs is pre-specified (for example $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$). This focuses the search on cutoffs that are likely to matter in practice and avoids wasting evaluations on many redundant nearby values.

4. Monotonicity constraint across dimensions. LongRoPE enforces a monotonic non-decreasing constraint on the $\lambda_i$ sequence:
\[
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}.
\]
This reflects the NTK-inspired prior that lower-index (higher-frequency) dimensions should be scaled less than higher-index (lower-frequency) dimensions. The monotonic constraint greatly reduces the effective search space (pruning permutations that violate this useful prior) and prevents anomalous solutions where a high-frequency dimension would be interpolated more than a lower-frequency one, which would more likely destroy short-range positional cues.

5. Population initialization with known baselines. The evolutionary search is seeded with sensible initial individuals corresponding to established interpolation schemes (PI, NTK, YaRN). Including these prior solutions in the initial population ensures the search starts from good baselines and improves search efficiency.

6. Practical population and iteration limits. To keep compute bounded, LongRoPE uses fixed population sizes, mutation and crossover parameters, and a maximum number of iterations. These hyperparameters are chosen to trade off evaluation cost vs. search thoroughness for each target window size (the paper reports different settings when searching for $>512$k windows).

Together these constraints preserve plausibility (solutions that respect frequency ordering and bounded rescaling), enable efficient search, and make the learned rescalings learnable by the downstream model. They also produce RoPE modifications that maintain useful pretrained behavior for early tokens and high-frequency dimensions, which is crucial for low-loss next-token prediction at the extended length.

# Putting it all together: the LongRoPE search objective

Collecting the pieces, LongRoPE solves the following constrained search problem: find discrete-valued $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ within the allowed ranges and satisfying monotonicity, such that the pretrained LLM equipped with the modified RoPE

\[
\text{RoPE}_n^{(i)} =
\begin{cases}
\cos\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\big) & n<\hat{n},\\[4pt]
\cos\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\big) & n\ge\hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]

minimizes the language modeling loss $\mathcal{L}$ computed on long inputs of target context length $L'$. The evolutionary search procedure evaluates candidate rescalings by computing perplexity on a small set of long validation samples, and iteratively refines the population by mutation and crossover constrained by monotonicity until a low-perplexity rescaling is discovered. This rescaling then serves either as a usable non-fine-tuned extension (for moderate $s$) or as an initialization for light fine-tuning (for very large $s$), supporting the progressive extension strategy that ultimately enables context windows up to 2,048k in the paper's experiments.

LongRoPE Search: Space, Algorithm, and Efficiency

# Search space design
The LongRoPE search is built to discover per-dimension rescale factors for RoPE and an index threshold for token positions. Concretely, for a rotary embedding with $d$ dimensions (i.e., $d/2$ complex-frequency pairs), the search must choose a rescale value $\lambda_i$ for each dimension $i\in\{0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1\}$ and a token-position threshold $\hat{n}$. The chosen formulation in LongRoPE uses the rescale factor such that the modified rotary angle at position $n$ and dimension $i$ becomes
\[
\cos\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),\quad
\sin\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),
\]
where $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) = 1$ for $n<\hat{n}$ and $1/\lambda_i$ for $n\ge\hat{n}$, and $\beta$ is RoPE's base ratio.

To make the search concrete and tractable, the paper defines a discrete grid for each $\lambda_i$ and for $\hat{n}$:

- Each $\lambda_i$ is searched over a uniform grid from $1.0$ up to $1.25\times s$ in steps of $0.01$, where $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ is the extension ratio (target length $L'$ divided by original length $L$). The lower bound $1.0$ corresponds to extrapolation (no interpolation) and values above $1.0$ correspond to degrees of interpolation; the upper limit $1.25\times s$ allows slightly more aggressive interpolation than naive linear PI.
- The token-threshold $\hat{n}$ is searched over the set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\). A value of $\hat{n}=0$ means every token position can be interpolated; positive values retain the first $\hat{n}$ positions with original RoPE (no rescaling).

This search space captures two axes of non-uniformity identified in the analysis: (i) per-dimension heterogeneity among RoPE frequencies and (ii) special treatment of early token positions. The discretization balances expressiveness (a fine-grained per-dimension choice) and tractability (a finite grid and a fixed small set for $\hat{n}$).

# Combinatorial explosion and why naive search is infeasible
Even with discrete grids, the number of possible configurations is astronomical. Consider a typical case where each $\lambda_i$ has roughly 400 choices (for example, when $s$ is modest and the step $0.01$ yields this many entries) and there are $\tfrac{d}{2}=64$ RoPE complex-dimensions, and 14 choices for $\hat{n}$. Then the total number of candidates is on the order of
\[
400^{64}\times 14,
\]
which the paper simplifies in an example to demonstrate the magnitude (they compute a similar expression and report about $4\times 10^{167}$ choices for a $4\times$ extension in a simplified counting). This combinatorial explosion makes exhaustive evaluation impossible. Each candidate requires running the model over long input sequences and computing perplexity; that inference cost is non-trivial, especially for long $L'$.

Because evaluating every vector of $\lambda_i$ values is infeasible, a guided, sample-efficient search strategy is required. LongRoPE adopts an evolutionary search with additional design choices to drastically prune the space of candidates it needs to evaluate.

# Evolutionary search workflow: population, fitness, mutation, crossover
LongRoPE frames the optimization as an evolutionary search. The key elements of the workflow are:

- Population: Maintain a population of candidate individuals, where each individual is a vector of $\{\lambda_i\}$ plus a chosen $\hat{n}$. The paper uses concrete hyperparameters for different target lengths: for target windows within 256k they use population size $P=64$, mutation size $N_1=16$, crossover size $N_2=16$, iterations $\mathcal{T}=40$, and mutation probability $p=0.3$. For windows beyond 512k they halve population and the mutation/crossover sizes to reduce cost.

- Fitness: The fitness score for an individual is language-model perplexity computed on a small set of long validation samples. For searches up to 256k the authors computed perplexity on 5 random PG19 validation samples (each sample having at least the target length). For very large windows ($>512\text{k}$) the perplexity is measured on 3 random Pile-Books3 samples. Perplexity directly reflects next-token predictive quality under the candidate RoPE rescaling and serves as the scalar objective to minimize.

- Selection: After evaluating the current population, the top-$k$ individuals (by lowest perplexity) are retained as parents for the next generation. The paper states they select top-32 in each generation in the within-256k setting.

- Mutation: Generate mutated children by perturbing parent individuals. Mutation is applied with probability $p$ to each dimension's $\lambda_i$ value (and potentially $\hat{n}$), producing $N_1$ mutated candidates per generation. The mutation respects constraints (see monotonicity below) so many mutated vectors are rejected early if they violate the constraint.

- Crossover: Create new children by combining components of parent vectors (e.g., slice-and-concatenate across dimensions), producing $N_2$ crossover candidates per generation. Again, candidates violating the monotonicity constraint are discarded.

- Iteration: Repeat evaluation, selection, mutation, and crossover for $\mathcal{T}$ iterations, tracking and updating a Top-k pool of the best-found individuals across generations. Return the individual in Top-k with the lowest perplexity.

This evolutionary approach is chosen because it can explore structured, high-dimensional discrete spaces and exploit good solutions via recombination and localized perturbation. Using perplexity as a scalar fitness makes candidate evaluation straightforward, albeit somewhat costly, so the search also requires engineering choices to avoid wasted evaluations.

# Seeding with PI, NTK, and YaRN to jump-start evolution
A plain random initialization of the population would waste many evaluations exploring very poor regions early. To accelerate convergence, LongRoPE seeds the initial population with three informative individuals that correspond to existing interpolation schemes: linear positional interpolation (PI), the NTK-inspired rescaling, and the YaRN grouping-based configuration. These three candidates are inserted directly into the initial population; the remaining $P-3$ individuals are created by randomly mutating those seeds with probability $p$.

Seeding achieves two beneficial effects. First, it injects known-strong solutions so evolution starts from sensible areas of the search space rather than from random noise. Second, by mutating seeds the search explores local neighborhoods around these strong baselines, which is an efficient way to find improved non-uniform patterns that preserve good properties of the seeds while correcting their deficiencies. In practice the authors found this initialization strategy helpful for convergence and for avoiding very poor early evaluations.

# Monotonically non-decreasing constraint: $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$
To further reduce wasted evaluations and to enforce a principled structure on solutions, LongRoPE imposes a monotonicity constraint across dimensions:
\[
\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\quad\text{for }i=0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-2.
\]
This constraint requires that rescale factors are non-decreasing with the RoPE dimension index (which corresponds to decreasing frequency, i.e., lower-index dimensions are higher-frequency components). The constraint is motivated by NTK theory and prior empirical observations which indicate that higher-frequency dimensions should be interpolated less (smaller scaling) while lower-frequency dimensions can accept more interpolation (larger scaling) without as much harm. By enforcing monotonicity:

- The effective search space shrinks drastically because many arbitrary permutations of per-dimension scalings are disallowed.
- Candidate generation (mutation/crossover) can be filtered early: any mutated or crossed child that violates monotonicity is discarded and not evaluated, saving evaluation cost.
- The search stays in a physically reasonable subspace that matches inductive knowledge about RoPE frequency roles, which empirically leads to better candidates and faster convergence.

Because monotonicity is a soft physical prior consistent with NTK reasoning, it preserves expressiveness while giving large efficiency gains in required evaluations.

# Evaluation protocol: long-text subsets and sliding windows
Fitness computation and final evaluation both depend on long-text datasets and sliding-window scoring that reflect the target use case of extended context. The paper uses these protocols for search and later model comparisons:

- Datasets used for evaluation and perplexity during search include PG19 and Books3 (Pile-Books3). For searches targeting up to 256k, perplexity is computed on 5 random PG19 validation samples whose lengths meet the required minimum. For searches targeting beyond 512k, perplexity is computed on 3 random Pile-Books3 validation samples due to the extreme length requirements.

- For final benchmarking (after applying the chosen rescale vector), the authors evaluate on datasets including PG19, Proof-pile (for within-256k comparisons), and Books3 for million-token evaluations. They compute perplexity over the full test splits when feasible (PG19 uses 100 documents) or on randomly selected long samples (e.g., 10 Proof-pile samples of at least 128k tokens; 20 Books3 books of length >2048k for the extreme-length evaluation).

- Perplexity on long documents is calculated using a sliding window of tokens. For within-256k settings they used a sliding window of length 256 tokens across the document; for Books3 extreme-length evaluation they used a sliding window of 256k (i.e., longer windows) to avoid excessive computation but still capture the long-range behavior.

- During search the authors set a minimum sample length equal to the search target length so that fitness truly reflects model performance at the intended extended length.

This evaluation protocol concentrates on long-sample perplexity as the primary metric during search, which directly aligns with the objective to preserve prediction quality at long contexts while minimally disturbing shorter-context behavior.

# Outcome: reliable 8× extension without fine-tuning
A central empirical outcome in the paper is that the evolutionary search — combining a rich per-dimension search space, seed initialization, monotonicity constraint, and small-sample perplexity evaluation — finds RoPE rescalings that enable large extension ratios without any fine-tuning. Concretely, applying the searched non-uniform rescale factors to a pretrained LLaMA2-7B allowed extending its context window from 4k to 32k (an 8× extension) without performing further model fine-tuning, while maintaining acceptable perplexity. By contrast, previously used schemes such as PI, NTK, and YaRN exhibited perplexity explosions after 2× extensions in the same non-fine-tuned setting.

The key reasons the search achieves this are:

- Per-dimension tailoring preserves important high-frequency dimensions (small $\lambda_i$) while allowing lower-frequency dimensions to absorb interpolation (larger $\lambda_i$), so the effective positional information needed to disambiguate near tokens is retained where it matters most.

- Allowing early token positions to remain un-rescaled (via the threshold $\hat{n}$) respects the empirical finding that starting tokens often have large attention weights and should be treated gently.

- The monotonic prior and seeding focus search on sensible regions, enabling the evolutionary algorithm to find high-quality candidates with a relatively small number of full-model perplexity evaluations.

These outcomes make the evolutionary search an effective component of LongRoPE: it produces strong no-fine-tune extensions up to 8×. The same search is also used in the paper as a building block in a progressive extension pipeline (search → fine-tune at moderate target lengths like 128k/256k → second search) to reach the paper’s larger goal of 2048k context windows with limited fine-tuning.

# Practical search hyperparameters and cost-control choices
Some practical configurations used in the paper are worth summarizing because they show how the algorithm balances search quality and compute cost:

- For target windows up to 256k: population $P=64$, mutation size $N_1=16$, crossover size $N_2=16$, iterations $\mathcal{T}=40$, mutation probability $p=0.3$, and selecting top-32 parents each generation. Perplexity is computed on 5 PG19 validation samples.

- For target windows beyond 512k: halve population, mutation and crossover sizes (e.g., $P=32$, $N_1=N_2=8$), and compute perplexity over 3 Pile-Books3 samples to limit evaluation cost.

- Initial population includes PI, NTK, and YaRN configurations plus mutated variants of them for faster convergence.

- Monotonicity filtering is applied at mutation/crossover time to avoid running full-model perplexity computations on candidates that violate the inductive prior.

These choices produce a search that is tractable on realistic compute budgets while still exploring a rich space of per-dimension rescalings. The paper emphasizes that this search is a light-weight, inference-only procedure: it does not require gradient-based optimization of model weights, only repeated evaluation of perplexity under different RoPE transforms.

# Summary
The LongRoPE search design is a deliberate response to a huge discrete optimization problem: choosing per-dimension RoPE rescalings and an initial-token threshold in a massively-sized combinatorial space. The method in the paper combines (1) a discretized but expressive search grid for $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$, (2) an evolutionary search algorithm with informed seeding by PI/NTK/YaRN, (3) a monotonicity constraint grounded in NTK reasoning to prune search and keep solutions sensible, and (4) a small-sample perplexity evaluation protocol using long-text slices. This design yields efficient discovery of non-uniform RoPE transforms that enable an 8× extension of context without fine-tuning (e.g., from 4k to 32k) and provides strong initialization for the progressive fine-tuning recipe that ultimately reaches million-plus token contexts.

Progressive Extension to 2048k

# Stage 1: Search for 128k and 256k on the pretrained model

The first stage of the progressive extension strategy is a targeted search for non-uniform RoPE rescale factors on the original pretrained model, aiming at intermediate extended context lengths (specifically 128k and 256k). Rather than attempting to jump directly from the original short context (e.g., 4k) to an enormous target like 2048k, LongRoPE finds per-dimension rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ and an initial-token cutoff $\hat{n}$ that minimize perplexity when the model processes sequences of the intermediate length $L'$. Concretely, the RoPE transformation tested during search takes the form (see Eq. (3) / Eq. (problem) in the paper):

RoPE(n)_i = [\ldots, \cos(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \times \tfrac{n}{\beta^i}), \sin(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \times \tfrac{n}{\beta^i}), \ldots]

with
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n} \\
{1}/{\lambda_i} & n \ge \hat{n}
\end{cases}

The search is posed as an evolutionary optimization over a large discrete space of $\lambda_i$ values (from 1.0 up to $s\times1.25$ in steps of 0.01, where $s=L'/L$) and a small discrete set of $\hat{n}$ candidates (e.g., \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}). To make search practical, the algorithm seeds the initial population with PI, NTK, and YaRN solutions and enforces a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ so that lower (higher-frequency) dimensions receive no more interpolation than higher (lower-frequency) dimensions. Perplexity evaluations during search use a small set of validation samples: for target window sizes within 256k, perplexity is computed on 5 random PG19 validation samples that meet the minimum length requirement. For large windows (over 512k) a reduced set (3 Pile-Books3 samples) is used.

Empirically, this stage discovers rescale patterns that preserve important RoPE dimensions and the initial positions that matter most for attention, producing much better initialization than uniform linear interpolation (PI) or previous hand-crafted non-uniform schemes. The paper reports that these searched rescale factors already enable substantial non-fine-tuned extension (up to 8×) for many scenarios, and provide a robust starting point for the lightweight fine-tuning that follows.

# Stage 2: Lightweight fine-tuning to 256k (e.g., 400 steps at 128k factors, then 600 steps at 256k factors)

After finding good rescale factors for 128k and 256k on the pretrained model, LongRoPE proceeds with short, resource-efficient fine-tuning to concretely adapt the model to longer contexts. The fine-tuning is deliberately lightweight: rather than running many epochs on extremely long sequences, the strategy uses small numbers of gradient steps at intermediate lengths, leveraging the better initialization from the search to converge quickly.

The concrete schedule used for LLaMA2 in the paper is two consecutive short fine-tuning phases. First, the pretrained model is fine-tuned for 400 steps using the RoPE rescale factors found for 128k. After this phase completes, the RoPE factors are replaced by the ones found for 256k, and fine-tuning continues for an additional 600 steps. In total this constitutes 1000 fine-tuning steps but avoids any direct fine-tuning at the extremely large target length. The intuition is that the model adapts more stably when each fine-tuning step only needs to accommodate a moderate extension ratio (e.g., 32× for 128k, 64× for 256k in the LLaMA2 example) rather than a single huge jump.

The paper documents the training hyperparameters used for these short fine-tuning phases. For LLaMA2-7B, the fine-tuning used a learning rate of $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ with linear decay and a global batch size of 32. The 128k phase was trained on 8 A100 GPUs (using distributed training infrastructure), while the 256k phase required 16 A100 GPUs. The training data for LLaMA2 fine-tuning was RedPajama, chunked into 128k segments bookended with BOS and EOS tokens. For Mistral-7B experiments the authors used a constant learning rate of $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ and a global batch size of 64; for both 128k and 256k they followed YaRN's setup and trained 400 steps on Together Computer’s Long-Data Collections with a 16k training length using 4 A100 GPUs.

This two-stage fine-tuning design yields two benefits emphasized in the paper. First, it reduces the computational cost compared to directly fine-tuning at extreme lengths: shorter sequence lengths require less memory and per-step time, and fewer steps are sufficient due to the strong initialization. Second, smaller per-stage extension ratios improve convergence stability and reduce the risk of catastrophic values from new untrained positions.

# Stage 3: Second search on the 256k-finetuned model to reach 2048k without further fine-tuning

Having obtained a model fine-tuned to 256k, LongRoPE applies a second evolutionary search for RoPE rescale factors—this time on the 256k-finetuned checkpoint—targeting the final 2048k context window. Because the fine-tuned model has already adapted to much of the distributional shift introduced by extending positions up to 256k, the second search can find rescale patterns that permit a further large non-fine-tuned jump (an additional 8× in many cases) with only RoPE manipulation and no further gradient updates.

Operationally, the secondary search is the same algorithm used previously (population-based evolution, seeded initial individuals, monotonicity constraint), but it runs on the already-adapted model. For very large target windows (e.g., 2048k, which implies overall ratios like $512\times$ relative to 4k), the paper reduces search population and mutation sizes to keep inference-based perplexity evaluations affordable. Perplexity for candidate individuals at these scales is measured on a few long validation samples (the paper uses 3 random Pile-Books3 samples for windows over 512k). The selected rescale vector is then used at inference to realize an effective 2048k context window without any additional fine-tuning.

The empirical result reported is that this progressive two-stage fine-tune-plus-search method produces models capable of handling 2048k contexts (the paper names LongRoPE-2048k) while retaining strong perplexity and retrieval performance across the full range from short contexts up to 2M tokens. The secondary search is critical: applying the same search directly to the pretrained model without the intermediate fine-tuning was found to be less effective or impractical, whereas searching on the 256k-adapted model finds RoPE settings that bridge the remaining gap to 2048k.

# Rationale: smaller per-stage extension ratios improve stability and efficiency

The progressive strategy is motivated by two complementary observations about positional interpolation and fine-tuning. First, introducing many unseen position indices at once creates catastrophic (out-of-distribution) values in the positional embedding, making optimization difficult and often causing unstable or slow convergence. Second, long-context fine-tuning is resource intensive because it requires long training sequences (large memory and compute per step) and many steps to adapt.

By splitting the full extension into smaller per-stage ratios (pretrained → 128k → 256k → 2048k via a second search), LongRoPE keeps each optimization problem modest. The initial evolutionary search provides a rescale initialization that preserves key RoPE dimensions and critical early token positions, reducing the information loss that hampers naive interpolation. Fine-tuning at intermediate lengths requires far fewer steps (the paper uses 400 then 600 steps) because the model sees a much smaller distribution shift per phase. Finally, the second search on the fine-tuned checkpoint leverages the model's adapted internal representations to extrapolate further without gradient updates.

This design yields strong practical advantages: it dramatically reduces GPU hours compared to naively fine-tuning directly at 2048k, improves convergence stability by avoiding extreme per-step distribution shifts, and still attains the desired 2048k capability. The paper demonstrates that with only about 1k fine-tuning steps at lengths within 256k, the method can reach a 2048k effective context with good perplexity and downstream performance.

# Implementation notes: datasets, batch sizes, GPUs, practical runtime

The paper provides concrete implementation details that are useful for reproducing the progressive extension pipeline. Data: for LLaMA2 fine-tuning, RedPajama was used, chunked into 128k segments with BOS/EOS markers. For Mistral, Together Computer’s Long-Data Collections were used (following YaRN's setup) with 16k training length. Validation for search used PG19 samples for windows within 256k and Pile-Books3 samples for very large windows.

Hyperparameters and resource footprints reported in the paper include: for LLaMA2-7B fine-tuning a learning rate of $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ with linear decay and global batch size 32; the 128k phase used 8 A100 GPUs while the 256k phase used 16 A100 GPUs. For Mistral-7B, a constant learning rate $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$, global batch size 64, and 4 A100 GPUs were used for the reported fine-tuning experiments. The evolutionary search used population and mutation hyperparameters tuned by scale: for target windows within 256k, $P=64$, $N_1=N_2=16$, mutation probability $p=0.3$, $\mathcal{T}=40$ iterations, and top-32 individuals selected for mutation/crossover each iteration. For windows larger than 512k the population, mutation, and crossover sizes were halved to reduce search cost. Perplexity during search was computed on 5 PG19 samples (within 256k) or 3 Pile-Books3 samples (over 512k).

On practical runtime, while the paper does not provide precise total wall-clock numbers, the authors emphasize that the progressive approach is far more efficient than attempting direct fine-tuning at million-token lengths. The critical cost drivers are: (1) the short fine-tuning phases on 128k and 256k (which still require multi-GPU distributed training but for only hundreds of steps), and (2) the search evaluations, which require repeated forward passes over long sequences but can be constrained to a small set of long validation samples and a reduced evolutionary population for very large windows. The monotonicity constraint and seeding the population with PI/NTK/YaRN substantially reduce wasted evaluations and thus practical runtime during search.

Together, these implementation choices yield a reproducible, resource-conscious path to extend RoPE-based LLMs from standard short windows up to 2048k while preserving performance across short and long contexts.

Short-Context Performance Recovery

# Problem: high-ratio interpolation crowds short positions and degrades accuracy

When a pre-trained language model's context window is extended very aggressively—say from an original length $L$ (e.g., 4k) to a much larger target length $L'$ (e.g., 2048k)—the positional embedding scheme must map many new token indices into the model's existing positional encoding space. In LongRoPE (and other positional interpolation approaches) this mapping is implemented by rescaling the rotary angles of RoPE. For a particular RoPE dimension $i$ and token position $n$, the original rotary angle is of the form $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ (where $\beta$ encodes the base frequency schedule). Extension is achieved by applying a rescale factor $\lambda_i$ (or equivalently $\hat{\lambda}_i = 1/\lambda_i$ in the paper's notation) so the angle becomes $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \times \frac{n}{\beta^i}$ for tokens where the rescaling applies.

A key empirical phenomenon identified in the paper is that very large extension ratios (large $s = \frac{L'}{L}$ and hence large $\lambda_i$ values for many dimensions) compress the rotary angle values corresponding to positions inside the original short-window region. Intuitively, when you downscale rotation angles across many dimensions to accommodate far-away positions, all the angles for the original positions are pushed into a narrower range. This is described in the paper as a "crowding" effect: the representation space for token positions inside the original short window becomes compressed and the model has a harder time distinguishing nearby token positions. The immediate practical consequence is measurable degradation in language modeling quality and downstream task accuracy when operating at short lengths (for which the model was originally optimized). The paper reports this as a notable drop in performance after extreme positional interpolation, and cites earlier work that observed similar degradation (e.g., PI causing short-window degradation).

Thus, even though extreme extension can enable usage on very long documents, it creates a trade-off: improvements at very long lengths can come at the expense of performance within the original short-range context. Any robust extension approach therefore needs an explicit mechanism to recover or preserve short-context fidelity.

# Recovery search: reduce max \lambda at short windows to limit interpolation

LongRoPE addresses the short-context degradation by performing an additional search specifically targeted at shorter windows (for example 4k and 8k). The central idea is simple and principled: when the model will be used with a relatively short sequence length, it is unnecessary—and harmful—to apply the same aggressive rescale factors that were chosen to support million-token contexts. Therefore, LongRoPE re-searches RoPE rescale factors with a constrained search range that favors less interpolation (i.e., smaller $\lambda$).

Concretely, the recovery search re-uses the same evolution-based search machinery described for the main non-uniform interpolation search, but modifies two important aspects:

1. Search range constraint. The maximum allowable $\lambda_i$ is reduced relative to the values used to reach very large $L'$. That is, whereas for extending to very large windows the search allowed $\lambda_i$ up to approximately $s \times 1.25$, in the recovery search the upper bound on $\lambda_i$ is decreased so the algorithm is encouraged to select rescale factors closer to 1.0. This prevents overly compressing the angular range for positions inside the short window, because smaller $\lambda_i$ means less downscaling of rotary angles.

2. Same two-form non-uniformity model. The recovery search still models the two non-uniformities identified earlier: a per-RoPE-dimension rescale $\lambda_i$ and a token-position threshold $\hat{n}$ that leaves the initial $\hat{n}$ tokens unscaled. In other words, it can still decide both which dimensions to interpolate and whether to exempt the initial tokens from rescaling, but it does so within the reduced interpolation budget appropriate for short windows.

Because the search is guided by perplexity on sample inputs of the target short length, it finds rescale factor configurations that better preserve modeling fidelity inside that short range. The paper emphasizes that this is a targeted readjustment: it does not undo the large-window rescale choices used for long-context operation; instead it produces an alternate set of RoPE parameters that the model can switch to when the user requests short-length inference.

# Dynamic selection at inference: choose factors based on sequence length

To make the recovery actionable, LongRoPE applies dynamic selection of RoPE rescale factors at inference time. Rather than using a single global rescale set regardless of the query length, LongRoPE stores multiple rescale profiles discovered via search: (a) the profile tuned for extremely long context lengths (e.g., the profile discovered for the progressive extension to 2048k), and (b) the profiles discovered by the recovery searches for short contexts (e.g., 4k and 8k).

At inference, the model inspects the sequence length of the current input. If the sequence length is below a short-window threshold (for example less than 8k), the model applies the RoPE rescale factors found by the recovery search. If the sequence length is larger, it applies the rescale factors tuned for long contexts (or an appropriate intermediate profile, if multiple levels are stored). This runtime selection avoids the need to sacrifice short-window performance when supporting million-token contexts: the model dynamically uses the RoPE interpolation that suits the current sequence length.

This mechanism is lightweight in deployment: it changes only the positional embedding behavior and requires no architectural changes beyond storing a few RoPE rescale vectors. It is also compatible with the other operational goals of LongRoPE (keeping model architecture and most optimizations unchanged).

# Observed gains: restored perplexity and benchmark accuracy at 4k–8k

The paper reports clear empirical benefits from the recovery search and dynamic factor selection. After extending LLaMA2 and Mistral to extremely large windows, LongRoPE observed performance drops at the original short context sizes. Applying the recovery search—i.e., re-searching RoPE rescale factors with a tighter maximum $\lambda$—yields significant improvements on short-window language modeling metrics and downstream benchmarks.

Specifically, the recovery search reduced perplexity at 4k and 8k evaluation lengths on Proof-pile and PG19 relative to the aggressive long-window rescale factors. The paper summarizes these results in Table~\ref{tbl:shortperformance} (referred to in the Experiments section): the readjusted RoPE factors meaningfully improved perplexity at the short lengths while preserving the long-length abilities when the model switches profiles at inference. In addition, benchmark accuracies on standard tasks within the original context window (e.g., MMLU, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA) remained comparable to the pre-extended baselines and in some instances improved slightly; this indicates that the recovery does not merely improve perplexity but also translates into preserved or restored practical task performance.

Two additional qualitative points support the approach. First, the recovery search is efficient because it reuses the same evolutionary search framework but restricts the search bounds; thus it does not require full re-finetuning of model weights. Second, because this procedure produces multiple RoPE rescale configurations, it gives practitioners a simple trade-off surface: they can pick the rescale factors that balance long-range capacity and short-range fidelity according to their application needs.

In summary, the Short-Context Performance Recovery in LongRoPE is a focused, empirically validated mitigation for the crowding effect caused by extreme positional interpolation. By re-searching with reduced maximal interpolation, preserving the two-form non-uniformity parameterization, and selecting rescale profiles dynamically at inference by sequence length, LongRoPE restores perplexity and benchmark accuracy at 4k–8k while retaining the ability to handle million-token contexts.

Experimental Setup and Benchmarks

# Models

This work evaluates LongRoPE primarily on two off-the-shelf decoder-only LLMs that use RoPE positional embeddings: LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B. Both models were kept architecturally unchanged apart from modifications to the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) parameters: we replace the pretrained rotary-angle scaling with the searched per-dimension rescale factors (and optionally the initial-token threshold) described in LongRoPE. All reported extended-context models reuse the original model weights; only the RoPE rescale factors are changed before fine-tuning or inference, except when specified that a short fine-tuning stage was applied (see the Fine-tuning subsection).

When referring to extended variants in experiments we use the following notation: LongRoPE-2048k (ft=128k) denotes a model whose RoPE rescale factors were searched and whose fine-tuning used a 128k context training length; LongRoPE-2048k (ft=256k) denotes analogous fine-tuning at 256k. Comparisons are made against counterparts produced by applying other positional interpolation strategies (PI, NTK-based, YaRN) followed by the same fine-tuning schedules when appropriate.

# Datasets

Two types of data are used in our experiments: (1) datasets for evaluation of long-sequence language modeling and retrieval-style tasks; and (2) datasets used for the short fine-tuning stage required to learn to exploit extremely long contexts.

Evaluation datasets

- PG19: A long-document language modeling benchmark used for perplexity evaluation. For PG19 we evaluate perplexity using the whole test split of 100 documents and a sliding evaluation window of length 256 tokens.

- Proof-pile: A subset of the Pile-like long-document mixes used in the paper for long-sequence evaluation. For experiments reported in the paper we randomly select 10 samples from Proof-pile with at least 128k tokens each and compute perplexity using a sliding window of 256 tokens across longer evaluation lengths.

- Books3: Used to probe extremely long-context behavior (extensions beyond 2000k). For Books3 evaluation the paper randomly selects 20 books that each exceed 2,048k tokens and uses a sliding evaluation window of 256k tokens.

- Passkey retrieval synthetic task: A controlled generation-style evaluation where a randomly sampled five-digit passkey is hidden somewhere in a very long context and the model is prompted to retrieve it. The evaluation places the passkey at a uniformly random location across the target evaluation context length, and results are averaged across 10 iterations per evaluated length to measure retrieval accuracy as a function of context distance.

Training / fine-tuning datasets

- RedPajama: Used for fine-tuning LLaMA2 in the progressive extension procedure. Fine-tuning is performed on RedPajama data chunked into segments matching the target training context length (e.g., 128k segments for the first stage) and bookended with BOS/EOS tokens as described in the paper.

- Together Computer Long-Data Collections: Used for Mistral fine-tuning experiments following the setup used in YaRN. For Mistral the paper uses these long-data collections with a 16k training length during fine-tuning for 128k and 256k targets (mirroring YaRN’s training choices).

# Metrics and Evaluation Protocols

Perplexity across lengths

Perplexity is the primary language-modeling metric used to measure how well an extended model can model long contexts. Perplexity is computed in the conventional next-token prediction sense and is reported at multiple evaluation context lengths. For most long-sequence evaluations the paper uses a sliding window evaluation with window stride 256 tokens. For PG19 and Proof-pile the sliding window length used for scoring is 256 tokens; for Books3 experiments to evaluate extremely long contexts the sliding window used is 256k tokens (coarser-grained but suitable for very long documents).

When presenting perplexity curves, the x-axis corresponds to evaluation context length (for example, 4k, 8k, 16k, ... up to 2048k) and the y-axis corresponds to measured perplexity using the sliding-window protocol described above.

Passkey retrieval accuracy

Passkey retrieval measures a model’s ability to pick out a short target (a five-digit number) placed somewhere in a long context. For each target evaluation context length, the passkey is placed uniformly at random within the context; the model is prompted according to the template used in the paper and the retrieved token sequence is scored for exact recovery of the five-digit passkey. Each length is tested over 10 random placements, and accuracy is reported as the fraction of trials where the model returned the exact passkey. This metric serves as a concrete test of whether the model can both (a) attend to and represent distant tokens and (b) use that information in generation.

Standard short-context benchmarks

To ensure extension does not catastrophically harm the original short-context performance, the paper evaluates models on several standard benchmarks at the original short context window (within 4096 tokens). Benchmarks and settings are:

- ARC-Challenge (ARC-C): 25-shot evaluation.
- HellaSwag: 10-shot evaluation.
- MMLU: 5-shot evaluation.
- TruthfulQA: 0-shot evaluation.

These tasks are evaluated via zero-/few-shot prompts, following the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard evaluation protocol as used in the paper. The main goal here is to check whether LongRoPE modifications and any fine-tuning produce substantial regressions on established short-context tasks.

Slide-window evaluation specifics

Perplexity evaluations use overlapping windows as in prior long-context LM assessments. For example, when evaluating at a target length $L$ with sliding-window stride $r$ (the paper uses $r=256$ tokens for many experiments), each position in the text will appear in multiple windows except at boundaries. Log-likelihoods for tokens are computed in the usual autoregressive manner across the windows covering each token, and aggregated to produce the overall perplexity for that document/set.

# Baselines

The paper compares LongRoPE variants against a set of state-of-the-art context-extension baselines that also modify RoPE or the training process. The baselines include open models extended via:

- PI (Position Interpolation): Linear scaling of rotation angles by the global extension ratio $s$ (i.e., $\lambda=s$ across all RoPE dimensions), optionally followed by fine-tuning.

- NTK-based interpolation / Dynamic NTK: Methods that distribute interpolation pressure unevenly across RoPE dimensions based on NTK theory so that lower (high-frequency) dimensions are scaled less than higher (low-frequency) ones; these can be applied without fine-tuning for small extension ratios and sometimes with fine-tuning.

- YaRN: A method that partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups and applies extrapolation, NTK-based or linear interpolation per group. YaRN’s grouping is based on empirical rules; the paper includes YaRN-extended LLaMA and Mistral checkpoints as baselines.

Open models with long-context fine-tuning cited/compared in the paper include Together-32k, Code LLaMA, LongLoRA-full-FT-100k, YaRN-LLaMA, and YaRN-Mistral. Where available, the paper compares to those models’ reported perplexities and retrieval results at corresponding evaluation lengths.

All baselines are evaluated using the same evaluation protocols (sliding-window perplexity, passkey retrieval, and short-benchmark evaluations) to provide apples-to-apples comparisons.

# Fine-tuning schedules and infrastructure

Fine-tuning for progressive extension

The progressive extension strategy used by LongRoPE minimizes the need to fine-tune at the final extremely large target length. The paper’s fine-tuning schedule for LLaMA2-7B is as follows:

- Stage 1 (128k target): After obtaining searched RoPE rescale factors for a 128k target, fine-tune the pre-trained LLaMA2-7B model for 400 gradient steps on RedPajama data chunked into 128k segments. The learning rate used is $2\times10^{-5}$ with linear decay and a global batch size of 32. This stage is trained on 8 A100 GPUs with the distributed training system referenced in the paper.

- Stage 2 (256k target): Replace the RoPE rescale factors with those searched for 256k and continue fine-tuning for an additional 600 gradient steps (so in total 1k steps across both stages). The same optimizer settings are used; the 256k stage is run on 16 A100 GPUs because of the larger context length and memory requirements.

For Mistral-7B the fine-tuning setting differs as described in the paper:

- A constant learning rate of $1\times10^{-6}$ and a global batch size of 64 are used for fine-tuning. For both the 128k and 256k fine-tuning runs, the paper follows YaRN’s setup and uses the Together Computer Long-Data Collections with a 16k training length. Fine-tuning runs used 4 A100 GPUs for the training described.

These short fine-tuning runs (400 + 600 steps for LLaMA2, 400 steps for Mistral settings as used) are intentionally small to reduce computational cost while still enabling the model to adapt to the substantially expanded RoPE parameterization.

# Search hyperparameters

The core of LongRoPE is an evolutionary search to find per-dimension RoPE rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ and an initial-token threshold $\hat{n}$ that together define the position-wise scaling rule. The paper reports two groups of search hyperparameters depending on the target context length.

For target window sizes up to and including 256k the search setup is:

- Population size: $P=64$.
- Mutation size: $N_1=16$.
- Crossover size: $N_2=16$.
- Mutation probability: $p=0.3$ (used both when augmenting initial individuals and per-mutation).
- Max iterations: $\mathcal{T}=40$.
- Top-k selected in each iteration: 32 (the top-32 individuals are used as parents for mutation and crossover).
- Perplexity evaluation samples: 5 random PG19 validation samples that meet the minimum length requirement for the target context window.

For windows larger than 512k (i.e., very large search where inference for each candidate is more expensive), the paper halves the population, mutation, and crossover sizes to reduce search cost. Concretely, for >512k searches:

- Population size and mutation/crossover sizes are halved relative to the ≤256k setup (so effectively $P=32$, $N_1=N_2=8$ in typical runs).
- Perplexity evaluation samples: 3 random Pile-Books3 validation samples (used because Books3 contains extra-long documents suitable for very-large-context evaluation).

Search space specifics

- Each per-dimension scaling factor $\lambda_i$ is searched over the range $[1.0,\, s\times 1.25]$ in increments of 0.01, where $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ is the global extension ratio (so $s$ equals e.g., $32$ for 128k when starting from a 4k original length).
- The initial-token threshold $\hat{n}$ (the number of initial tokens that keep original RoPE without rescaling) is selected from the discrete set $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$.
- A monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ is enforced during sampling and evolution to restrict candidate solutions to those with non-decreasing scale factors across RoPE dimensions. This dramatically reduces the effective search space and is motivated by NTK intuition that low-index (high-frequency) dimensions should be scaled less.

Initial population optimization

Rather than purely random initialization, each search begins with a population that includes three known strong baselines as seeds: the PI (uniform $\lambda_i=s$), the NTK-derived pattern, and YaRN’s grouped strategy. The remainder of the initial population ($P-3$ individuals) is produced by randomly mutating these seed individuals with probability $p$ to encourage exploration around reasonable solutions.

Stopping and selection

At the end of the evolutionary iterations the individual with the lowest measured perplexity over the held-out validation samples is selected and used as the RoPE rescale-factor set for downstream fine-tuning (if any) or inference.

# Practical evaluation notes and reproducibility details

- Perplexity computation for candidates: For efficiency during search the paper evaluates each candidate by applying its RoPE rescale factors to the frozen LLM and computing perplexity on a small number of long validation samples (5 PG19 samples for ≤256k searches, 3 Books3 samples for >512k searches). This keeps search tractable while still correlating with downstream fine-tuned perplexity improvements.

- Monotonicity constraint: Enforcing $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ both decreases the number of invalid or implausible candidates and improves search efficiency. All mutation and crossover operators are constrained to produce monotone candidates; candidates violating monotonicity are rejected without running a costly model evaluation.

- Short-context recovery: After producing an extremely long RoPE parameterization (e.g., for 2048k), the authors run a final small search targeted for short context lengths (e.g., 4k and 8k) with a reduced maximum allowable $\lambda$ to find per-length rescale factors that restore short-context performance. During inference the model selects the appropriate RoPE rescale factors dynamically by the current sequence length (i.e., use the short-length rescale set when the active context is within 4k or 8k).

# Summary of the experimental setup

In summary, the experimental setup combines per-dimension and per-position RoPE rescale-factor search via an evolutionary optimizer (with seed initialization and monotonic constraints), short progressive fine-tuning stages (400 + 600 steps for LLaMA2; 400-step-like schedules for Mistral variants following YaRN), and evaluations that measure perplexity across many scales, retrieval accuracy via the passkey task, and short-context standard benchmarks. Search hyperparameters are more aggressive for target lengths up to 256k and are reduced for >512k searches to control compute. Baselines include PI, NTK-style schemes, and YaRN, and comparisons are reported on the same evaluation protocols to ensure fairness.

Results: From 4k to 2048k

# Within 256k: decreasing perplexity with length and outperformance over baselines

A central empirical finding of LongRoPE is that, when evaluated up to 256k context lengths, models extended using our method exhibit a consistent improvement as more context is provided: perplexity generally decreases as evaluation context length grows from 4k toward 256k. This trend indicates that the extended models are able to leverage additional, distant tokens to improve next-token prediction, rather than being confused or harmed by the presence of many new positions.

How this was measured. Perplexity was computed using a sliding window evaluation (window stride 256) over long documents from two distinct test collections: PG19 and Proof-pile. For PG19 the entire test split of 100 documents was used; for Proof-pile we followed prior work and sampled 10 documents each exceeding 128k tokens. Perplexity was reported at multiple evaluation lengths to demonstrate how model quality changes as more context becomes available.

Comparison to prior methods. Across these within-256k evaluations, LongRoPE-extended models (both LLaMA2 and Mistral variants) outperform state-of-the-art baselines that also target long contexts (implementations using PI, NTK-like strategies, YaRN, and other recent extended models such as LongLoRA and YaRN-based models). Two points are worth emphasizing. First, the decreasing-perplexity trend demonstrates that LongRoPE produces usable long-context representations: additional context yields net benefit rather than confusion. Second, even though LongRoPE models are ultimately extended to much larger window sizes (see next sections), they still outperform competing extended models when measured inside the 256k regime, showing that our non-uniform interpolation plus the progressive fine-tuning yields strong language-modeling performance in the medium-long regime as well.

Intuition behind the gains. The improvement stems from two complementary effects: (1) the evolution search finds non-uniform rescale factors that preserve the most informative RoPE dimensions and early token positions, reducing the catastrophic distortion that naive interpolation introduces; (2) when fine-tuning is applied at moderate extended lengths (e.g., 128k/256k), the model learns to adapt remaining parameters to the shifted positional embedding while starting from a better initialization. Together, these let LongRoPE retain discriminative positional structure even as context length grows, which manifests as falling perplexity with longer evaluation windows up to 256k.

# Beyond 2000k: stable perplexity on Books3 and differences between LLaMA2 and Mistral

We evaluated LongRoPE-extended models at truly extreme scales (up to 2,048k tokens) by using long books from Books3. For this test we randomly selected 20 Books3 documents each exceeding 2,048k tokens, and computed perplexity with a sliding window of 256k. These experiments test whether the progressive extension strategy and second-stage non-uniform interpolation can maintain reasonable modeling quality at million-token scales.

Overall outcome. LongRoPE successfully extended both LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B to a 2,048k context window while maintaining sensible perplexity behavior across scales. In many cases LongRoPE matches or surpasses baselines at shorter long-context lengths (8k–128k), and delivers competitive perplexity even at 1,024k and 2,048k evaluation lengths.

Model-specific differences. Two important differences emerged between LLaMA2 and Mistral:

- LLaMA2: LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k shows an overall trend where perplexity decreases as more context is provided, with only marginal upticks at the largest (1,024k and 2,048k) windows. In other words, the model continues to benefit from added context up to the million-token range, and the final 2,048k extension does not catastrophically increase perplexity.

- Mistral: LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k performs very well at shorter long-context lengths — it outperforms baselines for many lengths up to a few hundred thousand tokens — but its perplexity grows beyond about 256k and is noticeably higher at the longest ranges. This difference is plausibly attributable to differences in our fine-tuning setup for Mistral: for Mistral the fine-tuning runs (both 128k and 256k variants) followed YaRN’s setting of using 16k training chunk length, which appears to limit Mistral’s ability to adapt as readily to the very large secondary extension ratio required to reach 2,048k. In contrast, LLaMA2 benefited from a fine-tuning schedule (including a 256k fine-tune) that left a smaller secondary extension ratio for the non-fine-tuned second-stage interpolation, producing better final million-token behavior.

Why this matters. These results demonstrate that LongRoPE’s progressive extension — search to 128k/256k, short fine-tune at those lengths, then a second non-uniform interpolation to reach 2,048k — can produce usable long-context LLMs at scales previously unattained by position-interpolation approaches, while model-specific fine-tuning choices influence the final robustness at the extreme longest lengths.

# Passkey retrieval: ≥90% accuracy up to 2048k (LLaMA2), strong results for Mistral up to ~1800k

Beyond perplexity, we also evaluated LongRoPE on a targeted retrieval-style generation task called passkey retrieval. In this synthetic task, a five-digit passkey is embedded at a random location uniformly distributed across a very long document; the model is prompted to find and output that passkey. This task emphasizes whether the model can preserve and attend to a specific short piece of information buried among a sea of irrelevant tokens — a demanding test of effective long-range memory and attention.

Evaluation protocol. Ten independent trials were run for each evaluation context length; in each trial the passkey location is sampled uniformly across the evaluation window. Retrieval accuracy is the fraction of trials in which the model correctly outputs the passkey.

Key results. The passkey experiments show striking differences between LongRoPE and prior methods:

- LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k (fine-tuned at 256k) achieves very high retrieval accuracy across the entire sweep from 4k up to 2,048k, maintaining accuracy at or above 90%. This demonstrates that LongRoPE-LLaMA2 can reliably keep a token’s identity accessible and retrievable even when that token is buried among over two million tokens.

- LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k (fine-tuned at 128k) attains 100% accuracy up to approximately 1,800k, then degrades to about 60% at the 2,048k length. This drop at the final length mirrors the perplexity behavior noted earlier: Mistral’s longer-range modeling weakens at the very largest extension ratio, causing some failure modes for pinpoint retrieval at the extreme end.

Contrast with baselines. Existing extended models’ retrieval accuracy collapses quickly beyond 128k in these experiments, often going to near-zero accuracy at much larger lengths. The strong passkey performance of LongRoPE models — especially LLaMA2 — demonstrates that our non-uniform interpolation preserves essential positional discrimination and that the progressive fine-tuning strategy yields practical generative capability at million-token scales.

Implication. High passkey retrieval accuracy indicates not just lower perplexity but task-relevant preservation of salient token identity and addressability across extremely long contexts, which is crucial for real applications (searching, long-document reasoning, stateful agents) that require locating and using small pieces of information distributed widely across long inputs.

# Original-window tasks: comparable accuracy to base models; slight trade-offs depending on fine-tuning length

A fundamental constraint for any long-context extension is preserving performance on the native, original short context tasks (e.g., benchmarks designed for 4k or 8k windows). Positional interpolation can crowd original positional embeddings and degrade performance within the original window unless care is taken. LongRoPE explicitly addresses this with a short-context readjustment step (a targeted search optimizing RoPE rescale factors for e.g. 4k and 8k), but some trade-offs remain.

Zero/few-shot benchmark results. We evaluated LongRoPE-2048k models on standard benchmarks in zero- and few-shot configurations: ARC-Challenge (25-shot), HellaSwag (10-shot), MMLU (5-shot), and TruthfulQA (0-shot). The extended models achieve results comparable to their base counterparts and other competitive open models on these tasks. There are two practical observations:

- Comparable overall: For most benchmarks LongRoPE models remain close to the base models’ performance, indicating that the extension process did not catastrophically harm short-window reasoning and knowledge.

- Small degradations tied to fine-tune choices: LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k that used a 256k fine-tune shows modest performance drops on some benchmarks relative to the original LLaMA2 baseline; the drop is visible but within reasonable bounds for many tasks. LongRoPE-Mistral sometimes slightly outperforms the original Mistral on some specific measures (e.g., a small improvement on TruthfulQA in our reported results), indicating that the interaction of interpolation and fine-tuning can occasionally improve certain behaviours.

Why small trade-offs occur. When RoPE is rescaled heavily to accommodate huge extension ratios, the distribution of rotation angles that correspond to the original short-range positions becomes more concentrated (i.e., “crowded”), potentially reducing the model’s ability to discriminate nearby positions inside the original window. LongRoPE mitigates this by performing an additional search to re-optimize rescale factors for short lengths and dynamically switching rescale factors at inference based on the actual sequence length; nevertheless, a small residual cost can remain, and the magnitude of that cost depends on the fine-tuning regime (see next section).

Practical mitigation. The paper’s operational strategy is to (1) perform targeted readjustment searches for short lengths after finishing the progressive extension, (2) restrict the maximum allowed \lambda during these short-length searches to encourage less interpolation, and (3) at inference time apply the length-specific rescale factors so that short queries use rescale factors optimized for the short range. These measures substantially recover short-window performance in practice.

# Effect of fine-tuning window (128k vs 256k) on final 2048k performance

The progressive extension strategy in LongRoPE uses an intermediate fine-tuning stage at a moderate extended length (128k or 256k in the experiments) followed by a second-stage search to reach 2,048k without further fine-tuning. The choice of that intermediate fine-tune window materially affects final performance at million-token scales; our experiments illuminate how and why.

Observed differences. For LLaMA2-7B, fine-tuning at 256k produced a better final LongRoPE-2048k model than fine-tuning at 128k. The practical reason is the size of the second-stage extension ratio: going from 256k to 2,048k is an 8× secondary extension, whereas going from 128k to 2,048k is a 16× secondary extension. The smaller secondary ratio produces a less aggressive non-fine-tuned interpolation step, making the final million-token model easier to realize and yielding lower perplexity and stronger passkey performance for LLaMA2.

For Mistral-7B, the experiments showed the opposite pattern: the model fine-tuned at 128k produced better final behavior than the 256k-fine-tuned variant. The paper attributes this to training settings used for Mistral: both the 128k and 256k fine-tuning runs for Mistral followed YaRN’s configuration of using 16k training chunk lengths. That training setup limits how well the model can learn to generalize to much larger context windows when the intermediate fine-tune is at the larger 256k window; hence the smaller intermediate fine-tune (128k) produced better final extension results for Mistral in these experiments.

Implications and practical guidance. These results imply two practical recommendations when using LongRoPE in practice:

- Prefer a larger intermediate fine-tune length (e.g., 256k rather than 128k) when your fine-tuning setup provides sufficiently long training chunks and resources, because it reduces the subsequent non-fine-tuned extension ratio and yields more stable million-token models (as seen with LLaMA2).

- If your fine-tuning setup must use relatively short training chunks (e.g., 16k), you may get better final results by choosing a smaller intermediate fine-tune window (e.g., 128k) because that better matches the training-time distribution the model sees and helps the model adapt more effectively.

In both cases the evolution-based search for non-uniform rescale factors is key: the same search procedure is used at both the intermediate stage (to find rescale factors that initialize fine-tuning) and the second-stage (to interpolate the fine-tuned model to the final 2,048k window). The choice of intermediate fine-tune length therefore sets the difficulty of the second-stage interpolation and empirically changes final behavior.

# Summary

The Results section demonstrates that LongRoPE can extend RoPE-based LLMs from standard 4k windows to unprecedented 2,048k windows while preserving or improving language-modeling performance across the medium-long regime (up to 256k), delivering usable perplexity and retrieval performance at million-token scales, and retaining acceptable performance on original short-window benchmarks. The key empirical levers are (a) searching non-uniform rescale factors that exploit both per-dimension and per-position non-uniformities, (b) a progressive extension strategy that limits the largest fine-tuning step to a tractable mid-range (128k or 256k), and (c) a final readjustment to recover short-window performance. Model- and training-configuration-specific choices (e.g., whether to fine-tune at 128k vs 256k and what training chunk lengths are used) influence the final behavior at the very largest scales; the paper documents these effects and shows practical ways to choose the strategy to get robust performance up to 2,048k.

Ablations and Analysis

# Second interpolation after 256k fine-tuning vs PI/YaRN: perplexity stability at 512k–2048k

One central ablation in the paper evaluates the effect of performing a second, non-uniform positional interpolation on a model that has already been fine-tuned to an intermediate extended length (256k), compared to applying simpler interpolation schemes like linear positional interpolation (PI) or YaRN on that same fine-tuned checkpoint. The motivation for this experiment is practical: directly fine-tuning to extremely large lengths (e.g., 2048k) is expensive and unstable, so the progressive pipeline used in LongRoPE fine-tunes only to 256k and then applies a second RoPE rescale search to reach 2048k. The ablation isolates whether that second search provides measurable benefits beyond simply applying PI or YaRN on the fine-tuned 256k model.

Empirically, the paper reports that applying LongRoPE’s searched non-uniform interpolation after fine-tuning to 256k produces substantially better perplexity stability when extending further to 512k, 1024k, and 2048k. Table results summarized in the paper (see the ablation section) show that PI and YaRN applied to the fine-tuned 256k checkpoint lead to rapidly increasing perplexity as the evaluation length grows. In contrast, the second LongRoPE search maintains a more consistent perplexity curve across the extension ratios. The explanation offered is that LongRoPE’s search preserves critical RoPE dimensions and token-position behavior learned during fine-tuning, producing an interpolation that minimizes information loss and catastrophic shift for the model when it must handle drastically larger indices.

This ablation supports two practical conclusions. First, performing a targeted, model-specific non-uniform rescaling after a moderate fine-tuning stage is more effective than naively applying generic interpolation rules to a fine-tuned checkpoint. Second, the second interpolation acts as a fine-grained alignment step: because the 256k fine-tuning has already adapted weights to a particular RoPE geometry, the second search identifies rescale factors that keep that geometry informative when extrapolated to 2048k. In short, the progressive strategy (fine-tune to 256k, then search on the fine-tuned model) yields better perplexity stability across 512k–2048k than PI or YaRN applied at the same stage.

# Short-length recovery: improved perplexity and benchmark accuracy at 4k–8k

A recurring concern with large context-window interpolation is degradation on the original, shorter contexts. When RoPE is rescaled heavily to accommodate very large token positions (e.g., a 512× overall extension), the encoding for positions within the original 4k window becomes “crowded” into a much smaller angular range. This crowding can degrade the model’s ability to distinguish nearby tokens and therefore harm performance in standard short-context benchmarks.

LongRoPE addresses this with an explicit short-length recovery ablation: after obtaining the extremely extended model (2048k), they run an additional evolution search constrained to encourage less interpolation for short context lengths (4k and 8k). Practically, this entails lowering the maximum allowed scale factors during the search so that the RoPE rescale pattern for short lengths remains closer to the pretrained RoPE geometry. The paper reports that this readjustment noticeably improves perplexity measured at 4k and 8k on Proof-pile, and also recovers performance on standard LLM benchmarks in the original context window (e.g., ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA) to levels comparable with baselines.

Quantitatively, the ablation tables in the paper show that perplexity at 4k and 8k drops after recovery search, and average benchmark accuracy either returns to near-original values or improves slightly in some datasets (for example, LongRoPE models match or slightly exceed the original Mistral on TruthfulQA). This demonstrates that dynamic selection of RoPE rescale factors based on the current inference sequence length can mitigate the short-context degradation inherent to extreme extensions. The practical recommendation is therefore to perform a restricted search targeting short lengths after producing an ultra-extended model, and to apply those recovered RoPE settings when the runtime sequence length falls below the recovery threshold (the paper uses 8k as a practical cutoff).

# Dimension-only vs dimension+position non-uniformities at 16k–32k and 2048k

The paper decomposes the non-uniformity problem into two axes: heterogeneity across RoPE dimensions (different rescale \lambda per frequency dimension) and heterogeneity across token positions (preserving some initial \hat{n} positions from interpolation). The ablation contrasts three strategies: (i) PI (uniform across dimensions and positions), (ii) searching for non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions only, and (iii) searching jointly for dimension-wise non-uniformity plus an initial-token no-interpolation region (dimension+position non-uniformity).

At intermediate target lengths like 16k and 32k (i.e., modest extension ratios), the ablation shows that searching for non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions alone significantly reduces perplexity relative to PI. Adding token-position non-uniformity (preserving the first \hat{n} tokens) yields an additional improvement at these lengths. This aligns with the intuition that at modest extensions, initial tokens receive high attention and are important to preserve—so exempting them from interpolation helps the model maintain signal where attention is concentrated.

However, when the target extension becomes extreme (e.g., going from 256k to 2048k without further fine-tuning), the ablation observes that token-position non-uniformity contributes less, and in some settings it saturates. The paper reports that preserving only the initial tokens without interpolation becomes less useful at very long lengths. A plausible reason provided is that when the overall length is extremely large, the relative benefit of preserving a small initial window reduces because attention patterns and RoPE geometric distortions are dominated by the massive scale changes across the full sequence. Meanwhile, dimension-wise non-uniform rescaling continues to yield gains even at extreme lengths because it preserves higher-frequency dimensions that are crucial to distinguish nearby positions everywhere in the sequence.

Therefore, the ablation supports a nuanced design principle: for small-to-moderate extensions, jointly optimizing dimension and early-token position non-uniformities is most beneficial; for ultra-large extensions, prioritizing careful dimension-wise rescaling is essential and early-token preservation yields diminishing returns.

# When early-token preservation helps and when it saturates

The early-token preservation hyperparameter \hat{n} controls how many initial positions keep the original RoPE angles (i.e., are exempt from interpolation). The empirical study in the paper shows a clear pattern: preserving a nonzero initial segment improves perplexity for modest extension ratios (e.g., 8k or 16k targets) compared to applying interpolation uniformly across all positions. The intuition is straightforward—early tokens often receive disproportionate attention and carry critical context (beginning of documents, prompts, or recently inserted important content). If their rotational encodings are kept unchanged, attention layers can more reliably access the representations the model was trained with, reducing out-of-distribution shifts.

However, the benefit is not monotonic and depends on the target extension length. The paper’s experiments vary \hat{n} across values like 0, 2, 4, 8, ... up to 256 and observe that the optimal \hat{n} changes with the target length. For smaller extensions the sweet spot can be small but nonzero; for larger extensions the optimal \hat{n} can be larger, but beyond some point the gains saturate or disappear. In the extreme-length experiments (e.g., extending to 2048k), preserving a small initial region stops yielding improvements and may even become irrelevant. The paper suggests this is because the very large-scale reshaping of angle space overwhelms the local benefit of preserving a handful of initial tokens.

From a design perspective, the takeaway is to treat \hat{n} as a tunable parameter whose best value depends on the extension ratio and on whether fine-tuning is used. The LongRoPE search includes \hat{n} as part of the search space precisely to find the task- and model-appropriate preservation length automatically. Practically, one should expect early-token preservation to help most when extending by factors up to a few tens (e.g., 2×–32×) and to have diminishing benefit for multi-hundred× or greater extensions.

# Takeaways for designing interpolation schedules

The ablations and analyses in the paper yield several actionable rules for designing positional interpolation schedules when extending RoPE-based LLMs:

1. Favor non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions. Allowing per-dimension rescale factors (with a monotonic constraint \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}) captures the fundamental heterogeneity of information encoded across frequencies. This consistently reduces perplexity compared to uniform PI and is the most robust lever at both moderate and very large extension ratios.

2. Include early-token preservation for modest extensions. When the extension ratio is modest (up to a few tens), jointly searching for a small initial no-interpolation region (\hat{n} > 0) further improves performance. However, treat \hat{n} as dependent on the target length—LongRoPE’s search on \hat{n} is therefore useful.

3. Use a progressive strategy for extreme extensions. Rather than directly targeting extremely large lengths for fine-tuning, first find a robust non-uniform schedule and fine-tune at an intermediate length (the paper uses 128k then 256k). After fine-tuning, perform a second non-uniform search on the fine-tuned model to reach the final target. This two-step strategy reduces training cost and yields better perplexity stability than one-shot fine-tuning or naive interpolation.

4. Recover short-context performance explicitly. After producing an ultra-extended model, run a constrained search focused on short lengths (e.g., 4k–8k) to find RoPE settings that avoid crowding for small contexts. Apply these settings dynamically at inference when sequence length is below the recovery threshold.

5. Enforce monotonicity in dimension rescaling. Imposing \lambda_i non-decreasing with dimension both reflects NTK-based intuition and drastically reduces the effective search space, improving search efficiency and avoiding pathological rescalings that would violate the frequency ordering.

6. Automate rather than handcraft grouping. Prior approaches group RoPE dimensions manually (e.g., YaRN’s three-group scheme). The ablations indicate that an automated search over per-dimension factors (with appropriate regularization/constraints) finds superior, model-specific schedules and avoids brittle hand-tuned grouping choices.

Taken together, these takeaways point to a practical workflow: (a) perform an evolution search for per-dimension \lambda_i and \hat{n} using a small held-out set for the desired intermediate target; (b) fine-tune at that intermediate length; (c) run a second search on the fine-tuned checkpoint to extrapolate to the final target; and (d) run a final constrained search to recover short-context performance and apply the recovered RoPE when needed. The ablations in the paper provide empirical backing for each step of this schedule and quantify the benefits over PI and prior non-uniform heuristics.

In summary, the ablation suite in the paper dissects the relative contributions of the second interpolation, short-length recovery, dimension-wise and position-wise non-uniformities, and early-token preservation. The consistent conclusion is that per-dimension non-uniform rescaling is the dominant factor, early-token preservation helps at modest extensions, and a progressive two-stage fine-tune-plus-second-search pipeline combined with short-length recovery yields the best practical balance of long-range capability and short-range fidelity.

How to Apply LongRoPE in Practice

# Pre-requisites: RoPE-based architecture and inference stack
LongRoPE is built on the assumption that your model uses rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) as its positional encoding. Concretely, the model must compute per-dimension rotary angles of the form $n/\beta^{i}$ (or equivalently $n \theta_i$ as in Eq.\,(1) and Eq.\,(3) of the paper). If your model uses other positional schemes (absolute learned embeddings, ALiBi, etc.), LongRoPE as described cannot be applied directly; you would first need to convert or adapt the model to RoPE.

On the infrastructure side, LongRoPE is intentionally lightweight: it preserves the original Transformer architecture and only modifies the per-dimension RoPE rescale factors. That means you can reuse existing inference kernels, attention implementations, quantization, and any optimized dispatch in your stack. However you must be able to:
- Replace or override the per-dimension RoPE rescale factors at inference time based on sequence length.
- Run perplexity evaluations efficiently on long windows (sliding-window perplexity is used during search and validation).
- (For fine-tuning) run distributed training for the chosen long sequence lengths (the paper used 8–16 A100 GPUs for fine-tuning 128k/256k).

# Step-by-step: initial search, short fine-tuning to 256k, second search to target length
LongRoPE follows a progressive pipeline: (A) search non-uniform rescale factors on the pre-trained model for intermediate long lengths, (B) fine-tune to an intermediate length (256k in the paper), (C) search again on the fine-tuned checkpoint to reach the final target (2048k), and (D) optionally recover short-context performance via a short-length search.

1. Initial search on pre-trained model
   - Choose intermediate target lengths to make fine-tuning tractable. The paper searched for 128k and 256k rescale factors on the pre-trained models first (these correspond to extension ratios $s=\tfrac{128\text{k}}{4\text{k}}=32$ and $s=\tfrac{256\text{k}}{4\text{k}}=64$ for a 4k base).
   - Run the evolution-based search (details in next sections) to produce per-RoPE-dimension rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ and an initial-token threshold $\hat{n}$ that keeps the first $\hat{n}$ positions unmodified.
   - Use the best-found rescale factors to initialize fine-tuning.

2. Short fine-tuning to 256k
   - Fine-tune on long-chunked data using the searched rescale factors appropriate for the intermediate length.
   - Paper hyperparameters (LLaMA2-7B example): learning rate 2e-5 with linear decay, global batch size 32. Fine-tune 400 steps using the 128k rescale factors, then replace with 256k rescale factors and fine-tune another 600 steps (total 1k steps). The paper used distributed setups of 8 A100s for 128k and 16 A100s for 256k.
   - For Mistral, the paper used learning rate 1e-6, batch size 64, and 400 steps (following YaRN’s 16k training length setting).

3. Second search on the fine-tuned extended LLM
   - After fine-tuning at 256k, run the evolution search again on that checkpoint, this time targeting the final length (2048k). The paper’s approach does not require further fine-tuning after this second search; it leverages the fine-tuned 256k model together with the newly searched rescale factors to reach 2048k.
   - The secondary search benefits from a smaller effective secondary extension ratio (e.g., 256k → 2048k is an $8\times$ rescale), which simplifies finding effective non-uniform rescale factors.

4. Short-context recovery (optional but recommended)
   - Extending to huge lengths can degrade performance for the original short context (4k/8k). To restore performance, perform an additional evolution search on the extended model but restrict the maximum allowable $\lambda$ (encourage less interpolation) and target short lengths like 4k and 8k. At inference time, dynamically select the appropriate rescale factor set depending on the current sequence length.

# Selecting datasets and long-text shards for perplexity evaluation
Search and evaluation are guided by perplexity measurements on real long-text samples. The paper used:

- Short / validation samples for search within 256k: 5 random samples from PG19 validation (minimum length = target context length).
- For windows over 512k: fewer samples (3 random samples) from Pile-Books3 validation.
- Final long-range evaluation: Books3 (randomly selected 20 books > 2,048k) with sliding windows of 256k.
- Fine-tuning data: RedPajama (for LLaMA2) chunked into 128k segments; Together Computer’s Long-Data Collections (for Mistral) with 16k training length to match YaRN’s setup.

Guidelines:
- Choose evaluation samples that are at least as long as your target context length so the perplexity calculation is meaningful.
- Use a sliding window evaluation with a window stride matching your validation intent (the paper used stride/window of 256 for many evaluations and 256k when evaluating multi-megabyte sequences).
- For search speed, use a small number of long validation samples (e.g., 3–5) but ensure they are representative of the target domain (books, code, etc.). The search uses these samples as the fitness function.

# Choosing population sizes, mutation rates, and evaluation windows
The paper provides concrete search hyperparameters that worked in their experiments; use these as starting points and scale them down for extremely large targets to save computation.

Typical settings used in the paper:
- For target window sizes within 256k:
  - Population size $P=64$
  - Mutation size $N_1=16$, crossover size $N_2=16$
  - Mutation probability $p=0.3$
  - Max iterations $\mathcal{T}=40$
  - Select top-32 individuals for mutation/crossover each iteration
  - Perplexity measured on 5 PG19 samples

- For target windows over 512k (heavier to evaluate), reduce the search budget:
  - Halve population, mutation, and crossover sizes (e.g., $P\approx 32$) and evaluate on 3 Pile-Books3 samples.

Search-space definition:
- For each RoPE dimension $i$ (there are $d/2$ dimensions), search $\lambda_i$ in $[1.0,\; s\times 1.25]$ with step $0.01$, where $s$ is the extension ratio.
- Search $\hat{n}$ from the discrete set \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}.

Search algorithm optimizations (important for efficiency):
- Seed the initial population with PI, NTK, and YaRN configurations (i.e., do not start purely random).
- Randomly mutate those seeds to create the rest of the population (mutation probability $p$).
- Enforce a monotonic non-decreasing constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ across dimensions; this both reduces the search space and is consistent with NTK intuition (higher-frequency dims should have smaller interpolation).
- Evaluate perplexity only for individuals that satisfy the monotonic constraint.

Practical advice:
- If compute is limited, reduce $P$ and $\mathcal{T}$ and accept a coarser search; seeding with PI/NTK/YaRN helps the evolutionary search find good regions faster.
- Use fewer, longer validation samples to keep fitness evaluations meaningful while limiting runtime.

# Monitoring stability: perplexity curves, loss spikes, and retrieval checks
Monitoring during both search and fine-tuning is critical.

Perplexity / loss curves
- Track perplexity over sliding-window evaluation across sequence lengths. Stable, improving behavior is a decreasing or flat perplexity trend as context grows; spikes or sudden jumps indicate problematic interpolation for that region.
- During search, compare candidate rescale factor sets by their perplexity on the validation samples. The algorithm selects the lowest-perplexity individual.

Loss spikes during fine-tuning
- Short fine-tuning (the paper uses 1k steps total for 256k) can be sensitive to initialization. If you see large loss spikes or divergence during the early steps, consider:
  - Using the rescale factors for a slightly smaller target first (paper fine-tuned 128k for 400 steps before switching to 256k).
  - Lowering the learning rate or using a smoother schedule.
  - Ensuring the training chunks match the expected input format (bookended with BOS/EOS, consistent chunking).

Functional checks: passkey retrieval
- Use synthetic retrieval tasks (passkey retrieval as in the paper) to validate actual retrieval behavior across context lengths. The passkey task places a small secret (e.g., a 5-digit number) at a random location in a very long document; a robust long-context LLM should retrieve it reliably.
- Plot retrieval accuracy vs. context length. If perplexity looks okay but retrieval accuracy drops, that indicates attention behavior or index-disambiguation issues not captured purely by perplexity.

Other practical signals
- Compare short-context evaluation (4k/8k) after extension. If short-context performance degrades unacceptably, plan to run short-context recovery (next section).
- Watch for over-crowding effects: very large $\lambda$ values can crowd rotary angles for original positions and cause short-context degradation. That motivates limiting maximum $\lambda$ when searching for short-length rescale factors.

# Integrating short-context recovery and dynamic factor selection at inference
Problem: A single global rescale set optimized for 2048k may hurt performance on 4k/8k. LongRoPE addresses this via a short-context recovery search and dynamic selection at inference.

Short-context recovery
- After you obtain the 2048k rescale factors, run an additional evolution search on the extended model but restrict the maximum allowed $\lambda$ to encourage less interpolation (because 4k/8k need less scaling).
- Target short lengths explicitly (e.g., 4k and 8k) and evaluate perplexity on short validation samples. The paper reports significant improvements after this recovery step.

Dynamic selection at inference
- Maintain multiple rescale-factor profiles indexed by the inference request length ranges (for example: [<=8k], (8k,256k], (256k,2048k]).
- At inference time, choose the profile whose target length best matches the requested sequence length. If the current request length is less than 8k, load the short-length rescale profile; otherwise use the long-length profile.
- Implementation: apply the chosen per-dimension $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ when computing RoPE rotations. This is a simple parameter switch and does not require model reloading.

# Compatibility: preserving model architectures and optimizations
One of LongRoPE’s main advantages is minimal invasiveness:

- No changes to weights or Transformer layers other than modifying the computation of rotation angles for RoPE. This keeps compatibility with:
  - Existing optimized kernels for attention and matrix multiplications.
  - Quantized or fused inference backends, provided you can override the small RoPE precomputation step.
  - Existing deployment stacks that already support per-request positional handling.

- Fine-tuning footprint is small compared to full re-training of positional schemes. The paper used a total of 1k fine-tuning steps at mid-range lengths (128k/256k), which is tractable for teams with multi-GPU resources.

- LongRoPE can be used together with other efficiency methods (LoRA-like parameter-efficient fine-tuning, or LongLoRA style approaches), since the positional rescale is independent of the choice of fine-tuning parameterization.

Practical caveats:
- Ensure any caching layer that depends on positional indices (e.g., KV caches that assume a fixed positional mapping) is updated to use the new rotation angles consistently. Because LongRoPE changes how positions map to rotary angles, cached keys/values must use the same rescale profile as is used for the query tokens in that same session.
- If your service does position-dependent batching or chunking logic (e.g., streamed tokenization), make sure the threshold $\hat{n}$ semantics (i.e., first $\hat{n}$ positions are exempt from rescale) are consistently applied.

# Quick checklist and troubleshooting tips
- Before search: verify the model uses RoPE and that you can override per-dimension rotation angles.
- During search:
  - Seed the initial population with PI, NTK, and YaRN to improve convergence speed.
  - Enforce monotonicity $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ to reduce search noise and evaluations.
  - Start with $P=64$, $p=0.3$, $\mathcal{T}=40$ for targets ≤256k; reduce budget for bigger targets.
- During fine-tuning:
  - Fine-tune in stages (e.g., 128k → 256k) rather than directly to the largest intermediate length.
  - Monitor loss; if you see instability, reduce LR or increase number of warmup steps and/or fine-tune more steps at the intermediate scale.
- After extension:
  - Run passkey retrieval checks to validate practical long-range memory/retrieval.
  - Run short-length validation and perform short-context recovery via search if needed.
- In deployment:
  - Maintain multiple RoPE profiles and dynamically select based on input length.
  - Ensure KV cache and streaming logic use the same positional rescale policy.

By following the above procedure—seeded evolutionary searches, staged fine-tuning to 256k, a secondary search on the fine-tuned model to reach 2048k, and a lightweight short-context recovery—you can apply LongRoPE to RoPE-based LLMs while reusing existing architectures and optimizations. The paper shows this pipeline enables practical extension to 2,048k tokens with only ~1k fine-tuning steps (at 128k/256k training lengths) and maintains strong performance both at very long contexts (perplexity and passkey retrieval) and at original short-context benchmarks.

Relation to Other Long-Context Approaches

# Retrieval-augmented generation: pros/cons vs native long context
Retrieval-augmented generation uses an external memory or retrieval component to supply relevant context at inference time rather than relying solely on the model's internal sequence buffer. In the paper's discussion this class of methods is characterized as bringing complementary capabilities: they can effectively recall and reintroduce distant information without changing the model's positional encoding or dramatically increasing the autoregressive attention cost. Because retrieval stores and fetches relevant chunks, it sidesteps the need to represent millions of contiguous token positions inside a single forward pass.

However, retrieval-based designs also have important trade-offs relative to a native long context like LongRoPE's. Retrieval requires a separate index, retrieval logic, and typically additional I/O and latency at inference; it also depends on the coverage and relevance of the stored corpus. Retrieval can be very effective for tasks that are naturally decomposable into discrete facts or documents to look up, but it is less natural for tasks that require reasoning across a continuous sequence of tokens (for example, summarizing a single very large document in which the relative ordering and dense interactions across many nearby tokens matter). In contrast, native long-context methods such as LongRoPE keep the entire sequence available in a single attention graph, preserving the fine-grained positional relationships and enabling attention-mediated reasoning over arbitrarily long contiguous text.

The paper positions LongRoPE as a lightweight, architecture-preserving approach: it modifies only the RoPE positional embedding (via searched, non-uniform rescale factors) and reuses most existing model optimizations. That means LongRoPE can be used alone when the application benefits from having the entire raw sequence in context (e.g., long-form summarization, in-context example-heavy prompting, or retrieving a short passkey buried inside millions of tokens), or it can be combined with retrieval when the combination addresses latency, index coverage, or memory constraints. In short: retrieval is complementary—good for fetching relevant items at lower inference memory or compute cost, while native long context (LongRoPE) preserves dense token interactions and ordering with a single model pass.

# Attention-mask and windowing strategies (e.g., StreamingLLM, LM-Infinite)
Another family of approaches extends effective context by manipulating attention masks or by applying windowing/streaming strategies. The paper mentions StreamingLLM and LM-Infinite as representative works that avoid changing positional encodings and instead control how the attention operation aggregates information across positions. These methods mitigate the computational and numerical problems that arise when attention must consider many tokens by splitting, streaming, or aggregating older context so that the model never attends densely to every token at every step.

Attention-mask/windowing methods have useful properties: they keep the model weights unchanged, they can be made memory- and compute-efficient (by limiting the number of pairwise interactions computed), and they often fit scenarios where recent tokens are most important while older tokens can be compressed or summarized. They are therefore well suited for streaming applications or for infinite-history agents in which only rolling summaries or sparse memory are needed.

But such masking and windowing approaches are also different in capability from LongRoPE. Where LongRoPE extends RoPE so the model can still compute dense attention across an extremely long contiguous context (up to 2,048k tokens in this work), attention-mask strategies intentionally reduce or reshape the attention graph. That reshaping can be ideal for efficiency, but it changes the inductive bias: the model no longer has full pairwise attention over all pairs in the long context. The paper points out these two approaches are complementary. If an application needs full dense attention and fine-grained ordering across very long contiguous text (for example, retrieving a passkey located anywhere in a multi-million-token document), LongRoPE is the natural fit. If the application tolerates summarized or sparsely attended history (streaming dialogue agents, continuous monitoring), attention-mask/windowing techniques may be preferable for lower compute or latency.

# Large-base RoPE and long-context fine-tuning (Code LLaMA, ScaledRoPE, LLaMA2-Long)
A third route toward long context is to adjust RoPE's base frequency or fine-tune the model under a larger positional radius from scratch or via extended fine-tuning. The paper references works such as Code LLaMA, ScaledRoPE, and LLaMA2-Long as examples that use a larger base value for RoPE and then fine-tune the model on long sequences to make the positional representation naturally cover longer windows. This approach directly attacks the root cause — RoPE frequencies are set assuming an original maximum $L$, so enlarging the base or re-parameterizing RoPE can allow a model to represent larger absolute positions without rescaling.

The advantage here is conceptual simplicity: if the position embedding was trained (or re-trained) to span the target length, the model can learn to use those higher absolute positions in a way consistent with its internal representations. The downside is cost. Fine-tuning a full model on very long sequences is resource-intensive; it requires long training sequences (which may be scarce), large compute, and careful optimization to avoid introducing catastrophic values from untrained position indices. The paper explicitly highlights these constraints — scarcity of extremely long texts, fine-tuning compute costs, and the difficulty of convergence when most position indices are new.

LongRoPE offers an alternate trade-off: rather than changing the RoPE base or fully fine-tuning at maximal length, it uses a searched, non-uniform interpolation of RoPE to preserve critical dimensions and initial token positions, providing an effective initialization for a much smaller amount of fine-tuning. The paper’s progressive strategy (search to 128k/256k, fine-tune for relatively few steps at 128k/256k, then search on the fine-tuned model to reach 2048k) demonstrates that achieving million-token context windows can be done with far less fine-tuning than naively training to the final length. In other words, LongRoPE trades a small amount of guided search plus limited fine-tuning for the large cost of training end-to-end at the target length.

# Efficiency-focused fine-tuning (LongLoRA, PoSE) and orthogonality to LongRoPE
There are also methods designed specifically to reduce the fine-tuning cost when adapting models for long context. The paper mentions methods like LongLoRA and PoSE as efforts to make long-context fine-tuning cheaper. Such approaches typically adapt a small subset of parameters (e.g., low-rank adapters, parameter-efficient modules) or otherwise change training dynamics to avoid full-model updates while still enabling extension to longer windows. The key benefit of these methods is reduced GPU memory and training time compared with full fine-tuning.

LongRoPE is presented as orthogonal to these efficiency-focused fine-tuning approaches. Because LongRoPE operates primarily by modifying the RoPE positional embedding and uses a search-based procedure to find non-uniform rescale factors, it can be combined with parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques. For example, one could use LongRoPE’s searched rescale initialization and then apply LongLoRA-style updates (or other adapter-based fine-tuning) during the short fine-tuning stage (the paper uses as few as 1k fine-tuning steps within 256k training lengths). This combination can further reduce the compute and time required to reach very long context windows while preserving the benefits of both strategies: LongRoPE’s stronger initialization and the smaller optimization footprint of parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

The paper emphasizes that LongRoPE preserves the original model architecture except for minor positional-embedding changes, enabling reuse of many pre-existing optimizations. That design choice is what allows LongRoPE to be combined with other optimization or efficient-adaptation strategies without complex re-engineering.

# When to combine methods for best cost–quality trade-offs
Putting the above together, the paper suggests practical guidance about which approach or combinations to use depending on task requirements and resource constraints.

If the task requires dense, order-sensitive reasoning across very long contiguous text — for example, retrieving a short secret buried anywhere in a multi-million-token document, long-form document summarization where global coherence depends on pairwise interactions, or in-context learning with very many examples — LongRoPE (possibly combined with modest fine-tuning) is a strong option. It provides native dense attention across the entire extended window and—thanks to the searched, non-uniform rescale factors—avoids many of the catastrophic-value and crowding issues that make high extension ratios difficult.

If latency, memory footprint, or inference cost is the primary constraint and the task can tolerate compressed or retrieved context, retrieval-augmented and attention-masking/windowing techniques may be preferable. Retrieval is particularly cost-effective when relevant information is sparse and can be fetched selectively; attention-windowing/streaming is ideal for continuous or streaming settings where the model only needs detailed access to recent tokens.

When fine-tuning resources are limited but better-than-non-fine-tuned behavior is required, a hybrid strategy is attractive: use LongRoPE's evolutionary search to find a non-uniform interpolation for an intermediate extension (e.g., 128k or 256k), perform limited fine-tuning (the paper shows good results with only hundreds to a thousand fine-tuning steps), and then either apply a second search to reach the final long window or combine with parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LongLoRA or PoSE) to reduce update costs. This progressive strategy captures much of the performance advantage of full long-length fine-tuning while dramatically cutting compute/time demands.

Finally, combining retrieval with LongRoPE can be sensible in mixed workloads. One can keep LongRoPE-enabled native long-context capability for parts of the input where dense ordering matters (e.g., the main document body) while using retrieval for large external corpora or for low-value historical context that does not need to be represented densely. Because LongRoPE minimally changes the architecture, such hybrid deployments are operationally feasible.

In summary, the paper frames LongRoPE as a practical, architecturally-light method that complements existing paradigms. Its core contribution—searching and exploiting non-uniformities in RoPE across dimensions and token positions—yields strong native long-context performance while remaining compatible with retrieval, attention-masking, large-base RoPE reparametrizations, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Choosing which to use (or to combine) should be driven by the task’s need for dense full-context attention, the availability of long training sequences, and compute/latency budgets; LongRoPE widens the feasible region in that design space by lowering fine-tuning cost and preserving short-context performance through targeted readjustment.

Limitations, Risks, and Future Directions

# Search cost at extreme targets and dependence on long-text validation corpora

LongRoPE relies on an evolutionary search procedure to discover non-uniform RoPE rescale factors that minimize next-token loss (perplexity) for a target extended length. Although the paper outlines optimizations that make this search tractable (optimized initial population, a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, and population/crossover/mutation tuning), the procedure still requires repeated forward passes of the base LLM on held-out long examples to evaluate candidate individuals. As the target context length $L'$ grows (for example to hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens), each perplexity evaluation becomes more expensive in wall-clock time and memory. The paper therefore reduces the search workload in practice by using fewer samples (e.g., 3–5 validation samples for very large $L'$) and by shrinking population sizes for the largest targets, but these are pragmatic trade-offs rather than eliminations of cost.

Closely related is the dependence on long-text validation corpora. The search objective in Eq.~(1) of the paper minimizes $\mathcal{L}(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE},\mathbf{X}))$ where samples $\mathbf{X}$ must be at least as long as $L'$. For extreme $L'$ (e.g., $>256\text{k}$), such long validation samples are scarce, so the search either must (a) reuse a very small set of ultra-long examples (increasing variance and risk of overfitting the rescale factors to those specific texts), or (b) use shorter proxy sequences and hope the discovered rescale factors generalize. The paper addresses this in practice by using different validation corpora depending on $L'$ (PG19, Pile-Books3, Proof-pile) and by reducing population size for the largest windows, but the fundamental limitation remains: the quality and representativeness of long validation text affect the reliability of the search outcome. Thus, search cost and the availability of suitable long-text validation data form a key boundary for LongRoPE when targeting extreme context lengths.

# Model-specific tuning: transferability across architectures and sizes

LongRoPE is developed and demonstrated on RoPE-based LLMs (LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B in the paper). Its central mechanism—searching per-RoPE-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and a token-position threshold $\hat{n}$—assumes the same RoPE parameterization (same number of rotary dimensions and the same frequency schedule) across target models. The experiments in the paper show effectiveness across the two evaluated models, but they also hint at model-dependent behavior. For instance, the paper observes differing optimal fine-tuning lengths and final performance between LLaMA2 and Mistral: Mistral benefits differently from a 128k versus 256k fine-tuning setup, likely because of differences in their training setup and how fine-tuning was performed (e.g., training length used during fine-tuning). These observations imply that the search results are not trivially transferable across architectures, sizes, or even fine-tuning protocols.

Consequently, LongRoPE requires model-specific tuning: the evolutionary search should be run per model (or at least per model family/configuration) to find the rescale factors that preserve the original RoPE information patterns for that model. While the monotonicity constraint and initializing the population with PI/NTK/YaRN priors reduce search variance and improve robustness, practitioners should expect to repeat search (and possibly short fine-tuning) when moving to a different model size, RoPE base, or attention implementation. This model-dependence is a practical limitation when deploying LongRoPE across many distinct models.

# Very large extension ratios and diminishing returns in some models

The LongRoPE approach leverages two strengths: (1) an ability to find non-uniform interpolation that enables up to an 8× extension without any fine-tuning, and (2) a progressive extension strategy that combines a modest amount of fine-tuning (e.g., 1k steps within 256k training lengths) with a secondary search to reach a 2048k context window. However, the paper also documents that very large extension ratios (e.g., the final 512× extension from 4k to 2048k) pose challenges: (a) they increase the risk of crowding original short-window positions and hence degrade short-context performance unless corrective steps (shorter context recovery search) are taken; (b) different models respond differently to the same extension ratios—Mistral in the paper exhibits higher perplexity beyond 256k in one setting, while LLaMA2 shows a more graceful perplexity decrease as context increases.

These observations point to a practical diminishing-returns phenomenon: beyond some extension ratio, additional scaling of position indices yields smaller incremental gains in long-context perplexity and may introduce trade-offs in short-context behavior or robustness. Achieving extreme lengths like 2048k is feasible, but requires a careful progressive protocol (search → modest fine-tuning at an intermediary length → second search) and additional recovery tuning for short contexts. In practice, the point of diminishing returns—where more extension is costly but gives little benefit—will likely be model- and task-dependent.

# Memory/runtime costs at million-token inference

While LongRoPE itself is implemented with only minor changes to the positional embedding and preserves the original model architecture, running a transformer model with million-level context windows has inherent computational costs that LongRoPE does not eliminate. The transformer attention complexity is quadratic in sequence length for standard dense attention, so inference over $L'$ tokens can be extremely expensive in both memory and runtime. The paper focuses on the positional embedding and shows the model can make use of much longer contexts, but it does not change the underlying attention algorithm or the asymptotic compute required for full dense attention over $L'$ tokens.

In practice, deploying LongRoPE-extended models for million-token inference requires either (a) substantial hardware resources (GPU memory and compute) capable of handling the large attention matrices, (b) modified attention implementations (sparse/linear approximations, windowing, block processing) to reduce memory/time cost, or (c) application-level strategies that limit how many tokens are jointly attended to at once. The paper mentions that LongRoPE models can reuse most pre-existing optimizations, but those optimizations must themselves be designed to scale to million-token scenarios. Therefore, the memory and runtime cost of inference remains a hard constraint on real-world usage of ultra-long-context models.

# Potential risks: long-context hallucinations, privacy in massive prompts

Extending the context window to millions of tokens changes the threat profile of model behavior. Two risks highlighted by the paper’s scope are long-context hallucination and privacy concerns in massive prompts.

First, with vastly larger context, the model can attend to and integrate far more (and often noisy or partially relevant) information. While LongRoPE improves the model's ability to use longer context to reduce perplexity and to perform tasks like passkey retrieval, larger context also increases the chance that the model will combine disparate facts incorrectly or over-weight spurious correlations across distant tokens, producing hallucinations that are coherent but false. The paper demonstrates improved passkey retrieval accuracy up to very large lengths, but the broader risk of incorrect generations informed by enormous, heterogeneous context is an open behavioral concern.

Second, massive prompts may contain private or sensitive data. A model with a million-token context window can ingest and reason over extremely large documents that may include personally identifiable information, proprietary content, or confidential communications. If such a model is used in multi-tenant environments or with user-supplied long documents, there are new privacy vectors: accidental leakage of sensitive tokens in generations, inadvertent retrieval-like behavior across documents, or persistence of sensitive patterns in model outputs. The paper does not focus on privacy mitigation, so practitioners must treat LongRoPE-extended models with the same (and arguably heightened) care used for any system processing large private corpora.

# Future work: adaptive, data-driven \hat{n}, multi-objective search (short+long), layer-wise factors, learned controllers

The paper outlines a number of design choices and practical remedies (e.g., searching for a global threshold $\hat{n}$ of tokens to keep un-interpolated and a per-dimension sequence $\lambda_i$), and it presents evolution search as an effective method. These choices suggest several promising directions for future research, some of which the paper explicitly motivates by reporting observations and ablations.

Adaptive, data-driven \hat{n}. The paper finds that preserving the original RoPE for the initial $\hat{n}$ tokens often improves performance, and that the optimal $\hat{n}$ depends on the target length. An immediate extension is to make $\hat{n}$ adaptive and data-dependent—either varying by input or learned from data—so the model uses less interpolation for tokens that are currently receiving high attention. This could be done by searching a function $\hat{n}(\mathbf{x})$ or by learning a small controller that predicts how many leading tokens to exempt from rescaling for each example.

Multi-objective search (short + long). One practical problem LongRoPE must handle is the trade-off between long-context capability and preserved short-context performance. The paper mitigates this by a recovery search for shorter windows after the final extension. A more principled approach is to perform multi-objective optimization that jointly minimizes perplexity at multiple target lengths (e.g., a weighted sum of losses at 4k and 2048k), explicitly trading off short-window fidelity against long-window utility during the search. Evolutionary or gradient-based multi-objective solvers could be used to produce Pareto-front solutions from which deployers select a desired operating point.

Layer-wise factors. The current formulation searches per-RoPE-dimension rescale factors that are shared across layers. Yet the information processing role of positional signals can differ across Transformer layers. A plausible extension is to search or learn layer-wise rescale patterns, i.e., $\lambda_{i,\ell}$ for dimension $i$ in layer $\ell$. This would increase search dimensionality, but could better preserve the positional information needed at lower layers while allowing more interpolation at higher layers (or vice versa), potentially improving both short- and long-context behavior.

Learned controllers and differentiable approaches. Evolutionary search is effective and practical, but differentiable parameterizations of rescale functions (for example parameterizing $\log \lambda_i$ with a small neural network conditioned on model statistics) could allow gradient-based fine-tuning of the rescale factors jointly with small-scale fine-tuning of model weights. Such controllers could also be trained to adapt rescale factors per-example at inference time. This line of work would convert the discrete search into a continuous learning problem, potentially reducing the dependence on ultra-long validation corpora and expensive repeated forward evaluations.

Integration with attention and retrieval strategies. Finally, since memory/runtime costs for million-token inference remain a limiting factor, future work should combine LongRoPE’s position interpolation with scalable attention mechanisms and retrieval or memory architectures so that the model can both represent very long contexts and process them efficiently. The paper notes these approaches are complementary; concrete integration strategies (e.g., hybrid attention + RoPE rescale + chunked retrieval) are a natural next step.

Concluding remarks

LongRoPE demonstrates that careful, non-uniform manipulation of RoPE parameters plus a progressive, search-driven fine-tuning strategy can push a RoPE-based LLM’s usable context window into the million-token regime. At the same time, the method surface reveals practical limitations: search cost and validation-data requirements at extreme lengths, model-specific tuning, diminishing returns at very large extensions, and the unchanged computational burden of attention during inference. There are also behavioral and privacy risks associated with making models attend to much larger amounts of text. The directions sketched above—adaptive thresholds, multi-objective optimization, layer-wise factors, and learned controllers—are consistent extensions suggested by the paper’s findings and represent concrete paths for improving robustness, efficiency, and safety for ultra-long-context language modeling.

Summary and Key Takeaways

# Two non-uniformities: dimension-wise and position-wise

LongRoPE's core insight is that rotary positional encodings (RoPE) are not uniform in how information is distributed across their internal components. RoPE represents a token at position $n$ with pairs of sine and cosine terms formed from rotation angles $n\theta_i$, where $\theta_i$ varies across dimensions (commonly $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ for embedding dimension $d$). Standard positional-interpolation methods treat all dimensions and all token positions uniformly when rescaling angles to extend a model's context window: for an extension ratio $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ they typically apply a single rescale factor (e.g., $\lambda=s$ for linear interpolation) or a simple parametric scheme across dimensions (as in NTK-aware scaling or YaRN's three-group scheme).

LongRoPE shows empirically that two distinct non-uniformities matter:

1. Dimension-wise (frequency) non-uniformity. Different RoPE dimensions operate at different rotation frequencies: low-index dimensions correspond to higher angular frequencies and high-index dimensions to lower frequencies. The information content and sensitivity to interpolation differ across these frequencies. Empirical searches reveal that optimal rescale factors vary across RoPE dimensions: lower-frequency dimensions (or conversely, lower index/higher frequency, depending on indexing convention) generally require less interpolation, while other dimensions can tolerate or even benefit from stronger rescaling. This deviates from methods that use a single scaling or coarse grouping: a per-dimension rescale vector can better preserve critical positional information and thus reduce perplexity when evaluating at longer lengths.

2. Position-wise non-uniformity. Token positions within a sequence are not equally important for attention and downstream predictions. Early tokens in a long sequence typically receive strong attention and serve as anchors; when those early positions are heavily compressed by interpolation, downstream attention dynamics degrade. LongRoPE empirically demonstrates that excluding an initial block of positions (denoted $\hat{n}$) from interpolation — i.e., using the original RoPE for positions $n < \hat{n}$ and applying rescaling only for $n \ge \hat{n}$ — improves performance. The optimal value of $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extended length: for some extension ratios a modest number of initial tokens (e.g., up to a few hundred) should be preserved, while at extremely large lengths the benefit can diminish.

Formally, LongRoPE parameterizes the modified RoPE as:
\[
\text{RoPE}(n)_i =
\begin{cases}
\cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),\; \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right) & n < \hat{n},\\[6pt]
\cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}\right),\; \sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}\right) & n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
where $\beta$ is the base factor derived from the RoPE base (so original rotary angle is $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$) and the search finds per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and a threshold $\hat{n}$.

Recognizing and leveraging these two non-uniformities reduces the information loss that naive interpolation introduces, and provides much better initialization for any subsequent fine-tuning, enabling practical extension to much longer contexts.

# Evolutionary search with monotonic constraints for efficiency

Allowing a distinct rescale factor for each RoPE dimension and a separate token-threshold $\hat{n}$ creates an enormous combinatorial search space. For even moderate extension ratios the space explodes: with fine discretization for $\lambda_i$ and a list of possible $\hat{n}$, the number of combinations becomes intractable for brute force. LongRoPE formulates finding good $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ as an optimization search problem driven by a held-out perplexity objective on long sequences.

Key elements of the proposed search procedure:

- Search space design. Each $\lambda_i$ is searched over a grid from $1.0$ (no interpolation, i.e., extrapolation) up to $s\times 1.25$ (slightly larger interpolation than linear PI), with a small step (example: $0.01$). The threshold $\hat{n}$ is selected from a discrete set (e.g., \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}). This discretization makes the problem finite but still large.

- Evolutionary algorithm. LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search loop: initialize a population of candidate solutions, evaluate each candidate by applying its RoPE modification to the frozen LLM and computing perplexity on a small set of long validation samples, select top candidates, then produce new candidates via mutation and crossover. Iteratively this refines the population toward low-perplexity rescale vectors.

- Optimized initialization. To speed convergence and avoid wasting evaluations on poor regions, the initialization injects known strong baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN rescale patterns) into the initial population. Remaining members are generated by mutating these good baselines with moderate probability, biasing the search into promising neighborhoods.

- Monotonicity constraint. Drawing on NTK intuition (lower-index dimensions should be less interpolated than higher-index ones), LongRoPE enforces $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ across dimensions during mutation and crossover. This reduces the effective search space and prevents obviously implausible solutions, so fewer perplexity evaluations are needed. Only candidates satisfying this monotonic, non-decreasing structure are evaluated.

Using this evolutionary search with the two optimizations makes it feasible to discover high-quality per-dimension rescale vectors and $\hat{n}$ values even when the total combination count is astronomically large. Empirically, this yields rescale patterns that enable an $8\times$ extension without any fine-tuning and provide excellent initialization for subsequent, limited fine-tuning at larger target lengths.

# Progressive extension: 4k → 256k (short FT) → 2048k (search only)

Scaling directly from a standard context window (e.g., 4k) to multi-million-token windows is impractical: fine-tuning with extremely long sequences is costly and long documents of such length are scarce. LongRoPE addresses this by combining its non-uniform interpolation search with a progressive, two-stage strategy that limits expensive fine-tuning to a tractable intermediate length. The pipeline is:

1. Search-based extension of the pretrained LLM to intermediate lengths (e.g., 128k and 256k) without fine-tuning. Because LongRoPE's per-dimension rescale vectors and position-exception $\hat{n}$ can deliver up to an $8\times$ extension without fine-tuning, it yields good candidate RoPE modifications to reach these intermediate target lengths.

2. Short fine-tuning on the intermediate length. Fine-tune the model at a practical sequence length that is much smaller than the final target, for a modest number of steps. The paper reports, for LLaMA2: fine-tuning for 400 steps with the 128k rescale, and then swapping in 256k rescale factors and fine-tuning an additional 600 steps — totaling about 1k fine-tuning steps at lengths within 256k. This staged approach works better than trying to fine-tune directly to the 256k target because the two-step ramp allows the model to adapt more stably.

3. Second-stage search from the fine-tuned intermediate model to the final massive length (e.g., 2048k). After the 256k fine-tuning, LongRoPE performs a second evolutionary search, this time on the fine-tuned checkpoint, to find new per-dimension rescale factors that target $L'=$ 2048k. Crucially, this second stage is a search-only step: it does not require additional fine-tuning at the multi-million length, because the fine-tuned intermediate model plus a carefully chosen non-uniform rescale provides a viable mapping for the much larger positions. The effective final extension ratio reported is up to $512\times$ relative to the original 4k.

This progressive design both reduces the required fine-tuning compute and avoids reliance on rare datasets of extremely long documents. It leverages the observation that LongRoPE's non-uniform interpolation both (a) provides strong non-fine-tune extensions (up to 8×) and (b) supplies favorable initializations that make short fine-tuning at intermediate scale effective for later massive extension via search.

# Short-context recovery for practical usability

A known problem with aggressive positional interpolation (especially when extending by very large ratios) is degradation of performance on the original, short context window: by compressing the rotary angles to pack many new positions into the original angle range, positions within the original window become "crowded", hurting model accuracy and perplexity at short lengths. LongRoPE addresses this via an additional adjustment step called short-context recovery.

The recovery step uses the same evolutionary search machinery but restricts the search domain to smaller maximum $\lambda$ values (i.e., encourage less interpolation) and targets short context lengths (e.g., 4k or 8k). In practice, after producing a 2048k model via the progressive extension (with its per-dimension rescale factors tuned to long contexts), LongRoPE runs a constrained search to find alternate rescale vectors that improve performance when the model is used on short sequences. During inference the system dynamically selects the appropriate set of rescale factors depending on the current input length: if the length is below a threshold (e.g., 8k), it applies the short-length rescale factors that were found to recover performance.

Empirically this recovery step significantly reduces perplexity at short lengths and restores a model's utility on standard benchmarks and few-shot tasks designed within the original context window, while keeping the ability to operate at million-token contexts when the long-length rescale is used.

# Empirical validation across tasks and models

LongRoPE is evaluated on multiple models (LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B) and multiple task families to validate both long-context abilities and short-context preservation.

Language modeling perplexity on long documents. Perplexity is measured on datasets with very long documents (PG19, Proof-pile, Books3) using sliding evaluations. Within intermediate extended lengths (up to 256k) LongRoPE-extended models show an overall decreasing perplexity trend as context length increases, demonstrating the model uses longer context effectively. Beyond 256k and up to 2048k, models extended by LongRoPE retain reasonable perplexity compared to prior interpolation methods. The experiments show LongRoPE can extend to 2048k with better perplexity than state-of-the-art positional interpolation schemes across many length scales.

Passkey retrieval (synthetic retrieval task). This task tests whether the model can find a small "passkey" token string embedded among many irrelevant tokens scattered across a long context. Existing models' retrieval accuracy falls sharply beyond 128k. LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k (with 256k fine-tuning) maintains very high retrieval accuracy (≥ 90%) from 4k to 2048k. LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k (with 128k fine-tuning) achieves 100% up to about 1.8M tokens, dropping to 60% at 2.048M, consistent with its perplexity trends. This shows that LongRoPE's rescale patterns plus progressive fine-tuning preserve the model's ability to localize small signals in extremely long contexts.

Standard benchmarks at original short context. The authors evaluate on common zero/few-shot tasks (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA) using the original short context window setups. After applying the short-context recovery search, LongRoPE models exhibit comparable performance to the original models and to baselines, with small degradations in some configurations but even modest improvements on some tasks (e.g., TruthfulQA in one reported case). This validates the practical usability of LongRoPE models both for very-long-context applications and for standard short-context tasks.

Ablations and analyses. The experiments include ablations isolating the two forms of non-uniformity. Searching for per-dimension rescale factors alone yields large perplexity reductions compared to uniform linear interpolation (PI). Adding the position-wise exception $\hat{n}$ further improves results at moderate extension lengths (e.g., 16k or 32k). For extremely large extensions (e.g., 2048k) the benefit of keeping a small initial prefix un-interpolated becomes less clear, suggesting the interaction between position-wise preservation and ultra-long extension requires further study.

Search efficacy. The evolutionary search with monotonic constraint enables practical discovery of good rescale factors: the paper reports using a population-based evolutionary method with top-k selection, mutation and crossover, initialization that includes PI/NTK/YaRN, and a monotonicity constraint to reduce evaluations. For target windows up to 256k this search used reasonably small populations and iterations (numbers reported in the experimental section), and for larger windows the algorithm scales by reducing population sizes, keeping the overall computational cost manageable.

# Key takeaways

- Positional interpolation need not be uniform. RoPE contains two exploitable non-uniformities: per-dimension (frequency) differences and per-position differences (especially the importance of initial tokens). Modeling both leads to better extensions than prior single-scalar or coarse-group approaches.

- Searching for per-dimension rescale factors plus a small position exception $\hat{n}$, guided by perplexity, produces RoPE modifications that (a) give an up-to-8× extension without fine-tuning, and (b) provide superior initialization for limited fine-tuning to intermediate lengths (e.g., 128k/256k).

- A practical progressive pipeline — search to 128k/256k, short fine-tuning at that scale, then a second search to reach 2048k — realizes multi-million-token context windows while avoiding direct fine-tuning at the final huge length. This reduces both dataset requirements and GPU cost.

- Short-context recovery using a constrained search to find alternate rescale sets preserves performance on original small context tasks. During inference, the model can switch rescale factors depending on the input length.

- Extensive experiments on LLaMA2 and Mistral confirm the approach: LongRoPE-extended models achieve low perplexity over very large contexts, maintain high passkey retrieval accuracy across long ranges, and retain acceptable performance on standard benchmarks.

In summary, LongRoPE demonstrates that carefully exploiting RoPE's internal non-uniformities and solving for per-dimension, position-aware rescale factors via an efficient evolutionary search enables dramatic context window extension (to 2,048k tokens) with modest fine-tuning, while keeping the model useful for conventional, short-context tasks.
