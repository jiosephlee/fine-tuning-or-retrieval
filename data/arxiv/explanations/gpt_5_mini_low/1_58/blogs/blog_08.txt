\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# MoE and Custom Chips: Building for a 1-bit Future

Large sparse models like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) and the emerging class of 1-bit LLMs—exemplified by BitNet b1.58—are a natural match. The paper "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits" shows that constraining weights to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\} (what they call 1.58-bit weights) yields large wins in memory, latency, throughput and energy while matching full-precision performance at reasonable scales. That combination—much smaller memory footprint per parameter and dramatically cheaper arithmetic—changes how we should think about MoE deployment and about the hardware that will best run next-generation LLMs.

Below I summarize why MoE + 1.58-bit weights is compelling, how it can shrink multi-node footprints, and what kinds of custom hardware and memory hierarchies those models invite.

## Why MoE naturally pairs with 1.58-bit weights

MoE models route each token to a small subset of expert sub-networks. This yields large computational sparsity (fewer FLOPs per token) but has two deployment pain points: (1) high memory pressure from storing many expert parameters, and (2) communication/activation transfer overhead when experts live on different devices.

1.58-bit models directly attenuate both problems:

- Lower memory per expert: Ternary weights pack at effectively 1.58 bits per parameter versus 16 bits in FP16/BF16. That reduces the DRAM capacity required to store the large expert banks, decreasing the number of devices needed to host a given MoE model. The BitNet b1.58 results show substantial memory reductions (e.g., multiple-fold lower GPU memory usage vs FP16 LLaMA baselines), which directly applies to MoE experts as well.

- Lower interconnect traffic: When an activated expert is remote, the system must transfer its weights (or at least activations and possibly parameters) or perform remote compute. Smaller-weight representations and narrower activations reduce that bandwidth. The paper explicitly notes that the reduced memory footprint and activation size cut the cost of transferring parameters and activations across devices; for MoE this means fewer costly cross-chip transfers per token.

- Sparse routing amplifies the benefits: Since each token touches only a small subset of experts, the aggregate memory+bandwidth savings multiply—you pay much less per-token remote cost because each touched expert is cheaper to fetch and operate on.

Put simply: MoE gives you compute sparsity but needs lean memory/bandwidth to be deployable at scale; 1.58-bit weights move you toward that lean operating point.

## How 1.58-bit weights can shrink multi-node MoE footprints

The paper highlights a few concrete system-level outcomes that transfer directly to MoE:

- Fewer devices to host the same model: With much smaller parameters, the same total expert set can fit on fewer machines. For MoE, this reduces the number of chips involved in routing decisions and decreases cross-device synchronization and communication complexity.

- Lower activation transfer cost: The BitNet b1.58 design uses 8-bit activations and points out that reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits allows doubling context length for the same KV cache budget. For MoE, smaller activations mean that forwarding activations between routing stages and experts is cheaper.

- Smaller inter-node transfer for sparse routing: Because only a few experts are invoked per token, the combination of sparse routing and small ternary parameters reduces worst-case and tail-case network traffic. This reduces latency variability and improves throughput under realistic workloads.

The paper even suggests a dramatic arithmetic efficiency advantage for matrix multiplication: an estimate of ~71.4× savings in arithmetic energy for matmul on 7 nm processes (since bit/adder-dominated ops replace FP multiplies and adds). Energy savings are particularly relevant when MoE increases the number of model parameters: the cost per parameter goes down, so large expert banks become more practical.

## Hardware opportunity: add-only matrix engines

A key architectural insight from BitNet b1.58 is that matrix multiplication with ternary weights can be implemented largely as integer additions (and sign flips), not FP multiplies. That opens the door for simpler, much more energy-efficient compute primitives:

- Add-dominated MAC units: Instead of full multiply–accumulate (MAC) datapaths, accelerators could implement specialized add-and-accumulate units where multiply is replaced by a sign selection and conditional accumulation. This simplifies datapath logic and reduces energy per operation.

- Bit-slice or ternary-aware datapaths: Ternary weights mean per-weight storage and read logic can be optimized—e.g., a weight read yields trit values \{-1,0,+1\} that directly control adds, subtracts, or zeroing. This reduces control complexity relative to arbitrary low-bit multipliers.

- Minimal multipliers on the critical path: For some parts of a model you still need higher-precision compute (embeddings, final layers), but the bulk of expert matrix work could be handled by add-only engines, with a small co-processor for the exceptions.

The paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 replaces costly FP16 multiply-adds with INT8 additions in practice and gets large throughput and energy wins as a result. For MoE, which concentrates compute in matrix multiplies inside experts, a specialized add-heavy engine aligns particularly well with workload characteristics.

## Memory hierarchies tuned for ternary weights

Ternary weights change the tradeoffs between on-chip SRAM, off-chip DRAM, and persistent storage:

- Denser on-chip caches: Because each parameter is ~1.58 bits, more expert parameters can be cached in limited SRAM. That reduces DRAM traffic and latency for hot experts, letting single-chip deployments host many more usable experts without remote fetch.

- Reduced memory bandwidth needs: Lower bits per weight and smaller activations reduce the required DRAM-to-SRAM bandwidth. For MoE, bandwidth often becomes the bottleneck when routing many batches through different experts; reducing per-expert bandwidth requirement relaxes that bottleneck.

- Different compression/packing: The ternary alphabet is natively compressible—specialized packing and addressing schemes can tightly store weights and enable fast scatter/gather of expert parameters. The paper uses an absmean quantization and per-matrix scaling (\gamma), which suggests an organization where a small scaling factor and a packed ternary matrix are loaded together. Hardware that exposes a low-latency path for reading both the scale and the packed ternary block will be efficient.

- KV cache and longer context: The authors note that halving activation bitwidth lets you double context for a given KV cache budget. For MoE models that benefit from long context, smaller activation representations help keep the whole working set on-chip or within the node, further reducing cross-node traffic.

## Putting it together: a plausible deployment picture

Combining MoE with 1.58-bit weights and custom hardware yields several practical outcomes:

- High-expert-density chips: A single chip can store and serve many more experts in on-chip or near-chip memory, making it possible to keep routing and most inference local and avoid high-latency interconnects.

- Lower per-token energy and latency: Sparse routing invokes a few experts, each processed via add-heavy engines and small activation footprints—this yields low end-to-end energy and higher throughput per chip.

- Easier scaling in datacenter and edge: On the datacenter side, reduced inter-node traffic simplifies network design and lowers cost for MoE. On edge devices, the smaller memory and add-friendly compute make deployment of large sparse models more realistic.

- Incremental co-design: The paper’s results (e.g., multi-fold memory/latency improvements and large arithmetic energy savings) argue for a co-design approach—algorithm (MoE + ternary weights), software (routing and compact kernels), and hardware (add-only engines and ternary-friendly caches) optimized together.

## Caveats and open questions

The paper demonstrates parity with FP16 baselines from about 3B parameter scale upward and reports system-level benefits across latency, memory, throughput and estimated energy. Still, there are design questions that need careful work for MoE deployments:

- Expert precision mix: Some components (embeddings, output layers) remain full precision in the reported setups. Hardware must accommodate mixed-precision efficiently.

- Routing overheads: MoE routing logic and potential load imbalance remain nontrivial. Reduced parameter sizes ease but do not eliminate system-level complexity.

- Kernel engineering: BitNet b1.58 already uses specialized 2-bit kernels; more optimized kernels for ternary weights and sparse expert scheduling will be required to realize the full hardware promise.

The paper itself flags future directions such as 1-bit MoE LLMs and new hardware specialized for the BitNet paradigm—both are sensible next steps.

## Takeaway

MoE and 1.58-bit LLMs are complementary. Sparse activation from MoE multiplies the system and hardware benefits of ternary weights: fewer devices to host experts, less interconnect traffic, and much cheaper arithmetic per expert. At the same time, ternary-weight models invite new hardware designs—add-only matrix engines, ternary-aware memory layouts, and KV/activation caches optimized for smaller bitwidths—that can make large-scale MoE models far more practical and energy-efficient. The paper’s empirical gains (memory/latency/throughput improvements and large arithmetic energy savings) make a clear argument for pursuing this joint direction: algorithm and hardware co-design for an efficient 1-bit future.
