# Speed, Memory, Energy: How BitNet b1.58 Rewrites the Efficiency Playbook

Large language models keep getting bigger—and so do their compute, memory, and energy bills. BitNet b1.58 is a clean, architecture-level answer: train the model with ternary weights \{-1, 0, +1\} (1.58 bits per weight) and 8-bit activations, and you get large, real-world wins in inference speed, memory footprint, and arithmetic energy, without losing model quality at scale.

Below I walk through the headline numbers, why they arise (with a simple analogy for what “matrix multiplication with additions only” really means), and why the efficiency advantage grows as models scale up.

## The headline wins

All numbers below come from the BitNet b1.58 experiments (trained on RedPajama for 100B tokens) and comparisons against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline:

- Inference speed: 2.4× to 4.1× faster versus FP16 LLaMA (examples: 2.4× at 3.9B, 4.1× at 70B).
- GPU memory: roughly 3.3× to 3.6× lower GPU memory use (3.32× at 3.9B; 3.55× at 3B).
- Arithmetic energy for matrix multiply: up to 71.4× reduction (energy model estimate for matrix multiply on 7nm chips).
- Throughput at 70B: up to 8.9× higher throughput, supporting up to 11× larger batch sizes in the experiment.
- Quality: matches FP16 LLaMA perplexity and end-task zero-shot performance starting around 3B model size (i.e., no accuracy sacrifice at that scale).

These are not just microbenchmarks—the gains come from replacing the model’s dominant FLOP-heavy layers with a fundamentally cheaper arithmetic pattern and reducing weight bandwidth and storage.

## What BitNet b1.58 actually is (short)

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer that replaces standard linear layers (nn.Linear) with BitLinear, trains from scratch with ternary weights constrained to \{-1, 0, +1\}, and uses 8-bit activations. The ternary quantization uses an “absmean” scaler:

- Scale each weight matrix by its per-matrix mean absolute value
- Round and clip to \{-1, 0, +1\}

Formally:
- \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\)
- \(\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right)\)

The model keeps common LLaMA-style components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) so it fits standard LLM stacks with minimal friction.

## Why the efficiency gains happen (an analogy)

Think of a matrix multiply Y = XW. With FP16 weights and activations, each output element is a sum of many products: multiply then add again and again.

Analogy: imagine baking a layered cake where every layer requires mixing flour and sugar precisely (multiplication) and then folding (addition). Multiplication is the expensive, careful step.

BitNet b1.58 turns many of those “mix precisely” steps into much cheaper operations. With weights in \{-1, 0, +1\}, each multiply is either:
- multiply by +1: copy the activation,
- multiply by -1: copy and negate,
- multiply by 0: skip.

So instead of costly multiplies, the core work becomes additions, sign flips, and skipping. In the cake analogy: you mostly copy pre-made layers, flip them if needed, or skip adding a layer at all—far cheaper than re-mixing ingredients for each layer.

Two practical consequences:
- Arithmetic: replace FP16 multiply-adds with integer additions / sign operations and skips. Integer adds are cheaper and often have lower energy per operation.
- Memory bandwidth: weights are ~1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, so moving weights from DRAM to on-chip memory is much cheaper and faster.

## Why larger models amplify the benefit

Two reasons make gains grow as models scale:

1. nn.Linear dominates cost as model size increases. Matrix multiplies account for a bigger fraction of work in larger models, so making them cheaper gives a larger overall win. The experiments show larger speedups and energy savings at 70B than at smaller sizes.

2. The relative cost of the embedding and other full-precision components shrinks with model size, so the ternary-weighted linear layers' efficiency has a larger impact on total latency, memory, and energy.

Consequence: BitNet b1.58 shows bigger speedups (up to 4.1×) and improved memory ratio for 70B versus FP16 baselines than for smaller sizes.

## Energy: where the 71.4× number comes from

The paper focuses the energy comparison on the matrix multiplication arithmetic, which dominates LLM compute. Using an established energy model, BitNet b1.58 reduces the arithmetic energy for matrix multiply by about 71.4× on a 7nm chip, because:
- FP16 baseline performs FP16 multiply + add for many terms.
- BitNet b1.58 replaces most of those with INT8 additions and sign operations, which are much cheaper per operation.

Note: the 71.4× figure is for arithmetic energy of matrix multiply (the dominant term) under the paper’s energy model—end-to-end energy savings are still large and increase with model size because matrix multiply becomes a larger share of total cost.

## Memory, latency, and throughput in practice

The experiments measured runtime memory and tokens-per-second latency using FasterTransformer and an integrated 2-bit kernel for BitNet b1.58. Key takeaways:

- Memory: BitNet b1.58 uses roughly a third of the GPU memory of FP16 LLaMA on comparable configurations (e.g., 3.32–3.55× reduction in the reported comparisons).
- Latency: Time per output token is 2.4×–4.1× faster depending on model size.
- Throughput: On 2×80GB A100s with pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 70B could sustain up to 11× the batch size of FP16 LLaMA 70B in the experiment, yielding about 8.9× higher throughput.

These gains come from both reduced compute per token and the lower cost of loading and keeping weights on-chip.

## No quality compromise (from 3B upwards)

A critical point is that BitNet b1.58 matches the FP16 LLaMA perplexity and zero-shot performance starting around the 3B model size when trained on the same data. The experiments show the performance gap shrinks with model size, and by 3B the ternary model attains parity with the FP16 baseline on both perplexity and downstream zero-shot tasks.

This means you can pick much faster, smaller, and less power-hungry models without paying a price in model quality (for the reported scales).

## Where this helps most and what's next

BitNet b1.58’s combination of lower memory, latency, and arithmetic energy makes it attractive for:

- Large-scale inference where throughput and cost matter (data centers).
- Long-context models: with smaller activation precision and weight size, KV cache memory pressure is reduced, enabling longer context for a given resource budget.
- MoE and multi-expert designs: lower per-device memory could reduce device count and inter-chip communication overhead.
- Edge and mobile: the reduced memory and arithmetic cost bring large models closer to practical deployment on constrained hardware.
- Hardware co-design: the computation pattern (integer additions, sign flips, skips) invites specialized accelerators optimized for ternary-weight LLMs.

## Bottom line

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that a well-designed ternary-weight LLM trained from scratch can be a practical, high-quality alternative to FP16 LLMs at scale. The wins are tangible: 2.4–4.1× inference speedups, roughly 3.3–3.6× lower GPU memory for comparable models, up to 71.4× arithmetic energy reduction for matrix multiplies, and large throughput gains in multi-GPU runs—while matching FP16 performance starting at 3B.

If you care about serving LLMs faster, cheaper, and cooler (literally), ternary-weight models like BitNet b1.58 are worth watching—and they make a strong case for software and hardware co-design around 1–2 bit class LLMs.
