# From Quantization to Foundation: Training 1.58-bit Models From Scratch

Large models have pushed quantization from a niche engineering trick to a first-class design choice. Most production systems have relied on post-training quantization (PTQ) to reduce memory and inference cost: train in 16-bit (FP16/BF16), then quantize weights and/or activations for serving. BitNet b1.58 takes a different path: it designs and trains the model from scratch with ternary weights \{-1, 0, +1\} (what the authors call "1.58-bit" weights) and 8-bit activations. The result is a Transformer that matches FP16 baselines in perplexity and zero-shot tasks (starting at 3B parameters), while delivering large wins in latency, memory and energy.

Below I walk through the key ideas that make this possible, explain the simple but effective "absmean rounding" used to get ternary weights, clarify the activation scaling choice (no zero-point), and summarize the main empirical trade-offs.

## Why not just post-training quantize?

PTQ is attractive because it avoids the costly step of re-training. However, PTQ also has inherent limits:

- Quantizing an already-trained FP16 model often forces representational compromises that are hard to undo without fine-tuning. Errors introduced by quantization are baked into downstream behaviour.
- PTQ solutions typically focus on inference kernels and calibration heuristics; they do not change the model architecture or training dynamics to be aware of very-low-bit weight formats.
- For extreme reductions (e.g., moving to 1–2 bits), PTQ becomes increasingly brittle and sub-optimal.

BitNet b1.58 embraces a different trade: design the training recipe and architecture to work natively with low-bit weights. That lets the model learn robust representations in the constrained space, avoiding many of the PTQ pitfalls.

## What is BitNet b1.58 (in a sentence)

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer that replaces dense linear layers with BitLinear: weights are ternary \{-1, 0, +1\} (1.58 bits per parameter) and activations are 8-bit. It uses a LLaMA-like backbone (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and is trained from scratch on the same data and compute budgets used for FP16 baselines.

Two practical advantages of adding the zero state (0) to the original 1-bit idea are emphasized: (1) explicit feature filtering (sparsity) and (2) easier optimization / better modeling capacity than pure \{-1,+1\} representations.

## The absmean rounding trick — simple, stable, effective

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights with a per-matrix scaling followed by clipping-and-rounding. Concretely, for a weight matrix \(W\) of size \(n\times m\) they compute a scalar scale

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|,
\]

then quantize as

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]

where \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \operatorname{round}(x)))\) and \(\epsilon\) is a tiny stabilizer.

Why this works and why it is appealing:

- The scale \(\gamma\) is just the average absolute weight magnitude. It is robust, easy to compute, and avoids the instability of per-row or per-channel learned scales in extremely low-bit regimes.
- Dividing by \(\gamma\) roughly normalizes the dynamic range so typical weight values land near \{-1,0,1\} after rounding rather than being crushed into a single bin.
- The addition of the 0 bin (ternary rather than binary) allows the quantizer to effectively zero-out small-magnitude weights, giving the model the option to suppress features rather than force them into \(\pm 1\). That explicit feature filtering improves capacity compared to pure 1-bit \{-1,+1\} representations.
- Implementation-wise this is cheap (one scalar per weight matrix), stable, and interoperable with common training pipelines.

The upshot: a very small, deterministic quantization operation that the model can learn around during training, rather than a brittle post-hoc mapping.

## 8-bit activations without zero-point per token — cleaner and practical

For activations, BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit representations but avoids a per-tensor or per-channel zero-point quantization. Instead, activations are scaled per token to lie in \([-Q_b, Q_b]\). In other words, instead of mapping activations to \([0, Q_b]\) plus a zero-point, the activations are symmetric and token-wise normalized.

Why that matters:

- Removing the zero-point simplifies hardware and kernel implementations: symmetric quantization reduces bookkeeping and conditional offsets.
- Per-token scaling handles dynamic ranges in activation patterns across the sequence, which is especially relevant for autoregressive decoding where token statistics vary.
- According to the authors' experiments, this choice makes implementation and system-level optimization simpler while having negligible negative effect on accuracy.

Combined with ternary weights, these 8-bit symmetric activations are enough to preserve model quality when training from scratch.

## Training-from-scratch vs PTQ: why the gap closes

When the optimizer and gradients operate in the constrained weight space from the start, the model learns representations compatible with ternary weights and 8-bit activations. That avoids two major PTQ failure modes:

- PTQ must retrofit a low-bit representation onto learned features that may be sensitive to fine-grained weight values. Training-aware quantization lets the model reallocate capacity so critical computations survive quantization.
- Training-time regularization, optimizer dynamics, and architecture choices (e.g., removing biases, using RMSNorm and SwiGLU) can be tuned jointly for low-bit weights, producing a model that naturally lives in the constrained parameter manifold.

The BitNet b1.58 experiments show this pays off: starting at 3B parameters, models trained from scratch in 1.58-bit match FP16 baselines in perplexity and end-task zero-shot performance.

## Empirical highlights (what to expect)

The paper reports a range of measurements comparing BitNet b1.58 to reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style models trained on the same RedPajama data for 100B tokens. Key observations include:

- Parity in quality: BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision baseline (FP16) in perplexity and downstream zero-shot tasks from 3B parameters and up.
- 3B regime efficiency: at 3B, BitNet b1.58 is about 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA baseline.
- Better-for-less: a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster, uses 3.32× less memory, and performs significantly better than a 3B FP16 model.
- At large scale the benefits grow: a 70B BitNet b1.58 model runs about 4.1× faster than a 70B FP16 LLaMA baseline (measured on optimized kernels), and throughput comparisons show an 8.9× higher throughput for 70B BitNet b1.58 in a two-A100 setup because the ternary format lets it support much larger batch sizes.
- Energy savings: focusing on matrix multiplications (the dominant cost), the arithmetic energy for BitNet b1.58 is estimated to be 71.4× lower than FP16 matmuls on 7nm chips, since most work reduces to INT8 additions instead of FP16 multiplies+adds.

Those are substantial system-level wins coming from a fairly small, practical change: ternary weights via absmean rounding and 8-bit symmetric activations.

## Practical implications and where this fits

BitNet b1.58 suggests that extreme quantization need not be a last-mile optimization; it can be a core design point. Running models natively in a 1.58-bit regime unlocks:

- Much lower memory footprint and memory bandwidth for weights, which reduces DRAM transfers and enables larger models on the same hardware.
- Lower arithmetic energy and simpler compute primitives (mostly integer adds) that are more hardware-friendly.
- Better support for large batches, long context lengths (smaller KV caches), and even potential for deploying MoE-style models with reduced communication overhead.

The authors also point to future avenues: specialized hardware for 1-bit-style LLMs, additional compression of activations (potentially to 4 bits), and MoE variants that become more practical when weights and activations are tiny.

## Takeaway

BitNet b1.58 shows that training Transformers from scratch with ternary weights and 8-bit activations is a viable, practical path to close the gap with FP16 models while dramatically reducing inference cost. The approach is technically simple—one-scalar per-matrix absmean scaling plus round-and-clip—and system-friendly (symmetric per-token activation scaling). For practitioners this means extreme quantization can be part of the model design rather than an afterthought, and for hardware designers it highlights a promising computation paradigm built around integer additions and tiny weight representations.
