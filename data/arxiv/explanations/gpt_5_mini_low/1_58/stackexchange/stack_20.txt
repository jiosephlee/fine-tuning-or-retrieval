\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Fairness of comparisons: are tokenizers, data deduplication, and evaluation prompts identical?
Question:
For the FP16 LLaMA-like baseline vs b1.58, were tokenizers identical, training data deduplication and filtering identical, and evaluation prompt templates exactly the same (e.g., lm-evaluation-harness settings)? Small differences can shift zero-shot accuracies, so a detailed comparison protocol would help interpret the reported parity and improvements.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper says they used the same pretraining corpus (RedPajama) and “followed the pipeline from lm-evaluation-harness” for evaluation, but it does not explicitly confirm that the FP16 LLaMA-like baseline and BitNet b1.58 used identical tokenizers, identical deduplication/filtering of training data, or absolutely identical evaluation prompt templates. Because small differences in those pieces can shift zero-shot accuracies, the omission means the claim of parity should be interpreted with caution until the authors publish a detailed comparison protocol or the exact artifacts.

What the paper does state (and what that implies)
The paper explicitly states they pre-trained both models “on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens” and that they “reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM” as a baseline. For evaluation they say they “followed the pipeline from lm-evaluation-harness.” Those statements indicate an effort to keep the comparison close and to use a standard evaluation harness, which is good practice and reduces many sources of discrepancy.

What the paper does not state (and why it matters)
The paper does not explicitly state that the tokenizer, tokenization hyperparameters (vocabulary file, normalization, byte-pair merge/training seed, etc.), deduplication and filtering steps applied to the training data, or the exact prompt templates / harness configuration (lm-evaluation-harness version, per-task prompt variants, normalization, truncation, random seeds, temperature, etc.) were identical between the two runs.

These items matter because small differences can change zero-shot numbers by a non-trivial amount. For example, using a different tokenizer vocabulary or normalization can alter tokenization of rare tokens and thus model behavior; differing deduplication/filters can alter effective training distribution and memorization; different harness settings or prompt templates (even small punctuation/whitespace changes or whether a trailing newline is present) can shift reported accuracies.

Practical checklist to establish a fair, reproducible comparison
If you want to be sure the comparison really isolates quantization architecture vs FP baseline, request or check the following from the authors (or provide them when reproducing):

- Exact tokenizer files for both models (vocab/merges or tokenizer.json), tokenizer library and version, normalization / pre-tokenization steps.
- Explicit statement that the tokenizer was identical for both models, or the exact differences.
- Full data recipe: which RedPajama snapshot, exact dataset shards used, order of shards, any preseeding, plus the deduplication/filters applied (tool and parameters) and whether those were identical for both runs.
- Training hyperparameters and random seeds that affect tokenization-dependent preprocessing (e.g., data shuffling).
- Exact evaluation harness version (commit hash) and the command-line/config used. For lm-evaluation-harness, that includes the task list, prompt template variant (some tasks have multiple templates), normalization (e.g., string matching vs argmax), and whether few-shot examples were included.
- All generation/evaluation settings: decoding algorithm, temperature, top-k/top-p, max tokens, handling of multiple-choice tasks (greedy argmax vs logprob normalization).
- Any post-processing used to convert model outputs to answers (e.g., mapping generated text to choice labels).

Why you should ask for those and what to expect
Given the paper’s claim that BitNet b1.58 “matches” FP16 starting at 3B, the burden is on the authors to show that the only substantive difference is the weight representation and system-level speed/memory tradeoffs. Asking for the items above is reasonable and common in reproducibility requests. If they provide the artifacts and they are identical, that strengthens the parity claim; if there are small differences, those need quantifying (e.g., run ablations where only the tokenizer or only the dedup step differs).

If you’re reproducing the experiments yourself, use the exact tokenizer, dataset snapshot and dedup script, and run the identical lm-evaluation-harness commit and configs. If the paper authors publish a model and eval scripts, that will let you validate whether the zero-shot parity holds under identical preprocessing/eval.

Bottom line
The paper documents shared dataset and the use of a standard evaluation pipeline, but does not explicitly certify that tokenizer, deduplication/filtering, and prompt templates were exactly the same between runs. Those are plausible sources of small but non-negligible shifts in zero-shot accuracy, so request the concrete artifacts/configs from the authors (tokenizer files, data recipe and dedup params, exact lm-eval-harness commit and prompt templates) to fully trust the parity claim.
