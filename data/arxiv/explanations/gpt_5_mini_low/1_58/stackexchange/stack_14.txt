\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is meant by a “new scaling law” for 1.58-bit LLMs?
Question:
The paper states BitNet b1.58 defines a new scaling law relating model performance and inference cost. Is this an empirical Pareto frontier (accuracy vs latency/memory/energy) or a formal relation akin to Chinchilla/compute-optimal laws (loss vs parameters vs tokens)? If formal, can you state the functional form and data used to fit it? If empirical, can you share the curve and methodology for the model-size equivalences (13B b1.58 ≈ 3B FP16, etc.)?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper is describing an empirical “scaling law” (a practical Pareto-style relation between model performance and inference cost), not a new analytic Chinchilla-style loss vs. parameters vs. tokens law. The authors do not present a closed-form functional equation; instead they report measured performance (perplexity / zero-shot accuracy) together with measured or estimated inference costs (latency, GPU memory, energy, throughput) across a handful of discrete model sizes, and then state equivalences (e.g., “$13\mathrm{B}\ \mathrm{b}1.58$ is more efficient than $3\mathrm{B}\ \mathrm{FP16}$”) based on those measurements and plots.

Why this is empirical and not formal: the paper’s experiments section describes training BitNet $\mathrm{b}1.58$ and FP16 LLaMA-alike baselines at specific sizes ($3\mathrm{B}, 3.9\mathrm{B}, 7\mathrm{B}, 13\mathrm{B}, 30\mathrm{B}, 70\mathrm{B}$; plus a $2\mathrm{T}$-token experiment for a $3\mathrm{B}$ run). Performance is reported as perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy; inference costs are measured with FasterTransformer (latency/time-per-token), GPU memory observed at runtime, throughput via batch scaling, and energy estimated with an energy model cited from prior work. The “scaling law” language is used to summarize the empirical trend that as model size grows, BitNet’s advantage in latency/memory/energy grows—so larger $1.58$-bit models give you FP16-equivalent or better accuracy while being much cheaper in inference cost. There is no derivation of a parametric formula like the Chinchilla-style power-law loss $\sim f(N, D)$ in the paper.

How the equivalences were obtained (methodology you can reproduce)
1. Train comparable BitNet $\mathrm{b}1.58$ and FP16 models on the same data recipe (the paper uses RedPajama, $100\mathrm{B}$ tokens for the main runs; a $2\mathrm{T}$-token run is reported separately). Use identical architecture/hyperparameter choices except for quantization/BitLinear changes so accuracy comparisons are fair.
2. Measure model quality (validation perplexity and zero-shot accuracies on the listed benchmarks).
3. Measure inference cost per model: time per output token (latency) and runtime GPU memory using an optimized runtime (they used FasterTransformer with a 2-bit kernel), throughput by increasing batch size until memory limits, and energy using an arithmetic-energy model cited in the paper.
4. Plot cost (latency, memory, energy) vs. model size and also performance vs. model size. The “equivalence” statements come from reading those plots/tables and identifying where a BitNet model achieves either (a) comparable performance to an FP16 model at a smaller size while using less cost, or (b) comparable or better performance at strictly lower inference cost. For example, the paper reports that $13\mathrm{B}\ \mathrm{b}1.58$ has lower latency/memory/energy than a $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLaMA while matching or exceeding its perplexity/accuracy—hence the statement “$13\mathrm{B}\ \mathrm{b}1.58 \approx 3\mathrm{B}\ \mathrm{FP16}$” in a cost-performance sense.

What the paper does and does not provide
- Provides: empirical measurements (tables and figures) of perplexity, zero-shot accuracies, latency, GPU memory, throughput, and estimated energy for discrete model sizes; statements of pairwise equivalences based on those numbers; description of measurement setup (FasterTransformer, 2-bit kernel, energy model citations) and training data/tokens.
- Does not provide: a closed-form functional law (e.g., an analytic power law with fitted exponents like Chinchilla), a parametric formula mapping (parameters, tokens) to perplexity for $1.58$-bit models, or raw numeric tables for every plotted point in the paper text. The “scaling law” is therefore best interpreted as an empirical cost-vs-performance frontier observed in their experiments.

If you want a precise curve or to reproduce the equivalence table, the practical next step is to request or extract the underlying numeric measurements (time-per-token, peak-memory, energy estimate, and performance metrics) for each model size. With those you can (a) plot performance vs. each cost metric and draw the empirical Pareto frontier, or (b) fit a simple interpolating curve (e.g., power-law or log-linear) to the measured points if you want a compact functional approximation for planning/hardware-provisioning purposes. If helpful, I can outline a short script to take measured numbers and (i) plot Pareto frontiers and (ii) compute the “equivalent FP16 size” of a given BitNet size under a chosen criterion (same accuracy, same latency, or same energy).
