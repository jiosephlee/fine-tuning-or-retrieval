\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Are all Transformer parameters ternary, or are some layers left in higher precision?
Question:
The paper says "every parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary" but elsewhere notes that the embedding "remains full precision" in scaling analyses. Which components are ternary versus higher precision at inference: token embeddings, positional/rotary embedding parameters, attention projection matrices (Q/K/V/O), MLP (SwiGLU) matrices, layer norm/RMSNorm parameters, and output softmax head? If some remain FP16/BF16, how does that affect the reported memory and speed numbers?
Answer:
Short answer: in the paper the bulk of model weights (all linear layers used in the Transformer) are ternary, while a few small parameter groups are treated in higher precision in the measurements. Concretely, attention projection matrices (Q/K/V/O), the MLP (SwiGLU) weight matrices, and the output (softmax) projection are trained/represented as ternary weights in BitNet b$1.58$. Activations are $8$-bit. By the paper's statement about memory proportions, token embeddings (and, in practice, small normalization or embedding-like parameters) are kept in full precision for the reported memory/latency numbers.

Why this is the case and what it changes for the reported results
The paper emphasizes “every parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary” as the design goal of BitNet b$1.58$, and the model is built by replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear and training from scratch with ternary weights. That wording refers to the model’s main weight matrices: attention and feed‑forward (MLP) matrices and the output projection. However, when reporting runtime memory and latency the authors explicitly call out that “the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models.” That tells us that, in their runtime/counts, token embeddings were left as $ \mathrm{FP16}/\mathrm{BF16}$ (full precision) while the main linear layers used the $1.58$-bit ternary representation (and the inference kernels used a $2$-bit implementation from Ladder for execution). RMSNorm and similar tiny parameter vectors (scale / norm statistics) are extremely small, and the paper treats them as part of the non-dominant $ \mathrm{FP16}$ component in the memory breakdown; practically they remain negligible in cost whether $ \mathrm{FP16}$ or quantized.

Practical consequences on memory, latency and energy numbers
Because embeddings can be large for small models (and proportionally smaller for very large models), leaving them $ \mathrm{FP16}$ makes the reported memory savings slightly conservative compared to a hypothetical fully-ternarized model including embeddings. For example, the paper reports that BitNet b$1.58$ is $3.55\times$ lower GPU memory at $3$B and that this improvement increases with model size; that trend is explained by the fixed $ \mathrm{FP16}$ cost of embeddings becoming a smaller fraction of total parameters for bigger models. Similarly, latency/speed numbers reflect two things: (1) compute kernels for matrix multiplies are run on low-bit ($2$-bit/ternary) kernels for the bulk of nn.Linear ops, giving large speedups; (2) any remaining $ \mathrm{FP16}$ copies (embeddings, tiny norm params) must be moved/used in $ \mathrm{FP16}$, so they slightly reduce the theoretical maximum speedup. The authors measured with a $2$-bit kernel for the ternary weights and left embeddings $ \mathrm{FP16}$, so reported speed/memory/energy are the real measured end-to-end numbers under that mixed-precision runtime.

Intuition and broader context
Embeddings sit at the interface between discrete tokens and continuous networks and are relatively easy to keep full precision: doing so avoids some engineering complications (e.g., token-table lookups with very low bitwidth and preserving representation capacity for rare tokens). But matrix multiplies (attention and MLP) are the dominant FLOPs and bandwidth consumers; quantizing those yields the big wins in latency, energy and memory. Thus the pragmatic mixed choice (ternary for the big linear layers, $ \mathrm{FP16}$ for embeddings and tiny normative params) gives most of the system benefit while keeping a few convenient components in higher precision. For very small models the embedding share is larger, so fully-ternary embeddings would yield noticeably higher relative memory savings; for large models the embedding $ \mathrm{FP16}$ overhead is marginal.

Short practical takeaway
- Ternary in the paper = attention projection matrices, MLP matrices, and output projection are ternary (BitLinear). Activations are $8$-bit.  
- Token embeddings (and effectively small norm/gain vectors) were left full precision in the reported measurements.  
- The reported memory/latency/energy numbers were measured with this mixed-precision setup (ternary bulk weights + $ \mathrm{FP16}$ embeddings), so they are slightly conservative compared to an all-ternary-weights measurement; the difference matters most for smaller models where embeddings are a larger fraction of total memory.
