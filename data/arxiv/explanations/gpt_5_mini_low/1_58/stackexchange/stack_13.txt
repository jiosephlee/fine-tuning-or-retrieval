\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does BitNet b1.58 match FP16 perplexity only from 3B and up?
Question:
Results suggest parity begins at around 3B parameters. Is there a known capacity or expressivity threshold for ternary-weight Transformers that causes underperformance at smaller scales? Did you attempt architectural tweaks (e.g., wider MLPs, more heads) at smaller sizes to recover performance, or is the gap mostly due to quantization noise being amortized at scale?
Answer:
Short answer: There is no mysterious single “ternary expressivity threshold” proved in the paper; the most plausible explanation the authors give (and that matches known quantization/overparameterization principles) is that quantization noise and representational loss from ternary weights are amortized only once the model is large enough and sufficiently redundant. The paper reports that with the same LLaMA-like configuration, training recipe and $100\ \text{B}$ tokens, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 starting at $\approx 3\ \text{B}$ parameters; they do not report architecture-level redesigns (wider MLPs, more heads, etc.) to recover smaller-scale performance.

Why this happens (intuitively and technically)
- Quantization noise vs. model capacity. Ternary weights restrict each parameter to $\{-1,0,1\}$. For small models that are close to the minimum capacity needed for the task, that restriction removes degrees of freedom that were critical for representing fine-grained feature combinations. Larger models are overparameterized; they have redundancy so that many parameter configurations implement similar functions. That redundancy lets them “absorb” the coarse-grained quantization without as much hurt to loss or downstream metrics.
- Signal-to-quantization ratio improves with scale. As models grow, per-weight magnitudes and aggregated contributions to activations concentrate (by concentration-of-measure / law-of-large-numbers intuition). The scaling/absmean quantizer used in BitNet b1.58 (paper: divide by $\gamma = \text{mean}\,|W|$ before rounding) tends to preserve the dominant directions in large matrices better than in small ones, so quantization error becomes relatively smaller in larger layers.
- Effective expressivity: the ternary constraint is a hard nonlinearity on parameter space. Small networks have less capacity to reconfigure around that constraint, while large networks can reallocate representational burden (wider MLPs, more heads, or deeper layers implicitly) so the net function remains close to the FP16 baseline.
- The paper also keeps some elements full/higher precision (e.g., embeddings are full precision, activations are $8$-bit). Those design choices interact with size: the relative contribution of the full-precision parts is larger in small models, so the fractional effect of quantizing the linear layers is different by scale.
- Empirical scaling-law behavior. The authors claim a new scaling relationship where b1.58 becomes increasingly efficient and closer in performance to FP16 as size grows; this is consistent with other findings that aggressive quantization or pruning harms small models far more than large, overparameterized ones.

What the paper did and did not try
The paper trains BitNet b1.58 from scratch with LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) and the absmean ternary quantizer, and compares models trained with the same config and token budget. It reports that parity with FP16 appears at $\approx 3\ \text{B}$, and that the gap narrows as size increases ($3.9\ \text{B}$ b1.58 even outperforms LLaMA $3\ \text{B}$). The paper does not describe experiments that specifically change micro-architecture at small sizes (e.g., increasing MLP width, increasing attention heads, or layerwise mixed precision) to close the small-model gap. So there is no reported evidence in this work that architectural tweaks were (or were not) effective at small scales.

Practical hypotheses and simple experiments you could try
If you want to probe whether the deficit is strictly capacity vs. an artifact of the quantizer/recipe, try these controlled experiments: (1) keep depth fixed but expand MLP hidden width for a $1$–$3\ \text{B}$ model (see if extra flop-budget recovers FP16 performance), (2) allow a small fraction of layers or parameters to remain higher precision (per-channel mixed precision) to localize critical capacity, (3) try per-row or per-channel learned scales instead of global abs-mean scaling, and (4) increase training tokens or apply distillation from the FP16 teacher. If gains appear from (1) or (2), that supports a capacity/expressivity explanation; if not, it suggests the quantizer or optimization dynamics at small scale are the main issue.

Broader connection
This phenomenon sits at the intersection of quantization theory and the modern overparameterization view: model robustness to aggressive compression (quantization/pruning) grows with size because large networks have many near-equivalent parameterizations. Similar effects have been observed for pruning and low-bit quantization in CNNs and Transformers: compression hurts small networks strongly, but large networks degrade gracefully.

Summary
The paper attributes parity beginning around $3\ \text{B}$ to the ability of larger models to accommodate the ternary constraint under the same training recipe; it does not report architectural interventions to rescue smaller models. The likely causes are quantization-induced information loss being cheaper to hide in overparameterized models and the specifics of the absmean ternary quantizer interacting more favorably with large-layer statistics. If you need to recover small-scale performance, try increasing effective capacity (wider MLPs), per-channel/learned scaling, hybrid precision, or teacher distillation—these are standard remedies consistent with the paper’s setup.
