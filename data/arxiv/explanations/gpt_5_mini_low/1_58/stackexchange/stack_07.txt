\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Clarifying activation quantization: per-token scaling to [−Q_b, Q_b] without zero-point
Question:
The paper states activations are 8-bit and scaled per token to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), removing zero-point. 1) How is the per-token scale determined (max-abs, EMA, percentile)? 2) Is scaling applied before or after attention softmax, and how is numerical stability ensured for attention scores and softmax? 3) Does per-token scaling break any invariances or cause sequence-position-dependent artifacts compared to per-channel or per-tensor schemes? 4) Why was the previous BitNet activation scaling to \([0, Q_b]\) dropped—did it hurt performance or complicate kernels?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not give a single hard formula, but the design and wording make clear the practical choices and trade‑offs. The activations are quantized per token into a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (no zero point) for implementation simplicity and negligible measured loss. In practice that implies using a per‑token scalar (most commonly max‑abs or a robust variant) applied to the token's activation vector; the symmetric scale is applied to the activations (so that quantized $Q/K/V$/hidden activations are centered about zero); numerical stability for softmax is preserved by computing attention logits/softmax in higher precision (or by dequantizing before softmax) and by keeping relative magnitudes through the chosen scaling; and per‑token scaling does not meaningfully harm model invariances in this architecture because components like RMSNorm and the linear layers absorb or cancel a per‑token scalar, which matches the paper's observation of negligible effect. Finally, the old BitNet choice to scale to $[0, Q_b]$ was dropped because a symmetric, zero‑centered range removes the zero‑point complexity and simplifies kernels and system integration—again with negligible performance impact.

Expanded explanation and technical grounding from the paper

1) How is the per‑token scale determined?
The paper only states that activations are “scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token” and does not give an explicit estimator. In quantization practice there are three common, reasonable choices:

- Max‑absolute per token: choose scale = $\max_i |x_i|$ for the token so the largest magnitude maps to $Q_b$. This is simple and exactly enforces the dynamic range.
- Robust (percentile) clipping: choose a high percentile (e.g. $99.9$th) of $|x|$ across the token to reduce outlier sensitivity, then clip and scale. This reduces quantization noise when outliers exist.
- EMA or running statistics: keep a running estimate of typical token magnitude (useful in streaming or pipelined systems), then use that per token (or per microbatch) to be stable across steps.

Which one the authors used is not specified; the paper emphasizes simplicity and system convenience, so max‑abs or a simple percentile/clipping scheme is the most likely. Any of these yields a single scalar per token that maps the token’s continuous activation vector into the symmetric $8$‑bit range.

2) Is scaling applied before/after attention softmax, and how is softmax stability kept?
The paper phrases scaling as “the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token,” i.e. the activation tensors used throughout inference are quantized in this symmetric range. In a Transformer the relevant places are: (a) the hidden activations that feed the linear layers which produce queries/keys/values, and (b) the attention score computation $\text{softmax}(QK^{T} / \sqrt{d})$. Two practical points follow and are standard in quantized LLM implementations:

- The quantized activations (after per‑token scaling) are used as inputs to the int8/low‑bit linear kernels, but attention logits (the $QK^{T}$) and the softmax are typically computed with higher precision accumulation. That is, $Q$ and $K$ may be represented in low precision for storage/compute but their dot products should be accumulated in $\mathrm{FP16}/\mathrm{FP32}$ (or dequantized to FP prior to dot product) so logits have enough dynamic range and precision for a stable softmax.
- Numerical stability of softmax is preserved by the usual logit shifting (subtract max) and by using higher precision for accumulation and exponentiation. If $Q/K$ are stored quantized, kernels either compute integer dot products with FP accumulation or first dequantize to FP before softmax. The paper integrates with standard optimized runtimes (FasterTransformer, Ladder kernels) which provide these numerically stable ops.

In short: the symmetric per‑token scaling is applied to activations as stored and passed through linear layers, but the softmax internals are handled in sufficiently high precision (or by dequantization) so stability is unchanged compared to FP16 implementations.

3) Does per‑token scaling break invariances or cause position‑dependent artifacts?
A per‑token scalar multiplies the entire token vector; mathematically this is equivalent to a scalar factor applied to all channels of that token. Two important observations make this benign in the BitNet b1.58 design:

- Normalization: BitNet b1.58 uses RMSNorm (a per‑token normalization) and other LLaMA‑alike components. RMSNorm normalizes a token’s vector magnitude, so a global scalar per token is largely removed by the normalization that follows (or is equivalently absorbed into learned affine parameters). Thus many layerwise invariances cancel a pure scalar multiplier.
- Linear layers: a global scalar on activations can be absorbed by the next linear layer’s weights (or simply scale the resulting activations), so it does not change the direction of token vectors, only their amplitude, which is often handled by downstream normalization and nonlinearity.

So per‑token scaling can introduce a token‑dependent amplitude, but because Transformer blocks include per‑token normalization and learned linear mappings, this does not produce systematic sequence‑position artifacts in practice. The paper explicitly reports “negligible effects to the performance in our experiments,” which supports that no measurable degrading artifacts were observed. That said, compared to per‑channel or per‑tensor schemes, per‑token scaling is a different inductive choice: per‑token is more flexible (adapts to token‑by‑token amplitude variation) while per‑channel/per‑tensor enforces consistent scaling across tokens. If you worry about subtle position‑dependent behavior you can validate with controlled ablations (e.g., check logits/attention patterns vs. FP16 or run synthetic tests), but the architecture choices (RMSNorm, removal of biases) were made to make per‑token scaling safe.

4) Why was the earlier BitNet scaling to $[0, Q_b]$ dropped?
The paper states that they “do not scale the activations before the non‑linear functions to the range $[0, Q_b]$. Instead, the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero‑point quantization. This is more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimization, while introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments.” Concretely, scaling to $[0,Q_b]$ requires a zero point (an offset) in integer representation, which complicates quantized kernels and memory layout; symmetric quantization around zero removes the need for a zero‑point and simplifies integer arithmetic (signed ints, symmetric clipping). Symmetric quantization also better matches activations centered on zero (post‑RMSNorm and common nonlinearities), avoids separately handling negative values, and makes bit‑wise/adder‑based kernels simpler and faster. The reason given in the paper is primarily engineering and system simplicity, not a large accuracy gain; they report negligible effect on performance while making implementation and system‑level optimization easier.

Summary
The paper chooses per‑token symmetric scaling primarily for engineering simplicity (zero‑point removal) and reports negligible impact on performance. The scale is a single scalar per token (likely max‑abs or a robust variant), softmax stability is preserved by performing logits/softmax in higher precision or with dequantization and standard numerically stable softmax tricks, and per‑token scaling is harmless in this architecture because normalization and linear layers absorb the scalar. If you need to reproduce or tweak the approach, start with per‑token max‑abs scaling and test percentile clipping or EMA for robustness; ensure attention dot‑products and softmax run with FP accumulation or dequantize $Q/K$ before softmax.
