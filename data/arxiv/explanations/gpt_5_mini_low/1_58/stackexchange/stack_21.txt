\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Does ternary quantization encourage structured sparsity that kernels can exploit?
Question:
With \{−1, 0, 1\} weights, zeros may appear frequently. Do your trained models exhibit significant block or channel-level sparsity that could be exploited by sparse kernels to further reduce compute/memory bandwidth? If so, what sparsity patterns and densities are observed across layers and model sizes?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not claim or report meaningful block- or channel-level sparsity that sparse kernels could exploit. The ternary design (weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$) produces many zeros, but those zeros are produced per-weight by a simple round-and-clip quantizer and are therefore largely unstructured by construction. The authors use dense 2-bit kernels (e.g., Ladder) for speed/memory gains rather than sparse-block kernels.

Why this is the case (intuitive + technical): BitNet b1.58 quantizes each weight matrix by scaling with the matrix mean absolute value
$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
$$
and then applying
$$
\widetilde W = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(\tfrac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\big).
$$
This is a per-matrix scalar thresholding and rounding operation: each weight that after scaling lies near 0 is mapped to 0, otherwise to $\pm 1$. That process creates sparsity at the individual-weight level (i.e., unstructured zeros where small weights are rounded to 0), but it does not include any mechanism that encourages contiguous blocks, entire channels/rows/columns, or structured groups of weights to go to zero. In other words, zeros are the byproduct of magnitude-based rounding, not the result of a structured pruning objective.

What the paper reports and what it does not: The paper emphasizes that adding the 0 value helps “feature filtering” and improves modeling capability, and it shows system-level gains by using low-bit representations and a 2-bit kernel; but it does not give layer-wise or block-wise zero rates, nor does it present statistics about channel/block sparsity patterns or densities across layers and model sizes. The reported runtime/memory/throughput/energy gains come from bit-width reduction and optimized dense low-bit kernels, not from exploiting structured sparsity.

Practical implications for sparse kernels: Because the zeros are created independently per weight by rounding, they will typically be randomly distributed across the matrix, which is difficult for sparse-block or channel-sparse kernels to exploit efficiently. Sparse inference engines gain most when sparsity is structured (e.g., entire rows/columns, blocks, or channels zeroed) so the compute/communication patterns can be simplified; unstructured sparsity usually requires irregular sparse kernels with lower hardware efficiency. Therefore, out-of-the-box BitNet b1.58 models are unlikely to yield large additional speedups from structured sparse kernels unless you explicitly train or fine-tune to induce structure.

If you want structured sparsity that kernels can exploit: use targeted methods during training or fine-tuning. Examples that are consistent with the paper’s setup would be (a) adding group-L1 or block-sparsity regularizers, (b) applying structured pruning (channel/row/column pruning) and then re-quantizing/finetuning in the ternary regime, or (c) using sparsity-aware distillation where student weights are encouraged to zero out whole channels/blocks. After such training you should report per-layer metrics such as per-channel zero-rate, block-zero-rate for chosen block sizes, and the distribution of nonzero counts to judge kernel applicability.

How to measure this for your models (recommended checks): compute for each weight matrix and each candidate block size (e.g., $4\times4$, $8\times8$) the fraction of blocks that are entirely zeros, the fraction of zeroed channels/rows/columns, and plot histogram of per-row/column sparsity. Also compare these statistics across layer types (attention projections vs. MLP weights) and model sizes (3B/7B/13B/70B) to see trends. If you find high whole-block or whole-channel zero rates, then sparse-block kernels may be worthwhile.

Conclusion: BitNet b1.58 does introduce many zeros because of ternary rounding, but the paper does not demonstrate structured sparsity exploitable by sparse kernels. The zeros are primarily unstructured per-weight zeros; to get hardware-friendly structured sparsity you would need to add structure-inducing training (pruning/regularization/distillation) and then measure the per-layer/block/channel sparsity statistics.
