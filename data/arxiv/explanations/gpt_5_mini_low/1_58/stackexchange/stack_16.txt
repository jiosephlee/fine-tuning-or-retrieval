\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is rotary embedding handled with 8-bit activations and ternary weights?
Question:
Rotary positional embeddings are multiplicative/rotational transforms on Q/K. Given 8-bit activations and ternary weights, are rotary operations performed in higher precision (e.g., FP16) before re-quantization, or directly in INT8? If done in FP16, does this materially affect the claim of near-multiplication-free compute? What precision is used for attention score computation and softmax?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not explicitly specify every operator’s micro-precision, but the only implementation consistent with the paper’s claims and with common practice is a mixed-precision pipeline: the rotary rotation and the softmax/attention-scaling are done in higher precision (FP16/BF16 or FP32 accumulators) on dequantized activations, while the bulk matrix multiplies (the expensive nn.Linear-like work) are performed with the ternary/INT8 compute kernels. Doing the few FP multiplies for rotary and softmax is negligible compared to the cost saved by replacing the dense weight multiplications with addition-based ternary/INT8 kernels, so it does not materially break the “near-multiplication-free” claim for the dominant cost.

Why this is the most plausible reading from the paper
The paper states weights are ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$ and activations are 8-bit, and it repeatedly attributes most of the energy/latency savings to converting the heavy matrix multiplications into INT8 additions (and specialized 2-bit kernels). It also names rotary embeddings, softmax, RMSNorm and attention as part of the LLaMA-like components it keeps. But it does not claim every scalar op (rotary, softmax, scaling) is executed in integer arithmetic. Practically, most quantized LLM implementations (and the FasterTransformer codebase the authors used) keep a small set of ops in floating (FP16/BF16 or FP32 accumulators) for numerical stability and simplicity while moving the large-cost GEMMs to the quantized kernel. That matches the paper’s energy breakdown: “The majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation,” i.e., the heavy FLOP reductions are in the big matmuls, not in tiny pointwise ops.

What typically happens to rotary in a quantized model
Rotary applies a small, elementwise linear rotation to $Q$ and $K$ (multiplication by sines/cosines or a $2\times2$ rotation). Two practical ways to implement this with 8-bit activations are (1) dequantize the 8-bit activation to FP16/BF16, apply the rotary (FP multiplies), then continue (re-quantize if needed) or (2) pre-scale the 8-bit representation and perform the small rotations in integer arithmetic (more complex, uncommon). The paper’s design choices (ease of integration with existing toolchains like FasterTransformer/llama.cpp and removing zero-point) favor option (1): perform rotary in FP16/BF16, then feed the rotated $Q$/$K$ to the attention path.

Why doing rotary in FP16 does not defeat the “near-multiplication-free” claim
Rotary and other pointwise ops are cheap relative to the cost of the big matrix multiplies. The paper’s performance and energy claims hinge on reducing the dominant cost (many-large-GEMMs) from FP16 multiply–adds to largely integer additions. A handful of FP16 multiplications per token position (the rotary rotations, layernorm scaling, the softmax and scaling by $\sqrt{d}$) are a negligible fraction of total arithmetic and memory traffic for large models, so the overall conclusion about large savings remains valid.

Precision for attention score computation and softmax
Attention score computation (the $QK^T$ dot-products) is another place where mixed precision is normally used. In quantized inference pipelines you often see:

- The linear projection producing $Q$ and $K$ can be computed by the quantized kernel, but the outputs are typically dequantized to FP16 or accumulated into a higher-precision accumulator (FP16/BF16 or int32 accumulators then converted to FP16) before computing $Q K^T$.
- The $QK^T$ matmul itself is done in a precision that preserves dynamic range: either FP16/BF16 or using int8 kernels with wider integer accumulators (e.g., int32) and then converted to FP16 for softmax.
- The softmax and the subsequent weighted sum (attention * $V$) are normally performed in FP16/BF16 (or FP32 if extreme stability is required).

The paper’s references to FasterTransformer and standard practice imply attention scores and softmax are computed in floating point (FP16/BF16) or use integer accumulators converted to FP16 for the softmax. Softmax is numerically sensitive (requires exponentials and normalization), so it’s overwhelmingly common to compute softmax in FP16/BF16 or FP32 rather than trying to do it directly in INT8.

Recommendation / how to verify
The paper integrates FasterTransformer and a 2-bit kernel from Ladder. Inspecting the actual inference kernels (their FasterTransformer config, ladder kernel, or the repo they integrated) will show whether rotary and softmax are done in FP16. If you need a strict “no FP multiplies ever” guarantee, that is not what this paper claims; instead it claims the dominant matmul work is converted to low-bit integer arithmetic, and a few pointwise FP ops remain.

Summary
The most consistent implementation is mixed precision: rotate $Q$/$K$ and compute softmax in FP16/BF16 (or use int accumulators converted to FP16), while the heavy linear layers are handled by ternary/INT8 kernels. This keeps numerical stability and integration simple and does not materially undermine the large computational and energy savings reported for BitNet b1.58.
