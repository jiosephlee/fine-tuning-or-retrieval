\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Where does the scaling factor go after absmean quantization of weights?
Question:
The quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|\). After rounding, are the ternary weights used as-is (\{-1,0,1\}) or is there an implicit multiplication by \(\gamma\) somewhere (e.g., folding \(\gamma\) into activations or a layerwise scaling)? If \(\gamma\) is thrown away, how is dynamic range preserved? If it is kept, where is it applied at inference so that matrix multiplies remain multiplication-free?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper’s absmean scale $\gamma$ is not discarded — it is a per-weight-matrix (layerwise) scale that must be kept and applied to recover the original dynamic range. In practice you quantize to ternaries $\widetilde W\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and then treat the real-valued weight matrix as $W\approx (\gamma+\epsilon)\,\widetilde W$. During inference the integer (ternary) matrix–vector products produce integer accumulators which are then multiplied by the scalar $\gamma+\epsilon$ (or have that scalar folded into some neighbouring scale), so you do not lose dynamic range.

Why this is the correct reading and how it is used. The paper defines
$\widetilde W=\mathrm{RoundClip}\big(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1\big)$
with $\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$. That formula normalizes by $\gamma$ to decide the ternary sign/zero; it is therefore the natural inverse factor to restore the approximate magnitude of $W$. If you threw away $\gamma$ you would reduce every retained $\pm1$ to exactly unit magnitude and lose the per-matrix scale information — the model would lose dynamic range and accuracy.

How it is implemented efficiently so matrix multiplies remain mostly multiplication-free. The usual practical ways to handle the scale are: keep a single scalar $\gamma$ per quantized weight matrix and apply it after the integer (here ternary) accumulation, or fold that scalar into the next operation’s scale (for example into an activation quantization scale, a succeeding layer’s weight scale, or the post-linear-layer bias/normalization scaling). Concretely, if activations are $A$ and you compute $Y = W A$, you actually compute
$Y \approx (\gamma+\epsilon)\,(\widetilde W A)$.
The inner product $\widetilde W A$ can be done with integer additions (and sign flips for $-1$) and yields integer accumulators; only after accumulation do you multiply those accumulators by the scalar $\gamma+\epsilon$. Folding $\gamma$ into the next layer’s input scale means that scalar multiplication can be merged with that layer’s existing rescaling (so it does not add a dense per-element multiply inside the heavy GEMM inner loop). That’s why the paper states “almost no multiplication operations” — the expensive MACs are replaced by integer add/sign operations, and only a comparatively cheap scalar rescale remains per channel/tensor block or is absorbed into existing quantization/normalization.

Relation to the paper’s activation quantization comment. The paper quantizes activations per-token to a fixed range and avoids zero-point; that per-token activation scale is another place where the weight $\gamma$ can be folded: either multiply the ternary accumulation by $\gamma$ then by the activation scale, or combine $\gamma$ with the activation scale so the thin scalar that remains is applied outside the core integer accumulation.

Practical summary: keep $\gamma$ (one scalar per weight matrix), perform the ternary integer accumulation $\widetilde W A$ with addition-only primitives, and apply $\gamma+\epsilon$ as a cheap scalar rescale afterwards or fold it into adjacent quantization/normalization operations. This preserves dynamic range while keeping the heavy inner loops multiplication-free, which is consistent with the system and hardware claims in the paper.
