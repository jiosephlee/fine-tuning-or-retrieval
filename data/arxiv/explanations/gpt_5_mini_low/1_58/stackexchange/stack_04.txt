\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What exactly is meant by “no multiplication operations” in matrix multiplication?
Question:
With weights in \{-1,0,1\}, a dot product with 8-bit activations can be implemented as additions and sign flips (and skips for zeros). But the paper also mentions using a 2-bit kernel from Ladder. Does the actual kernel use any multiplications (e.g., bit-serial popcount tricks or table lookups) or is it strictly addition/subtraction and shift? Can you detail the primitive operations used per MAC and how that translates to the claimed energy savings?
Answer:
Short answer: When the paper says “almost no multiplication operations” it means that the heavy inner-loop floating-point multiplications of a conventional $\text{FP16}$ MAC are replaced by much cheaper integer operations — conditional adds/subtracts, bitwise ops and population counts — not by general FP multipliers. For the ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$ a multiply with an $8$‑bit activation reduces to “add the activation” (for $+1$), “subtract the activation” (for $-1$), or “skip” (for $0$). The $2$‑bit Ladder kernel the paper integrates is implemented with bitwise/bitpacked integer primitives (e.g. XNOR/AND + popcount or small LUTs) and integer accumulation, not full-precision multiplies.

Why that is true (intuitive + technical):
- A conventional MAC for $\text{FP16}$ does: output += $(\text{FP16 weight}) \times (\text{FP16 activation})$. That uses a floating-point multiply and a floating-point add for every weight–activation pair.
- If weights are constrained to $\{-1,0,1\}$ and activations are integers ($8$‑bit in the paper), then weight $\times$ activation is exactly one of three outcomes: activation, $0$, or $-$activation. So the multiply operation is logically replaced by a simple selection and sign: output += sign $\times$ activation or skip if zero. On hardware this is implemented as an integer add/subtract (accumulate the $8$‑bit activation into a wider integer accumulator), plus a branchless sign flip (negation) or two’s‑complement subtraction. No FP multiply units are needed in the inner loop.
- For kernels that pack many low-bit weights (the Ladder $2$‑bit kernel or other bitpacked kernels), the low-bit multiplies are implemented via bitwise operations and a population count (popcount) rather than arithmetic multiply. Example approaches:
  - For binary weights $\{-1,+1\}$ one can pack bits and compute XNOR (or XOR)+popcount over activations to obtain dot-products; the final dot-product is reconstructed from popcount results and simple integer arithmetic.
  - For $2$‑bit weights, implementations typically decompose the $2$‑bit multiply into a small number of bitwise ANDs/XNORs and popcounts (or use a tiny lookup table per packed word) and then combine the popcounts with integer adds/subs and shifts. These are integer/bitwise primitives, not FP multipliers.
- The Ladder kernel referenced in the paper is integrated to implement $2$‑bit/low-bit GEMM efficiently; the paper does not claim Ladder uses FP multipliers — the usual Ladder-style kernels use bitpacking + popcount/lookup + integer accumulation.

Primitive operations counted per MAC (conceptually)
- $\text{FP16}$ MAC (baseline): $1$ $\text{FP16}$ multiply + $1$ $\text{FP16}$ add (plus normalization/accumulation overhead).
- Ternary weight + $\text{INT8}$ activation MAC (BitNet idea): for each nonzero weight, one $\text{INT8}$ add or subtract into an accumulator (often widening to int32). Zero weights skip work. In packed implementations you amortize many such logical MACs into a few bitwise + popcount + integer adds.
- Packed $2$‑bit kernel (Ladder-like): a small number of bitwise ops (AND/XNOR/XOR), $1$ popcount per packed word, and a handful of integer adds/shifts to turn popcounts into partial sums. These are integer/bitwise primitives.

Where multiplications may still appear (practical caveats)
The inner GEMM can avoid FP multiplies, but some scalar multiplications remain in the system: scaling factors (the paper’s $\gamma$ or per-tensor scales), dequantization to $\text{FP}$ for downstream layers or output, and some activation scaling or normalization may use FP or integer multiplies. But these are far less frequent than the per-weight multiplies that dominate FP MAC cost. Also, an implementation on commodity GPUs may use integer multiply instructions in microkernels for packing/unpacking or for scale application, but the dominant bulk multiply engine (FP multipliers) is not used for the inner dot products.

How that maps to the claimed energy savings
Energy models in the paper (citing prior work like “energycost” and “pokebnn”) show that an FP multiply (and FP MAC) costs orders of magnitude more energy than integer adds or bitwise ops on modern process nodes. Replacing FP multiplies by $\text{INT8}$ adds, popcounts and bitwise ops reduces arithmetic energy significantly for the MAC-dominated portion of LLM inference. The paper reports an estimated $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on a $7\ \mathrm{nm}$ process. Intuitively this comes from (a) eliminating the expensive $\text{FP16}$ multiply and (b) using very compact integer/bitwise kernels that do more logical MACs per hardware instruction (bitpacking amortizes many logical multiplies into one popcount). The presence of many zeros (sparsity from ternary quantization) further reduces operations because skipped weights eliminate adds entirely.

Bottom line
“Almost no multiplications” in the paper means you no longer execute a FP multiply for each weight–activation pair during the main GEMM. Instead you use integer additions/subtractions for ternary weights and bitwise+popcount + integer arithmetic for bitpacked $2$‑bit kernels (Ladder). That substitution is what drives the large per-MAC energy reduction reported, though a few scalar multiplies for scaling/dequantization still exist outside the inner loop.
