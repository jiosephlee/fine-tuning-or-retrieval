\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the inclusion of 0 in ternary weights improve “feature filtering”?
Question:
The paper claims adding the 0 state explicitly improves modeling by enabling feature filtering. Is there empirical evidence at the weight or activation level (e.g., sparsity patterns) showing that a significant fraction of weights or effective connections become zero? Does the training encourage structured sparsity (e.g., at channel or block level), and is there any regularization that promotes the 0 state?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper claims that adding the explicit 0 state enables “feature filtering,” but in the text you provided it does not present direct empirical diagnostics (per‑weight or per‑activation sparsity numbers, histograms, or structured‑sparsity analyses) that show how many parameters become zero, whether zeros cluster by channel/block, or whether any regularizer was used to promote zeros. The paper describes the ternary quantizer and training recipe, but does not include the detailed weight/activation sparsity statistics you asked about.

Why the paper can claim feature filtering even without explicit sparsity diagnostics
The paper’s quantization design makes zeros a natural outcome: weights are scaled by the mean absolute value $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|$ and then rounded and clipped to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Intuitively, any weight whose scaled magnitude is below $0.5$ (after adding numerical eps/rounding behavior) will round to $0$. So the model can “filter” features by assigning small effective weights to zero instead of only choosing sign. That is a plausible mechanism for improved modeling power compared to purely bipolar $\{\pm1\}$ weights because zeros allow turning off contributions rather than only inverting them.

What the paper does say (implementation-level) that matters to sparsity
The paper states BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with that absmean RoundClip quantizer and uses LLaMA‑like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, no biases). It does not mention adding explicit L1, group‑Lasso, or structured sparsity regularizers, nor does it mention constraints that would force channel/block‑level sparsity. So any zeros arise implicitly from the quantizer and the training dynamics (and likely via the straight‑through estimator used in BitNet‑style quantized training, though that specific optimizer/estimator detail isn’t spelled out in your excerpt).

What you should expect to see if you inspect weights/activations
If you examine trained models from this paper you would likely find a nontrivial fraction of weights quantized to $0$, but the fraction and its distribution will depend on layer, module (attention vs FFN), and model size. Typical empirical patterns in similar ternary/quantized networks are: (1) earlier/lower layers often keep more nonzero structure; (2) FFN matrices (especially the expansion or projection rows) can be denser than some attention projection matrices; (3) zeros are more commonly unstructured (individual scalar zeros) unless a structural regularizer is used. Without explicit structured regularization, zeros usually do not align neatly with entire channels or blocks, so effective sparsity is fine‑grained rather than channel‑wise.

Analyses the paper did not include but that would answer your questions
To provide the evidence you asked for you would compute: (a) global fraction of weights equal to $0$ and per‑layer fractions; (b) per‑matrix and per‑channel zero rates (to detect channel/block sparsity); (c) histograms of pre‑quantization weight magnitudes to show how many values fall into the rounding‑to‑zero bin; (d) activation sparsity (fraction of activations quantized to $0$ per token/layer); and (e) ablations: train identical models constrained to $\{\pm1\}$ vs $\{-1,0,1\}$ and measure downstream and PPL differences plus sparsity. The paper’s claim of “feature filtering” would be much stronger if accompanied by these plots and an ablation that removes the zero state.

How training dynamics implicitly encourage zeros (mechanistic intuition)
Even without an explicit regularizer, the absmean scaling makes zero an attractive stable state for small‑magnitude weights: during optimization small gradients will not overcome the quantizer’s rounding threshold, so weights with small effective value are “snapped” to $0$ and remain $0$ unless gradients push them sufficiently large. In other words, the quantizer itself acts like a deadzone: it indirectly biases small weights to $0$ (a kind of implicit magnitude regularization). This is different from explicit L1 or structured regularization because it operates at the quantization step rather than as an extra loss term, and it typically yields unstructured sparsity.

If you want structured sparsity or to promote more zeros
The paper does not report any additional regularization that encourages structured sparsity. If you need channel/block sparsity you should add explicit penalties (group L1 / group Lasso on entire channels or blocks), use structured pruning during training, or add proximal operators that encourage entire rows/columns to go to $0$. Another approach is magnitude‑based channel pruning after training, followed by fine‑tuning in the ternary quantized regime.

Practical recommendation
If your goal is to verify the “feature filtering” claim empirically, run these checks on a trained BitNet b1.58 checkpoint: compute per‑layer and per‑channel zero fractions, plot pre‑quantization magnitude distributions to see how many weights fall in the rounding‑to‑zero bin, compare activation sparsity across tokens and layers, and do an ablation comparing ternary vs bipolar training. Those diagnostics will directly answer whether zeros are abundant, whether they are structured, and whether they materially change effective connectivity.

Summary
The paper’s architecture and absmean quantizer make zeros possible and plausibly useful for feature filtering, but the provided text does not give the empirical weight/activation‑level evidence or claims of structured sparsity or an explicit regularizer. To validate the claim you need the sparsity statistics and ablations described above; absent those, the improvement is explained mechanistically (deadzone rounding lets small contributions be turned off) but not demonstrated with per‑weight/structured sparsity analyses in the excerpt.
