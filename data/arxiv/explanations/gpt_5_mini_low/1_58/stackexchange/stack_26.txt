\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is there any calibration or post-training fine-tuning after from-scratch quantized training?
Question:
After training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights, did you run any short calibration or post-finetuning pass (e.g., collecting activation ranges, adjusting per-layer scales, or distillation on a held-out set) to improve final inference performance? If not, would such a step further narrow any remaining gaps at sub-3B scales?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the paper reports training BitNet b1.58 entirely from scratch with the ternary weight quantization (the absmean RoundClip scheme) and 8-bit activations, and does not describe any separate post-training calibration, activation-range collection, per-layer re-scaling pass, or distillation-based fine-tuning after the main pretraining. The activation handling they mention (per-token scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ and avoiding a zero-point) is part of the training/inference quantization design, not a separate calibration/fine-tune step.

Why that matters and what you could try
Intuitively, the authors built the quantization into the training loop so the model learned to operate with ternary weights and 8-bit activations throughout pretraining. That typically reduces the benefit you’d get from a later calibration pass, because the network has already adapted its internal representations to the constrained weight/activation formats. For the sizes they report, the trained 1.58-bit models already match FP16 baselines from roughly 3B up, which suggests the from-scratch quantized training is sufficient at those scales.

That said, for sub-3B regimes (where the paper admits gaps exist) a short post-training intervention could plausibly help. Practical techniques that are consistent with the paper’s setup and that you could try are:

- Activation-range calibration: run a small held-out batch through the model in inference mode to collect per-layer (or per-channel) activation statistics and set quantization scales. This is cheap and sometimes closes small gaps when activations at inference differ from training-time statistics.
- Per-layer/per-channel scale adjustment or bias correction: learn tiny multiplicative scales (or additive corrections) for weights/activations on a small calibration set (e.g., a few hundred to few thousand examples) while keeping the main weights frozen. This is low-cost and often stabilizes layer outputs after quantization.
- Short quantization-aware fine-tuning: perform 1–5 epochs of LM fine-tuning (very low learning rate, frozen or lightly regularized embeddings) with the quantized operators in the loop. This lets the model re-adapt any remaining mismatches.
- Distillation from an FP16 teacher: run a brief distillation pass (student is the quantized model) with a teacher loss (logits/MSE) on a modest dataset; distillation often helps smaller models and can correct systematic output shifts.
- Techniques from post-training quant methods (AdaRound, GPTQ-style rounding tweaks, LSQ-like learned scales) adapted to ternary weights: instead of hard rounding, optimize rounding decisions/scales on a small calibration set to reduce reconstruction error.

Expected effect sizes and trade-offs
A calibration-only pass (collecting ranges and setting scales) is nearly-free and can give small but reliable improvements when activations shift between training and inference. Short fine-tuning or distillation will typically give larger gains, especially for smaller models (<3B) that have less overcapacity to absorb quantization noise. The trade-off is compute/time: a few epochs of low-LR fine-tuning or a distillation pass costs more than calibration but is still far cheaper than full pretraining.

Practical recipe to try
If you want to experimentally test this on a sub-3B BitNet b1.58 model, a pragmatic path is: (1) run activation-range calibration with a few thousand in-distribution tokens to set per-layer scales; (2) if gaps remain, run 1–3 epochs of quantization-aware fine-tuning on the same held-out corpus with a small LR (e.g., $1\times10^{-5}$–$5\times10^{-5}$) and monitor perplexity and downstream zero-shot metrics; (3) if further improvement is needed, add a short distillation loss from the FP16 teacher.

Summary
The paper did not report any post-training calibration or fine-tuning beyond the built-in quantized training and the activation scaling scheme described. Nevertheless, for smaller models where a residual gap remains, light-weight calibration, learned-scale adjustment, short QAT fine-tuning, or distillation are all plausible, low-cost ways to further narrow performance gaps.
