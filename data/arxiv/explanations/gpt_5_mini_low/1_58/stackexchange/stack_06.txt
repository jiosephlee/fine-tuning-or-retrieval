\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why are biases removed, and how does that interact with ternary constraints?
Question:
The model removes all biases and uses RMSNorm and SwiGLU (LLaMA-like). Is bias removal primarily for numerical stability/quantization compatibility, for simplicity, or to match LLaMA configs? In ternary networks, biases can help shift activation distributions. Did keeping biases degrade training stability or performance here, or was removal just for reproducibility with LLaMA?
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper bias removal is mainly a pragmatic design choice to match the “LLaMA‑alike” configuration (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary, toolchain compatibility) and to simplify integration with open-source runtimes. A useful side effect is that it simplifies the ternary quantization pipeline (scaling/rounding around zero) and avoids subtle bias-related interactions with the absmean quantizer, so it also helps implementation- and quantization‑robustness. The authors do not report that keeping biases improved performance, and their results show BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 from 3B up, so removing biases did not hurt their empirical outcome.

Why that makes sense (intuitively + technically)
1) Matching LLaMA and ecosystem compatibility. The paper explicitly says the model “adopts the LLaMA‑alike components” and “removes all biases” so BitNet b1.58 can be dropped into popular open‑source software (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp) with minimal effort. That is a concrete engineering motivation: many LLaMA forks omit biases and the toolchain expects that structure.

2) Simpler quantization and scaling. BitNet b1.58 enforces ternary weights via an absmean scale and a round/clip to $\{-1,0,1\}$. When you quantize around zero it is simpler if there are no per-neuron/per-output additive terms to account for. Biases introduce constant offsets in pre-activations which (a) must be quantized themselves or folded into other ops, (b) can shift means away from zero and thus change the effective sparsity (frequency of zeros) after rounding. Removing biases makes the absmean normalization and the RoundClip step conceptually and numerically cleaner.

3) Interaction with normalization. The architecture uses RMSNorm (which, unlike LayerNorm, typically has no learned bias term). If normalization layers are already normalizing and providing affine scaling, learned linear-layer biases become less critical for representational power. In many transformer variants the combination of pre/post‑norm and gating nonlinearities can absorb the function of biases, so removing them causes little functional loss.

4) Ternary-specific considerations. In ternary/1.58‑bit networks biases can have outsized effects because small constant offsets get quantized to nonzero discrete values and persist across signals. That can (depending on scale) either force more nonzero weights (reducing sparsity advantage of the added $0$ state) or create systematic activation shifts that the model must compensate for elsewhere. By removing biases you avoid these brittle interactions and keep the weight distribution centred for the absmean scaler to produce sensible quantization thresholds.

Did leaving biases hurt stability or performance? The paper does not present an explicit ablation where biases were retained vs removed, so we cannot claim they empirically failed. However, the authors emphasize reproducibility with the LLaMA config and report that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines (from 3B up) after using the biasless design. That implies removing biases did not degrade final performance in their training regime; pragmatically it simplified the quantization recipe and integration with existing implementations.

What to test if you worry biases matter
If you want a definitive answer for your own experiments, run a small ablation: train (or fine‑tune) identical BitNet models with and without biases and measure (a) training stability (loss curves, gradients), (b) resulting sparsity / fraction of zeros in ternary weights, and (c) validation perplexity and downstream tasks. That will show whether biases improve representational efficiency, merely duplicate capacity already provided by RMSNorm/SwiGLU, or cause quantization brittleness.

Takeaway
Removing biases in this paper is primarily an engineering and compatibility choice aligned with the LLaMA design, which also makes the ternary quantization pipeline cleaner (less shifting of activation/weight means, more predictable sparsity). The authors’ results indicate this choice did not prevent the ternary model from matching FP16 baselines at scale, but a direct bias ablation (not reported) would be needed to quantify any small gains or losses from keeping biases.
