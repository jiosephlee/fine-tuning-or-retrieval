\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How do SwiGLU activations behave under per-token 8-bit scaling?
Question:
SwiGLU involves elementwise products and nonlinearity. With per-token 8-bit quantization, how do you prevent saturation or information loss at the gates? Are intermediate products computed in higher precision and then requantized? Any calibration or learned scale parameters per channel to stabilize training/inference?
Answer:
Short answer: BitNet b1.58 prevents catastrophic saturation of SwiGLU gates mainly by using per-token symmetric 8-bit activation scaling (no zero point) and by training the network from scratch with the quantized recipe, rather than relying on a brittle post-hoc quantization. The paper does not introduce learned per-channel activation scales for SwiGLU nor does it explicitly state that the elementwise product is computed in higher precision; however, standard and practical implementations that match the paper’s description typically either perform the nonlinearity/multiplication in a higher precision accumulator (INT32/FP16) and then requantize, or dequantize to FP16/FP32 for the activation/GELU/SwiGLU step and requantize afterwards. Below I explain the intuition and how this connects to the paper.

Why per-token symmetric scaling helps
The paper explicitly says activations are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (no zero point). That means each token’s activation vector is linearly rescaled by a single per-token factor so the dynamic range of the activation that enters SwiGLU fits the representable $8$-bit symmetric range. Intuitively, this avoids two common failure modes: (1) a global/static scale that is too small will underflow and destroy gate contrast; (2) a global scale that is too large will saturate the clipping/rounding. Per-token scaling adapts the scale to the instantaneous activation magnitude, preserving relative contrasts across the two SwiGLU branches (the gating and the transformed branch) and reducing saturation at the inputs to the nonlinearity and the elementwise product.

SwiGLU structure and potential failure modes
SwiGLU does elementwise multiplication between a gated branch (usually a sigmoid-like gate) and a transformed branch (Swish/GELU applied to another linear projection). If both branches are low-precision, two problems can happen: the nonlinearity can lose its smooth shape when inputs are coarsely quantized, and the elementwise product can underflow/overflow. The per-token symmetric scaling directly addresses the quantization dynamic range for both branches simultaneously, so the gate retains usable resolution and the product still carries useful information.

What the paper states about intermediates and training
The paper reports training from scratch with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations and describes the per-token symmetric activation scaling. It does not report learned per-channel activation scales, nor does it state explicitly that intermediate elementwise products in SwiGLU are computed in higher precision. Instead, the design choices they emphasize are: (a) absmean-based weight quantization to $\{-1,0,1\}$; (b) per-token symmetric activation scaling (no zero point); and (c) training the quantized model end-to-end so the model can learn to operate within the quantized dynamic ranges.

Practical and hardware-aware implementations (consistent with the paper)
Although not spelled out in the paper, there are two common and compatible implementation patterns you should assume when reasoning about SwiGLU in an $8$-bit-activation system like BitNet:

1) Compute linear matmuls in low-bit kernels (their $2$-bit/INT8 kernels), accumulate in higher-bit integer (INT32) or FP16, then apply the nonlinearity and elementwise product in that higher precision before requantizing. This approach preserves the shape of the nonlinearity and avoids catastrophic rounding in the product, and it is consistent with the paper’s usage of optimized low-bit kernels together with overall $8$-bit activations.

2) Alternatively, dequantize the two SwiGLU branches to FP16/FP32, apply the activation (GELU/Swish) and elementwise product there, then requantize the result back to $8$-bit for downstream layers. This is slightly more expensive but keeps the nonlinear and multiplicative math accurate; it is compatible with the paper’s claim of $8$-bit activations while recognizing that some intermediate higher-precision compute is a practical necessity on current hardware.

Both choices are consistent with the paper’s statement that the model uses $8$-bit activations (scaled per-token) and highly quantized weights while leveraging optimized low-bit inference kernels. The paper’s performance numbers imply that either the network learned to be robust under these quantization choices or that the implementation used higher-precision intermediates where it matters.

About calibration or learned scales
The paper uses absmean-based scaling for weights (a global-ish scale computed from the weight matrix) and per-token scaling for activations. It does not report per-channel learned activation scales or explicit calibration steps for activations entering SwiGLU. Instead, the combination of per-token activation scaling and training-from-scratch appears to be their stabilization mechanism. This is a different design point from many post-training quantization works that rely on per-channel scales or learned scale/zero-point parameters.

Takeaway and broader context
Per-token symmetric scaling is the core stabilization mechanism described in the paper for $8$-bit activations feeding nonlinearities like SwiGLU. In practice, to avoid final precision loss you normally compute the nonlinearities and elementwise products in a higher accumulator precision (or dequantize to FP16/FP32), then requantize the outputs; the paper doesn’t contradict that approach. Finally, training the model end-to-end with the quantized weight/activation recipe lets the network adapt its internal statistics and avoid pathological saturations that would plague naive post-training quantization.
