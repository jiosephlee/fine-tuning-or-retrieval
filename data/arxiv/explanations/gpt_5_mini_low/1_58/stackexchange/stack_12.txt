\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Energy model assumptions behind the 71.4× arithmetic savings
Question:
Figure references claim 71.4× energy savings in matrix multiplication arithmetic on 7nm. Which per-operation energy costs were assumed for FP16 multiply/add versus INT8 add? Are memory access energies included or only ALU operations? If the latter, how does the end-to-end energy claim incorporate memory and control overheads? A concrete equation or table with the constants used would clarify the derivation.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not list an explicit per‑operation constant table in the main text. The $71.4\times$ “arithmetic operations” saving is quoted with the citation pair [energycost, pokebnn], and the text and figure make clear that the $71.4\times$ is an arithmetic (ALU) energy comparison for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm process (i.e., it compares compute energy per op, not a full system energy number). The paper therefore treats arithmetic energy separately from memory/control costs; end‑to‑end energy claims are derived by combining that arithmetic saving with the relative share of time/energy spent in $nn.Linear$ (matrix multiplies) versus other blocks. Below I explain the assumptions implicit in that workflow, give the exact algebra the authors must have used, and show how to reproduce the $71.4\times$ and a full end‑to‑end estimate if you plug in constants from the cited energy models.

What the paper actually says and implies
- The $71.4\times$ factor is introduced in the “Energy” paragraph immediately after the latency/memory discussion and is attributed to the energy model in the cited works (those two refs are the source of the per‑operation numbers). The paper states “We focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication … According to the energy model in [energycost, pokebnn], BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips.” That wording and the figure make it clear the comparison is arithmetic (ALU) energy for the matrix multiply kernel itself (FP16 multiply+add vs INT8 add), not a complete DRAM+SRAM+control energy accounting in that $71.4\times$ number.

Algebra / equation used to produce the arithmetic saving
Let $N$ be the number of scalar multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations required by the FP16 matmul. Typical matmul cost per element is one multiply + one add per MAC; for ternary/1‑bit schemes the computation can reduce to integer additions and sign operations. Define the per‑operation energies:

$E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} = $ energy per FP16 multiply  
$E_{\text{fp16\_add}} = $ energy per FP16 add  
$E_{\text{int8\_add}} = $ energy per INT8 add

Total FP16 arithmetic energy for the matmul (counting multiplies and adds):
$$E_{\text{FP16\_total}} = N \cdot (E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}})$$

Total BitNet arithmetic energy (assuming multiplication largely removed and replaced by INT8 additions; use $N'$ for the number of integer additions required by the ternary scheme — often $N' \approx N$ but could be lower if sparsity/packing is used):
$$E_{\text{INT8\_total}} = N' \cdot E_{\text{int8\_add}}$$

Arithmetic saving $S_{\text{arith}}$ is then
$$S_{\text{arith}} = \frac{E_{\text{FP16\_total}}}{E_{\text{INT8\_total}}} = \frac{N\cdot (E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}})}{N' \cdot E_{\text{int8\_add}}}.$$

If $N' = N$ (1:1 replacement of MAC by one INT8 add), this simplifies to
$$S_{\text{arith}} = \frac{E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}}}{E_{\text{int8\_add}}}.$$

This is the formula you should use to reproduce the $71.4\times$ number. The paper’s citation implies they used values from [energycost, pokebnn] for those $E_{\cdot}$ constants to get $S_{\text{arith}} \approx 71.4$ on a 7 nm process.

Why memory/control are excluded from the $71.4\times$ and how the paper handles end‑to‑end energy
The $71.4\times$ is explicitly for arithmetic energy only. Memory transfer (SRAM/DRAM), activation storage, caches, control flow, and other fixed costs are not included in that number. The paper does present “end‑to‑end energy cost for models with 512 tokens” elsewhere: those end‑to‑end numbers are formed by combining the arithmetic energy (with the $71.4\times$ reduction for the matmul portion) with other model components’ energy shares. Concretely the end‑to‑end energy $E_{\text{total\_model}}$ is:

$$E_{\text{total\_model}} = E_{\text{matmul}} + E_{\text{other}} = E_{\text{matmul\_arith}} + E_{\text{matmul\_mem}} + E_{\text{other\_arith}} + E_{\text{other\_mem}} + E_{\text{control}}.$$

In the paper they reduce $E_{\text{matmul\_arith}}$ by the $71.4\times$ factor for BitNet and leave the other terms either unchanged or scaled by bit‑width changes (for example, activations become 8‑bit so activation memory and activation arithmetic change accordingly). The dominant modelling assumption used in the paper is that matrix multiplies ($nn.Linear$) dominate overall energy as model size grows, so cutting matmul arithmetic energy by $71.4\times$ yields a large end‑to‑end reduction; but the precise end‑to‑end factor depends on the fraction $f$ of total energy that matmul arithmetic represented in the FP16 baseline. If in the baseline $ \text{matmul\_arith}$ is fraction $f$ of total energy, then

$$E_{\text{total\_bitnet}} = E_{\text{total\_fp16}} - f \cdot E_{\text{total\_fp16}} \cdot (1 - 1/S_{\text{arith}}) + \text{adjustments\_for\_activation\_and\_memory\_reduction}$$

so the end‑to‑end speedup $< S_{\text{arith}}$ and approaches $S_{\text{arith}}$ only if $f \to 1$.

Reproducing / checking the number yourself (recommended)
Because the paper cites external energy models rather than listing constants, the correct way to reproduce the $71.4\times$ is to pull the per‑op constants from the cited refs and plug them into the formula above. Typical referenced quantities used in the community (Horowitz 2014 style numbers and later adjusted for 7 nm) give the following order‑of‑magnitude numbers (these are examples commonly used in papers; see the cited refs for the actual values the authors used):

- DRAM access (per 64‑bit word) $\sim O(100)\ \text{pJ}$  
- SRAM access (per 64‑bit word) $\sim O(5)\ \text{pJ}$  
- 32‑bit floating add/mul $\sim$ a few pJ  
- 16‑bit floating multiply/add $\sim 0.5$–$1\ \text{pJ}$ each (process dependent)  
- 8‑bit integer add $\sim 0.01$–$0.05\ \text{pJ}$ (much lower than FP ops)

If you use numbers of that order in the formula $S_{\text{arith}} = (E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}})/E_{\text{int8\_add}}$ you can easily get a multi‑order reduction. For instance, if $(E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}}) \approx 2.0\ \text{pJ}$ and $E_{\text{int8\_add}} \approx 0.028\ \text{pJ}$, then $S_{\text{arith}} \approx 71.4$. That shows why the ratio can be large: FP multiply cost is relatively expensive while simple integer adds at 8‑bit logic are very cheap on modern processes. But note these example constants are illustrative; the exact constants and the $71.4\times$ value should be checked against the two cited energy‑model papers the authors reference.

Practical check: how to get a full end‑to‑end estimate
1) From a profiler (or model flop breakdown) obtain the counts and fractions: number of FP16 multiplies, FP16 adds, INT8 adds (for BitNet), memory reads/writes for weights and activations (bytes), and estimated control/other costs.  
2) Plug in per‑op energies from the cited refs (or measured/simulated for your target 7 nm process) into the formulae above.  
3) Sum all energy terms to get $E_{\text{total}}$ for FP16 baseline and for BitNet, then compute the final end‑to‑end ratio.

If you want a concrete reproducible table, you need the exact constants from the two cited references. The paper itself does not print them; my best practical advice is to consult the two cited energy‑model papers ([energycost] and [pokebnn]) for their per‑op numbers on 7 nm, or ask the authors for their exact constant table. Using those exact constants and the algebra $S_{\text{arith}} = (E_{\text{fp16\_mul}} + E_{\text{fp16\_add}})/E_{\text{int8\_add}}$ will reproduce the $71.4\times$ arithmetic saving, and inserting the matmul fraction $f$ and memory/access changes will reproduce their end‑to‑end energy figures.

Bottom line
- $71.4\times$ is an arithmetic (ALU) energy ratio based on per‑operation energies from the paper’s cited energy models. The paper does not print the constants.  
- Memory (SRAM/DRAM) and control energy are not part of the $71.4\times$ number; they are accounted for separately when the authors build their end‑to‑end energy plots by combining the arithmetic savings with the measured or estimated share of other costs.
