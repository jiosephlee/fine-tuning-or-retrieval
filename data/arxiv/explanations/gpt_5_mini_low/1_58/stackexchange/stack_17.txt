\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### KV cache quantization: how do you maintain accuracy with 8-bit (and proposed 4-bit) caches?
Question:
The paper claims that reducing activations from 16 to 8 bits allows doubling context length, and even "lossless" compression to 4 bits for 1.58-bit LLMs is suggested as future work. 1) How are K and V cached quantization scales selected and updated across time steps? 2) What error control ensures negligible degradation at long contexts? 3) On what basis is 4-bit "lossless" considered feasible—do you rely on constrained dynamic ranges from ternary weights, and is there empirical evidence?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses per-token activation scaling before quantization and stores $8$-bit $K$/$V$ (with their per-token scales) so each timestep’s $K$ and $V$ are quantized using that token’s scale. This keeps quantization error local and bounded, which combined with the model’s redundancy and the modest precision reduction ($16\to8$ bits) yields negligible accuracy loss across long contexts. The suggestion of “lossless” $4$-bit caches is presented as future work and is plausible in principle (because BitNet’s ternary weights and constrained activation ranges reduce entropy), but the paper gives no empirical $4$-bit results — it’s a hypothesis that would require additional techniques (per-block scaling, entropy coding, or learned quantizers) and experiments to verify.

Detailed explanation and intuition, tied to the paper

1) How $K$ and $V$ quantization scales are selected and updated across time steps
BitNet b1.58 quantizes activations by scaling each token’s activation vector to a symmetric range. Concretely, before non-linearities activations are scaled per token to lie in $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (the paper’s change from BitNet). That per-token scale is the normalization factor used to map floating activations into the integer range used for quantization. When a token is processed at time $t$, its computed key $K_t$ and value $V_t$ are quantized using the scale computed for that token and then written into the $K$/$V$ cache in quantized ($8$-bit) form. The scale (or equivalently the inverse scale) for that token must be stored alongside the quantized vectors so that, at later decoding steps, the dequantization back to the approximate floating-point representation is possible. In short: scale is computed per token at the time the token is produced, saved with its quantized $K$/$V$, and reused when those cached $K$/$V$ are read.

This per-token scaling is explicitly described in the paper’s activation quantization paragraph: activations are scaled to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token, avoiding zero-points and simplifying implementation. The same idea applies to the $K$/$V$ that get cached.

2) What error control ensures negligible degradation at long contexts
There are three closely related reasons the paper reports negligible degradation when using $8$-bit cached $K$/$V$ over long contexts.

First, per-token scaling keeps the dynamic range of each cached vector small and well-adapted to that token. Quantization error is then a bounded, mostly zero-mean perturbation relative to that token’s magnitude, rather than being amplified by global range mismatch across many timesteps.

Second, attention uses inner products between queries and (dequantized) keys and then a weighted sum with values. If quantization introduces small additive noise in $K$ or $V$, the downstream effect is a (roughly) linear perturbation of logits and outputs. LLMs are empirically robust to small additive noise because their representations are highly redundant; small per-element errors average out in inner products and in the softmax. The paper demonstrates empirically (through perplexity and end-task evaluations) that reducing activations to $8$ bits — and caching those $8$-bit $K$/$V$ — produces negligible degradation when compared to $FP16$.

Third, the model design itself ($1.58$-bit ternary weights, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, etc.) and training from scratch with these quantization-aware choices helps preserve representation stability and makes the network less sensitive to activation quantization.

Operationally, error control relies on: (a) per-token scale computation and storage so dequantization is accurate for each cached item, (b) using an $8$-bit resolution which already provides sufficient fidelity for keys/values in modern LLMs, and (c) the model’s inherent robustness and the authors’ empirical validation over long contexts (they report doubled context lengths with the same memory budget and no meaningful performance loss).

3) Why $4$-bit “lossless” is considered feasible and what evidence exists
The paper says $8$-bit activations allow doubling the context and that this can “be further losslessly compressed to $4$ bits or even lower for $1.58$-bit LLMs,” but this is presented as future work rather than a demonstrated result. The argument for plausibility rests on two observations in the paper: BitNet’s ternary weights and per-token activation scaling substantially constrain the activation statistics (smaller effective dynamic ranges, sparser or low-entropy distributions), and the current experiments already show strong robustness at $8$ bits. Those facts make more aggressive compression conceivable.

However, the paper does not provide empirical $4$-bit $K$/$V$-cache results nor a concrete method that achieves “lossless” $4$-bit caching. In general, making $4$-bit caching effectively lossless would likely require additional techniques beyond naive uniform quantization: block- or channel-wise scaling, mixed precision (store some tokens or components at higher precision), learned quantizers, entropy coding, or run-length / variable-bit packing of low-entropy representations. The paper mentions the idea at a high level but does not supply experiments or detailed algorithms showing that $4$-bit is lossless in practice.

So the takeaway: $4$-bit $K$/$V$ caching is a plausible next step because BitNet narrows activation distributions and reduces entropy, but the claim in the paper is a forward-looking hypothesis rather than an empirically validated result here. Demonstrating it would need careful ablation: measuring per-token activation entropy, trying $4$-bit quantization with per-block scales and/or auxiliary high-precision side-information, and comparing perplexity and downstream metrics over long contexts.

Broader context
This approach follows common quantitative practice in efficient LLM inference: use per-channel or per-token scaling to keep quantization error bounded, store scales with cached vectors, and exploit model robustness to small noise. The paper’s novelty is showing these principles work well inside a $1.58$-bit-weight-trained model (BitNet) and that moving activations from $16$ to $8$ bits yields immediate practical gains. The jump from $8$ to $4$ bits is a known harder step (often requiring careful mixed-precision or learned schemes), so the paper’s $4$-bit remark should be treated as a promising research direction rather than an established result.
