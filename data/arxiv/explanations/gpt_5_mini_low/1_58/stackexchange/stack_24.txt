\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does adding the 0 state compare to scaled binary weights (±α) in practice?
Question:
Some 1-bit methods use binary weights with a learned or estimated per-channel/per-tensor scale \(\alpha\) to recover dynamic range. Your approach uses ternary \{-1,0,1\} with an absmean normalization. Did you compare against binary-with-scale baselines of similar complexity? Where does ternary help most (e.g., attention vs MLP), and is the gain mainly from induced sparsity or better approximation of small-magnitude weights?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report a direct head-to-head with a binary-with-scale ($( \pm \alpha)$) baseline of matched complexity; instead it argues and demonstrates that adding a zero state with a simple absmean normalization (ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$) produces practical gains that let BitNet b1.58 match FP16 at scale. Intuitively, the benefit of the zero state is twofold: it explicitly filters small-magnitude weights (improving approximation where many weights should be near zero) and induces sparsity that can be exploited by hardware/software (reduced effective compute and noise). Both effects help, and which dominates depends on the layer type and distribution of weights.

Why the paper chose ternary absmean rather than $\pm \alpha$-binary
The paper’s quantizer scales each weight matrix by its average absolute value
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
$$
and then rounds/clips to $\{-1,0,+1\}$. This is a single, simple per-matrix normalization (no learned per-channel scale). The rationale shown in the text is pragmatic: it’s simple to implement, avoids per-channel learned parameters and zero-point complications in activations, and enables an explicit $0$ state for feature filtering. The results (matching FP16 from 3B and large runtime/memory/energy wins) are presented as evidence that this simple ternary scheme is sufficient at scale.

How that compares, conceptually, to $\pm \alpha$ binary
A $\pm \alpha$ binary quantizer represents weights as $\alpha\cdot\operatorname{sign}(W)$ (with $\alpha$ learned or estimated per tensor/channel). That recovers dynamic range but forces every weight to be nonzero; small weights are mapped to $\pm \alpha$ rather than $0$. The main trade-offs are:
- Approximation: $\pm \alpha$ can preserve magnitude via $\alpha$, but it cannot represent that a true optimal weight should be essentially zero. Ternary can approximate small weights better by mapping them to $0$, reducing bias for parameters that should be suppressed.
- Sparsity: ternary produces literal zeros, which can be exploited by sparse computation or by reduced noise in aggregated dot-products; $\pm \alpha$ gives no sparsity.
- Complexity: per-channel learned $\alpha$ adds parameters and optimization complexity; the paper’s absmean is cheaper (no extra learned $\alpha$) and stable in training.

Where ternary helps most (attention vs MLP)
The paper does not include layerwise ablations, but you can reason from how weights behave in transformers. MLP (feedforward) layers often have many small-magnitude parameters that act as fine-grained feature selectors; inserting a zero state helps these layers by letting truly small weights vanish rather than being forced to a uniform $\pm$ scale. Attention projection matrices (Q/K/V/O) can be more sensitive to precise scaling because they directly shape attention scores; there the per-tensor scale of $\pm \alpha$ can help preserve relative magnitudes. Empirically in many quantization studies (and consistent with the paper’s design choices emphasizing feature filtering), adding a zero state tends to favor MLPs and projection matrices where sparsity/thresholding reduces noise, while scaled-binary can be competitive where preserving global scale matters most. The paper’s overall success (matching FP16 from 3B) suggests ternary with absmean is sufficient across the network at their scale and training recipe.

Is the gain mainly from induced sparsity or better approximation of small weights?
Both mechanisms contribute. The absmean + rounding explicitly thresholds small weights to $0$, so part of the gain is improved approximation of the true weight distribution (many weights should be near zero). The resultant sparsity also reduces the effective interference/noise in matrix products and can be exploited by system kernels (the paper reports large latency/memory/energy benefits). The paper emphasizes “explicit support for feature filtering” as an advantage of having $0$ in the alphabet, which highlights the modeling/approximation benefit; the runtime/energy sections highlight the systems benefit of sparsity.

What’s missing / recommended ablations
Because the paper does not present a matched $\pm \alpha$ baseline, a fair experimental comparison you could run is: (a) binary-with-per-matrix or per-channel learned $\alpha$ trained from scratch with the same architecture and training tokens, (b) the paper’s ternary absmean, and (c) hybrid variants like per-channel absmean or learned ternary thresholds. Important diagnostics are validation perplexity, per-layer nonzero density, distribution of quantization error, and a layerwise swap experiment (ternary in MLPs vs attention only). Measuring where the nonzeros concentrate (MLP vs attention, early vs late layers) will show whether gains come from sparsity patterns or better magnitude approximation.

Practical takeaway
The paper shows that a very simple ternary quantizer (absmean + RoundClip to $\{-1,0,1\}$) is enough to reach FP16 parity at scale and to unlock major system-level savings. Conceptually, ternary is superior to plain $\pm \alpha$ when many true weights are near zero or when explicit sparsity can be exploited; $\pm \alpha$ keeps magnitude but cannot suppress small unrelated weights. For a definitive claim about relative performance one should run the matched $\pm \alpha$ baselines the paper didn’t report and do layerwise ablations to isolate sparsity vs approximation effects.
