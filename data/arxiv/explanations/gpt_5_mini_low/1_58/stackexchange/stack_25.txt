\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Do residual connections accumulate quantization error, and how is it controlled?
Question:
In Transformers, residual connections can amplify quantization noise across layers. With 8-bit activations and ternary weights, do you observe drift or instability in deep stacks? Are there normalization tweaks (e.g., post-norm RMSNorm parameters in higher precision) or periodic rescaling to keep residual streams within safe dynamic ranges?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes, in principle residual connections can accumulate quantization noise, but BitNet b1.58 controls that in several concrete ways so that you do not observe problematic drift or instability in deep stacks (empirically it matches FP16 baselines from 3B up). The paper does not report adding ad‑hoc “periodic rescaling” or higher‑precision norm parameters — instead stability is obtained by the quantization recipe plus architecture / training choices.

Why accumulation is a concern (intuitive). Each residual add injects whatever rounding/quantization error is present in the layer outputs; if those errors are biased or the residual stream grows in magnitude layer by layer, errors can amplify and the activations can leave the dynamic range of the low‑bit format, causing drift or divergence in very deep networks.

How BitNet b1.58 prevents that (technical, from the paper). First, weights are constrained to $\{-1,0,1\}$ after an explicit scaling step: each weight matrix is divided by its mean absolute value $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|$ then rounded/clipped. That absmean scaling keeps the effective weight scale predictable and prevents uncontrolled per‑layer gain that would amplify residuals. Second, activations are represented in 8 bits but are explicitly scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (no zero‑point). Per‑token symmetric scaling reduces dynamic‑range mismatches across tokens and layers, so the residual stream stays within the representable range layer to layer. Third, the model uses RMSNorm (the LLaMA‑alike components) and removes biases; RMSNorm is scale‑stabilizing and bias‑free layers remove a persistent additive offset that could otherwise accumulate. Finally, the model is trained from scratch under these quantized constraints, so the optimizer and training dynamics adapt the learned magnitudes and activations to be compatible with ternary weights + 8‑bit activations rather than relying on post‑hoc quantization of an FP16 trained net.

Empirical evidence in the paper. The authors report matching perplexity and downstream performance starting at 3B, and successful scaling to 70B models with large speed/memory/energy gains. These empirical results indicate no practical drift/instability across deep stacks under their recipe.

What the paper does not do (and practical notes). The paper does not state that they used higher‑precision RMSNorm parameters, nor that they inserted periodic rescaling/renormalization hooks in the residual stream. Those are reasonable additional controls in other low‑precision work, but here they were unnecessary given: (1) absmean weight scaling, (2) symmetric per‑token activation scaling, (3) RMSNorm and bias removal, and (4) training from scratch under the quantized scheme.

If you are experimenting beyond the paper. If you still worry about residual accumulation in other settings (e.g., post‑training quantization of a pretrained FP16 model, or more aggressive activation quantization), common remedies are: keep normalization layers in full or higher precision, apply per‑layer learnable scaling factors (layer scale) initialized small, periodically renormalize residual norms, or fine‑tune under quantization so weights learn to avoid gain buildup. The paper’s empirical approach shows those extra measures were not required for their $1.58$-bit training recipe.

Summary: accumulation is a real theoretical risk, but BitNet b1.58 prevents it through absmean weight scaling, symmetric per‑token activation scaling, RMSNorm + bias removal, and training-from-scratch in the quantized regime — and the reported results show no instability or drift in deep models.
