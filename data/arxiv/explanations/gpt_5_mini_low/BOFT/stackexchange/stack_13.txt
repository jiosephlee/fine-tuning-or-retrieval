\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### BOFT vs. LoRA: When would low-rank additive updates be preferable to multiplicative orthogonal updates?
Question:
Conceptually, LoRA learns a low-rank additive update \(\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\), while BOFT learns a multiplicative orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Are there scenarios where low-rank structure better matches the task’s inductive bias than near-isometric transformations? For example, if a task primarily needs to change the spectrum of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), would LoRA be more effective since BOFT preserves singular values?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — there are clear situations where a low-rank additive update like LoRA is a better inductive match than an orthogonal multiplicative update like BOFT/OFT. The essential difference is that LoRA can change the singular values and the rank of the pretrained weight matrix, while orthogonal left-multiplication preserves the singular spectrum and is (near-)isometric. Which is preferable depends on whether the downstream task mainly needs to re-weight/amplify/suppress particular linear directions (spectral/rank change) or instead mostly needs to rotate/permute/redistribute existing features while keeping norms and relative geometry stable.

Why this follows from the paper (intuition + technical detail). BOFT/OFT apply an orthogonal matrix $R$ on the left so the finetuned weight is $R W^0$. If $W^0 = U \Sigma V^{T}$, then $R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^{T}$ — the singular values (the diagonal of $\Sigma$) are unchanged. The paper highlights this property explicitly: “both OFT and BOFT multiply an orthogonal matrix to the left and obtain the finetuned weights … which does not affect the largest singular value (i.e. the spectral norm).” Thus BOFT is inherently near-isometric and preserves the spectrum of $W^0$. LoRA, with additive updates $\Delta W = A B$, can change $\Sigma$ (and the effective rank) because you are adding new components to $W^0$; low-rank updates are capable of introducing new singular directions and altering singular values.

When to prefer LoRA (low-rank additive):
- Tasks that require changing the spectrum: If the downstream task needs to amplify or suppress specific linear modes (for example, boost sensitivity to some feature directions, down-weight noisy subspaces, or alter singular value gaps), LoRA can accomplish that by adding components that change $\Sigma$. If the effective change is concentrated in a few directions (intrinsic low-rank difference between pretrained and target task), a low-rank update is a very natural inductive bias.
- Tasks that require adding new degrees of freedom or new features: If you need to expand the span of the row/column space of $W^0$ (e.g., learn new specialized detectors that were not present in pretraining), a low-rank additive term can add those directions directly.
- If empirical diagnostics show the finetuned full-weight difference $\Delta W = W^{*} - W^0$ has low-rank structure or large singular-value changes, LoRA will be parameter-efficient and effective.

When to prefer BOFT/OFT (multiplicative orthogonal):
- Tasks that should preserve pretraining geometry and norms: BOFT is designed to keep pairwise neuron angles and spectral norm, which the paper argues helps stability and generalization. If you want to preserve semantic structure learned during pretraining and only need to reorient or mix those features (rotate within the same singular-spectrum), BOFT is a better match.
- When training stability/generalization is critical: The paper emphasizes that orthogonal finetuning “perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix” and that this preservation benefits stability and generalization. For tasks sensitive to overfitting or that benefit from small, isometric changes (e.g., controlled generation, some diffusion-model adaptations), BOFT’s inductive bias is desirable.
- When you want parameter-efficient dense orthogonality: BOFT’s butterfly factorization gives a compact way to represent dense orthogonal transforms (parameter cost roughly $O(d \log d)$), enabling richer isometric transforms than block-diagonal OFT while maintaining the orthogonality constraint and regularizing the update.

Practical diagnostics and heuristics. A practical way to decide: take a small fully-finetuned $W^{*}$ (or a few gradient steps) on your task and examine the singular values of $W^{*}$ versus $W^0$ and the matrix $\Delta W = W^{*} - W^0$. If the singular values of $W^{*}$ differ substantially from $W^0$ (large spectral shifts) or $\Delta W$ is well-approximated by a low-rank matrix, that suggests LoRA-like additive low-rank updates are appropriate. If the spectrum is largely preserved and the main change is a re-orientation of singular vectors, BOFT/OFT will likely do well and may generalize better.

Hybrid strategies. The paper itself contrasts low-rank and sparse/structured orthogonal parameterizations as two complementary families of parameter-efficient structure. In practice you can combine them: use a small LoRA plus BOFT (additive low-rank plus multiplicative orthogonal), or alternate/stack them, so you get the ability to both re-scale and rotate. That captures both kinds of inductive bias and is often effective when the required change is mixed.

Summary. Use LoRA when the task requires changing singular values, adding new directions, or when the $\Delta W$ is low-rank; use BOFT/OFT when you want to preserve pretrained geometry, spectral norms, and prefer isometric rotations/mixings (with BOFT giving a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal family). The paper’s spectral discussion and the information-transmission/inductive-bias arguments make these differences explicit and provide the theoretical basis for choosing between them (or combining them).
