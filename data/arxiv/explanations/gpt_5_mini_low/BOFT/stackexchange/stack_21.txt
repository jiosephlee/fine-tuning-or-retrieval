\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Practical initialization: Is identity initialization sufficient when stacking many butterfly components?
Question:
BOFT initializes each butterfly component as identity. With \(m=\log d\) or larger, could this create optimization issues (e.g., many layers initially doing nothing, leading to small gradients)? Have you tried small random skew-symmetric initializations in \(\mathbf{Q}\) or a warm-start schedule where components are unfrozen progressively?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses identity initialization (Cayley with $Q=0$) by design, and that is a reasonable default because it exactly preserves the pretrained model and gives stable training. However, in practice stacking many small orthogonal factors (large $m$, e.g. $m \approx \log d$) can reduce the initial “signal” coming from the learned factors and so can reduce the immediate gradient magnitudes to those parameters — this is a plausible optimization issue. The paper does not report explicit experiments with small random skew‑symmetric initialization or progressive unfreezing; it only states that butterfly components are initialized to identity and proposes multiplicative Dropout for regularization.

Why identity init is used and why it helps. BOFT initializes each butterfly component to identity ($Q=0$ in Cayley). That gives zero net change to the pretrained weights at the start of finetuning, matching the usual PEFT desideratum of starting from the pretrained model exactly. Also, because BOFT multiplies an orthogonal $R$ on the left, starting at identity preserves spectral properties (singular values) and tends to make optimization stable — the paper highlights this spectral-preservation benefit.

Why identity init can be problematic when $m$ is large. If $R = \prod_{i=1}^m B_i$ and each $B_i$ starts at $I$, then initially $R \approx I$ and small updates to many $B_i$ compound multiplicatively. Two practical consequences follow: (1) the per‑factor gradient wrt the skew matrix $Q$ may be small because the forward/backward paths see little effect of each tiny rotation when all others are near $I$; (2) optimization can become reliant on coordinated updates across many factors, which is harder if gradients are tiny or noisy. Intuitively, many “do‑nothing” factors make the parameterization very redundant at initialization and can slow early learning of nontrivial transforms.

What the paper provides instead. The paper does not claim optimization problems arise in practice, and their empirical results show BOFT works across many tasks with identity init. They also introduce multiplicative Dropout (randomly replacing some butterfly components or blocks with identity during training), which both regularizes and — by intermittently forcing some components to be identity — can increase robustness and encourage nontrivial factors to be learned. But the paper does not report exploring non‑zero $Q$ initializations or staged unfreezing.

Practical recommendations (grounded in the paper’s parameterization and Cayley form). If you encounter slow learning or very small gradients with large $m$, try one or more of these strategies:

1) Small skew‑symmetric noise in $Q$. Initialize the skew matrix $Q_i$ for each butterfly component with a small antisymmetric random matrix: $Q \leftarrow \varepsilon\cdot(A - A^T)$ where $A_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon$ is small. This gives small initial rotations. Reasonable starting scales are $\varepsilon \in [10^{-3}, 10^{-2}]$ (or set each $2\times 2$ block to a tiny rotation angle $\theta$ where $\theta \in [0.01,0.1]$ radians, approximately $0.6$–$6$ degrees). This preserves the “near identity” property but gives more gradient signal. Because BOFT uses Cayley, small $Q$ maps to a near‑identity orthogonal; you retain stability while increasing initial sensitivity.

2) Small random rotations per $2\times 2$ blocks. Since BOFT builds orthogonality from many $2\times 2$ blocks, you can directly initialize each $2\times 2$ block as a rotation by small angle $\theta$ sampled from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ ($\sigma \approx 0.01$–$0.05$). This is equivalent to a structured skew init and is inexpensive to implement.

3) Progressive (warm) unfreezing. Start with only the first $k$ butterfly components trainable (others kept identity), then progressively unfreeze more components (increase $k$) over the first $N$ steps/epochs. This reduces the coordination burden and lets earlier components learn a coarse transform before later components refine it.

4) Scale learning rates or use per‑factor LR. Give slightly larger LR to the Cayley parameters $Q$ (or use adaptive optimizers) until the factors move away from identity. Monitor the norm of $R-I$ or $Q$ to decide when to reduce LR.

5) Use multiplicative Dropout / stochastic depth on components. The paper’s multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces some components with identity during training. This serves both as regularization and as a way to force different components to carry signal; tuning the dropout percentages ($p_1,p_2$ in paper) can help escape a purely identity regime.

What to monitor. Track gradient norms of $Q$ (or of the $2\times 2$ block params) and the norm of $R-I$ per layer. If gradients on $Q$ are vanishingly small and $R$ remains extremely close to $I$ for many steps, try the small skew init + higher LR for those parameters or progressive unfreeze.

Summary recommendation. Identity init is principled and usually stable (the paper uses it and reports good results), but when $m$ is large you may see weaker initial gradient signal. The simplest practical remedies are tiny skew‑symmetric noise ($\varepsilon \approx 10^{-3}\ldots10^{-2}$) or small per‑block rotation angles, possibly combined with a slightly larger LR or staged unfreezing. The paper itself does not evaluate these variants, so these are empirical suggestions consistent with BOFT’s Cayley parameterization and the paper’s multiplicative Dropout idea.
