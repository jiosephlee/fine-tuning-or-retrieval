\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Are there known failure cases where BOFT’s inductive bias hurts performance?
Question:
The paper argues that butterfly structure provides a beneficial implicit bias tied to classic transforms. Are there tasks or layers where this structured prior is too restrictive (e.g., needs non-isometric scaling or highly localized, non-mixing changes)? Any empirical observations or diagnostics to detect when BOFT underfits due to its structure?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report concrete real-world “failure cases”, but it explicitly recognizes and explains where BOFT’s structured orthogonal prior can be too restrictive. In particular, any downstream adaptation that requires changing the pretrained layer’s singular values (i.e., non‑isometric / scaling changes) or that needs highly unstructured, neuron- or coordinate‑specific transformations can force BOFT to underfit. The paper also gives practical ways to diagnose and remedy this (and a few concrete tests you can run).

Why BOFT can hurt performance (intuitive + technical)
BOFT constrains the finetuning update to be a left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $R$ composed from a butterfly factorization. Two important consequences follow directly from the paper’s analysis:

1) Spectral invariance: multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight $W^0$ (the paper states this explicitly via SVD: $R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^T$, so $\Sigma$ is unchanged). Intuitively, BOFT is an isometric reorientation of the weight space, not a rescaling. If a downstream task requires modifying the magnitude spectrum of $W^0$ (e.g., amplifying or damping certain singular directions), BOFT (and any pure orthogonal finetuning method) cannot implement that, and will underfit.

2) Structured expressivity: BOFT restricts $R$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group (butterfly matrices with chosen depth $m$ and block size $b$). That structured prior is helpful when the needed transformation resembles classic fast transforms or smooth global mixing, but it can be too rigid when the optimal adaptation requires very localized, highly unstructured, or coordinate‑dependent changes. The paper’s expressivity discussion and simulation (approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix) show that decreasing $m$ or increasing block size $b$ changes expressivity; small $m/b$ give less expressive hypothesis classes and higher approximation error.

When you should suspect BOFT underfits
- Tasks or layers where you expect necessary changes in scale (singular values) rather than only rotations. Examples (conceptual): reweighting certain features heavily, adapting pretrained features whose norms need to change to match new data distributions.
- Layers that historically benefit from additive or low‑rank updates (LoRA) rather than global reorientation: e.g., final classification heads, or layers where only a few neurons must shift amplitude or be suppressed.
- Small-data regimes where the model must drastically change a few directions to fit new labels (orthogonality may overly constrain the model).

Diagnostics grounded in the paper
The paper suggests several tools (explicit or implicit) you can use to detect BOFT’s underfitting and whether the inductive bias is the problem:

- Compare performance vs baselines (full FT, LoRA, original OFT). If BOFT trails LoRA but OFT/full FT improve substantially, that indicates the orthogonal (or butterfly) restriction is limiting expressivity.

- Monitor the approximation/error curves like the paper’s simulation: attempt to fit a target orthogonal (or directly a target transformation) with $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ and measure approximation error vs number of effective parameters. If the approximation error is large for your chosen $(m,b)$, BOFT cannot represent the needed rotation. This is a quick layer‑level test: try to approximate the empirical $R = W_{\text{new}} (W^0)^{-1}$ (or the left transform you would need) with $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ and check residual.

- Inspect singular values. Since BOFT preserves singular values, compute $\mathrm{SVD}(W_{\text{pre}})$ and $\mathrm{SVD}(W_{\text{finetuned}})$ for a baseline finetuning that you consider “good” (full FT). If the baseline changed $\Sigma$ significantly while BOFT’s best solution cannot, that points to spectral mismatch: BOFT will fail if changing $\Sigma$ is required.

- Check the learned $R$ deviation from identity. BOFT is identity‑initialized (paper), so if training gets stuck near identity (small $\|R - I\|$) while validation loss still high, the structure may be preventing necessary changes. You can also compare layerwise magnitudes of $R - I$ across layers to find which layers are constrained.

- Underfitting stress test: attempt to fit random labels or a synthetic task that requires specific scaling/coordinate changes. If BOFT cannot fit while LoRA/full FT can, that demonstrates expressivity limitations.

How to remedy or rule out the inductive‑bias problem
The paper makes clear the design knobs and tradeoffs: increase expressivity by increasing $m$ (more butterfly layers) or increasing block size $b$; move towards the kaleidoscope/generalized composition if needed. Practically, run a small ablation grid over $(m,b)$ and check whether performance improves as expressivity increases (this is exactly what the paper’s simulations show for approximation error). If performance improves with larger $m/b$ and eventually matches the more flexible baselines, the inductive bias was the limiting factor.

If you need non‑isometric updates, orthogonal finetuning is fundamentally mismatched. In that case either combine BOFT with a small additive update (e.g., LoRA) or switch to a method that permits singular value changes. The paper contrasts orthogonal (multiplicative) updates with additive low‑rank methods and notes they have different inductive biases, so a hybrid approach is natural.

Summary
BOFT’s inductive bias is beneficial for many tasks (the paper demonstrates improved generalization and parameter efficiency), but it can hurt when the task requires changing singular values or very localized, unstructured adjustments that lie outside the structured orthogonal subset BOFT represents. The paper provides the conceptual diagnostics (compare against baselines, approximation tests, singular‑value checks, monitoring $\|R - I\|$, and ablations on $(m,b)$) and the explicit remedies (increase $m/b$, consider the kaleidoscope composition, or combine with additive updates) to detect and mitigate underfitting caused by BOFT’s structure.
