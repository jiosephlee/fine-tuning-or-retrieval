\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Implementation detail: Where exactly is \(\mathbf{R}\) applied inside Transformers and CNNs?
Question:
For a pretrained linear layer with weights \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), BOFT uses \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). In practice, for MHA and MLP modules in Transformers, do we apply \(\mathbf{R}\) to the input dimension (rows) of all linear sub-layers (e.g., Q/K/V/O, and MLP in/out) or selectively? How are biases handled? Are there layers where applying \(\mathbf{R}\) harms performance or violates architectural constraints (e.g., layer norm placements)?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT (and OFT) multiply an orthogonal $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of a pretrained layer's weight matrix, so $ \mathbf{W} $ becomes $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $. Concretely this means $ \mathbf{R} $ acts on the input (feature) dimension of a linear map: for a layer whose forward is $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{b} $ you replace it by $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $. You therefore apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to those linear sublayers whose weight shape matches the "input dimension" (the dimension of $ \mathbf{x} $) and you leave biases as-is.

Why this is the right interpretation (intuitive + technical). The paper defines OFT/BOFT as learning an orthogonal $ \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} $ and changing the forward pass from $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x} $ to $ \mathbf{z}=((\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top)\mathbf{x} $. That formula shows $ \mathbf{R} $ multiplies the pretrained weight on the left, i.e. it transforms the input basis (it rotates/permutes the input/neuron coordinates) so the pairwise geometry of columns of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ is preserved up to orthogonal transform. Because $ \mathbf{R} $ is orthogonal the spectral properties of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ are preserved, which the paper emphasizes as a benefit for stability and generalization.

How this maps to Transformer MHA / MLP components. In standard Transformer blocks every linear sublayer is just a matrix times the hidden vector:

- For the Q/K/V projections each is a linear map from the model hidden dimension $ d $ to some head/ per-head dimension (their weight typically shaped $ d\times n_{qkv} $). You apply $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of those pretrained weight matrices (so effectively $ \mathbf{R} $ acts on the hidden/input dimension before the projection). The same applies to the output (O) projection of attention and to the MLP input and output linear layers: wherever the forward is $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $ with $ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} $, replace $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ by $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $. In short: apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to all linear sublayers whose input is the model hidden vector (the paper explicitly formulates the forward pass this way).

Practical caveats and selective application. The paper does not mandate applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to every single matrix in the network; the constraint is simply shape/semantics: $ \mathbf{R} $ must match the input dimension. So:

- Only apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to layers whose input dimension equals the orthogonal transform size $ d $. If a projection has a different input size (e.g. token embedding table or small adapter layers), you either do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ or you make a separate $ \mathbf{R} $ of the appropriate size.
- If weights are tied (e.g. input embeddings tied with output head), you must be careful to apply the same transformation consistently, otherwise you break the tying. The paper assumes transforming pretrained weights in-place, so tied/shared weights should receive the same $ \mathbf{R} $.
- Do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to non-linear or parameter types that are not linear maps on the model hidden vector (LayerNorm scale/shifts, positional embeddings, scalar biases that are not part of a pretrained linear weight) — those are not represented as left-multipliable weight matrices in the paper’s formulation.
- When a linear layer changes dimensionality (e.g. down-projection from $ d $ to $ d_{\text{mid}} $), $ \mathbf{R} $ is still valid because it multiplies the pretrained weight on the left and the weight already maps from the same input space. If the input to that layer is not the same $ d $ you used for $ \mathbf{R} $, you cannot apply it without changing shapes.

Biases. The paper’s forward replacement keeps biases unchanged: $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $. BOFT/OFT initialize $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $ so the initial function is identical to the pretrained one; biases remain intact and are not transformed by $ \mathbf{R} $. (If you wanted to be formal: applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to the left does not induce a change in additive bias unless you explicitly change the bias term.)

LayerNorm, residuals, and architectural constraints. The paper does not propose modifying LayerNorm parameters or other normalization blocks. Because BOFT preserves spectral properties and is initialized to identity, it is designed not to violate the pretrained model’s normalization/residual behaviour at initialization. Nevertheless, practical points to watch:

- LayerNorm placement: LayerNorms expect certain calibrated activations from the pretrained network. Although orthogonal left-multiplication preserves norms globally, intermediate activation distributions can change during training. The paper mitigates this by identity initialization of $ \mathbf{R} $ (so initial behavior is unchanged) and by the orthogonality constraint that prevents wild scaling. Still, you may prefer to finetune carefully (small lr, etc.) or avoid applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to very early blocks if you observe instability.
- Tied weights and shared parameters: as noted, do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ inconsistently across tied layers.
- Layers that are not linear maps from the model hidden state (positional embeddings, absolute/relative bias tensors, LayerNorm affine parameters): the paper’s formulation does not touch these and you should not substitute $ \mathbf{R} $ there.
- If a layer’s role is to produce key/value/query indices for e.g. rotary or other position-aware projection, you should check that applying $ \mathbf{R} $ does not break any invariants specific to that mechanism. The paper does not report such failures, but that is an architecture-dependent consideration.

Other implementation notes from the paper. BOFT uses Cayley parameterization (skew-symmetric $ \mathbf{Q} $ with $ \mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} $) to maintain orthogonality and initializes $ \mathbf{Q}=0 $ so $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $. BOFT also proposes a multiplicative dropout that temporarily replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity during training; this is possible because the transform is multiplicative (left of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $) rather than additive.

Summary recommendation. For Transformers apply $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of every linear weight that consumes the model hidden vector (Q/K/V/O projections, attention output projection, MLP in/out), keep biases unchanged, initialize $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $, and ensure consistency for tied/shared weights. Do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to LayerNorm parameters, positional embeddings, or other non-linear parameter types unless you deliberately design matching transforms and shapes. The paper’s empirical results and theoretical discussion assume this left-multiplication (input-dimension) interpretation.
