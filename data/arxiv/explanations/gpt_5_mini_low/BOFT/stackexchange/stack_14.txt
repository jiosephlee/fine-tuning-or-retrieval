\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Clarifying the information-transmission graph: How do sparse patterns map to permutations between levels?
Question:
The paper states each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is a block-diagonal matrix whose nonzero pattern is a permutation of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\). Could you show explicitly, for \(d=8\), how the edges (nonzeros) of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(8,4)\) can be obtained from permuting the blocks of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(8,2)\)? A concrete mapping would help me understand the level-to-level connectivity evolution.
Answer:
Short answer: tilde B(8,4) is the same $2\times2$ “butterfly” building block pattern as $\tilde B(8,2)$, only with the $2\times2$ blocks re-grouped (permuted) so that they become $4\times4$ block-factors. Concretely one can realize this regrouping by a permutation that interleaves odd and even indices; applying that permutation to both rows and columns of $\tilde B(8,2)$ collects adjacent $2\times2$ blocks into $4\times4$ blocks and yields the nonzero pattern of $\tilde B(8,4)$.

Explanation with an explicit mapping ($d = 8$)

1) Pattern of $\tilde B(8,2)$.
$\tilde B(8,2)$ is block-diagonal with four $2\times2$ blocks on the diagonal. Label the 8 node indices $1\ldots8$. The nonzero (edge) pattern of $\tilde B(8,2)$ connects within each adjacent pair:
- block 1 on indices $(1,2)$,
- block 2 on indices $(3,4)$,
- block 3 on indices $(5,6)$,
- block 4 on indices $(7,8)$.

Viewed as a connectivity matrix between level $L$ and level $L+1$, each $2\times2$ block means inputs $\{1,2\}$ connect only to outputs $\{1,2\}$, inputs $\{3,4\}$ only to outputs $\{3,4\}$, etc.

2) What $\tilde B(8,4)$ looks like.
$\tilde B(8,4)$ is block-diagonal with two $4\times4$ blocks: one acting on indices $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and the other on $\{5,6,7,8\}$. Each $4\times4$ block is itself a butterfly factor (see Eq. (1) in the paper): the $4\times4$ block has the $2\times2$ diagonal-block structure
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
where each $\operatorname{diag}(d_*)$ is a $2\times2$ diagonal matrix (so the $4\times4$ block is assembled from four $2\times2$ diagonal pieces). The essential point the paper emphasizes is that the nonzero pattern of each $4\times4$ block is a permutation (block-wise reordering) of the $2\times2$ blocks that appear in $\tilde B(8,2)$.

3) A concrete permutation that shows the mapping.
Take the permutation $p$ that interleaves odds then evens within each 4-block:
$p = [1,3,2,4,5,7,6,8]$.
Interpreting $p$ as a permutation matrix $P$ (so $P$ reorders indices by $p$), the mapping is

$\displaystyle \tilde B(8,4) = P^T \cdot \tilde B(8,2) \cdot P$

(we permute rows and columns the same way to regroup the $2\times2$ blocks into $4\times4$ blocks).

Check intuitively what this permutation does on indices $1\ldots4$:
- The order $[1,3,2,4]$ takes the original $2\times2$ blocks on $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$ and interleaves them so that the $2\times2$ blocks now sit inside the $4\times4$ block acting on $\{1,3,2,4\}$ (which we then relabel as $\{1,2,3,4\}$ after the permutation). The same happens on indices $5\ldots8$ with $[5,7,6,8]$.

4) Edge-level description.
Before permutation, the nonzero $2\times2$ blocks are
$(1\leftrightarrow1,\,1\leftrightarrow2,\,2\leftrightarrow1,\,2\leftrightarrow2)$, $(3\leftrightarrow3,\,3\leftrightarrow4,\,4\leftrightarrow3,\,4\leftrightarrow4)$, $(5\leftrightarrow5,\,5\leftrightarrow6,\,6\leftrightarrow5,\,6\leftrightarrow6)$, $(7\leftrightarrow7,\,7\leftrightarrow8,\,8\leftrightarrow7,\,8\leftrightarrow8)$.
After conjugating by $P$ (the interleaving), those four $2\times2$ blocks are rearranged into the two $4\times4$ blocks whose internal pattern equals the $4\times4$ butterfly factor pattern (four diagonal $2\times2$ subblocks arranged in the $2\times2$ layout). In other words, each $4\times4$ block’s nonzero pattern is literally a permuted arrangement of two of the original $2\times2$ blocks.

Intuition and connection to the paper
The paper states that every butterfly component $\tilde B(d,k)$ has a nonzero pattern which is a permutation (block-wise) of $\tilde B(d,2)$. The permutation $p$ above is the concrete instance of that statement for $d=8, k=4$: it reorders indices so that adjacent $2\times2$ interaction blocks (the $\tilde B(8,2)$ blocks) are collected into $4\times4$ butterfly factors. This is precisely the same mechanism used recursively in Cooley–Tukey style factorizations: at each stage you regroup (permute) elements so that local $2\times2$ mixing operations act on the correct larger subsets, enabling global dense connectivity after stacking the levels.

If you want, you can write down the $8\times8$ sparse pattern matrices before and after permutation and verify that $P^T \cdot \tilde B(8,2) \cdot P$ has two $4\times4$ nonzero blocks in positions $\{1\ldots4\}$ and $\{5\ldots8\}$, each with the $4\times4$ butterfly layout described above.
