\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Comparing BOFT with Givens/Householder: Are we just choosing a different sparse orthogonal basis?
Question:
Classical linear algebra provides parameter-efficient orthogonal factorizations using Givens rotations (sparse with \(2\times 2\) active sub-blocks) or Householder reflections. BOFT also uses products of sparse orthogonals arranged in a butterfly pattern. How does BOFT’s expressivity, parameter sharing, and computational layout compare to (a) a schedule of Givens rotations and (b) a sequence of Householder reflectors, under similar parameter budgets and parallelism constraints?
Answer:
Short answer: No — BOFT is not just “Givens/Householder with a different sparsity pattern.” All three families use sparse $2\times 2$ or rank-1 building blocks to build orthogonals, but they make different trade‑offs in degrees of freedom (expressivity), how parameters are reused (sharing), and the computational/parallel layout (depth, locality). BOFT deliberately picks the butterfly connectivity to get dense global mixing with far fewer free parameters and low algorithmic depth than typical Givens schedules or naïve Householder sequences at the same parameter budget, at the cost of restricting you to a structured subset (an inductive bias) of the orthogonal group.

Longer explanation (intuitive + technical, tied to the paper)

What is common and what differs
- Commonality: BOFT, Givens-rotation factorizations, and Householder reflectors all factor an orthogonal matrix into simpler orthogonal blocks. BOFT (as presented) is explicitly built from many small orthogonal block factors (the paper implements each butterfly component via $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks via Cayley/rotations), which is the same low-level building block type that Givens rotations use ($2\times 2$ rotations). Householder reflectors are different in form (reflections represented by a length‑$d$ vector) but also compose to give arbitrary orthogonals.
- Key difference: the graph of interactions (which coordinates are mixed when) and the reuse/sharing of parameters. BOFT imposes the butterfly connectivity (Cooley–Tukey style), which guarantees that $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ sparse factors suffice to connect every input coordinate to every output coordinate (dense connectivity) with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ free parameters if you use $2\times 2$ blocks. Givens schedules and Householder sequences can also realize arbitrary orthogonals, but to get the same global mixing with comparable parameter count or similar wall-clock parallelism you must choose schedules/compositions that typically require many more sequential steps or more parameters.

Expressivity under comparable parameter budgets
- Full expressivity: Both Givens rotations and Householder reflectors are universal: with an appropriate (possibly long) sequence you can represent any orthogonal matrix. A well-known fact is that $\mathcal{O}(d)$ Householder reflectors (or $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ independent Givens rotations) can represent any orthogonal; more precisely the orthogonal group has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, so you ultimately need that many free parameters for full coverage.
- BOFT (butterfly factorization) targets a different point: with $m$ stages and block size $b$, BOFT has about $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$ effective trainable parameters (the paper), and for the canonical butterfly $m\approx\log d$, $b=2$ gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. This is asymptotically far under the $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ DOF of the full group: BOFT therefore represents a structured (but large) subset of orthogonals. The paper’s Theorem and discussion note that by stacking multiple butterfly-like products (kaleidoscope constructions) you can approximate the entire orthogonal group, but that requires many more factors (and parameters), returning toward the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ regime. In short: at equal low parameter budgets (e.g. $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$), BOFT is more expressive than a single block‑diagonal OFT and typically more expressive than simple Givens schedules that only allow limited mixing, but it is not universal unless you increase the number of components accordingly.

Parameter sharing and inductive bias
- BOFT reuses the same structured pattern of small orthogonal blocks across log-depth stages, so each parameter participates in many coordinate mixes (strong parameter sharing). This yields a structured inductive bias: fast global mixing with few parameters, and the ability to exactly recover many classical fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT) as the paper emphasizes. That inductive bias is what the authors argue helps generalization in finetuning tasks.
- A Givens-factorization schedule that uses many distinct $2\times 2$ rotations with no structured reuse will have many more independent parameters for the same coverage; you can force sharing across rotations but then you are imposing a different inductive bias (and typically losing universality).
- Householder reflectors are high‑impact but less “local”: one reflector (parameterized by a full $d$-vector) can change many coordinates simultaneously, so sequence depth can be small (you can get full expressivity with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ reflectors), but each reflector has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters (so parameter count and memory cost are larger). Householder sequences therefore trade fewer sequential steps for more parameters (or conversely, for a strong parameter budget constraint you’d have to heavily compress or share Householder vectors, again imposing a different bias).

Computational layout and parallelism
- BOFT gives a small algorithmic depth: the canonical butterfly has $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages, each stage consisting of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ independent small blocks. This maps well to parallel hardware: within a stage many $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) blocks can be applied in parallel and the overall depth is logarithmic. Matrix-vector cost for a butterfly orthogonal $R$ is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, so it is both parameter- and compute-efficient in inference and (after fusion) in training. The paper highlights this depth and $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter count as its central advantage.
- Givens rotations: naive dense coverage via Givens rotations typically requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ rotations if you want universality, and many rotations are sequentially dependent; however, you can design parallel Givens schedules that do disjoint pair rotations in a stage (like bitonic sorting networks), achieving $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ depth for some structured mixes—but to get the same dense mixing and universality you again need many stages or many independent rotations, pushing parameters up. So depending on your chosen schedule, Givens can mimic BOFT but generally with worse parameter efficiency unless you adopt the same butterfly-like scheduling.
- Householder reflections: each reflector is dense, so applying a single reflector is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ unless you exploit structure; the required number of reflectors for universality is $\mathcal{O}(d)$, so cost is $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ naïvely for full composition, but optimized implementations use rank-1 updates to do each reflection in $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per vector (matrix-vector multiplication still $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ if you form full matrix). Parallelism is weaker because reflectors are typically applied sequentially and each touches all coordinates.

Practical consequences for finetuning (as in the paper)
The paper’s goal is parameter-efficient finetuning where you want strong, global re‑mappings of neuron geometry but with far fewer trainable parameters than full OFT. BOFT’s butterfly pattern balances global connectivity (dense effective $R$) with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters and low depth, making it a sweet spot: it is more parameter‑efficient than block‑diagonal OFT or naïve Givens schedules, and cheaper to apply than stacks of Householder reflectors with comparable expressivity. Importantly the BOFT construction uses Cayley/$2\times 2$ rotations so it preserves orthogonality throughout and allows identity initialization and multiplicative dropout as described in the paper, giving the stability benefits the authors emphasize.

Bottom line
BOFT is not merely “a different sparse orthogonal basis” in the sense of being equivalent to a single Givens schedule or a short Householder chain. It is a specific graph/topology choice (butterfly) that (a) achieves dense input$\to$output connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters and $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ depth, (b) shares parameters heavily across many coordinate mixes yielding a useful inductive bias for finetuning, and (c) can be made universal only by stacking many more butterflies (kaleidoscope), which then approaches the same $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter regime as Givens/Householder universality. Thus BOFT trades universality for a highly useful structured subset of orthogonals that is especially attractive under strict parameter and parallelism constraints, which is exactly the trade made and validated empirically in the paper.
