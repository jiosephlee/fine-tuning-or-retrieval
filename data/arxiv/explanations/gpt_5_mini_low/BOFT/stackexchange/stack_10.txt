\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Spectral norm preservation: Is \(\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\rVert_2=\lVert \mathbf{W}^0\rVert_2\) always true for BOFT?
Question:
The paper argues that BOFT preserves the spectral norm since it left-multiplies by an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Is there any caveat due to finite precision or the Cayley transform’s numerical errors? Also, does the same argument imply that all singular values are preserved if \(\mathbf{R}\) multiplies on the left (i.e., only the left singular vectors change), or just the largest one?
Answer:
Short answer: In exact arithmetic BOFT preserves the entire singular value spectrum of the pretrained weight matrix, so $ \lVert R W^0\rVert_2=\lVert W^0\rVert_2$ (and in fact every singular value is unchanged). In floating-point arithmetic there are practical caveats: the Cayley parameterization and repeated matrix products introduce rounding and conditioning errors so in practice you get near-preservation, not mathematically exact preservation. You should monitor and optionally re-project to enforce orthogonality if exactness is important.

Why it holds in theory. Let $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$ be an SVD of the pretrained weight matrix. If $R$ is orthogonal ($R^\top R = I$), then
$$
R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^\top.
$$
Because $R U$ is still orthogonal, the singular values are exactly the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$. Hence left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix leaves every singular value (not just the largest) unchanged; only the left singular vectors change. In particular the spectral norm (largest singular value) is preserved.

Why numerical issues can break exact preservation. BOFT implements $R$ as a product of orthogonal factors (butterfly components), each parameterized (in this paper) via the Cayley transform $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$. In exact arithmetic this yields an orthogonal matrix. In floating-point arithmetic two sources of deviation arise:

1) Rounding and accumulation: multiplying many factors and carrying out matrix inversions and multiplications accumulates floating-point round-off, so the computed $\hat R$ will satisfy $\hat R^\top\hat R\approx I$ but not exactly equal. The singular values of $\hat R W^0$ will therefore be close to, but not exactly, those of $W^0$.

2) Conditioning of the Cayley map: computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is sensitive if $I-Q$ is ill-conditioned. Although $Q$ is skew-symmetric and typically initialized small (so $I-Q$ is well conditioned early), during training entries of $Q$ may grow and make $(I-Q)$ poorly conditioned; inversion then amplifies rounding errors and can produce larger departures from orthogonality. (Note also that the Cayley map is undefined if $I-Q$ is singular, but that situation is unlikely if parameters are kept moderate; nevertheless it is a theoretical caution.)

Practical implications and recommendations. In practice BOFT will "preserve" spectral norm up to small numerical error; you should not assume exact equality in floating point. Useful safeguards are: monitor the orthogonality residual $\lVert \hat R^\top\hat R - I\rVert_F$ or $\lVert \hat R^\top\hat R - I\rVert_2$ and monitor the change in singular values of $W^0$ (or just its spectral norm). If you see drift, re-orthogonalize factors occasionally (e.g., via QR or polar decomposition on blocks), or project the composed $\hat R$ back to the nearest orthogonal matrix by polar decomposition. Using small parameter steps / lower learning rates on the skew parameters also reduces conditioning problems, and initializing $Q$ near zero (identity initialization) helps stability (the paper uses this).

Summary. The theoretical claim in the paper is correct: left-multiplying by an orthogonal $R$ preserves all singular values of $W^0$, so $ \lVert R W^0\rVert_2=\lVert W^0\rVert_2$. Numerically, Cayley-based factors and matrix products give only approximate orthogonality due to finite precision and possible inversion conditioning; in practice the preservation is near-exact but you may want to monitor and (if needed) correct orthogonality during training.
