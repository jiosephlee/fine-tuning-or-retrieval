\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How exactly does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality in BOFT?
Question:
The paper uses the Cayley parameterization \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top\) to ensure orthogonality for each butterfly component. I understand that for skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal when \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) is invertible. But I'm confused about: (1) Are there conditions under which \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) can become nearly singular during training (leading to numerical instability)? (2) Since each butterfly component is a block permutation of \(2\times 2\) (or \(2b\times 2b\)) orthogonal blocks, do we apply a separate Cayley transform to each small block, or a single Cayley to the full sparse matrix? (3) Does this parameterization cover all of \(O(d)\) or just a subset (because Cayley misses matrices with eigenvalue \(-1\)) and how is that handled in practice?
Answer:
Short answers first: (1) For real skew-symmetric $Q$ the Cayley map $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ is always well-defined ($I-Q$ is invertible) and is well behaved numerically in typical ranges; catastrophic singularity does not occur because real skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues. (2) BOFT applies the Cayley transform to each small orthogonal block (each $2\times2$ or $2b\times2b$ block in a butterfly component), not one global Cayley on the full sparse matrix. (3) The Cayley parameterization does not cover every matrix in $O(d)$: it omits orthogonal matrices having eigenvalue $-1$ (and also pure reflections with $\det=-1$ when using only Cayley from skew-symmetric $Q$). The paper handles this in practice by identity initialization, by composing multiple butterfly-orthogonal factors (and their transposes) which increases expressivity and—in the kaleidoscope limit—can represent the whole orthogonal group.

Explanation and why each point holds (intuition + brief technical detail, tied to the paper):

1) Why Cayley gives orthogonality and when $I-Q$ is invertible
The Cayley formula $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q=-Q^T$ is a classical parameterization that maps skew-symmetric $Q$ to orthogonal $R$. Proof sketch: take $R^T R$ and use $Q^T=-Q$ to verify $R^T R=I$ (algebra cancels cleanly). For invertibility of $I-Q$, note that any real skew-symmetric $Q$ has eigenvalues that are purely imaginary (if $v$ is an eigenvector then $Qv=i\lambda v$ with $\lambda$ real). Thus the eigenvalues of $I-Q$ are $1-i\lambda$, which never equal zero in the complex plane, so $I-Q$ is always invertible for any real skew-symmetric $Q$. Consequently there is no theoretical singularity of $I-Q$ caused by $Q$ being skew-symmetric.

Intuitively: skew-symmetric perturbations rotate in infinitesimal directions; subtracting such a skew operator from the identity cannot produce a zero eigenvalue because the imaginary part prevents cancellation with the real $1$.

Numerical stability: because the eigenvalues of $I-Q$ have magnitude $\sqrt{1+\lambda^2}\ge 1$, $I-Q$ is not ill-conditioned for moderate $\lambda$. That means typical training where $Q$ parameters are small (and indeed BOFT initializes $Q=0$) will not produce near-singularity. In practice, numerical issues are unlikely from “$I-Q$ nearly singular” per se. That said, very large entries in $Q$ can increase the condition number of the linear algebra operations (floating-point rounding), and extreme $Q$ could produce large intermediate values in computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$; so standard practical safeguards apply: initialize $Q$ near zero (the paper uses identity initialization via $Q=0$), use reasonable optimizer steps / learning rates, and optionally clip or regularize $Q$ magnitudes if you see instability. The paper specifically initializes Cayley skew-symmetric blocks at zero (identity $R$), which both prevents large initial $Q$ and avoids numerical pathologies early in training.

2) Local (block) Cayley vs. single global Cayley in BOFT
BOFT constructs $R$ as a product of many sparse orthogonal butterfly components, and each butterfly component is block-diagonal with small blocks (the paper emphasizes $2\times2$ blocks or $2b\times2b$ blocks). The text explicitly says they “parameterize each $2\times2$ orthogonal block (or each $2b\times2b$ block) to be orthogonal” and use Cayley on those small blocks (or simply use a 2D rotation for the $2\times2$ case). So the Cayley transform is applied at the block level to guarantee orthogonality of each sparse factor. Because the other butterfly components are permutations/blockwise permutations of that 2-block pattern, they are turned into orthogonal matrices similarly. This is computationally cheap and numerically stable since inverting $I-Q$ for small blocks is trivial and well-conditioned. Applying one global Cayley to the entire sparse matrix (i.e., forming a full skew-symmetric $Q$ with the global sparsity pattern and inverting $I-Q$) would be unnecessary and more expensive; the paper chooses the small-block parameterization.

3) Coverage of $O(d)$ and how BOFT addresses the Cayley limitation
The Cayley map from skew-symmetric $Q$ produces orthogonal matrices with no eigenvalue equal to $-1$; equivalently it maps to a connected subset of $O(d)$ (more precisely it parameterizes a dense subset of $SO(d)$ excluding matrices with eigenvalue $-1$). So by itself a single Cayley parameterization cannot produce an orthogonal matrix that has $-1$ as an eigenvalue, and in particular it cannot produce certain reflections ($\det=-1$) unless you add an explicit reflection factor.

How the paper deals with that: first, they use identity initialization ($Q=0$) so optimization stays in the “near-identity” region where missing $-1$ eigenvalues is not a practical limitation. More importantly, the paper shows that composing many structured orthogonal factors greatly enlarges the reachable set. Theorem 1 and the discussion of the kaleidoscope hierarchy state that products of butterfly matrices (and products involving transposes) can represent the entire orthogonal group when you allow enough layers/factors (they give an explicit composition that spans all orthogonal matrices in the limit: a kaleidoscope-style product of butterfly matrices and their transposes can express any orthogonal matrix). Thus BOFT trades single-factor completeness for a factorized, structured parameterization: with a sufficient number of components (and both forward and transposed factors as in the generalized form in the paper) the construction can approximate/represent matrices that a single Cayley block cannot. In practice the paper keeps block sizes small and composes multiple factors (and reports that this gives good expressivity while preserving parameter efficiency), and that suffices for finetuning tasks they study.

Practical notes tied to the paper
Because BOFT uses small-block Cayley with identity initialization, the two practical concerns you asked about are mitigated: $I-Q$ singularity is not an issue for real skew-symmetric $Q$; numerical instability is unlikely if $Q$ stays moderate, and identity init plus usual optimizer settings keep $Q$ moderate; expressivity limits of a single Cayley are handled by composing many orthogonal butterfly components (and the paper even points out that in the full kaleidoscope limit you can represent the whole $O(d)$). If one does need an explicit reflection ($\det=-1$) one can append a fixed diagonal reflection or allow one factor to be a learned permutation with a sign flip; the paper does not report this being necessary for their finetuning experiments.

Summary
Cayley guarantees orthogonality because it maps skew-symmetric $Q$ to orthogonal $R$ and $I-Q$ is always invertible for real skew-symmetric $Q$ (no true singularity). BOFT applies Cayley (or simple 2D rotations) at the small block level for each butterfly block. Cayley does not cover orthogonal matrices with eigenvalue $-1$, but BOFT overcomes that practical limitation by composing multiple orthogonal butterfly components (and in the kaleidoscope limit can represent all of $O(d)$), and the identity initialization used in the paper keeps training in a numerically stable region.

### Deriving the parameter count \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) for BOFT(m, b)
Question:
The paper states that BOFT(\(m,b\)) "takes \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size \(d\times n\)." Could someone provide a step-by-step derivation? Specifically: (1) For a single butterfly component with block size \(2b\times 2b\), how many free parameters does one orthogonal block have? (2) How many such blocks exist per component at width \(d\)? (3) How does orthogonality reduce the parameter count compared to counting nonzeros? (4) Why does multiplying by \(m\) components give the \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) figure?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s formula comes from counting the intrinsic degrees of freedom of orthogonal $2b$-by-$2b$ blocks, how many such blocks appear in a width-$d$ layer, and then multiplying by the number of butterfly components $m$. A careful bookkeeping of the paper’s naming convention (their reported BOFT$(m,b)$ uses $2b$-by-$2b$ orthogonal blocks internally) yields

$$
\text{total effective params} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
$$

Derivation (step‑by‑step and intuitive):

1) Free parameters in one orthogonal $2b$-by-$2b$ block.  
An orthogonal matrix of size $K$ has exactly the dimension of the orthogonal group $O(K)$ as its degrees of freedom, namely $K(K-1)/2$. This is easy to see: an orthogonal matrix is determined by orthonormal column directions, and the constraints reduce the naive $K^2$ entries down to $K(K-1)/2$ independent parameters (equivalently the space of skew‑symmetric generators has that dimension, which is exactly what Cayley parameterization uses). For a block of size $2b$ we therefore have

$$
\text{DOF per block} \;=\; \frac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2} \;=\; b(2b-1).
$$

Intuition: a full $2b$-by-$2b$ dense matrix has $(2b)^2$ numbers, but the orthogonality constraints remove $2b$ constraints on column norms and $(2b)(2b-1)/2$ constraints on pairwise orthogonality; the remaining independent parameters equal the group dimension above.

2) Number of such blocks per butterfly component at width $d$.  
Each butterfly component (as defined in the paper’s block-butterfly construction) is block-diagonal with blocks of size $2b$-by-$2b$. Therefore the number of blocks in a $d$-by-$d$ component is

$$
\text{blocks per component} \;=\; \frac{d}{2b},
$$

assuming $d$ is a multiple of $2b$ (as in the paper’s radix-2 construction).

3) Why orthogonality reduces the parameter count versus counting nonzeros.  
If you counted nonzeros naively you would say each $2b$-by-$2b$ dense block contributes $(2b)^2$ parameters. Orthogonality couples those entries: they are not independent. Using Cayley/skew‑symmetric parameterization, each orthogonal block is generated from a skew‑symmetric matrix of size $2b$ whose number of independent entries is exactly $(2b)(2b-1)/2$. So orthogonality replaces the naive $(2b)^2$ free entries with the much smaller $b(2b-1)$. Intuitively, orthogonality enforces many linear and quadratic constraints among entries, drastically lowering the degrees of freedom.

4) Putting it together and the role of $m$ components.  
Multiply the DOF per block by the number of blocks per component to get the effective trainable parameters in one butterfly component:

$$
\text{params per component} \;=\; \frac{d}{2b}\cdot b(2b-1) \;=\; \frac{d(2b-1)}{2}.
$$

BOFT composes $m$ such components (each component is an orthogonal block-structured factor and you train the parameters of all $m$ factors), so total effective trainable parameters are

$$
\text{total} \;=\; m\cdot \frac{d(2b-1)}{2}.
$$

Now about the paper’s notation: the authors choose to report BOFT as BOFT$(m,b)$ where their internal blocks are size $2b$-by-$2b$ (they write “we denote BOFT with $\bm{R}(m,\tfrac{b}{2})$ as BOFT$(m,b)$”). If you rewrite the last expression in terms of the paper’s reported scalar $b$ (i.e., substitute $b_{\text{internal}} = b/2$), the factor $2b-1$ becomes $b-1$ and you obtain the stated formula

$$
\text{total} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
$$

which is exactly the paper’s claimed effective trainable parameter count for BOFT$(m,b)$.

Compact recap: one $2b$-by-$2b$ orthogonal block has $\tfrac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, there are $d/(2b)$ such blocks per component, orthogonality reduces free parameters from $(2b)^2$ to that group dimension, and multiplying by $m$ components (and applying the paper’s block-size naming convention) produces $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

### What does “effective trainable parameters” mean when edges are constrained by orthogonality?
Question:
In the information transmission view, the paper counts "free edges" and notes that orthogonality implies dependencies (e.g., in a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal matrix, one zero forces another zero). How should I interpret the statement that some edges "are not trainable" (e.g., must be \(\pm 1\))? Is there a formal mapping from edges to parameters after imposing orthogonality constraints, and how does this mapping yield the final parameter count?
Answer:
Short answer: “Effective trainable parameters” means the number of independent degrees of freedom that remain after you impose the orthogonality constraints. Orthogonality is a nonlinear constraint that couples many matrix entries, so counting raw non-zero entries (edges) overestimates how many independent parameters you can actually tune. Formally you should count the dimension of the orthogonal group on each independent block you parametrize; that dimension is what the paper calls the effective trainable parameters and is how the final formula in the paper is obtained.

Why some edges “are not trainable” (e.g. forced to ±1)
- Orthogonality forces rows (and columns) to be orthonormal. If a sparse pattern forces each row and column to have exactly one nonzero entry (a permutation-like pattern), then that nonzero must have squared magnitude $1$ to satisfy orthonormality, so it can only be $\pm 1$ (up to sign). Those entries are not continuous degrees of freedom you can tune: they are fixed by the orthogonality constraint and the sparsity pattern.
- More generally, zeroing a particular entry in an orthogonal matrix typically induces other zeros or fixed relations elsewhere. The $2\times 2$ example in the paper is the simplest illustration: impose a zero at $(1,1)$ and orthonormality of rows/cols forces particular values (and possibly other zeros) at the remaining positions. So a “missing edge” in the information-transmission graph can imply additional fixed structure.

Formal mapping from edges (sparsity pattern) to independent parameters
- Take a factorization $R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$ where each $B_i$ has a prescribed sparsity (the “edges” between adjacent levels). The sparsity pattern tells you which entries of each $B_i$ can be nonzero. To know how many independent trainable parameters you actually have you must:
  1. Partition each $B_i$ into independent orthogonal blocks (the paper does this with block butterfly components).
  2. For a block of size $k$ that is constrained to be orthogonal, the number of independent real parameters equals the dimension of $O(k)$, namely $k(k-1)/2$. This is the formal count of degrees of freedom for that block.
  3. Sum these degrees of freedom across all orthogonal blocks of all components. That sum is the number of effective trainable parameters.
- In practice the paper uses Cayley parameterization ($R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew-symmetric) or explicit $2\times 2$ rotations for the smallest blocks. Cayley/skew-symmetric parametrizations are just ways to realize the $k(k-1)/2$ degrees of freedom by parametrizing the independent skew-symmetric entries of $Q$.

How the paper’s counting formula arises
- Suppose each butterfly component is built from block-diagonal pieces where each block has size $b$, and there are $d$ dimensions total. Each block (of size $b$) has $b(b-1)/2$ independent parameters if constrained to be orthogonal. There are $d/b$ such blocks in a component, so one component contributes $\frac{d}{b}\cdot\frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2}$ parameters.
- BOFT composes $m$ such components, so total effective trainable parameters $= m\cdot\frac{d(b-1)}{2}$. This is exactly the formula quoted in the paper: $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$.
- Special cases check: $b = d$ and $m = 1$ gives the full orthogonal matrix count $d(d-1)/2$ (the usual $O(d)$ dimension). $b = 2$ and $m = \log d$ (the radix-2 butterfly) gives $O(d\log d)$ parameters as the paper emphasizes.

Intuition and broader connection
- Counting nonzeros (edges) is a combinatorial view but orthogonality drastically reduces independent freedom: you should always convert a sparsity pattern into a block partition and then count the manifold dimension of the orthogonal blocks. That is the right way to measure “how many real parameters you can tune.”
- This is a general principle: constrained matrix families live on manifolds whose dimension (not raw entry-count) is the right complexity measure. For orthogonal matrices the manifold dimension is $k(k-1)/2$ for an unconstrained $k\times k$ orthogonal block; BOFT reduces parameter count by trading many small orthogonal blocks across multiple components (butterfly factors) to still achieve dense overall connectivity while keeping the sum of block-dimensions small.

So: “not trainable” means not an independent continuous degree of freedom once orthogonality and sparsity are fixed. The formal mapping is: sparsity pattern → orthogonal block decomposition → sum of dimensions $k(k-1)/2$ over blocks → effective trainable parameter count, which gives the paper’s $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$ formula.

### How does the butterfly factorization ensure dense connectivity with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters?
Question:
The paper claims that stacking \(m=\log d\) butterfly components gives a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) using \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters. Could someone explain in graph-theoretic terms why every source node can reach every sink node after \(\log d\) butterfly layers? A small worked example for \(d=8\) would be very helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: the butterfly factorization is exactly the FFT / butterfly communication network viewed as a layered directed graph. Each butterfly layer mixes pairs of coordinates at a fixed stride so that after $m=\log_2 d$ layers the network can combine (and therefore route information from) every source coordinate to every sink coordinate. Graph-theoretically, each layer provides edges that flip one binary digit of the index; composing all $\log_2 d$ digits lets you reach any destination. Because each layer only uses $\mathcal{O}(d)$ $2\times2$ blocks, the full product uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

Explanation and $d=8$ worked example (intuition + technical detail). The paper models the factorization
$\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$
as information flowing through a $d\times(m+1)$ grid of nodes: the first column are source nodes (input coordinates), the last are sink nodes (output coordinates), and each $\mathbf{B}_\ell$ gives the edges from level $\ell$ to $\ell+1$. A dense $\mathbf{R}$ means every source has at least one path to every sink.

The radix-2 butterfly component at a given layer is a block-diagonal matrix of $2\times2$ mixing blocks whose pattern is a permutation of the adjacent-pair mixings used in the FFT. Concretely, for $d=2^N$ the usual (radix-2) three layers for $d=8$ are:

- Layer 1 (mix stride 1): pairwise mixes $(0,1)$, $(2,3)$, $(4,5)$, $(6,7)$.
- Layer 2 (mix stride 2): pairwise mixes $(0,2)$, $(1,3)$, $(4,6)$, $(5,7)$.
- Layer 3 (mix stride 4): pairwise mixes $(0,4)$, $(1,5)$, $(2,6)$, $(3,7)$.

Here indices are $0\ldots7$. Each mix is a $2\times2$ orthogonal block that allows information to flow between the two indices in that pair. Think of each layer as allowing you to toggle one particular bit in the binary index of a coordinate: layer 1 lets you mix indices that differ in bit $0$, layer 2 mixes those that differ in bit $1$, and layer 3 mixes those that differ in bit $2$.

Why this yields dense connectivity: represent source index and sink index in binary with $3$ bits. Any path from source to sink can be realized by, at each layer, choosing whether to use the local $2\times2$ mixing to flip the target bit or to keep it. More formally, after layer $\ell$ you can reach any index that matches the first $\ell$ (least significant) bits you have chosen; continuing through $\ell=1\ldots m$ lets you set all $m$ bits arbitrarily. Thus for any source index $s$ and any target index $t$ there exists a sequence of local choices (use the mixing in each relevant pair) yielding a path from $s$ to $t$. In the $d=8$ example, take source $s=2$ (binary $010$) and target $t=5$ (binary $101$). Layer-by-layer you can move:

- Layer 1 (bit0): $2$ ($010$) can mix with $3$ ($011$) if you want bit0=$1$, or stay if you want bit0=$0$. To reach $5$ whose bit0=$1$, use block $(2,3)$ to go to $3$.
- Layer 2 (bit1): now at $3$ ($011$), layer 2 mixes $(1,3)$ and $(0,2)$ etc; to get bit1=$0$ (target has bit1=$0$), choose the appropriate branch (use or skip the $(1,3)$ mixing) to end up with index whose bit1 is $0$.
- Layer 3 (bit2): finally control the most significant bit via the $(0,4),(1,5),(2,6),(3,7)$ blocks to set bit2=$1$ and arrive at $5$.

This constructive argument shows a path exists for any $s\to t$, so the composed matrix $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_3\mathbf{B}_2\mathbf{B}_1$ has no structural zeros — it is dense.

Parameter count: every layer is made of $d/2$ independent $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks (or $d/2$ blocks of size $2b\times2b$ in the block-generalization). Each $2\times2$ orthogonal block has one degree of freedom (an angle), so each layer contributes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ free parameters. With $m=\log_2 d$ layers the total free parameters scale as $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, matching the claim in the paper.

Broader connection: this is the same combinatorial property that makes the FFT $O(d\log d)$: the butterfly network achieves full pairwise mixing (global connectivity) by composing local pairwise mixes that each handle one binary digit. The paper uses this network to trade parameter count for depth: many sparse orthogonal factors (each cheap) compose to give a dense orthogonal transform (expressive), yielding parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

### Relationship between BOFT(m, b) and OFT: Why is m=1 equivalent to block-diagonal OFT(b)?
Question:
The paper states BOFT(1, b) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\). I’m missing the exact correspondence: (1) How does a single butterfly component with block size \(b\) reduce to a block-diagonal orthogonal transform? (2) Are permutations disabled in the \(m=1\) case, or do they cancel out? A concrete illustration for \(d=8\), \(b=2\) would clarify.
Answer:
Short answer: because with a single butterfly component the BOFT factor is literally a block‑diagonal matrix, there is no cross‑block mixing to produce off‑block entries, so $BOFT(1,b)$ is exactly the block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$. No “cancelling permutations” are needed — permutations that appear in deeper (multiple‑component) butterfly factorizations are not present when $m=1$, so the transform remains block‑diagonal.

Explanation tied to the paper’s definitions. The paper defines a block (scalar) butterfly factor for size $k$ as
$$
B^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2)\\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)\end{bmatrix},
$$
and a block‑butterfly component $\tilde B^b(d,k)$ as a block‑diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are such butterfly factors (with each scalar entry replaced by a $b\times b$ block when using the block generalization). The BOFT orthogonal matrix is built as a product of $m$ such components:
$$
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde B^b_{(d,i)},
$$
where $\tilde B^b_{(d,i)}=\tilde B^b(d,2^{m-i+1})$.

Now set $m=1$. There is only one factor, and the formula gives $\tilde B^b_{(d,1)}=\tilde B^b(d,2)$. By construction $\tilde B^b(d,2)$ is block‑diagonal: it is a diagonal stack of the size‑2 butterfly factors. But a size‑2 butterfly factor (scalar version) is a $2\times 2$ matrix whose four scalar diagonal blocks become four $b\times b$ blocks in the block version. Concretely the single component is a direct sum of independent $2b\times 2b$ blocks that do not interact with other blocks, and if you parameterize each such $2b\times 2b$ block as a pair of independent $b\times b$ orthogonal blocks (the Cayley or rotation parameterization the paper uses), the whole matrix is block‑diagonal with block size $b$. That is exactly the OFT block‑diagonal parameterization $\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ with $b r=d$.

Concrete illustration for $d=8, b=2$. Take $d=8$ and $b=2$. Then $r=d/b=4$ and block‑diagonal OFT uses
$$
R_{\text{OFT}}=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,R_2,R_3,R_4),
\qquad R_i\in\mathbb R^{2\times 2}.
$$
For BOFT with $m=1$ we get the single component $\tilde B^2(8,2)$. A scalar $2\times 2$ butterfly factor looks like
$$
B^F(2)=\begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{bmatrix},
$$
and the block version replaces $a,b,c,d$ by $2\times 2$ blocks $A,B,C,D$. The full $\tilde B^2(8,2)$ is block‑diagonal with four such $2\times 2$ block‑blocks along the diagonal, i.e.
$$
R(1,2)=\operatorname{diag}\big(B^F_1(2)_{\text{block}},\,B^F_2(2)_{\text{block}},\,B^F_3(2)_{\text{block}},\,B^F_4(2)_{\text{block}}\big),
$$
and each $B^F_j(2)_{\text{block}}$ is itself a $4\times 4$ matrix built from four $2\times 2$ subblocks. If you parameterize them so there is no coupling between distinct $2\times 2$ subblocks across the different block positions, the overall $8\times 8$ matrix decomposes into four independent $2\times 2$ blocks exactly matching OFT with block size $b=2$. Thus $BOFT(1,2) =$ block‑diagonal OFT with 4 blocks of size 2.

About permutations: the “permutations” that are important in a full butterfly factorization appear when you multiply several components (different $\tilde B^b_{(d,i)}$) so that the sparsity pattern and block alignments get mixed across stages; these permutations produce cross‑block connectivity and thus can produce a dense final matrix when $m$ is large enough. For $m=1$ there is no sequence of stages to permute across, so no cross‑block mixing occurs — nothing needs to cancel or be disabled, it simply never appears. Hence the equivalence in the paper is exact: $BOFT(1,b)$ reduces to block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$.

### How do we implement BOFT when \(d\) is not a power of two?
Question:
The construction assumes \(d=2^N\). In practice, layers often have widths like 768 or 3072. What are the recommended strategies: padding to the next power of two, mixed-radix butterfly, or grouping into near-equal powers with permutations? How does each choice affect orthogonality, parameter count, and runtime?
Answer:
Short answer: there is no single “official” fix in the paper, but all three strategies you suggested are practical and used in related butterfly literature. Which to pick depends on how much extra parameter/runtime budget you can tolerate and whether you want a very simple implementation. Below I summarize each option, explain why it works (and how to keep matrices orthogonal), and compare the effect on parameter count and runtime.

1) Pad to the next power of two (simplest)
If $d$ is not a power of two, embed your $d$-dimensional layer into a larger dimension $d_{\text{pad}} = 2^N \ge d$, build a standard radix‑2 butterfly on $d_{\text{pad}}$, and either (a) never touch the extra coordinates (keep those rows/cols of the pretrained $W$ frozen/identity) or (b) learn the full $d_{\text{pad}}$ orthogonal and slice the first $d$ coordinates for use. Orthogonality: the butterfly factors are still orthogonal on $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{pad}}}$, so the composed $R$ is orthogonal; when you slice back to $d$ dimensions you get a valid linear map applied to the original coordinates (if you initialized the extra subspace as identity you preserve zero-init behavior). Parameter count and runtime: both scale with $d_{\text{pad}}$ instead of $d$, so you pay the extra $\mathcal{O}\big(d_{\text{pad}}\log d_{\text{pad}}\big)$ parameters and $\mathcal{O}\big(d_{\text{pad}}\log d_{\text{pad}}\big)$ per‑step cost. Practical note: padding is the easiest to implement (no change to factorization code). If $d_{\text{pad}}$ is not much larger than $d$ this is often acceptable; if $d$ is near the next power boundary (e.g. $769 \to 1024$) the overhead can be large.

2) Mixed‑radix / non‑radix‑2 butterflies (more elegant, no padding)
Generalize the butterfly decomposition to mixed radices by using Cooley–Tukey style factorization for composite sizes: factor $d$ into small integers ($d = r_1\cdot r_2\cdot\ldots$) and build sparse factor blocks that permute/mix groups of size $r_i$ instead of always splitting by $2$. In practice one implements butterfly “factors” that are block permutations of $r\times r$ blocks (or $2b\times 2b$ blocks as the paper describes) and ensure each block is orthogonal (e.g., Cayley on each small block). Orthogonality: preserved as long as every factor block is constrained to be orthogonal (same trick as for $2\times 2$ Cayley blocks). Parameter count and runtime: roughly $\mathcal{O}\!\big(d \sum_i \log_{r_i} d\big)$ but in practice comparable to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ with a smaller constant; it grows with the number and sizes of factors. Mixed‑radix avoids padding overhead but requires implementing general block factors and permutations; it is the most parameter‑efficient way to avoid padding if you can factor $d$ into convenient small integers.

3) Partition into near‑power‑of‑two blocks + permutations (practical hybrid)
Split the $d$ coordinates into several groups whose sizes are powers of two (e.g., split $3072$ as $1024+1024+1024$ or $2048+1024$), apply separate radix‑2 butterfly factors within each block and add inter‑block permutations/merge layers that connect blocks so the overall transform becomes dense. In the paper’s terminology this is like using block butterfly components (they already allow blocks of size $b$) and adding permutations / kaleidoscope compositions to increase expressivity. Orthogonality: maintained if each block factor and each permutation is orthogonal (permutations are orthogonal by construction). Parameter count and runtime: parameter cost is sum over blocks $\mathcal{O}\!\big(\sum_i b_i\log b_i\big)$ and extra cost for the permutation/merge layers (usually negligible). This scheme often sits between naive padding and fully mixed‑radix in both complexity and implementation difficulty. It is attractive when $d$ can be expressed as a small sum of powers of two.

How each choice affects the three things you asked about

Orthogonality: All three methods can preserve orthogonality. Padding preserves orthogonality in the larger space; mixed‑radix and block partitioning preserve orthogonality by enforcing orthogonality on each factor block (use Cayley or local rotations on each small block, as the paper does). The only pitfall is if you implement ad hoc dense connections but forget to constrain factors to be orthogonal—then you lose the key spectral/stability guarantees.

Parameter count: Padding increases parameters to $\mathcal{O}\big(d_{\text{pad}}\log d_{\text{pad}}\big)$. Mixed‑radix and carefully chosen block partitionings keep parameter count closer to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ with smaller constants (no wasted padded coordinates). The paper’s block‑butterfly parameterization $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ already allows a block size $b$; choosing $b>2$ can help handle dimensions not power of two without substantial extra parameters.

Runtime: Runtime scales with the working dimension used by the factorization. Padding costs you extra runtime proportional to $(d_{\text{pad}}-d)\log d_{\text{pad}}$. Mixed‑radix and block partitioning reduce extra runtime versus padding, but they require additional bookkeeping for variable block sizes and permutations. The paper notes a slight runtime overhead relative to plain OFT because BOFT multiplies $m$ sparse factors each forward/backward pass; any of these approaches retain that multiplicative cost, but padding makes it worse by increasing $d$ in each factor.

Practical recommendations
If you want minimal engineering and the power‑of‑two slack is small, pad to the next power of two, initialize the padded subspace as identity (zero skew for Cayley) and slice back at runtime. This is robust, preserves the identity initialization and orthogonality, and lets you reuse a radix‑2 implementation.

If you care about parameter/runtime efficiency and are willing to add some implementation work, implement block butterflies (the paper already describes block components) and either: (a) use mixed‑radix factors based on the factorization of $d$, or (b) partition $d$ into multiple power‑of‑two blocks and introduce orthogonal permutations/merge layers (kaleidoscope composition). Both give you the same orthogonality guarantees but avoid the padding waste. The block size $b$ from the paper is a useful lever: choosing $b$ so that $b$ divides $d$ (or choosing multiple block sizes summing to $d$) reduces implementation complexity.

One final note connecting to the paper: BOFT already contemplates block butterfly components and kaleidoscope-style compositions (see Theorem and the generalized composition in the paper). That machinery is directly applicable to the non‑power‑of‑two case: use block components ($b>2$) or compositions of multiple butterfly-like orthogonal factors to cover arbitrary $d$ while preserving orthogonality and keeping parameter growth subquadratic.

Summary: padding is easiest but wastes parameters/runtime; mixed‑radix or block‑butterfly + permutations are more efficient and keep orthogonality but need slightly more implementation work. The paper’s block butterfly parameterization and kaleidoscope generalization are exactly the tooling you need to implement the latter approaches.

### Is the BOFT dropout (replacing sub-blocks by identity) unbiased and does it preserve orthogonality?
Question:
The multiplicative Dropout randomly picks a subset of butterfly components and diagonal blocks within components and replaces those sub-blocks by identities. (1) Is the resulting matrix exactly orthogonal during training when some sub-blocks are identity and others are learned? (2) Is there an argument that the expected transformed weight equals the un-dropped case under some scaling (like standard Dropout)? (3) How does this interact with the Cayley parameterization and gradient flow through inactive (identity) sub-blocks?
Answer:
Short answers up front: (1) Yes — replacing some butterfly sub-blocks (or whole butterfly components) by identity leaves the product orthogonal exactly. (2) No simple unbiased-scaling analogue of standard dropout exists that will make the expected transformed weight equal to the no-drop case while keeping each sample orthogonal. (3) With the Cayley parameterization, a dropped (identity) sub-block does not influence the forward pass and therefore receives no gradient signal while it is inactive; it only receives gradients on steps when it is not dropped.

Explanation and reasoning, with the paper’s setup in mind:

1) Orthogonality when some factors are identity
BOFT represents the final orthogonal transform as a product of orthogonal factors (butterfly components) $R = B_m B_{m-1} \dots B_1$, and each factor is constrained to be orthogonal (the paper enforces this via Cayley / $2\times2$ rotations). If you replace some of those factors (or some block-diagonal sub-blocks inside a factor) with the identity, you are simply inserting identity matrices into a product of orthogonal matrices. The product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, and inserting identities does not change that fact. Thus every sample of the multiplicative-dropout-modified $R$ is exactly orthogonal during training.

2) Expectation / unbiasedness
In conventional (additive or standard multiplicative) dropout on additive updates, a simple scaling (e.g. dividing by the keep probability) makes the expected update equal to the full-update (an unbiased estimator). BOFT’s dropout is multiplicative over factors: it randomly sets factors (or sub-blocks) to $I$. The expected final matrix is
$\displaystyle \mathbb{E}[R_{\text{dropout}}] = \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_i M_i B_i\right]$
where each $M_i$ is a random mask that is either $I$ (dropped) or the identity-on-non-dropped-blocks (retained). Because expectation does not distribute over a product ($\mathbb{E}[\text{product}] \neq \text{product of }\mathbb{E}\text{'s}$ in general), there is no general scalar rescaling of the retained factors that will make $\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{dropout}}]$ equal to the deterministic $R = \prod_i B_i$. Attempts to rescale retained orthogonal factors (e.g. multiply a retained orthogonal block by $\tfrac{1}{1-p}$) would also destroy orthogonality (a scalar multiple of an orthogonal matrix is not orthogonal except for $\pm 1$). Therefore you cannot have all three at once: (a) keep each sampled matrix orthogonal, (b) keep a simple per-factor scaling, and (c) make the expectation equal to the no-drop product. Concretely, the multiplicative dropout in BOFT trades unbiasedness of the final matrix in expectation for the benefits of stochastic structural regularization while preserving per-sample orthogonality.

If one cared only about unbiasedness (not preserving orthogonality), you could try different estimators (e.g. work in parameter space and compensate there), but those will generally break the orthogonality constraint of each sampled forward matrix or require complicated, factor-dependent corrections (and still generally not produce a closed-form simple correction because of the product structure).

3) Cayley parameterization and gradient flow through inactive blocks
BOFT parameterizes each block/factor as orthogonal via Cayley: $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew-symmetric (or parameterize small $2\times2$ rotations). Multiplicative dropout replaces a factor (or a block) by $I$ at the forward pass. If you implement dropout by masking the forward factor to $I$ while leaving its parameters ($Q$) as-is, the forward output on that example does not depend on those parameters, so the gradient of the loss with respect to those parameters is zero for that training step. In other words, masked/inactive sub-blocks are effectively “frozen” on that minibatch: they do not affect the loss and therefore receive no gradient signal until a step where they are not masked. If instead you actually set the parameters themselves to the zero-skew-symmetric ($Q=0$) during drop and keep them that way permanently, you would prevent future updates; but the usual stochastic-dropout implementation only masks forward evaluation, allowing the parameters to remain and be updated on non-dropped steps.

Because Cayley uses $Q$ inside a nonlinear rational map $(I-Q)^{-1}$, gradients when a block is active will flow through the Cayley map into $Q$ in the usual way. There is an implementation caveat: the Cayley map requires invertibility of $(I-Q)$; in practice $Q$ is small (and commonly initialized at zero), so this is satisfied; masking to identity corresponds to using $Q=0$ in the forward pass, and when unmasked the Cayley map produces an orthogonal block and gradients propagate through its inverse operation as normal.

Intuition and practical implications
The multiplicative dropout in BOFT is explicitly designed to keep every sampled transform orthogonal (to preserve spectral/stability guarantees), so it purposefully sacrifices simple unbiasedness of the expected matrix. The training dynamics therefore are those of a stochastic structural regularizer: parameters for a block only get updated on the steps that block is active, which increases robustness and reduces overfitting similarly to conventional dropout, but the averaging effect is not a simple scaled identity. In practice this is often fine: the paper also uses identity initialization ($Q=0$), so early training starts near the pretrained model and only gradually moves factors away from identity when they are not dropped.

If you need an estimator whose expectation equals the full $R$, you would have to either (a) work with additive parameter updates (LoRA style) where unbiased scaling is easy, or (b) accept non-orthogonal scaling corrections or complicated factor-dependent debiasing that will generally break per-sample orthogonality and complicate training.

Summary: replacing sub-blocks by identity preserves exact orthogonality per-sample; there is no simple orthogonality-preserving scaling to make the expected transformed matrix equal the no-drop product; and under Cayley masking, masked sub-blocks do not contribute to forward/backward on that example and so receive no gradients until they are active.

### Expressivity theorem: How does the kaleidoscope hierarchy span the full orthogonal group?
Question:
Theorem 1 mentions that multiplying a sequence of butterfly matrices \(\mathbf{B}_{i,1}(d)\mathbf{B}_{i,2}(d)^\top\) for \(i=1,\dots,d-1\) can represent all orthogonal matrices. Could someone sketch or reference a proof? Specifically: (1) What are the key properties of butterfly matrices that allow this spanning? (2) Is there a constructive procedure to decompose any \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) into that product? (3) How does the depth and parameter count compare with Givens or Householder factorizations that are known to generate \(O(d)\)?
Answer:
Short answer: the kaleidoscope / butterfly construction can span the full orthogonal group because butterfly factors are sparse orthogonal blocks (essentially disjoint $2\times2$ rotations) arranged so that, when you stack enough specially permuted layers and allow alternating transposes, every pairwise degree of freedom (every required Givens rotation) can be realized. There is a constructive reduction from a standard Givens (or QR/Givens-elimination) decomposition to a sequence of butterfly stages; the formal statement and proof appear in Dao et al. (”Kaleidoscope”, cited in the paper). Below I sketch the intuition and the constructive idea, and then compare depth / parameter counts to Givens and Householder factorizations.

1) Why butterfly matrices can span $O(d)$
Butterfly matrices are block-sparse orthogonal matrices built from many disjoint $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks (or $b\times b$ blocks) that are permuted across layers in a radix-2 (multiscale) pattern. Two properties matter:

- Local rotations with global reach by composition. Each butterfly layer performs only local $2\times2$ orthogonal transforms (rotations/reflections) on carefully chosen pairs, so one layer mixes only nearby coordinates. But the pattern of pairings changes between layers (the radix-2 permutations), so after several layers information from any input coordinate has been mixed with many others. Repeating these layers at multiple scales yields global mixing.

- Parallelizable packing of Givens rotations. Because each butterfly layer applies a set of disjoint $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks, it can realize many Givens rotations in parallel. Over multiple layers with different pairings, you can implement any schedule of Givens rotations required to reduce an arbitrary orthogonal matrix to identity (or generate any orthogonal matrix starting from identity).

Intuitively: think of Givens decomposition which expresses any $R\in O(d)$ as a product of planar rotations that zero out entries (as in QR with Givens). The butterfly network provides a fixed wiring that allows you to place those planar rotations into a small number of stages per scale; by repeating the multiscale pattern enough times (and allowing both a layer and its transpose / inverse), you can realize every needed rotation. This is the heart of the kaleidoscope result: permutations + layers of $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks can implement the full set of pairwise planar rotations.

2) Constructive procedure (high-level)
A direct, constructive route is obtained by starting from a Givens decomposition and then re-scheduling its planar rotations onto butterfly stages. Concretely:

- Use any standard Givens factorization: any $R\in O(d)$ can be written as a product of at most $d(d-1)/2$ Givens rotations, each acting on a coordinate pair $(i,j)$.

- Order those rotations so they eliminate entries in a triangular fashion (this is exactly what QR-with-Givens does). That ordering has a natural dependency graph: some rotations commute (act on disjoint pairs) and thus can be merged into the same stage.

- Map each stage of commuting Givens rotations onto a butterfly component by using the butterfly pairing (radix-2 pairings). When a needed rotation pair is not exactly one of the butterfly pairs, we use permutations (allowed in kaleidoscope: permuted butterfly components) and possibly route the rotation through multiple layers: a small sequence of butterfly pairings can move a coordinate to the position where the required Givens pair is adjacent, apply the $2\times2$ rotation, and then move it back.

- By carefully scheduling permutations and using up to $l=d-1$ repetitions of the multiscale butterfly pairings (with alternating transposes as in the paper’s product $\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}\mathbf{B}_{d-1,2}^\top\cdots$), every Givens rotation can be realized. The Dao et al. kaleidoscope paper gives the formal algorithm and counting that shows completeness.

So, while the paper does not give the full step-by-step code, the constructive path is: Givens decomposition → group commuting rotations into stages → embed each stage into permuted butterfly layers (using the radix-2 pairings and permutations to bring pairs together). That is exactly the approach cited by the paper (kaleidoscope hierarchy).

3) Depth and parameter-count comparison with Givens / Householder
Givens rotations and Householder reflectors are the classical unstructured ways to span $O(d)$:

- Givens: an arbitrary orthogonal matrix requires up to $d(d-1)/2$ independent planar rotations (degrees of freedom). Implementing them naively needs that many scalar rotation parameters. They can be organized into roughly $d$ stages if you schedule non-overlapping rotations in parallel, but total number of scalar parameters is $\Theta(d^2)$.

- Householder: any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of at most $d$ Householder reflectors; each reflector is specified by a vector of length up to $d$, so again the raw parameter count needed to realize arbitrary orthogonals scales like $\Theta(d^2)$ (though with different constant factors). Householder is attractive computationally because only $O(d^2)$ work/memory is needed and the number of factors is only $O(d)$.

- Butterfly / Kaleidoscope: a single butterfly factorization with $m = \log d$ layers (and block size $b = 2$) uses $O(d\log d)$ free parameters and therefore represents a strict subset of $O(d)$ (it is not universal). The paper’s Theorem says that if you compose many such butterfly matrices in the kaleidoscope pattern — specifically the product form written as $\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}\mathbf{B}_{d-1,2}^\top\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,2}^\top$ (equivalently $l = d-1$ repetitions with both a factor and its transpose per repetition in the paper) — then you can represent the entire orthogonal group. Practically, that means universality is recovered only after composing $O(d)$ butterfly “blocks” (each block itself has $O(d\log d)$ parameters if using a full multiscale butterfly), so the total free-parameter budget becomes $O(d^2\log d)$ in that naive parameter counting. However, the kaleidoscope construction is more subtle: it organizes the $O(d^2)$ degrees of freedom into structured layers, and the literature (Dao et al.) shows you can achieve universality with $l = d-1$ repetitions of relatively small building blocks (so asymptotically you still need $\Theta(d^2)$ degrees of freedom overall to get full $O(d)$, but arranged in a highly structured way).

In short: the butterfly factorization gives great parameter savings ($O(d\log d)$) only when you restrict to a single multiscale butterfly (good for a compact parameterization and useful inductive bias). To regain full universality you must stack many such blocks (the kaleidoscope hierarchy), and the total number of effective parameters required scales back to the same $\Theta(d^2)$ law as Givens/Householder—just structurally organized. The advantage of the kaleidoscope arrangement is not asymptotic parameter reduction for universality but a structured, parallelizable wiring that permits more parameter-efficient approximations (good empirical interpolation between expressivity and regularity) and algorithmic benefits (parallel application of many rotations).

References and where to look in the literature
The paper itself cites Dao et al. (”Kaleidoscope” and related “learning fast transforms”) for the formal expressivity results; those papers contain the full constructive proofs mapping Givens decompositions into sequences of butterfly factors and give the exact stage/parameter counts. For a practical constructive view, look at the Givens-to-butterfly scheduling arguments in Dao et al. 2019 (and follow-up work on learned fast transforms), which the current paper explicitly references as the basis for Theorem 1.

### Comparing BOFT with Givens/Householder: Are we just choosing a different sparse orthogonal basis?
Question:
Classical linear algebra provides parameter-efficient orthogonal factorizations using Givens rotations (sparse with \(2\times 2\) active sub-blocks) or Householder reflections. BOFT also uses products of sparse orthogonals arranged in a butterfly pattern. How does BOFT’s expressivity, parameter sharing, and computational layout compare to (a) a schedule of Givens rotations and (b) a sequence of Householder reflectors, under similar parameter budgets and parallelism constraints?
Answer:
Short answer: No — BOFT is not just “Givens/Householder with a different sparsity pattern.” All three families use sparse $2\times 2$ or rank-1 building blocks to build orthogonals, but they make different trade‑offs in degrees of freedom (expressivity), how parameters are reused (sharing), and the computational/parallel layout (depth, locality). BOFT deliberately picks the butterfly connectivity to get dense global mixing with far fewer free parameters and low algorithmic depth than typical Givens schedules or naïve Householder sequences at the same parameter budget, at the cost of restricting you to a structured subset (an inductive bias) of the orthogonal group.

Longer explanation (intuitive + technical, tied to the paper)

What is common and what differs
- Commonality: BOFT, Givens-rotation factorizations, and Householder reflectors all factor an orthogonal matrix into simpler orthogonal blocks. BOFT (as presented) is explicitly built from many small orthogonal block factors (the paper implements each butterfly component via $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks via Cayley/rotations), which is the same low-level building block type that Givens rotations use ($2\times 2$ rotations). Householder reflectors are different in form (reflections represented by a length‑$d$ vector) but also compose to give arbitrary orthogonals.
- Key difference: the graph of interactions (which coordinates are mixed when) and the reuse/sharing of parameters. BOFT imposes the butterfly connectivity (Cooley–Tukey style), which guarantees that $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ sparse factors suffice to connect every input coordinate to every output coordinate (dense connectivity) with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ free parameters if you use $2\times 2$ blocks. Givens schedules and Householder sequences can also realize arbitrary orthogonals, but to get the same global mixing with comparable parameter count or similar wall-clock parallelism you must choose schedules/compositions that typically require many more sequential steps or more parameters.

Expressivity under comparable parameter budgets
- Full expressivity: Both Givens rotations and Householder reflectors are universal: with an appropriate (possibly long) sequence you can represent any orthogonal matrix. A well-known fact is that $\mathcal{O}(d)$ Householder reflectors (or $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ independent Givens rotations) can represent any orthogonal; more precisely the orthogonal group has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, so you ultimately need that many free parameters for full coverage.
- BOFT (butterfly factorization) targets a different point: with $m$ stages and block size $b$, BOFT has about $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$ effective trainable parameters (the paper), and for the canonical butterfly $m\approx\log d$, $b=2$ gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. This is asymptotically far under the $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ DOF of the full group: BOFT therefore represents a structured (but large) subset of orthogonals. The paper’s Theorem and discussion note that by stacking multiple butterfly-like products (kaleidoscope constructions) you can approximate the entire orthogonal group, but that requires many more factors (and parameters), returning toward the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ regime. In short: at equal low parameter budgets (e.g. $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$), BOFT is more expressive than a single block‑diagonal OFT and typically more expressive than simple Givens schedules that only allow limited mixing, but it is not universal unless you increase the number of components accordingly.

Parameter sharing and inductive bias
- BOFT reuses the same structured pattern of small orthogonal blocks across log-depth stages, so each parameter participates in many coordinate mixes (strong parameter sharing). This yields a structured inductive bias: fast global mixing with few parameters, and the ability to exactly recover many classical fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT) as the paper emphasizes. That inductive bias is what the authors argue helps generalization in finetuning tasks.
- A Givens-factorization schedule that uses many distinct $2\times 2$ rotations with no structured reuse will have many more independent parameters for the same coverage; you can force sharing across rotations but then you are imposing a different inductive bias (and typically losing universality).
- Householder reflectors are high‑impact but less “local”: one reflector (parameterized by a full $d$-vector) can change many coordinates simultaneously, so sequence depth can be small (you can get full expressivity with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ reflectors), but each reflector has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters (so parameter count and memory cost are larger). Householder sequences therefore trade fewer sequential steps for more parameters (or conversely, for a strong parameter budget constraint you’d have to heavily compress or share Householder vectors, again imposing a different bias).

Computational layout and parallelism
- BOFT gives a small algorithmic depth: the canonical butterfly has $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages, each stage consisting of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ independent small blocks. This maps well to parallel hardware: within a stage many $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) blocks can be applied in parallel and the overall depth is logarithmic. Matrix-vector cost for a butterfly orthogonal $R$ is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, so it is both parameter- and compute-efficient in inference and (after fusion) in training. The paper highlights this depth and $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter count as its central advantage.
- Givens rotations: naive dense coverage via Givens rotations typically requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ rotations if you want universality, and many rotations are sequentially dependent; however, you can design parallel Givens schedules that do disjoint pair rotations in a stage (like bitonic sorting networks), achieving $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ depth for some structured mixes—but to get the same dense mixing and universality you again need many stages or many independent rotations, pushing parameters up. So depending on your chosen schedule, Givens can mimic BOFT but generally with worse parameter efficiency unless you adopt the same butterfly-like scheduling.
- Householder reflections: each reflector is dense, so applying a single reflector is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ unless you exploit structure; the required number of reflectors for universality is $\mathcal{O}(d)$, so cost is $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ naïvely for full composition, but optimized implementations use rank-1 updates to do each reflection in $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per vector (matrix-vector multiplication still $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ if you form full matrix). Parallelism is weaker because reflectors are typically applied sequentially and each touches all coordinates.

Practical consequences for finetuning (as in the paper)
The paper’s goal is parameter-efficient finetuning where you want strong, global re‑mappings of neuron geometry but with far fewer trainable parameters than full OFT. BOFT’s butterfly pattern balances global connectivity (dense effective $R$) with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters and low depth, making it a sweet spot: it is more parameter‑efficient than block‑diagonal OFT or naïve Givens schedules, and cheaper to apply than stacks of Householder reflectors with comparable expressivity. Importantly the BOFT construction uses Cayley/$2\times 2$ rotations so it preserves orthogonality throughout and allows identity initialization and multiplicative dropout as described in the paper, giving the stability benefits the authors emphasize.

Bottom line
BOFT is not merely “a different sparse orthogonal basis” in the sense of being equivalent to a single Givens schedule or a short Householder chain. It is a specific graph/topology choice (butterfly) that (a) achieves dense input$\to$output connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters and $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ depth, (b) shares parameters heavily across many coordinate mixes yielding a useful inductive bias for finetuning, and (c) can be made universal only by stacking many more butterflies (kaleidoscope), which then approaches the same $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter regime as Givens/Householder universality. Thus BOFT trades universality for a highly useful structured subset of orthogonals that is especially attractive under strict parameter and parallelism constraints, which is exactly the trade made and validated empirically in the paper.

### Spectral norm preservation: Is \(\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\rVert_2=\lVert \mathbf{W}^0\rVert_2\) always true for BOFT?
Question:
The paper argues that BOFT preserves the spectral norm since it left-multiplies by an orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Is there any caveat due to finite precision or the Cayley transform’s numerical errors? Also, does the same argument imply that all singular values are preserved if \(\mathbf{R}\) multiplies on the left (i.e., only the left singular vectors change), or just the largest one?
Answer:
Short answer: In exact arithmetic BOFT preserves the entire singular value spectrum of the pretrained weight matrix, so $ \lVert R W^0\rVert_2=\lVert W^0\rVert_2$ (and in fact every singular value is unchanged). In floating-point arithmetic there are practical caveats: the Cayley parameterization and repeated matrix products introduce rounding and conditioning errors so in practice you get near-preservation, not mathematically exact preservation. You should monitor and optionally re-project to enforce orthogonality if exactness is important.

Why it holds in theory. Let $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$ be an SVD of the pretrained weight matrix. If $R$ is orthogonal ($R^\top R = I$), then
$$
R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^\top.
$$
Because $R U$ is still orthogonal, the singular values are exactly the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$. Hence left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix leaves every singular value (not just the largest) unchanged; only the left singular vectors change. In particular the spectral norm (largest singular value) is preserved.

Why numerical issues can break exact preservation. BOFT implements $R$ as a product of orthogonal factors (butterfly components), each parameterized (in this paper) via the Cayley transform $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$. In exact arithmetic this yields an orthogonal matrix. In floating-point arithmetic two sources of deviation arise:

1) Rounding and accumulation: multiplying many factors and carrying out matrix inversions and multiplications accumulates floating-point round-off, so the computed $\hat R$ will satisfy $\hat R^\top\hat R\approx I$ but not exactly equal. The singular values of $\hat R W^0$ will therefore be close to, but not exactly, those of $W^0$.

2) Conditioning of the Cayley map: computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is sensitive if $I-Q$ is ill-conditioned. Although $Q$ is skew-symmetric and typically initialized small (so $I-Q$ is well conditioned early), during training entries of $Q$ may grow and make $(I-Q)$ poorly conditioned; inversion then amplifies rounding errors and can produce larger departures from orthogonality. (Note also that the Cayley map is undefined if $I-Q$ is singular, but that situation is unlikely if parameters are kept moderate; nevertheless it is a theoretical caution.)

Practical implications and recommendations. In practice BOFT will "preserve" spectral norm up to small numerical error; you should not assume exact equality in floating point. Useful safeguards are: monitor the orthogonality residual $\lVert \hat R^\top\hat R - I\rVert_F$ or $\lVert \hat R^\top\hat R - I\rVert_2$ and monitor the change in singular values of $W^0$ (or just its spectral norm). If you see drift, re-orthogonalize factors occasionally (e.g., via QR or polar decomposition on blocks), or project the composed $\hat R$ back to the nearest orthogonal matrix by polar decomposition. Using small parameter steps / lower learning rates on the skew parameters also reduces conditioning problems, and initializing $Q$ near zero (identity initialization) helps stability (the paper uses this).

Summary. The theoretical claim in the paper is correct: left-multiplying by an orthogonal $R$ preserves all singular values of $W^0$, so $ \lVert R W^0\rVert_2=\lVert W^0\rVert_2$. Numerically, Cayley-based factors and matrix products give only approximate orthogonality due to finite precision and possible inversion conditioning; in practice the preservation is near-exact but you may want to monitor and (if needed) correct orthogonality during training.

### Implementation detail: Where exactly is \(\mathbf{R}\) applied inside Transformers and CNNs?
Question:
For a pretrained linear layer with weights \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), BOFT uses \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). In practice, for MHA and MLP modules in Transformers, do we apply \(\mathbf{R}\) to the input dimension (rows) of all linear sub-layers (e.g., Q/K/V/O, and MLP in/out) or selectively? How are biases handled? Are there layers where applying \(\mathbf{R}\) harms performance or violates architectural constraints (e.g., layer norm placements)?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT (and OFT) multiply an orthogonal $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of a pretrained layer's weight matrix, so $ \mathbf{W} $ becomes $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $. Concretely this means $ \mathbf{R} $ acts on the input (feature) dimension of a linear map: for a layer whose forward is $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{b} $ you replace it by $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $. You therefore apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to those linear sublayers whose weight shape matches the "input dimension" (the dimension of $ \mathbf{x} $) and you leave biases as-is.

Why this is the right interpretation (intuitive + technical). The paper defines OFT/BOFT as learning an orthogonal $ \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} $ and changing the forward pass from $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x} $ to $ \mathbf{z}=((\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top)\mathbf{x} $. That formula shows $ \mathbf{R} $ multiplies the pretrained weight on the left, i.e. it transforms the input basis (it rotates/permutes the input/neuron coordinates) so the pairwise geometry of columns of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ is preserved up to orthogonal transform. Because $ \mathbf{R} $ is orthogonal the spectral properties of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ are preserved, which the paper emphasizes as a benefit for stability and generalization.

How this maps to Transformer MHA / MLP components. In standard Transformer blocks every linear sublayer is just a matrix times the hidden vector:

- For the Q/K/V projections each is a linear map from the model hidden dimension $ d $ to some head/ per-head dimension (their weight typically shaped $ d\times n_{qkv} $). You apply $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of those pretrained weight matrices (so effectively $ \mathbf{R} $ acts on the hidden/input dimension before the projection). The same applies to the output (O) projection of attention and to the MLP input and output linear layers: wherever the forward is $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $ with $ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} $, replace $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ by $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $. In short: apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to all linear sublayers whose input is the model hidden vector (the paper explicitly formulates the forward pass this way).

Practical caveats and selective application. The paper does not mandate applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to every single matrix in the network; the constraint is simply shape/semantics: $ \mathbf{R} $ must match the input dimension. So:

- Only apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to layers whose input dimension equals the orthogonal transform size $ d $. If a projection has a different input size (e.g. token embedding table or small adapter layers), you either do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ or you make a separate $ \mathbf{R} $ of the appropriate size.
- If weights are tied (e.g. input embeddings tied with output head), you must be careful to apply the same transformation consistently, otherwise you break the tying. The paper assumes transforming pretrained weights in-place, so tied/shared weights should receive the same $ \mathbf{R} $.
- Do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to non-linear or parameter types that are not linear maps on the model hidden vector (LayerNorm scale/shifts, positional embeddings, scalar biases that are not part of a pretrained linear weight) — those are not represented as left-multipliable weight matrices in the paper’s formulation.
- When a linear layer changes dimensionality (e.g. down-projection from $ d $ to $ d_{\text{mid}} $), $ \mathbf{R} $ is still valid because it multiplies the pretrained weight on the left and the weight already maps from the same input space. If the input to that layer is not the same $ d $ you used for $ \mathbf{R} $, you cannot apply it without changing shapes.

Biases. The paper’s forward replacement keeps biases unchanged: $ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $. BOFT/OFT initialize $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $ so the initial function is identical to the pretrained one; biases remain intact and are not transformed by $ \mathbf{R} $. (If you wanted to be formal: applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to the left does not induce a change in additive bias unless you explicitly change the bias term.)

LayerNorm, residuals, and architectural constraints. The paper does not propose modifying LayerNorm parameters or other normalization blocks. Because BOFT preserves spectral properties and is initialized to identity, it is designed not to violate the pretrained model’s normalization/residual behaviour at initialization. Nevertheless, practical points to watch:

- LayerNorm placement: LayerNorms expect certain calibrated activations from the pretrained network. Although orthogonal left-multiplication preserves norms globally, intermediate activation distributions can change during training. The paper mitigates this by identity initialization of $ \mathbf{R} $ (so initial behavior is unchanged) and by the orthogonality constraint that prevents wild scaling. Still, you may prefer to finetune carefully (small lr, etc.) or avoid applying $ \mathbf{R} $ to very early blocks if you observe instability.
- Tied weights and shared parameters: as noted, do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ inconsistently across tied layers.
- Layers that are not linear maps from the model hidden state (positional embeddings, absolute/relative bias tensors, LayerNorm affine parameters): the paper’s formulation does not touch these and you should not substitute $ \mathbf{R} $ there.
- If a layer’s role is to produce key/value/query indices for e.g. rotary or other position-aware projection, you should check that applying $ \mathbf{R} $ does not break any invariants specific to that mechanism. The paper does not report such failures, but that is an architecture-dependent consideration.

Other implementation notes from the paper. BOFT uses Cayley parameterization (skew-symmetric $ \mathbf{Q} $ with $ \mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} $) to maintain orthogonality and initializes $ \mathbf{Q}=0 $ so $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $. BOFT also proposes a multiplicative dropout that temporarily replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity during training; this is possible because the transform is multiplicative (left of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $) rather than additive.

Summary recommendation. For Transformers apply $ \mathbf{R} $ on the left of every linear weight that consumes the model hidden vector (Q/K/V/O projections, attention output projection, MLP in/out), keep biases unchanged, initialize $ \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} $, and ensure consistency for tied/shared weights. Do not apply $ \mathbf{R} $ to LayerNorm parameters, positional embeddings, or other non-linear parameter types unless you deliberately design matching transforms and shapes. The paper’s empirical results and theoretical discussion assume this left-multiplication (input-dimension) interpretation.

### How is backpropagation through a product of many orthogonal factors stabilized?
Question:
BOFT stacks \(m\) orthogonal components, so gradients must pass through repeated multiplications of orthogonal blocks and their Cayley transforms. Does this cause vanishing/exploding gradients in practice? Are there known tricks (e.g., re-orthogonalization, parameter rescaling, skipping connections between factors) to stabilize training, especially for large \(m\)?
Answer:
Short answer: In theory repeated multiplications of exact orthogonal factors do not cause vanishing or exploding gradients because orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norm (spectral norm $=1$). In practice, however, a few numerical and parameterization effects can destabilize training, so you still need some practical safeguards. The BOFT paper already adopts two important stabilizing choices (Cayley parameterization with skew-symmetric $Q$ and identity initialization) and proposes multiplicative dropout; beyond those, a small set of standard tricks (regularize $Q$, occasional re‑orthogonalization, smaller learning rates / gradient clipping, and limiting the effective depth $m$) address the remaining practical risks.

Why orthogonality helps (intuitive + technical). If each factor $B_i$ is exactly orthogonal then for any vector $v$ we have $\|B_i v\|_2 = \|v\|_2$, so the product $R = B_m \dots B_1$ also has spectral norm $1$. Backpropagating a gradient through a product of orthogonals amounts to multiplying by other orthogonals, so the norm of the backpropagated signal is preserved in the linear part. Thus, unlike arbitrary dense factors, repeated orthogonal multiplications do not intrinsically amplify or attenuate gradients (no exponential growth/decay from the linear factors alone).

Practical caveats coming from the paper’s parameterization. BOFT uses Cayley parameterization $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew‑symmetric and composes many such orthogonal factors (implemented as block $2\times 2$ rotations / butterfly blocks). Two practical sources of instability are (a) numerical conditioning of $(I-Q)^{-1}$ if $Q$ becomes large or approaches an eigenvalue $1$, and (b) finite-precision accumulation when you compose many factors (small rounding errors can accumulate). The paper mitigates these by initializing $Q=0$ so each factor starts as identity (exactly orthogonal) and by using $2\times 2$ (or small block) orthogonal blocks whose Cayley inverse is cheap and well‑conditioned when $Q$ is small.

Concrete stabilization techniques (what to do, many are consistent with or directly used in the paper)
- Identity initialization: initialize all skew parameters $Q$ to zero so every factor starts at identity. This keeps the model on the “safe” manifold at start and is explicitly recommended/used in BOFT.
- Work with small updates: use small learning rates for the orthogonal parameters (or layerwise lr); because the Cayley map is rational, keeping $Q$ small keeps $(I-Q)$ well conditioned.
- Parameterize via small blocks / $2\times 2$ rotations: BOFT uses $2\times 2$ (or small-block) Cayley rotations; such local rotations are numerically stable and make inversion trivial; they also reduce the degree to which a single parameter can push the inverse toward ill-conditioning.
- Regularize $Q$ norm: add a small penalty $\|Q\|_F^2$ or constrain its max norm to keep $(I-Q)$ well behaved.
- Multiplicative Dropout / identity-block replacement: BOFT proposes multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces some butterfly blocks with identity during training. This both regularizes and reduces effective “depth” transiently, helping stability (and is a multiplicative analogue of dropout appropriate for multiplicative updates).
- Re-orthogonalization / numerical correction: occasionally re-project a factor to exact orthogonality (e.g., polar decomposition or QR) if you detect drift due to numerical error; with Cayley parameterization this is rarely needed but can be used as a safety step.
- Gradient clipping and optimizer choices: clip gradients for the orthogonal parameters or use adaptive optimizers with conservative betas; this controls sudden large steps that could make $Q$ large.
- Limit effective $m$ or increase block size $b$: there is a trade‑off BOFT explains between $m$ and $b$ (more factors $m$ increases expressivity but also increases composition depth). If you see instability for large $m$, try slightly larger block size $b$ or fewer factors to reduce composition depth while preserving parameter budget tradeoffs.
- Mixed precision care: if you train with FP16, pay attention to the inversion $(I-Q)^{-1}$—use FP32 for the orthogonal parameter updates or the inversion to avoid precision-driven instability.

How these relate to BOFT design choices in the paper. The paper explicitly uses Cayley parameterization for exact orthogonality, identity initialization of skew $Q$, and multiplicative dropout tailored to multiplicative structure. It also recommends using $2\times 2$ (or small block) rotations so each butterfly component is composed of stable small rotations. The paper discusses the expressivity/regularity tradeoff (larger $m$ and smaller $b$ give higher expressivity but are “deeper” factorizations), so choosing $m$ and $b$ is another lever for stability versus capacity.

A short recommended checklist when training BOFT on large $m$
Start with identity init and small lr for orthogonal params. Use small-block Cayley rotations ($2\times 2$ or modest $b$). Add mild $\|Q\|$ regularization and enable multiplicative dropout. Monitor conditioning of $(I-Q)$ (e.g., its smallest singular value) and if it approaches zero reproject or reduce step sizes. If instability persists, reduce $m$ or increase $b$, use gradient clipping, or keep orthogonal updates in FP32.

Bottom line: pure math says a product of exact orthogonal factors preserves gradient norms, so vanishing/exploding from the factors themselves is not expected. In practice numerical conditioning of the Cayley inverse, large parameter steps, and accumulation of rounding error are the main hazards. Identity initialization, small-block Cayley rotations, multiplicative dropout (all in the paper), small learning rates / regularization, and occasional re‑orthogonalization together provide a robust recipe for stabilizing backprop through many orthogonal factors.

### BOFT vs. LoRA: When would low-rank additive updates be preferable to multiplicative orthogonal updates?
Question:
Conceptually, LoRA learns a low-rank additive update \(\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\), while BOFT learns a multiplicative orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Are there scenarios where low-rank structure better matches the task’s inductive bias than near-isometric transformations? For example, if a task primarily needs to change the spectrum of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), would LoRA be more effective since BOFT preserves singular values?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — there are clear situations where a low-rank additive update like LoRA is a better inductive match than an orthogonal multiplicative update like BOFT/OFT. The essential difference is that LoRA can change the singular values and the rank of the pretrained weight matrix, while orthogonal left-multiplication preserves the singular spectrum and is (near-)isometric. Which is preferable depends on whether the downstream task mainly needs to re-weight/amplify/suppress particular linear directions (spectral/rank change) or instead mostly needs to rotate/permute/redistribute existing features while keeping norms and relative geometry stable.

Why this follows from the paper (intuition + technical detail). BOFT/OFT apply an orthogonal matrix $R$ on the left so the finetuned weight is $R W^0$. If $W^0 = U \Sigma V^{T}$, then $R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^{T}$ — the singular values (the diagonal of $\Sigma$) are unchanged. The paper highlights this property explicitly: “both OFT and BOFT multiply an orthogonal matrix to the left and obtain the finetuned weights … which does not affect the largest singular value (i.e. the spectral norm).” Thus BOFT is inherently near-isometric and preserves the spectrum of $W^0$. LoRA, with additive updates $\Delta W = A B$, can change $\Sigma$ (and the effective rank) because you are adding new components to $W^0$; low-rank updates are capable of introducing new singular directions and altering singular values.

When to prefer LoRA (low-rank additive):
- Tasks that require changing the spectrum: If the downstream task needs to amplify or suppress specific linear modes (for example, boost sensitivity to some feature directions, down-weight noisy subspaces, or alter singular value gaps), LoRA can accomplish that by adding components that change $\Sigma$. If the effective change is concentrated in a few directions (intrinsic low-rank difference between pretrained and target task), a low-rank update is a very natural inductive bias.
- Tasks that require adding new degrees of freedom or new features: If you need to expand the span of the row/column space of $W^0$ (e.g., learn new specialized detectors that were not present in pretraining), a low-rank additive term can add those directions directly.
- If empirical diagnostics show the finetuned full-weight difference $\Delta W = W^{*} - W^0$ has low-rank structure or large singular-value changes, LoRA will be parameter-efficient and effective.

When to prefer BOFT/OFT (multiplicative orthogonal):
- Tasks that should preserve pretraining geometry and norms: BOFT is designed to keep pairwise neuron angles and spectral norm, which the paper argues helps stability and generalization. If you want to preserve semantic structure learned during pretraining and only need to reorient or mix those features (rotate within the same singular-spectrum), BOFT is a better match.
- When training stability/generalization is critical: The paper emphasizes that orthogonal finetuning “perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix” and that this preservation benefits stability and generalization. For tasks sensitive to overfitting or that benefit from small, isometric changes (e.g., controlled generation, some diffusion-model adaptations), BOFT’s inductive bias is desirable.
- When you want parameter-efficient dense orthogonality: BOFT’s butterfly factorization gives a compact way to represent dense orthogonal transforms (parameter cost roughly $O(d \log d)$), enabling richer isometric transforms than block-diagonal OFT while maintaining the orthogonality constraint and regularizing the update.

Practical diagnostics and heuristics. A practical way to decide: take a small fully-finetuned $W^{*}$ (or a few gradient steps) on your task and examine the singular values of $W^{*}$ versus $W^0$ and the matrix $\Delta W = W^{*} - W^0$. If the singular values of $W^{*}$ differ substantially from $W^0$ (large spectral shifts) or $\Delta W$ is well-approximated by a low-rank matrix, that suggests LoRA-like additive low-rank updates are appropriate. If the spectrum is largely preserved and the main change is a re-orientation of singular vectors, BOFT/OFT will likely do well and may generalize better.

Hybrid strategies. The paper itself contrasts low-rank and sparse/structured orthogonal parameterizations as two complementary families of parameter-efficient structure. In practice you can combine them: use a small LoRA plus BOFT (additive low-rank plus multiplicative orthogonal), or alternate/stack them, so you get the ability to both re-scale and rotate. That captures both kinds of inductive bias and is often effective when the required change is mixed.

Summary. Use LoRA when the task requires changing singular values, adding new directions, or when the $\Delta W$ is low-rank; use BOFT/OFT when you want to preserve pretrained geometry, spectral norms, and prefer isometric rotations/mixings (with BOFT giving a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal family). The paper’s spectral discussion and the information-transmission/inductive-bias arguments make these differences explicit and provide the theoretical basis for choosing between them (or combining them).

### Clarifying the information-transmission graph: How do sparse patterns map to permutations between levels?
Question:
The paper states each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is a block-diagonal matrix whose nonzero pattern is a permutation of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\). Could you show explicitly, for \(d=8\), how the edges (nonzeros) of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(8,4)\) can be obtained from permuting the blocks of \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(8,2)\)? A concrete mapping would help me understand the level-to-level connectivity evolution.
Answer:
Short answer: tilde B(8,4) is the same $2\times2$ “butterfly” building block pattern as $\tilde B(8,2)$, only with the $2\times2$ blocks re-grouped (permuted) so that they become $4\times4$ block-factors. Concretely one can realize this regrouping by a permutation that interleaves odd and even indices; applying that permutation to both rows and columns of $\tilde B(8,2)$ collects adjacent $2\times2$ blocks into $4\times4$ blocks and yields the nonzero pattern of $\tilde B(8,4)$.

Explanation with an explicit mapping ($d = 8$)

1) Pattern of $\tilde B(8,2)$.
$\tilde B(8,2)$ is block-diagonal with four $2\times2$ blocks on the diagonal. Label the 8 node indices $1\ldots8$. The nonzero (edge) pattern of $\tilde B(8,2)$ connects within each adjacent pair:
- block 1 on indices $(1,2)$,
- block 2 on indices $(3,4)$,
- block 3 on indices $(5,6)$,
- block 4 on indices $(7,8)$.

Viewed as a connectivity matrix between level $L$ and level $L+1$, each $2\times2$ block means inputs $\{1,2\}$ connect only to outputs $\{1,2\}$, inputs $\{3,4\}$ only to outputs $\{3,4\}$, etc.

2) What $\tilde B(8,4)$ looks like.
$\tilde B(8,4)$ is block-diagonal with two $4\times4$ blocks: one acting on indices $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and the other on $\{5,6,7,8\}$. Each $4\times4$ block is itself a butterfly factor (see Eq. (1) in the paper): the $4\times4$ block has the $2\times2$ diagonal-block structure
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
where each $\operatorname{diag}(d_*)$ is a $2\times2$ diagonal matrix (so the $4\times4$ block is assembled from four $2\times2$ diagonal pieces). The essential point the paper emphasizes is that the nonzero pattern of each $4\times4$ block is a permutation (block-wise reordering) of the $2\times2$ blocks that appear in $\tilde B(8,2)$.

3) A concrete permutation that shows the mapping.
Take the permutation $p$ that interleaves odds then evens within each 4-block:
$p = [1,3,2,4,5,7,6,8]$.
Interpreting $p$ as a permutation matrix $P$ (so $P$ reorders indices by $p$), the mapping is

$\displaystyle \tilde B(8,4) = P^T \cdot \tilde B(8,2) \cdot P$

(we permute rows and columns the same way to regroup the $2\times2$ blocks into $4\times4$ blocks).

Check intuitively what this permutation does on indices $1\ldots4$:
- The order $[1,3,2,4]$ takes the original $2\times2$ blocks on $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$ and interleaves them so that the $2\times2$ blocks now sit inside the $4\times4$ block acting on $\{1,3,2,4\}$ (which we then relabel as $\{1,2,3,4\}$ after the permutation). The same happens on indices $5\ldots8$ with $[5,7,6,8]$.

4) Edge-level description.
Before permutation, the nonzero $2\times2$ blocks are
$(1\leftrightarrow1,\,1\leftrightarrow2,\,2\leftrightarrow1,\,2\leftrightarrow2)$, $(3\leftrightarrow3,\,3\leftrightarrow4,\,4\leftrightarrow3,\,4\leftrightarrow4)$, $(5\leftrightarrow5,\,5\leftrightarrow6,\,6\leftrightarrow5,\,6\leftrightarrow6)$, $(7\leftrightarrow7,\,7\leftrightarrow8,\,8\leftrightarrow7,\,8\leftrightarrow8)$.
After conjugating by $P$ (the interleaving), those four $2\times2$ blocks are rearranged into the two $4\times4$ blocks whose internal pattern equals the $4\times4$ butterfly factor pattern (four diagonal $2\times2$ subblocks arranged in the $2\times2$ layout). In other words, each $4\times4$ block’s nonzero pattern is literally a permuted arrangement of two of the original $2\times2$ blocks.

Intuition and connection to the paper
The paper states that every butterfly component $\tilde B(d,k)$ has a nonzero pattern which is a permutation (block-wise) of $\tilde B(d,2)$. The permutation $p$ above is the concrete instance of that statement for $d=8, k=4$: it reorders indices so that adjacent $2\times2$ interaction blocks (the $\tilde B(8,2)$ blocks) are collected into $4\times4$ butterfly factors. This is precisely the same mechanism used recursively in Cooley–Tukey style factorizations: at each stage you regroup (permute) elements so that local $2\times2$ mixing operations act on the correct larger subsets, enabling global dense connectivity after stacking the levels.

If you want, you can write down the $8\times8$ sparse pattern matrices before and after permutation and verify that $P^T \cdot \tilde B(8,2) \cdot P$ has two $4\times4$ nonzero blocks in positions $\{1\ldots4\}$ and $\{5\ldots8\}$, each with the $4\times4$ butterfly layout described above.

### Real vs. complex transforms: Can BOFT recover the DFT exactly using only real orthogonal factors?
Question:
The FFT (DFT) is unitary and generally complex-valued. The paper claims butterfly structures can recover many classic fast transforms. When restricting BOFT to real orthogonal matrices, can we exactly recover the DFT (perhaps via a real-valued embedding of complex arithmetic), or only approximate it? If exact recovery is possible, what is the construction and parameterization required?
Answer:
Short answer: You cannot realize the DFT exactly as a $d\times d$ real orthogonal matrix when $d>2$, because the DFT is complex unitary with nonzero imaginary entries. However you can recover the DFT exactly in BOFT if you either (a) allow complex-valued butterfly factors, or (b) embed complex arithmetic into a $2d$-dimensional real space and realize the DFT as a product of real orthogonal butterfly factors acting on that doubled real vector. If you insist on staying in the original real $d$-dimensional space with only real orthogonal $d\times d$ factors, you can only approximate the complex DFT.

Why (intuitively). The DFT matrix $F_d$ is unitary over $\mathbb{C}$ and, except for trivial small $d$, has genuinely complex entries (nonzero imaginary parts). A real orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies $Q^T Q = I$ and has only real entries, so there is no equality $Q = F_d$ (over $\mathbb{C}$) unless $F_d$ happens to be real (it is not for general $d$). So exact equality in the same $d$-dimensional real space is impossible.

Exact recovery via complex BOFT. The paper already notes that butterfly structures (the Cooley–Tukey factorization) can recover classic fast transforms such as the FFT. If BOFT is allowed to use complex-valued butterfly components, you can parameterize each complex $2\times 2$ (or small) butterfly factor exactly with the complex twiddle factors $e^{-2\pi i k / n}$ that Cooley–Tukey prescribes. Multiplying those complex sparse factors reproduces the exact DFT matrix $F_d$. That is the standard FFT factorization expressed as a product of sparse complex matrices; BOFT with complex-valued factors is a direct fit.

Exact recovery by real embedding ($2d\times 2d$ real orthogonal BOFT). A constructive way to remain strictly in real arithmetic is to embed complex vectors into $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ by mapping each complex number $a + i b$ to the real pair $(a, b)$. Complex-linear maps (including multiplication by unit-modulus complex numbers) map to real $2\times 2$ rotation blocks. Concretely, represent $F_d$ acting on $\mathbb{C}^d$ by a $2d\times 2d$ real matrix
$$
F_{\mathrm{real}}=\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}(F_d) & -\operatorname{Im}(F_d)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{Im}(F_d) & \operatorname{Re}(F_d)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Because $F_d$ is unitary, $F_{\mathrm{real}}$ is real orthogonal. The Cooley–Tukey FFT factorization of $F_d$ into sparse complex factors can be converted blockwise into a product of sparse real orthogonal factors of size $2d$: replace each complex scalar multiplier $a+ib$ by the real $2\times 2$ rotation matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a & -b\\[2pt] b & a\end{pmatrix}$ and replace complex $2\times 2$ butterfly blocks by their corresponding $4\times 4$ real block forms (or, equivalently, keep the same logical butterfly connectivity but with $2\times 2$ real blocks). Using BOFT with block-size $b=2$ (meaning the atomic orthogonal blocks are $2\times 2$ rotations) and $m = O(\log d)$ stages you get a product of $O(\log d)$ sparse real orthogonal components whose product equals $F_{\mathrm{real}}$ exactly. In short, $\mathrm{BOFT}(m=\log d, b=2)$ on a $2d$-dimensional real layer can represent the DFT exactly by parameterizing the exact cosine/sine values (not learned approximations).

What parameters are required. For the complex BOFT you set the butterfly factors to the exact complex twiddle values that Cooley–Tukey prescribes (these are fixed deterministic scalars $e^{-2\pi i k / n}$). For the real-embedded BOFT you set each $2\times 2$ block to the rotation matrix corresponding to the twiddle phase: for twiddle $e^{i\theta}$ use
$$
R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\[2pt]\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The rest of each butterfly stage is the same sparse connectivity/permutation pattern as the FFT. Thus exact recovery requires deterministically setting those rotation parameters to the cosine/sine of the FFT twiddle angles (or equivalently initializing/forcing those entries rather than learning arbitrary entries). If you allow learning, BOFT can still learn to match these values (given enough capacity), but exact equality requires the precise twiddle parameters.

Practical remark and connection to the paper. The paper emphasizes that butterfly factorizations can recover classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, etc.). That statement is consistent: the FFT is a butterfly factorization but intrinsically complex; the paper’s BOFT uses small orthogonal blocks ($2\times 2$ rotations) and permutations, so BOFT exactly recovers those transforms either by using complex-valued factors or by doing the real $2d$ embedding where each complex multiply is a $2\times 2$ real rotation. If you restrict yourself to $d$-dimensional real orthogonal BOFT factors only, you cannot represent the complex phases exactly and therefore can only approximate the DFT.

Summary. Exact DFT recovery with only real orthogonal $d\times d$ factors is impossible. Exact recovery is possible if you either permit complex-valued butterfly factors (direct FFT factorization) or embed into $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ and implement the complex phases as $2\times 2$ real rotation blocks within the butterfly stages (BOFT with block size implementing $2\times 2$ rotations and $m = O(\log d)$ stages).

### What does the "weight interpolation" property mean concretely for BOFT?
Question:
The paper mentions an "intriguing weight interpolation" due to matrix factorization (referencing a figure). Could someone explain explicitly how varying a subset of butterfly components between identity and learned values induces a continuous path from \(\mathbf{W}^0\) to \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\)? Is there an analytical expression showing this acts like a multi-stage homotopy, and does this have any practical training benefits (e.g., curriculum over \(m\))?
Answer:
Short answer: "Weight interpolation" in BOFT means you can continuously move the finetuned linear layer from the pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$ to the fully transformed weights $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ by continuously turning on (or progressively composing) the individual orthogonal butterfly components. Because BOFT builds $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of orthogonal factors that are each identity at initialization, you get an explicit, continuous family of orthogonal matrices (a homotopy) connecting the identity to the learned $\mathbf{R}$. This is both analytically simple and practically useful (easier/stabler optimization, possible curriculum over components).

Concrete analytic description
BOFT represents the learned orthogonal transform as a product of m orthogonal butterfly components:
$$
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_1,
$$
and each $\mathbf{B}_i$ is initialized to the identity. BOFT enforces orthogonality of each $\mathbf{B}_i$ (the paper states this is done via Cayley parameterization / 2-by-2 rotations for the blocks). Let $\mathbf{Q}_i$ be the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter for component $i$ so that
$$
\mathbf{B}_i = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}_i)(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1}.
$$
Because $\mathbf{Q}_i$ is zero at initialization, $\mathbf{B}_i(0)=\mathbf{I}$. A simple, explicit continuous interpolation (a homotopy) from identity to the final $\mathbf{B}_i$ is obtained by scaling the underlying Cayley parameter:
$$
\mathbf{B}_i(\alpha) \;=\; \big(\mathbf{I}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}_i\big)\big(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{Q}_i\big)^{-1},\qquad \alpha\in[0,1],
$$
which is orthogonal for every $\alpha$. Using these, define a family of transforms
$$
\mathbf{R}(\alpha)=\mathbf{B}_m(\alpha)\mathbf{B}_{m-1}(\alpha)\cdots\mathbf{B}_1(\alpha),
$$
so $\mathbf{R}(0)=\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{R}(1)=\mathbf{R}$. Consequently the finetuned weights follow a continuous path
$$
\mathbf{W}(\alpha)=\mathbf{R}(\alpha)\mathbf{W}^0,
$$
giving an explicit multi-stage homotopy from $\mathbf{W}^0$ to $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$.

Multi-stage (component-wise) homotopy
If you prefer a staged interpolation that turns on components one-by-one (the "multi-stage" intuition in the paper/figure), define for integers $k=0,\dots,m$ and scalar $t\in[0,1]$:
$$
\mathbf{R}_{k}(t)=\mathbf{B}_m(1)\cdots\mathbf{B}_{k+1}(1)\;\mathbf{B}_k(t)\;\mathbf{B}_{k-1}(0)\cdots\mathbf{B}_1(0).
$$
Because each $\mathbf{B}_i(t)$ continuously interpolates between $\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{B}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_k(t)$ moves continuously from the state with the first $k-1$ components off and the remaining components as learned, to the state with the first $k$ components turned on. Stringing $k$ from 0 to $m$ yields a multi-stage homotopy that gradually grows capacity from identity toward full $\mathbf{R}$.

Why this is valid and intuitive
Each $\mathbf{B}_i(\alpha)$ is orthogonal for all $\alpha$ (Cayley preserves orthogonality), so products remain orthogonal; scaling a skew-symmetric parameter is a smooth operation so every mapping above is continuous. Intuitively, BOFT factors the global dense orthogonal change into many localized 2-by-2 (or small-block) rotations. Turning those small rotations on gradually is like opening channels for information flow layer-by-layer in the butterfly factorization: early stages preserve most of the pretrained mapping, later stages deliver finer global rewirings.

Practical training benefits (what the paper suggests / implies)
The paper emphasizes identity initialization and that BOFT smoothly interpolates between identity and expressive dense transforms. Practically, this gives several advantages. First, starting from identity and moving along a continuous path tends to stabilize optimization because you avoid abrupt, large changes to pretrained weights (spectral norms are preserved at every step, which helps stability). Second, the multi-stage viewpoint suggests a natural curriculum: you can progressively enable more components (increase $k$ or raise $\alpha$) during training so the model first adapts with low-capacity, then refines with higher-capacity components. That can act as regularization, reduce overfitting, and make optimization easier (similar spirit to gradual layer unfreezing or progressive networks). Third, the multiplicative-dropout scheme the paper proposes (randomly replace some components/blocks by identity during training) leverages the same idea: temporarily reducing active components enforces robustness and prevents co-adaptation of components.

Caveats and implementation notes
The simplest and most numerically-stable interpolation in the BOFT framework is scaling the Cayley/skew-symmetric parameter (as written above). Other continuous interpolations are possible (e.g., geodesics on the orthogonal group using matrix exponentials: $\mathbf{B}_i(\alpha)=\exp(\alpha\log(\mathbf{B}_i))$), but Cayley scaling is directly compatible with the parameterization the paper uses and keeps each intermediate matrix orthogonal without expensive matrix logarithms/exponentials. If you want a discrete curriculum instead of continuous $\alpha$, you can progressively unfreeze components (set them from identity to learnable) or increase the fraction kept by multiplicative-dropout; both implement the same multi-stage idea the paper illustrates.

Summary
Because BOFT composes the final orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ from factors that are identity at init and orthogonal for every scaled parameter, you obtain an explicit continuous family $\mathbf{R}(\alpha)$ or staged family $\mathbf{R}_k(t)$ connecting $\mathbf{I}$ to the learned $\mathbf{R}$. This is the concrete analytic meaning of the "weight interpolation" mentioned in the paper, and it gives natural training strategies (curriculum, staged unfreezing, multiplicative dropout) that can improve stability and generalization.

### Does composing many orthogonals increase training time significantly, and can we fuse them?
Question:
The paper notes runtime overhead during training because \(\mathbf{R}\) is a product of \(m\) sparse factors. (1) What are the actual asymptotic and measured costs relative to LoRA and block-diagonal OFT? (2) Can we fuse consecutive butterfly components periodically during training to reduce overhead while keeping gradients correct (e.g., by treating the fused block as a single orthogonal with a cached factorization)?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — composing many orthogonals increases per-step compute/communication roughly linearly in the number of factors (so BOFT is asymptotically more expensive than a single block-diagonal OFT or a small-rank LoRA), and a practical mitigation is to fuse/factor products in various ways, but you must be careful about where gradients live. You can either (a) implement a fused forward/backward kernel that applies the product efficiently while still backpropagating to the original factor parameters, or (b) periodically collapse factors into a single orthogonal for cheaper forward passes and then re-factorize or treat the fused block as the new parameterization — both approaches have tradeoffs (accuracy of gradient flow, implementation complexity, and overhead for re-factorization).

Below I give the precise asymptotic comparisons, an intuitive account of measured/runtime behavior reported in the paper, and explain the feasible fusion strategies and their implications for gradients.

Asymptotic costs (algorithmic intuition grounded in the paper)
- BOFT (product of $m$ sparse orthogonal "butterfly components"): each component has $O(d)$ nonzeros (block-butterfly structure), so applying one component to a $d\times n$ weight or vector costs $O(d n)$ work; applying the product of $m$ components costs $O(m d n)$. When BOFT is used in the "full" butterfly regime $m \approx O(\log d)$ (paper: a dense orthogonal can be obtained with $m = O(\log d)$ components), this becomes $O(d \log d \cdot n)$. Parameter count is $O(d m b)$ (paper: effective trainable parameters $\approx \frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$; for the canonical butterfly $b = 2$ this is $O(d m) \approx O(d \log d)$).

- Block‑diagonal OFT: $R$ is block‑diagonal with $r = d/b$ blocks of size $b$, so the nonzeros in $R$ are $O(d b)$. Multiplying this block-diagonal $R$ into a $d\times n$ matrix (or into activations) costs $O(d b n)$. The parameter count is $O(d b)$ (paper notes block-diagonal OFT uses $O(b d)$ parameters). If you choose $b$ large ($b = d$) you recover full OFT at $O(d^2)$ cost.

- LoRA: LoRA modifies weights additively with a product of two low-rank matrices of rank $r$. The extra cost per apply is the cost of applying the low-rank update, which is $O(r (d + n))$ per token/column when implemented as $A (B^\top x)$ or, if applied to a whole weight matrix multiplication, $O(r d n)$ if done naively for many columns. In practice LoRA chooses small $r$ so its runtime overhead is much lower than orthogonal reparameterizations.

Putting those together, for a layer of size $d\times n$ ($n$ = output dim or number of columns you apply to), the dominating multiply costs are roughly:
- BOFT: $O(m d n)$ ($\to O(d \log d \cdot n)$ when $m \approx \log d$)
- block-diagonal OFT: $O(b d n)$
- LoRA: $O(r d n)$ if you materialize the low-rank update for many columns, but practically LoRA often costs much less because $r$ is very small and implementations exploit low-rank structure.

Measured/runtime remarks from the paper
The paper states that BOFT introduces a "slightly larger" training runtime overhead than OFT (section Concluding Remarks and Limitations). It does not provide large-scale numeric timing tables in the excerpt you provided, so there are no exact wall‑clock numbers there to quote. Concretely, the paper’s qualitative points are: (1) BOFT reduces parameter count significantly (e.g., $O(d \log d)$ vs $O(d^2)$), (2) this comes at the cost of repeated application of $m$ sparse factors during training which increases runtime, and (3) there is no inference-time latency because the learned product can be multiplied into the pretrained weights after finetuning.

Practical interpretation: when $m$ is small (few factors) BOFT’s overhead is modest; when $m$ grows (to achieve denser expressivity) the per-step cost grows linearly with $m$ and can dominate. Compared to LoRA (very small $r$) BOFT will usually be slower per step; compared to block-diagonal OFT the runtime depends on chosen block size $b$ and $m$ (BOFT with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ is comparable to block-diagonal OFT with $b\approx\log d$ in parameter count but will have different multiply cost profiles).

Can you fuse consecutive butterfly components during training? (two different strategies and gradient correctness)
There are two distinct approaches to “fusion”, with different correctness/efficiency tradeoffs:

1) Software/kernel fusion without changing parameterization (recommended when you want exact gradients to the original parameters).
Implement a fused linear operator that applies the product $R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$ in one optimized kernel (or a small number of kernels) while keeping the original factor parameters as the canonical trainable tensors. In automatic differentiation frameworks you implement forward as a single fast multiply of the chain but ensure the backward pass either (a) uses the chain rule through that fused forward (automatic AD will do that if you expressed the fused forward as a composition of elemental ops or provide a custom backward), or (b) provide a custom backward that computes gradients for each factor by efficiently propagating sensitivities through the factors.

Pros: gradients are exactly the same as when applying the factors sequentially; no change in optimization trajectory. You reduce overheads due to kernel launch/fragmentation and can exploit contiguous memory/fused loops. This is the safest way to “speed up” without changing training dynamics.

Cons: you still pay the mathematical cost of backpropagating through all $m$ factors. The total arithmetic work is not asymptotically reduced (you still must compute derivatives w.r.t. all factors), although a fused kernel can reduce constant-factor overheads (less launch overhead, better memory locality, vectorized inner loops).

2) Periodic structural fusion (collapse and reparameterize) — i.e., multiply several consecutive factors into one orthogonal matrix and use that as a new single parameter (possibly re-factorizing later).
You can at checkpoints multiply $B_{j+1} B_j$ into a single orthogonal matrix $F = B_{j+1} B_j$ and then either (a) keep $F$ as a single trainable orthogonal parameter (thus reducing $m$), or (b) factor $F$ again into fewer factors (e.g., via a chosen factorization) and replace the original two factors.

Pros: reduces the number of active factors $m$ and thus the per-step arithmetic cost and potentially memory traffic for subsequent steps. If you permanently replace two factors by their product and no longer keep the original low-level parameters, you save work in all future steps.

Cons and gradient correctness issues: if you replace two trainable factors by their product and then treat the product matrix as the only trainable object, you have changed the parameterization. Gradients no longer flow to the original factor variables (they no longer exist), so you have changed the optimization landscape/trajectory. This may be acceptable if you view fusion as a reparameterization step (you are intentionally changing the hypothesis class/factorization in a controlled way), but it is not mathematically equivalent to continuing to train the original factors. If you want to remain mathematically equivalent, you must keep the original factors as trainable variables and simply cache the product for use in a fused forward/backward; then you are back to approach (1).

If you want to periodically collapse factors but keep original factorization, you can do the following hybrid: every $K$ steps compute full product $R = B_m \cdots B_1$ and cache it; for the next few steps use cached $R$ in the forward (cheap forward), but to get correct gradients w.r.t. factors you must either (A) compute gradients by differentiating the product (which requires the same chain of operations and hence erases the computational saving), or (B) treat cached forward as a surrogate and periodically synchronize gradients by backpropagating through the factors using a stored checkpoint — both are nontrivial and either reintroduce compute or introduce approximation error.

Re-factorization cost: if you collapse many factors into a single orthogonal and then want to re-express it as fewer factors of the same structured family (e.g., fewer butterfly components), you need a factorization step (for orthogonal matrices you can use algorithms like iterative decomposition into Givens/Householder rotations or Cayley re-parameterization). That decomposition itself has a non-negligible cost and may require numerical procedures (and may not be unique), so periodic re-factorization is an extra overhead and may perturb optimization.

Practical recommendations
If you want lower wall-clock training time but wish to keep the exact same optimization dynamics, invest in a fused forward+backward kernel that applies the $m$ factors in one pass and produces gradients to all factor parameters. This reduces constants and launch overhead while preserving mathematical correctness. For large $m$ (e.g., $m \approx \log d$) this often gives a big win because you avoid many tiny kernels.

If you are willing to change the parameterization occasionally and accept that the optimization path changes, you can periodically collapse factors into fewer trainable matrices. Do this infrequently (coarsely) and accept that you are reparameterizing the model (monitor validation loss). Re-factorization routines are required to keep structure (and they cost extra compute).

Finally, the paper itself calls out this runtime overhead as an open problem (they note training runtime is slightly larger and inference is free after folding), so the authors implicitly endorse either kernel-level engineering or occasional folding into pretrained weights post-finetuning.

Summary
BOFT: asymptotic per-apply cost $O(m d n)$ ($\to O(d \log d \cdot n)$ in full-butterfly mode). Block-diagonal OFT: $O(b d n)$. LoRA: typically much cheaper in practice because $r$ is small, with per-apply cost proportional to rank $r$. Measured in the paper the overhead is described as “slightly larger than OFT” but no explicit timings are reported in your excerpt. You can reduce overhead by kernel fusion that keeps gradients correct, or by periodically collapsing/factorizing factors at the cost of changing parameterization and potentially altering training dynamics.

### How does BOFT handle layers with different input/output dimensions (rectangular \(\mathbf{W}^0\))?
Question:
The formulation uses \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) and left-multiplies by \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). For layers where \(d\neq n\) (e.g., projection up or down), is there any constraint on choosing \(d\) for \(\mathbf{R}\)? Do we ever need a right-multiplicative \(\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{R}\) to affect the output channel interactions, and if so, how would BOFT be adapted while keeping orthogonality?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT as written always multiplies the pretrained weight matrix on the left by a $d$-by-$d$ orthogonal matrix $R$ because it is intended to rotate the input/neuron (row) space; nothing in the paper requires $n = d$. If you want to also mix the output (column) channels, you must introduce a separate $n$-by-$n$ orthogonal factor on the right. BOFT extends straightforwardly: parameterize any square orthogonal factor (left or right) with a butterfly factorization (or a block-butterfly when sizes are not powers of two) and enforce orthogonality per the paper (e.g., Cayley / $2\times2$ rotations). This preserves the orthogonality guarantees and the spectral properties the paper emphasizes.

Explanation and details

What the paper assumes and why left-multiplication is used
The paper defines the pretrained linear layer as $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and replaces the forward pass $z = (W^0)^T x$ by $z = (R W^0)^T x$ with $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ orthogonal. Here $R$ acts on the input / neuron (row) space: it rotates the $d$-dimensional input features / neuron coordinates and therefore preserves pairwise angles between the neurons (columns of $W^0$) as intended by Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). All of that is valid whether or not $n = d$; $R$ must match the input dimension $d$ because it multiplies on the left.

If $d \neq n$, does $R$ have to change?
No. $R$ must be a square orthogonal matrix with size equal to the input dimension ($d\times d$). There is no constraint forcing $d$ to equal $n$. BOFT parameterizes $R$ efficiently (butterfly / block-butterfly) for whatever $d$ is. The paper already treats the general case $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and repeatedly writes $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ left-multiplying $W^0$.

When do you need right-multiplication?
Left-multiplication only changes how inputs / neuron coordinates are mixed. If you want to change how output channels (the columns of $W^0$, i.e., the $n$-dimensional output space) interact with each other — for example to rotate/scramble the output basis or to preserve/encourage angle relationships among output channels — then you must also introduce a right-multiplicative orthogonal factor $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and use $R W^0 S^T$ (or equivalently $(S (W^{0})^T R^T)^T$). Right-multiplication affects column interactions; left-multiplication affects row/input interactions. The two are independent and both can be orthogonal (square) transforms.

How to adapt BOFT while keeping orthogonality
Apply the same BOFT construction independently to each square factor you need:

- For an input-side transform use BOFT to parameterize $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as in the paper: $R = \prod_{i} \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^{b}$ with each butterfly component constrained to be orthogonal (Cayley or $2\times2$ rotations). This keeps the identity initialization and spectral-preserving properties the paper emphasizes.

- For an output-side transform use a separate BOFT (or simpler block-diagonal OFT if you prefer) to parameterize an orthogonal $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. The forward pass becomes $z = (R W^0 S^T)^T x = S (W^{0})^T R^T x$ (same effect). Each square factor is orthogonal by construction, so the final product is valid and preserves singular-value-related properties (orthogonal left and right multipliers only change the left/right singular vectors, not the singular values).

Practical notes consistent with the paper
The paper’s BOFT assumes power-of-two-friendly structure in the standard radix-2 butterfly, but it already describes block-butterfly components and BOFT$(m,b)$ so you can handle arbitrary $d$ and $n$ by choosing block size $b$ (tile into $b\times b$ blocks) or by padding and then masking. Each butterfly component is enforced orthogonal using Cayley/$2\times2$ rotations, so you preserve the orthogonality constraints even when you have two different square sizes. Identity initialization and multiplicative dropout ideas transfer straightforwardly to a right-side BOFT as well.

Spectral / stability consequences
Multiplying $W^0$ on the left by an orthogonal $R$ preserves the singular values; multiplying on the right by orthogonal $S$ also preserves singular values (since $W^0 S^T$ has the same singular values). Thus using $R W^0 S^T$ keeps the spectral norm of $W^0$ intact, keeping the stability benefits the paper highlights.

Summary
BOFT in the paper is defined for $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ even when $W^0$ is rectangular; no constraint forces $d = n$. If you need to manipulate output-channel interactions, introduce a separate $n\times n$ orthogonal factor on the right; BOFT’s butterfly parameterization and orthogonality enforcement apply to either square factor independently (using block-butterfly or padding when sizes aren’t powers of two), preserving the paper’s orthogonality and spectral properties.

### Are there known failure cases where BOFT’s inductive bias hurts performance?
Question:
The paper argues that butterfly structure provides a beneficial implicit bias tied to classic transforms. Are there tasks or layers where this structured prior is too restrictive (e.g., needs non-isometric scaling or highly localized, non-mixing changes)? Any empirical observations or diagnostics to detect when BOFT underfits due to its structure?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report concrete real-world “failure cases”, but it explicitly recognizes and explains where BOFT’s structured orthogonal prior can be too restrictive. In particular, any downstream adaptation that requires changing the pretrained layer’s singular values (i.e., non‑isometric / scaling changes) or that needs highly unstructured, neuron- or coordinate‑specific transformations can force BOFT to underfit. The paper also gives practical ways to diagnose and remedy this (and a few concrete tests you can run).

Why BOFT can hurt performance (intuitive + technical)
BOFT constrains the finetuning update to be a left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $R$ composed from a butterfly factorization. Two important consequences follow directly from the paper’s analysis:

1) Spectral invariance: multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight $W^0$ (the paper states this explicitly via SVD: $R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^T$, so $\Sigma$ is unchanged). Intuitively, BOFT is an isometric reorientation of the weight space, not a rescaling. If a downstream task requires modifying the magnitude spectrum of $W^0$ (e.g., amplifying or damping certain singular directions), BOFT (and any pure orthogonal finetuning method) cannot implement that, and will underfit.

2) Structured expressivity: BOFT restricts $R$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group (butterfly matrices with chosen depth $m$ and block size $b$). That structured prior is helpful when the needed transformation resembles classic fast transforms or smooth global mixing, but it can be too rigid when the optimal adaptation requires very localized, highly unstructured, or coordinate‑dependent changes. The paper’s expressivity discussion and simulation (approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix) show that decreasing $m$ or increasing block size $b$ changes expressivity; small $m/b$ give less expressive hypothesis classes and higher approximation error.

When you should suspect BOFT underfits
- Tasks or layers where you expect necessary changes in scale (singular values) rather than only rotations. Examples (conceptual): reweighting certain features heavily, adapting pretrained features whose norms need to change to match new data distributions.
- Layers that historically benefit from additive or low‑rank updates (LoRA) rather than global reorientation: e.g., final classification heads, or layers where only a few neurons must shift amplitude or be suppressed.
- Small-data regimes where the model must drastically change a few directions to fit new labels (orthogonality may overly constrain the model).

Diagnostics grounded in the paper
The paper suggests several tools (explicit or implicit) you can use to detect BOFT’s underfitting and whether the inductive bias is the problem:

- Compare performance vs baselines (full FT, LoRA, original OFT). If BOFT trails LoRA but OFT/full FT improve substantially, that indicates the orthogonal (or butterfly) restriction is limiting expressivity.

- Monitor the approximation/error curves like the paper’s simulation: attempt to fit a target orthogonal (or directly a target transformation) with $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ and measure approximation error vs number of effective parameters. If the approximation error is large for your chosen $(m,b)$, BOFT cannot represent the needed rotation. This is a quick layer‑level test: try to approximate the empirical $R = W_{\text{new}} (W^0)^{-1}$ (or the left transform you would need) with $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ and check residual.

- Inspect singular values. Since BOFT preserves singular values, compute $\mathrm{SVD}(W_{\text{pre}})$ and $\mathrm{SVD}(W_{\text{finetuned}})$ for a baseline finetuning that you consider “good” (full FT). If the baseline changed $\Sigma$ significantly while BOFT’s best solution cannot, that points to spectral mismatch: BOFT will fail if changing $\Sigma$ is required.

- Check the learned $R$ deviation from identity. BOFT is identity‑initialized (paper), so if training gets stuck near identity (small $\|R - I\|$) while validation loss still high, the structure may be preventing necessary changes. You can also compare layerwise magnitudes of $R - I$ across layers to find which layers are constrained.

- Underfitting stress test: attempt to fit random labels or a synthetic task that requires specific scaling/coordinate changes. If BOFT cannot fit while LoRA/full FT can, that demonstrates expressivity limitations.

How to remedy or rule out the inductive‑bias problem
The paper makes clear the design knobs and tradeoffs: increase expressivity by increasing $m$ (more butterfly layers) or increasing block size $b$; move towards the kaleidoscope/generalized composition if needed. Practically, run a small ablation grid over $(m,b)$ and check whether performance improves as expressivity increases (this is exactly what the paper’s simulations show for approximation error). If performance improves with larger $m/b$ and eventually matches the more flexible baselines, the inductive bias was the limiting factor.

If you need non‑isometric updates, orthogonal finetuning is fundamentally mismatched. In that case either combine BOFT with a small additive update (e.g., LoRA) or switch to a method that permits singular value changes. The paper contrasts orthogonal (multiplicative) updates with additive low‑rank methods and notes they have different inductive biases, so a hybrid approach is natural.

Summary
BOFT’s inductive bias is beneficial for many tasks (the paper demonstrates improved generalization and parameter efficiency), but it can hurt when the task requires changing singular values or very localized, unstructured adjustments that lie outside the structured orthogonal subset BOFT represents. The paper provides the conceptual diagnostics (compare against baselines, approximation tests, singular‑value checks, monitoring $\|R - I\|$, and ablations on $(m,b)$) and the explicit remedies (increase $m/b$, consider the kaleidoscope composition, or combine with additive updates) to detect and mitigate underfitting caused by BOFT’s structure.

### Edge-case question: Does sparsity in \(\mathbf{W}^0\) interact poorly with BOFT’s dense mixing?
Question:
If a pretrained layer \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is already sparse or has strong local structure (e.g., block-sparse), does left-multiplying by a dense orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) harm that structure in a way that degrades downstream performance or interpretability? Are there variants of BOFT that respect a given sparsity pattern (e.g., restricted permutations or masked butterfly factors)?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — a dense left-multiplication can and will generally “mix away” an existing sparsity/local structure in a pretrained layer, which may hurt interpretability and (in some cases) task performance. But BOFT is designed to mitigate that risk and the paper explicitly gives you levers to trade off how much mixing you allow. You can also construct BOFT variants that respect or partially respect a known sparsity pattern (e.g., block structure, restricted permutations or masked butterfly factors) with only modest modification to the method.

Why dense left-multiplication changes sparsity (intuitively and technically)
When you replace the forward pass $z = (W^0)^\top x$ with $z = (R W^0)^\top x$, any nontrivial $R$ that is not block-diagonal will linearly combine rows of $W^0$. If $W^0$ has block-sparsity or strongly local structure, left-multiplying by a dense $R$ spreads (mixes) those row patterns across all output dimensions. Concretely, if $W^0$ has many zero entries in certain columns/rows, $R W^0$ will in general fill in those zeros because each row of $R W^0$ is a linear combination of rows of $W^0$. So a dense $R$ changes the sparsity pattern and the “local” neuron geometry you might have used for interpretability.

How BOFT mitigates the harm (what the paper says)
The BOFT paper frames the factorization $R = B_m \dots B_1$ as an information-transmission graph and explicitly uses sparse orthogonal factors (butterfly components). Two points from the paper are important for your concern:

1) Identity initialization: BOFT initializes all butterfly components to identity (via Cayley parameterization with zero skew-symmetric seeds). So at the start of finetuning $R = I$ and the pretrained sparsity/local structure is preserved exactly. Finetuning therefore produces only gradual departures from the original structure, which helps preserve pretraining knowledge and interpretability early in training.

2) Controlled expressivity via $(m, b)$: BOFT’s design parameters $m$ (number of butterfly components) and $b$ (block size per butterfly block) control how much mixing is possible. $BOFT(1,b)$ is exactly the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (no cross-block mixing). Increasing $m$ and/or decreasing $b$ increases mixing and expressivity. Thus you can intentionally choose small $m$ or larger $b$ to limit how much $R$ can change $W^0$’s sparsity.

Variants that respect a given sparsity pattern
The paper already points to several mechanisms and natural extensions that let you respect or partially respect an existing sparsity pattern:

- Block-butterfly (block size $b$): BOFT defines block butterfly components $\tilde{B}^b$. If $W^0$ is block-sparse (blocks align with neuron groups), pick $b$ equal to the block size and choose $m$ small (even $m=1$) to keep mixing inside blocks only. In the extreme $BOFT(1,b)$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT, which preserves block-sparsity completely.

- Restricted permutations / structured factors: The information-transmission view in the paper frames factor sparsity as an edge set on a grid. That makes it straightforward to restrict which edges (i.e., which nonzero positions) are allowed in each factor. You can therefore choose permutations or butterfly components that only permute/communicate within allowed groups. Technically this is just constraining the sparsity pattern of each $\tilde{B}_{(d,i)}$; as long as each factor remains orthogonal (you can parameterize the permitted subblocks with Cayley/rotations), BOFT still applies.

- Masked butterfly factors: Because each butterfly component is a product of small orthogonal blocks (the paper uses $2\times 2$ rotations as atomic units), you can impose masks that freeze certain $2\times 2$ blocks to identity (equivalently drop edges in the information-transmission graph). The multiplicative Dropout scheme the paper proposes (randomly replacing some blocks with identity) suggests exactly this mechanism and can be used deterministically (mask) to respect a known sparsity pattern during the whole finetuning run.

- Compositions that interpolate expressivity: Theorem 1 and the Kaleidoscope/generalized BOFT construction in the paper show you can compose multiple kinds of butterfly factors (or their transposes) to obtain greater expressivity. The same principle lets you interleave constrained (masked) components with unconstrained ones so that only a controlled amount of global mixing is permitted.

Trade-offs to keep in mind
Preserving the original sparsity (by using block-diagonal or heavily masked BOFT) keeps interpretability and prevents destructive mixing, but reduces the class of orthogonal transformations you can search over, which may limit adaptation if the downstream task truly needs global changes. Conversely, fully dense BOFT (large $m$, small $b$) can recover many classical global transforms and is more expressive, but will erase the original sparsity structure and may harm interpretability or even downstream performance if the pretrained sparsity encodes useful inductive bias.

Other helpful properties from the paper
Because BOFT multiplies an orthogonal $R$ on the left, it preserves the singular values of $W^0$ (the spectral norm is unchanged). That spectral preservation helps training stability and can reduce risk of catastrophic changes to the scale of $W^0$ even when the sparsity pattern changes. Also, identity init + small learning steps means the initial finetuned model is close to the pretrained one and mixing happens progressively.

Practical recommendations
If you are worried about destroying a known sparse/local structure: start with $BOFT(1,b)$ with block size $b$ matched to your blocks (this is block-diagonal OFT). If you need limited cross-block mixing, use a block-butterfly with that $b$ and small $m$, or apply deterministic masks that keep certain butterfly blocks identity. Use identity initialization so the network doesn’t instantly scramble structure. If you want to explore whether more global mixing helps, gradually increase $m$ or unmask a small number of butterfly blocks and monitor both validation performance and whether interpretability diagnostics (e.g., neuron localization, sparsity metrics) degrade.

In short: dense left-multiplication will in general alter sparsity and interpretability, but BOFT explicitly offers mechanisms (identity init, block-butterfly, mask/dropout, and tuneable $(m,b)$) to control and respect existing sparsity patterns while still gaining parameter efficiency and the benefits of orthogonal finetuning.

### Practical initialization: Is identity initialization sufficient when stacking many butterfly components?
Question:
BOFT initializes each butterfly component as identity. With \(m=\log d\) or larger, could this create optimization issues (e.g., many layers initially doing nothing, leading to small gradients)? Have you tried small random skew-symmetric initializations in \(\mathbf{Q}\) or a warm-start schedule where components are unfrozen progressively?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses identity initialization (Cayley with $Q=0$) by design, and that is a reasonable default because it exactly preserves the pretrained model and gives stable training. However, in practice stacking many small orthogonal factors (large $m$, e.g. $m \approx \log d$) can reduce the initial “signal” coming from the learned factors and so can reduce the immediate gradient magnitudes to those parameters — this is a plausible optimization issue. The paper does not report explicit experiments with small random skew‑symmetric initialization or progressive unfreezing; it only states that butterfly components are initialized to identity and proposes multiplicative Dropout for regularization.

Why identity init is used and why it helps. BOFT initializes each butterfly component to identity ($Q=0$ in Cayley). That gives zero net change to the pretrained weights at the start of finetuning, matching the usual PEFT desideratum of starting from the pretrained model exactly. Also, because BOFT multiplies an orthogonal $R$ on the left, starting at identity preserves spectral properties (singular values) and tends to make optimization stable — the paper highlights this spectral-preservation benefit.

Why identity init can be problematic when $m$ is large. If $R = \prod_{i=1}^m B_i$ and each $B_i$ starts at $I$, then initially $R \approx I$ and small updates to many $B_i$ compound multiplicatively. Two practical consequences follow: (1) the per‑factor gradient wrt the skew matrix $Q$ may be small because the forward/backward paths see little effect of each tiny rotation when all others are near $I$; (2) optimization can become reliant on coordinated updates across many factors, which is harder if gradients are tiny or noisy. Intuitively, many “do‑nothing” factors make the parameterization very redundant at initialization and can slow early learning of nontrivial transforms.

What the paper provides instead. The paper does not claim optimization problems arise in practice, and their empirical results show BOFT works across many tasks with identity init. They also introduce multiplicative Dropout (randomly replacing some butterfly components or blocks with identity during training), which both regularizes and — by intermittently forcing some components to be identity — can increase robustness and encourage nontrivial factors to be learned. But the paper does not report exploring non‑zero $Q$ initializations or staged unfreezing.

Practical recommendations (grounded in the paper’s parameterization and Cayley form). If you encounter slow learning or very small gradients with large $m$, try one or more of these strategies:

1) Small skew‑symmetric noise in $Q$. Initialize the skew matrix $Q_i$ for each butterfly component with a small antisymmetric random matrix: $Q \leftarrow \varepsilon\cdot(A - A^T)$ where $A_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon$ is small. This gives small initial rotations. Reasonable starting scales are $\varepsilon \in [10^{-3}, 10^{-2}]$ (or set each $2\times 2$ block to a tiny rotation angle $\theta$ where $\theta \in [0.01,0.1]$ radians, approximately $0.6$–$6$ degrees). This preserves the “near identity” property but gives more gradient signal. Because BOFT uses Cayley, small $Q$ maps to a near‑identity orthogonal; you retain stability while increasing initial sensitivity.

2) Small random rotations per $2\times 2$ blocks. Since BOFT builds orthogonality from many $2\times 2$ blocks, you can directly initialize each $2\times 2$ block as a rotation by small angle $\theta$ sampled from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ ($\sigma \approx 0.01$–$0.05$). This is equivalent to a structured skew init and is inexpensive to implement.

3) Progressive (warm) unfreezing. Start with only the first $k$ butterfly components trainable (others kept identity), then progressively unfreeze more components (increase $k$) over the first $N$ steps/epochs. This reduces the coordination burden and lets earlier components learn a coarse transform before later components refine it.

4) Scale learning rates or use per‑factor LR. Give slightly larger LR to the Cayley parameters $Q$ (or use adaptive optimizers) until the factors move away from identity. Monitor the norm of $R-I$ or $Q$ to decide when to reduce LR.

5) Use multiplicative Dropout / stochastic depth on components. The paper’s multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces some components with identity during training. This serves both as regularization and as a way to force different components to carry signal; tuning the dropout percentages ($p_1,p_2$ in paper) can help escape a purely identity regime.

What to monitor. Track gradient norms of $Q$ (or of the $2\times 2$ block params) and the norm of $R-I$ per layer. If gradients on $Q$ are vanishingly small and $R$ remains extremely close to $I$ for many steps, try the small skew init + higher LR for those parameters or progressive unfreeze.

Summary recommendation. Identity init is principled and usually stable (the paper uses it and reports good results), but when $m$ is large you may see weaker initial gradient signal. The simplest practical remedies are tiny skew‑symmetric noise ($\varepsilon \approx 10^{-3}\ldots10^{-2}$) or small per‑block rotation angles, possibly combined with a slightly larger LR or staged unfreezing. The paper itself does not evaluate these variants, so these are empirical suggestions consistent with BOFT’s Cayley parameterization and the paper’s multiplicative Dropout idea.
