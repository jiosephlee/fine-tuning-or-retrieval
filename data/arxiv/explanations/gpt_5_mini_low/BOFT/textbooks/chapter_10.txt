\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 10: Applying BOFT to Vision, Language, and Generative Models

# Large Vision Transformers: attention and MLP layers to target; typical $b, m$
When adapting large vision transformers (ViTs) with BOFT, the natural places to insert orthogonal transformations are the large, layerwise linear maps: the projection matrices inside the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) modules (queries, keys, values, and the output projection) and the two large linear layers inside each feed-forward/MLP block. These matrices are often the dominant parameter and computation loci in ViTs, and they are exactly the kinds of pretrained weight matrices $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ that BOFT was designed to modify multiplicatively, i.e. producing $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$.

From the BOFT parameterization in the paper, each orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is formed as a product of $m$ block-butterfly components with block-size parameter $b$: $\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Two canonical regimes are helpful to keep in mind. The block-diagonal OFT corresponds to $m=1$ (BOFT$(1,b)$), which is the most parameter-efficient per-layer option but has the least cross-coordinate mixing; the “butterfly” dense regime corresponds to $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, which yields a dense orthogonal matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective parameters and much greater expressivity. In practice for ViTs you can trade off expressivity and parameter cost by choosing intermediate $(m,b)$ pairs: larger $m$ (more components) and smaller $b$ (finer blocks) increase expressivity and parameter count; smaller $m$ or larger $b$ reduce parameters but limit mixing across coordinates.

Practical guidance grounded in the BOFT framework is to prioritize more expressive BOFT (larger $m$, smaller $b$) for the largest linear maps you wish to adapt, and use smaller $(m,b)$ for smaller layers. Start experiments with BOFT$(\log d,2)$ as a reference for full dense behavior and BOFT$(1,b)$ for cheap, localized adaptation. Always use the identity-based initialization prescribed in the paper (each butterfly component initialized to identity via Cayley transform with zero skew-symmetric parameters) so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained behavior and only gradually deviates. For training regularization use BOFT’s multiplicative Dropout strategy (randomly set some butterfly components or blocks to identity during training) to reduce overfitting in high-capacity settings.

# Large Language Models: QKV/O projections vs. MLPs; stability considerations
In large language models (LLMs) the same architectural observations apply: the attention QKV and output projection matrices and the feed-forward MLP layers contain large linear maps where BOFT can be applied. Which of these to target will depend on the adaptation objective and the parameter budget. The QKV projections are small per-head but numerous (many heads across many layers), while the attention output projections and the MLP dense layers are often the largest single matrices. BOFT is most attractive for matrices with large embedding dimensionality $d$ because the asymptotic parameter saving of the butterfly parameterization ($\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a dense orthogonal) becomes significant.

An important advantage of orthogonal finetuning (and therefore BOFT) emphasized in the paper is improved spectral properties: left-multiplying a pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix preserves the singular value spectrum of $\mathbf{W}^0$ (if $\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$, then $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ and the singular values remain $\mathbf{\Sigma}$). This preservation helps training stability compared to unconstrained additive updates because it avoids inflating or shrinking the spectral norm of pretrained layers. For LLMs—where stability is critical—this spectral invariance makes BOFT a compelling alternative to low-rank additive methods in regimes where multiplicative updates are acceptable.

Still, BOFT introduces some training overhead because the orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is implemented as a product of $m$ factors and each factor must be applied in the forward/backward passes. The paper notes this overhead is modest but nonzero during training; after finetuning, the learned $\mathbf{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight and there is no inference latency. For stability in LLM finetuning, use identity initialization, consider keeping $(m,b)$ conservative in the earliest experiments, and apply multiplicative Dropout to components/blocks if overfitting or instability appears. Because QKV weight shapes are often smaller in $d$, BOFT$(1,b)$ or BOFT with small $m$ may be preferable for per-head projections, while MLPs and attention output projections can get larger $m$ to capture more global mixing.

# Text-to-Image Diffusion Models: which modules benefit most; training stability
The motivating prior work on OFT in the paper applied orthogonal finetuning to text-to-image diffusion models; BOFT generalizes that approach with a parameter-efficient factorization. In diffusion models, the prominent large linear maps appear in U‑Net architectures (convolutions and linear projections in self-attention blocks) and conditioning pathways (text encoders to cross-attention matrices). BOFT is directly applicable to any linear projection or 1×1 convolution (viewed as a linear map with channel dimension $d$) where multiplicative orthogonal modification is appropriate.

Which modules benefit most depends on where you want to preserve pretrained relational structure. The paper’s motivation—preserving pairwise angles among neurons—suggests applying BOFT where the geometric relationships encoded in pretrained weights are important for generation quality (for example, the main residual blocks and attention projections that mediate global structure rather than very local convolutions). As with ViTs and LLMs, choose larger $(m,b)$ (more expressive BOFT) for the largest or most critical projections, and smaller options where you need strict parameter control. Again, identity initialization is essential to avoid distorting pretrained generation early in training.

Stability considerations are especially important in generative diffusion training because generation quality is sensitive to small spectral shifts; BOFT’s spectral-preserving property helps maintain stable generative behavior. The paper also notes that multiplicative Dropout can be applied to BOFT (turn some components or blocks to identity during training) to avoid overfitting and to improve generalization of the adapted generative model.

# Parameter budgeting across depth: heterogeneous $m$ and $b$ per layer
BOFT naturally lends itself to a heterogeneous allocation of budget across depth because $(m,b)$ can be chosen per-layer. The paper formalizes BOFT as BOFT$(m,b)$ for a layer of dimension $d$, and highlights that BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as BOFT$(1,b)$ and the fully expressive butterfly as BOFT$(\log d,2)$. This parametrization lets you place more mixture capacity where it is most needed and save parameters elsewhere.

A practical budgeting strategy consistent with the paper’s principles is to (1) identify the layers with the largest $d$ (embedding dimensionality or channel width) and the layers most sensitive to downstream distributional shift (often later layers or transformer blocks closest to the output), (2) assign higher expressivity there by increasing $m$ and decreasing $b$ (move toward BOFT$(\log d,2)$), and (3) assign cheaper BOFT$(1,b)$ or BOFT with small $m$ to less critical layers. This produces a smaller hypothesis class in aggregate than unrestricted finetuning while allowing targeted flexibility where it helps the most. The paper also suggests that BOFT’s structured subset-of-orthogonal-group nature imposes a favorable inductive bias: smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal transforms, which can improve generalization when you constrain some layers more tightly.

Because the effective parameter count for BOFT$(m,b)$ on a $d\times n$ linear layer is approximately $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable parameters (as derived in the paper), you can budget globally by summing this over layers and then tuning per-layer $(m,b)$ to meet a total parameter budget. Start with a uniform budget allocation that scales with $d$ across layers, then refine by moving capacity toward layers that give the largest validation gains.

# Interplay with quantization and other PEFT methods (e.g., QLoRA)
The paper contrasts BOFT with additive low-rank PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA) and notes key differences: BOFT is multiplicative and orthogonality-constrained, preserving spectral norms and providing a distinct regularizing inductive bias. It also emphasizes that BOFT’s learned orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ can be absorbed into the pretrained weight after finetuning so that there is no inference-time overhead.

That absorbability implies compatibility with downstream inference optimizations such as weight quantization: because the final model after BOFT finetuning is just a set of standard weight matrices (the modified $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$), it can be quantized or further optimized with the same tooling used for fully-finetuned networks. Consequently, BOFT does not inherently prevent deploying a quantized model or using quantization-aware compression techniques; you should apply the usual post-processing (e.g., quantize after merging) and re-evaluate accuracy. The paper points out a training-time caveat: BOFT introduces extra computation during finetuning because of the factorized orthogonal components, so techniques that compress the training footprint (mixed precision, gradient checkpointing) remain useful.

Regarding combining BOFT with alternative PEFT methods (for example, LoRA or adapters), the paper frames BOFT as an alternative parameterization family (sparse-factorized orthogonal transforms) rather than an additive low-rank correction. While the paper does not experimentally explore hybrid schemes in detail, one clear pathway implied by the framework is orthogonal multiplicative adaptation for preserving spectral structure in some layers and additive low-rank or adapter modules in others—this would let you combine the strengths of both families (BOFT’s spectral preservation and structured inductive bias with LoRA’s straightforward low-rank flexibility). If you choose a hybrid approach, be mindful of interactions: multiplicative BOFT changes the left singular vectors of a layer while preserving singular values; additive updates change singular values and singular vectors. Empirical validation would be necessary to find complementary allocations.

Finally, for approaches like QLoRA (which use quantization during finetuning), the training-time overhead of BOFT’s factorized application must be considered: BOFT requires applying its factorized orthogonal transforms in forward/backward passes, so combine with training-time quantized or memory-saving PEFT pipelines only after ensuring the implementation supports the necessary operations efficiently. Post-finetuning, however, BOFT’s merged weights are fully compatible with quantization-based inference pipelines.

# Summary recommendations
- Target the largest linear maps (attention output projections, MLP dense layers, large 1×1 convolutions); use identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout as described for regularization.
- Use BOFT$(1,b)$ (block-diagonal OFT) as a low-cost baseline; use BOFT$(\log d,2)$ to reach dense expressivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. Sweep intermediate $(m,b)$ to trade capacity against budget.
- Allocate budget heterogeneously across layers by scaling $(m,b)$ with layer width $d$ and empirical sensitivity; use the approximate parameter formula $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ per layer to plan totals.
- Expect modest training-time overhead from factorized orthogonal components; after finetuning, merge $\mathbf{R}$ into pretrained weights for zero inference cost and compatibility with quantization and other inference optimizations.
- For LLMs and diffusion models, leverage BOFT’s spectral-preserving property to improve finetuning stability, and combine with multiplicative Dropout when overfitting is a concern.

These guidelines follow directly from BOFT’s design and properties in the paper: a factorized orthogonal parameterization that preserves singular values, can interpolate between identity and full orthogonal groups through $(m,b)$ choices, and yields substantial parameter savings (down to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$) while remaining expressive for many practical adaptation needs.
