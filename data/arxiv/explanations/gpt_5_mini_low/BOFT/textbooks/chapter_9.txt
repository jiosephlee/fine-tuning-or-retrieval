\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 9: Complexity, Implementation, and Engineering Tips

# Time–space trade-off: repeated sparse multiplies vs. parameter savings
BOFT attains parameter efficiency by replacing a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal factors
$\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b$. This reparameterization reduces the number of trainable entries from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (full orthogonal) down to roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective parameters for BOFT($m$,$b$), and in the canonical butterfly case ($m=\log d$, $b=2$) to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. The cost of this space saving is that each forward (and backward) application of $\bm{R}$ requires sequentially applying the $m$ sparse factors rather than a single dense multiply. Practically this leads to a time–space trade-off that must be considered for engineering choices.

When evaluating this trade-off, keep three points in mind. First, each factor is very sparse and structured (blockwise $2\times 2$ rotations or their block generalizations), so the per-factor computation is much cheaper than a general dense multiply. Second, the cost scales linearly with $m$; choosing smaller $m$ (fewer factors, larger block size $b$) reduces runtime at the expense of parameter efficiency and expressivity. Third, the runtime overhead is most significant during training (where factors are repeatedly applied). After training, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ to remove any inference overhead. Thus, when inference latency is the dominant constraint, favor configurations with larger $m$ (to save parameters) during training because they do not penalize inference once merged.

Architecturally, exploit the structure of the factors to reduce constant overheads: implement the sparse multiplies as batched small-block operations (see next sections), fuse successive factors where possible, and avoid materializing full dense intermediate matrices. Empirically, the choice of $(m,b)$ should be guided by how much parameter savings are required vs. acceptable training-time cost: BOFT($1,b$) recovers block-diagonal OFT (fast, less expressive), while BOFT($\log d,2$) provides maximal parameter compression with higher training-time cost.

# Vectorized blockwise Cayley transforms and stable inverses
To maintain orthogonality of every factor during optimization, BOFT follows the Cayley parameterization used in OFT: for a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ we form
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. BOFT implements each factor as a block-structured orthogonal component built from many small orthogonal blocks (commonly $2\times 2$ rotations, or $2b\times 2b$ blocks for block-butterfly variants). This blockwise view affords two implementation advantages:

1) Vectorized parameterization. Instead of constructing full $d\times d$ skew-symmetric matrices, parameterize only the independent entries inside each small block. For a $2\times 2$ rotation block, a single scalar (or angle) suffices; for $2b\times 2b$ blocks, parameterize the skew-symmetric block at block resolution. Arrange these small blocks into tensors so that the Cayley transform is computed in parallel across blocks. This avoids dense linear-algebra on size $d$ and leverages efficient batched small-matrix kernels on modern accelerators.

2) Stable inverse computation. The Cayley formula requires computing $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ for each factor. Because each $\bm{Q}$ is block-sparse (and initialized to zero for identity initialization), the inverse reduces to many independent small inverses on the blocks. Those small inverses are stable to compute and can be implemented with closed-form formulas for $2\times 2$ blocks or with small LU solves for larger blocks. Working blockwise prevents numerical issues that might arise if one attempted to invert a dense $d\times d$ matrix for every factor.

Important engineering notes: initialize all skew-symmetric block parameters to zero so each Cayley-transformed factor starts as identity (zero deviation), which preserves the pretrained model initially. During backward passes, use automatic differentiation over the blockwise Cayley pipeline; because all operations are local to blocks, computational graphs remain small and memory friendly.

# Permutation-aware layout for efficient memory access
A key property of butterfly components is that their non-zero pattern is a structured permutation of the $2\times 2$ block interactions. Implementations should exploit this by storing data and parameters in a layout that matches the computation pattern, minimizing expensive gathers/scatters.

Concretely, each butterfly component can be viewed as a collection of disjoint small-block operations acting on specific pairs of indices. Arrange the input activation vectors so that pairs that will be jointly processed by the current layer of butterfly blocks are contiguous in memory. This permutation-aware layout lets you express each layer as a single batched block-matrix multiply over contiguous memory slices, eliminating many index indirections.

Two practical strategies:

- Precompute and cache the index permutations corresponding to each butterfly component. Use these permutations to apply reshapes and contiguous slices rather than scatter/gather kernels. On GPUs/accelerators, contiguous slices exploit coalesced memory access and give large speedups.

- Fuse permutation, block grouping, and small-block Cayley application into a single kernel when possible. For example, reshape the input as (batch, groups, block_size), apply one batched small-matrix multiply across groups, and then reshape/back-permute. This reduces kernel launches and intermediate memory writes.

Because BOFT factors are reused at every forward/backward step, the one-time cost of computing and encoding permutation metadata pays off quickly during training.

# Drop-in integration for linear/attention/MLP layers; handling non-power-of-two d
BOFT is designed to be a layer-wise multiplicative adapter: the finetuned weight for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0$ becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with a shared $\bm{R}$ per layer. This makes integration straightforward for different module types:

- Linear layers: replace the forward computation $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ by $((\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top)\bm{x}$. During training, implement this as applying $\bm{R}$ to the frozen $\bm{W}^0$ (or equivalently apply $\bm{R}^\top$ to inputs if that is cheaper), but avoid materializing $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ as dense unless merging for inference.

- Attention projections: apply BOFT to the key, query, value, and/or output projection matrices by left-multiplying each $\bm{W}^0$ with its layer-specific $\bm{R}$. Because BOFT uses a layer-shared orthogonal matrix that preserves spectral properties, it is directly compatible with attention heads and does not introduce head-specific state unless you choose to use distinct $\bm{R}$ per head.

- MLP (feedforward) layers: apply BOFT to the input-side linear weight $\bm{W}^0$ of each dense block so that neuron geometry is preserved across the layer.

Non-power-of-two dimensionalities: the canonical butterfly factorization assumes $d=2^N$ for neat recursive structure, but the BOFT paper explicitly discusses blockwise generalizations. In practice, handle non-power-of-two $d$ by using a block size $b>2$ and a suitable number of factors $m$ so that the block-partitioning covers $d$ (for example, partition into $\lceil d/b\rceil$ blocks and treat the last block specially). The block-butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ formalism supports $b>2$; parameterize the $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks in the smallest component and permute accordingly in higher components. If an exact tiling is not possible, pad the dimension to the next convenient multiple, initialize the padding to identity (so it has no effect), and ignore padded outputs when computing loss. These approaches preserve the BOFT conceptual framework without requiring strict powers of two.

# Initialization, learning rates, weight decay, and dropout schedules
Initialization. As the BOFT paper emphasizes, start with identity initialization: set the skew-symmetric block parameters to zero so every Cayley-transformed component is initially $\bm{I}$. This ensures the finetuned model begins identical to the pretrained model and prevents sudden departures during early optimization.

Learning rates and optimizer. BOFT is a multiplicative, structured reparameterization of the weight; avoid using the same large learning rates as used for training dense models from scratch. The paper highlights that orthogonal finetuning preserves spectral norms and tends to be stable; nevertheless, practical training should use modest learning rates for the BOFT parameters, often smaller than those applied to newly added linear heads. If you already use a small global finetuning rate for pretrained weights, treat BOFT parameters similarly or slightly larger depending on empirical behavior. Standard adaptive optimizers (Adam-family) or SGD can be used; because parameters are structured and few, monitor convergence and scale LR as needed.

Weight decay. Because BOFT parameters are not unconstrained dense weights but entries that represent skew-symmetric blocks feeding into Cayley transforms, weight decay interacts subtly with the induced orthogonal matrices. Use conservative weight decay or none initially; because orthogonality constrains the norm of the effective transform, aggressive weight decay is rarely necessary. If you apply standard weight decay schedules, apply them directly to the trainable block parameters (not to the frozen $\bm{W}^0$).

Dropout schedules (multiplicative dropout). The BOFT paper introduces multiplicative Dropout adapted to multiplicative updates: randomly replace a percentage $p_1$ of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of block-diagonal blocks within each component by identity matrices during training. This is different from conventional additive Dropout and is designed to regularize the multiplicative update. In practice, schedule these probabilities to be higher early in training and decay them as convergence nears, or keep them fixed if overfitting appears. Typical engineering strategy: start with small $p_1,p_2$ to avoid destabilizing early training (e.g., single-digit percentages), then increase if you observe overfitting. Because the paper does not prescribe exact values, tune on validation performance for your task.

Zero initialization + dropout synergy. Zero (identity) initialization coupled with multiplicative dropout provides a conservative starting point and regularizes the path away from the pretrained solution. This combination tends to promote stable fine-tuning, consistent with the paper’s motivation to preserve pretrained geometry.

# Merging learned R into W^0 after training for zero inference overhead
One of BOFT’s practical advantages is that after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ can be merged into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ by computing $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $\bm{W}^0$ is fixed, this is a one-time dense matrix multiply per finetuned layer and removes any inference-time cost associated with BOFT’s factorized representation.

Engineering checklist for merging:
- At the end of training, materialize $\bm{R}$ by composing the learned factors into a dense $d\times d$ matrix. Because $m$ is modest (e.g., $\log d$ factors), this composition is cheap compared to full training.
- Multiply $\bm{R}$ with $\bm{W}^0$ to produce $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ and replace the layer’s weights with $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ for deployment.
- Drop the BOFT factors and any auxiliary permutation metadata from the deployment artifact. The resulting model has identical inference behavior to a model that applied BOFT on-the-fly, but with no runtime overhead.
- Optionally, if storage is at a premium, compress $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ in the same way you would any dense weight (quantization, pruning, etc.). Because BOFT does not change the rank or singular values of $\bm{W}^0$ (left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values), post-merge compression behavior will be similar to compressing $\bm{W}^0$.

This merge is the recommended production path: accept training-time overhead in exchange for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, then produce a normal dense model for inference.

# Practical recipe and debugging tips
Putting the above pieces together, a minimal practical implementation plan is:

1. Decide target parameter budget and runtime trade-off. Choose $(m,b)$ so that $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ fits your budget and training-time overhead is acceptable. For maximal compression choose $b=2$, $m\approx\log d$; for faster training choose larger $b$ and smaller $m$.

2. Implement blockwise Cayley parameterization per factor: store skew-symmetric block parameters (initialized to zero), compute the Cayley transform blockwise with batched small-matrix kernels, and assemble the factor as a block-diagonal or permuted-block structure.

3. Implement permutation-aware batched application of each factor to activations or to $\bm{W}^0$ via contiguous reshapes and batched small multiplies rather than scatter/gather.

4. Add multiplicative Dropout: during training, randomly replace selected factors/blocks with identity (controlled by $p_1,p_2$). Keep parameters for these probabilities configurable and schedulable.

5. Train with conservative learning rates for BOFT parameters, modest weight decay, and monitor validation performance. If training is unstable, reduce the BOFT learning rate or increase identity-preserving regularization (higher dropout).

6. After convergence, compose $m$ factors into dense $\bm{R}$, compute $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and replace weights for inference.

Common debugging signals and remedies:
- Training instability (loss spikes): ensure identity initialization and reduce BOFT learning rate; increase multiplicative dropout temporarily.
- Slow training throughput: fuse permutation + block multiply operations and reduce kernel launches; consider increasing $b$ (fewer factors) if parameter budget allows.
- Poor generalization (overfitting): increase multiplicative dropout or reduce the number of trainable parameters (smaller $b$ or fewer factors). The structured inductive bias of butterfly factorizations can help, but excessive expressivity may overfit.

# Limitations to keep in mind
BOFT deliberately trades training runtime for parameter efficiency; expect slightly higher per-step cost compared to block-diagonal OFT or simple additive adapters. Also, while the block-butterfly formalism supports non-power-of-two extensions, implementations must handle padding or uneven block tiling carefully. Finally, because BOFT factors are constrained to be orthogonal blocks via Cayley transforms, interactions with standard weight-decay and regularization schemes may behave differently than with unconstrained parameters; tune conservatively.

Summary
BOFT is a practical, parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method that is implementable with careful attention to blockwise Cayley transforms, permutation-aware data layout, and multiplicative dropout. Engineer the $(m,b)$ trade-off to meet your training budget, exploit batched small-block kernels and cached permutations to reduce overhead, and merge $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ after training to obtain zero-cost inference.
