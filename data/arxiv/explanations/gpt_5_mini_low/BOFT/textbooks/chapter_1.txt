\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: From Full Finetuning to Parameter-Efficient Adaptation

# Scaling of foundation models and the cost of full finetuning

Over the last few years, foundation models—large pretrained models trained on massive uncurated datasets—have grown dramatically in both capability and size. Representative examples include GPT-3 in language and Stable Diffusion in vision: these models contain tens to hundreds of billions of parameters and exhibit strong generalization across many downstream tasks. This scale delivers impressive performance, but it also creates a practical barrier: training such models from scratch or even repeatedly fine-tuning all parameters for each downstream task is prohibitively expensive in compute, memory, and human time.

Full finetuning a foundation model means updating (and storing) the entire parameter set for each task. When models have on the order of $10^{10}$–$10^{11}$ parameters, that approach multiplies costs across tasks and users. Beyond raw monetary and energy costs, full finetuning is operationally cumbersome: cloning large checkpoints, performing lengthy optimization runs, and maintaining many task-specific parameter copies are all undesirable. Moreover, unrestricted changes to the full parameter set can destroy useful pretrained structure and sometimes harm generalization on small target datasets.

These limitations motivate an alternative paradigm: instead of modifying all parameters, adapt models in a parameter-efficient way that preserves most pretrained weights while allowing the model to specialize. Reducing the number of trainable parameters lowers storage and computational demands for adaptation, enables rapid experimentation, and often reduces the risk of overfitting by constraining how much the model can change.

# Three PEFT families: model finetuning, adapter tuning, prompt/prefix tuning

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods broadly fall into three families, each embodying a different strategy for where and how to introduce task-specific flexibility while keeping most of the pretrained model fixed.

The first family keeps the original architecture unchanged and selectively updates a subset of model parameters. This includes straightforward strategies such as tuning only biases, layer norms, or a small subset of weights, and using small learning rates so the model remains close to its pretrained state. The attraction of this family is that it avoids inference-time overhead (no extra modules) and remains simple to implement and reason about.

The second family augments the network with small trainable modules—so-called adapters—and leaves the pretrained weights fixed. Adapter tuning inserts lightweight learnable layers (for example, small MLPs) at various points in the network; only these adapter parameters are optimized for a downstream task. This approach is flexible: adapters can be placed where they are most effective, and their isolation from the pretrained weights makes it easy to compose multiple adapters or share them across tasks.

The third family is prompt or prefix tuning, where task adaptation is achieved by adding trainable tokens or prefixes to the model input. These extra tokens act like a learned prompt that steers the frozen model to produce task-specific behavior. Prompt tuning and related methods have the advantage of extreme parameter efficiency: only a small set of virtual tokens are learned. However, they are limited to architectures and tasks that can accept and exploit input prefixes effectively.

Each family has trade-offs. Adapter and prompt methods introduce extra inference components or rely on model interfaces, while updating a subset of weights retains the original architecture but can be limited in expressive power if the subset is too small. The remainder of this chapter focuses on a principled variant of the first family—reparameterization-based model finetuning—that explicitly constrains the form of weight updates to preserve desirable properties of the pretrained model.

# Reparameterization-based PEFT: LoRA and beyond

Reparameterization-based PEFT methods change how weights are updated by expressing the finetuned weight matrix as a function of the frozen pretrained weights plus a compact, learnable transformation. The key idea is to restrict the parameterization of the update so that it uses far fewer trainable parameters while still providing sufficient adaptation capacity.

A canonical example is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). LoRA replaces the additive full-rank update to a pretrained weight matrix with an additive low-rank decomposition: the finetuned weight is

z = (W^0 + \Delta W)^\top x, with \Delta W = A B,

where A and B are small matrices of rank r. By choosing r small, LoRA dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to full finetuning. It has proven effective for many language tasks and gained popularity because of its simplicity and low inference overhead: the low-rank updates can be merged into the pretrained weights after training.

Reparameterization approaches are attractive because they can be designed to reflect prior beliefs about which transformations are plausible or safe for a pretrained model. Low-rank updates assume that the task-specific changes lie in a low-dimensional subspace, which is often a reasonable approximation. However, low-rank structure is only one of several possible structured parameterizations. Other approaches explore diagonal, block-diagonal, or more exotic structured parameterizations to trade expressivity against parameter efficiency and regularity.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which we will discuss next, is another reparameterization approach—but it takes a multiplicative rather than additive view of adaptation and enforces orthogonality constraints on the learned transformation. This yields different theoretical and practical properties from additive low-rank updates.

# Motivation for orthogonal finetuning: preserving pretrained knowledge

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is motivated by a structural perspective on neural weights. Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix W^0. The columns of W^0 (the neuron weight vectors) encode relational information, including pairwise angles between neurons, which are meaningful because they reflect how different neurons partition or relate input features. Large-scale pretraining tends to arrange neurons and representations in structured geometric ways that capture useful semantic knowledge.

If finetuning arbitrarily changes the weight matrix, those relational structures can be distorted, potentially discarding useful pretrained structure and harming generalization. OFT addresses this concern by restricting the finetuning transformation to be an orthogonal matrix R applied multiplicatively to the rows (or equivalently, to the neuron vectors): the finetuned weights become R W^0. Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and lengths, applying R preserves pairwise angles between neuron vectors. Thus, OFT attempts to modify the model while maintaining the geometric relationships learned during pretraining, ensuring that pretrained knowledge is not destroyed.

Concretely, OFT parameterizes a learnable orthogonal matrix R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} and transforms the forward pass from z = (W^0)^\top x to z = (R W^0)^\top x, initializing R as the identity so the finetuning starts from the pretrained model. To maintain orthogonality during training, OFT uses parameterizations such as the Cayley transform, which maps skew-symmetric matrices Q (with Q = -Q^\top) to orthogonal matrices via R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}. The orthogonality constraint gives OFT desirable spectral properties: left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values of W^0, which helps retain stability and can aid generalization.

Despite these advantages, OFT in its unrestricted form requires learning a full d \times d orthogonal matrix, which consumes \mathcal{O}(d^2) parameters—too many to be parameter-efficient when d is large. OFT therefore originally introduced block-diagonal structure to reduce parameter count, but that introduces its own limitations, as we will summarize next.

# High-level overview of OFT vs. BOFT and their goals

OFT and BOFT share the core conceptual goal: enable stable, generalizable finetuning by transforming pretrained weights via an orthogonal matrix that preserves relational structure among neurons. They differ primarily in how that orthogonal matrix is parameterized to achieve parameter efficiency.

OFT in practice uses two main parameter regimes. The unconstrained OFT learns a full orthogonal R of size d \times d, preserving all expressivity at the cost of \mathcal{O}(d^2) parameters. To be more practical, OFT often restricts R to be block-diagonal, R = \text{diag}(R_1,\ldots,R_r), where each block R_i is of size b \times b and b r = d. This block-diagonal OFT reduces trainable parameters to \mathcal{O}(b d), which can be manageable. However, the block-diagonal assumption enforces that different contiguous groups of dimensions do not interact under the learned orthogonal transform. This hard sparsity pattern can limit expressivity: block-diagonal transforms cannot approximate many dense linear mappings or recover classic transforms like the discrete Fourier transform, and the particular grouping by contiguous indices has no principled justification.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) addresses this tension—achieving dense transform expressivity while keeping parameter counts low—by factorizing the dense orthogonal matrix R as a product of multiple sparse, structured orthogonal matrices. Instead of learning R directly, BOFT represents

R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1,

where each B_i is a sparse orthogonal factor structured according to a butterfly pattern inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. The butterfly structure is a layered sparse connectivity pattern that efficiently routes information between all input and output coordinates in O(d \log d) edges when d is a power of two. When each sparse factor is itself orthogonal, their product is orthogonal, so BOFT enforces orthogonality while dramatically reducing the number of free parameters.

There are several virtues to this approach. First, BOFT can produce dense orthogonal matrices (the product of sparse factors yields dense connectivity), avoiding the expressivity limitations of block-diagonal OFT. Second, the butterfly factorization reduces parameter complexity from \mathcal{O}(d^2) to \mathcal{O}(d \log d) when using radix-2 butterfly factors with block size b = 2 and m \approx \log d layers. Third, the butterfly structure is not an arbitrary sparsity pattern: it aligns with fast linear transform topologies (e.g., FFT, Hadamard) known to mix local and global information efficiently, which supplies an inductive bias that can help generalization.

BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases: if m = 1 and block size b varies, BOFT(m,b) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size b; if m = 1 and b = d, BOFT reduces to unconstrained OFT. At the other extreme, BOFT with m \approx \log d and small b yields a dense R with far fewer trainable parameters than the full OFT. Practically, BOFT initializes its butterfly factors to identity (so finetuning starts from the pretrained model) and uses an orthogonal parameterization (e.g., via Cayley transforms on small blocks) to preserve orthogonality during optimization. To mitigate overfitting BOFT can use multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity during training.

In short, OFT aims to preserve pretrained geometric structure via orthogonal multiplicative updates; BOFT refines this idea by finding a parameter-efficient factorization that yields dense orthogonal transformations with much lower parameter cost and an inductive bias aligned with fast mixing transforms. This combination yields a practical, principled PEFT method that balances expressivity and regularity and can be applied across modalities.
