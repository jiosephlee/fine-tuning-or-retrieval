\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 11: Empirical Results and Comparative Analysis

# Performance across ViTs, LLMs, and diffusion tasks

The paper reports an extensive empirical evaluation that applies BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) to three families of foundation models: large vision transformers (ViTs), large language models (LLMs), and text-to-image diffusion models. The goal of these experiments is to test whether the parameter-efficiency and inductive biases introduced by the butterfly orthogonal parameterization translate into consistent downstream improvements across modalities.

Across all three model families, BOFT is described as outperforming prior parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) baselines. The baselines specifically discussed in the paper include LoRA (low-rank additive updates) and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. The reported pattern is that BOFT attains better or comparable task accuracy while requiring fewer effective trainable parameters than full OFT and often matches or beats LoRA despite operating under the orthogonality constraint.

For ViTs, BOFT is applied to adapt pretrained vision transformers to downstream classification and image tasks. The claim is that BOFT yields favorable accuracy-vs-parameter trade-offs: it recovers much of the performance of full finetuning at a fraction of the trainable parameter cost, and it improves over OFT when the latter is constrained to parameter budgets comparable to BOFT’s.

For LLMs, BOFT is used to adapt large pretrained transformer-based language models to downstream NLP tasks. The reported result is that BOFT maintains model stability and generalization characteristics important for language fine-tuning, and achieves stronger parameter efficiency than OFT while remaining competitive with LoRA-type methods.

For diffusion models (text-to-image), BOFT inherits the strengths previously observed for orthogonal finetuning (preservation of pretrained structure and stable training) while reducing OFT’s parameter footprint. The paper highlights that BOFT performs well in controllable image generation adaptation problems where OFT had earlier shown advantages over low-rank approaches.

Across all modalities the unifying empirical observation is that BOFT’s structured orthogonal parameterization—via a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components—produces a compact hypothesis class that generalizes well across tasks, while allowing dense final orthogonal transforms when desired.

# Parameter efficiency vs. accuracy trade-offs

A central empirical theme is BOFT’s ability to trade off between parameter count and expressivity through two controllable hyperparameters: the number of butterfly layers (denoted $m$) and the block size $b$ inside butterfly components. Intuitively, $m$ controls how many sparse factors are multiplied to form the final orthogonal matrix, and $b$ controls the granularity of the smallest orthogonal blocks inside the factorization.

- Parameter scaling: BOFT with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ (the radix-2 butterfly) uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters for a $d$-dimensional layer. More generally, BOFT($m,b$) uses approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable scalar degrees of freedom for a $d\times n$ linear layer. This sits between the $\mathcal{O}(d)$ (very small block-diagonal OFT) and $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (full OFT) extremes and is typically much smaller than full finetuning.

- Accuracy behavior: Empirically, as BOFT increases $m$ or $b$ (thus increasing the number of effective parameters), model accuracy on downstream tasks generally improves, approaching the accuracy of full OFT or full finetuning. Importantly, BOFT typically achieves a target accuracy with far fewer trainable parameters than a naive block-diagonal OFT that matches the same parameter budget, demonstrating superior parameter-efficiency.

- Pareto frontier: The authors emphasize that BOFT moves the performance-versus-parameter Pareto frontier outwards compared to block-diagonal OFT. In other words, given a parameter budget, BOFT yields higher accuracy; conversely, given an accuracy target, BOFT requires fewer parameters. This improved frontier is attributed to the butterfly topology’s ability to create dense, expressive orthogonal transforms from many sparse factors while retaining structural regularization.

The practical consequence is that practitioners can tune $(m,b)$ to the desired accuracy vs. parameter trade-off in a predictable way: increase $m$ (more factor layers) or increase $b$ (larger block granularity) to gain expressivity when more parameters are acceptable; reduce them to push for extreme parameter efficiency.

# Stability and convergence characteristics compared to LoRA and OFT

Stability during finetuning and predictable convergence are recurring advantages associated with orthogonal finetuning approaches. BOFT preserves key spectral properties of the pretrained weights, and the paper highlights implications for training dynamics:

- Spectral preservation: Because BOFT multiplies the frozen pretrained weight matrix from the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ are preserved. Concretely, if $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the SVD of the pretrained weights, then the finetuned weights are $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which leaves $\bm{\Sigma}$ unchanged. Preserving $\bm{\Sigma}$ prevents uncontrolled growth or shrinkage of layer operator norms and thus promotes stable gradients.

- Improved training stability vs. LoRA: The paper notes that OFT already produced more stable training in previous work than additive low-rank updates like LoRA, and BOFT retains these stability benefits while being more parameter-efficient. In practical terms, this stability translates to less sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate and often more consistent convergence across random seeds.

- Convergence speed: BOFT introduces a small training-time overhead because the orthogonal matrix is represented as a product of multiple sparse factors that must be multiplied every iteration. Despite this extra computation, the paper reports that BOFT’s convergence (number of iterations to reach good validation performance) is similar to or better than block-OFT and competitive with LoRA in many experiments, owing to the improved inductive bias and regularization.

- Regularity vs. expressivity balance: The authors emphasize that better expressivity (e.g., full OFT or unconstrained finetuning) does not necessarily yield better generalization. BOFT’s controlled structure provides a balance that empirically leads to robust convergence and often superior generalization compared to both the highly constrained block-diagonal OFT and the unconstrained full orthogonal parameterization.

# Ablations: effect of m, b, identity init, multiplicative dropout

The paper reports several ablations that probe how BOFT’s components and hyperparameters influence empirical performance. While specific numerical values are not reproduced here, the qualitative conclusions are summarized below.

- Effect of $m$ (number of butterfly components): Increasing $m$—i.e., composing more sparse orthogonal blocks—improves expressivity and reduces approximation error to arbitrary dense orthogonal matrices. Empirically, going from small $m$ (few components) to larger $m$ typically raises downstream accuracy, up to diminishing returns. Very large $m$ tends toward the full-capacity orthogonal group (when combined with small $b$), but at the cost of more parameters and compute.

- Effect of $b$ (block size): Larger $b$ increases the per-component parameter budget because each basic orthogonal block grows. Increasing $b$ often yields similar qualitative effects as increasing $m$—more expressivity and higher accuracy—though the best way to allocate a fixed parameter budget between $m$ and $b depends on the task and layer size. The paper notes that configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (deep factorization) are generally more parameter-efficient than the opposite, because the butterfly topology leverages depth to mix coordinates effectively.

- Identity initialization: BOFT initializes every butterfly component to identity (achieved by initializing the underlying skew-symmetric matrices to zero in the Cayley transform). This mirrors zero-initialization in additive PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA) and is reported to be important for stability and to ensure that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model. The identity initialization serves both as a safety mechanism (no initial perturbation of pretrained behavior) and as a regularizer that biases optimization toward minimal deviation unless task demands otherwise.

- Multiplicative Dropout: Because BOFT’s update is multiplicative (left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix), conventional additive dropout schemes are not directly applicable. The paper introduces multiplicative Dropout: at each iteration, a random subset of butterfly components and random diagonal blocks inside them are temporarily replaced by identity blocks. This form of dropout reduces overfitting and acts as structural stochastic regularization. Ablations indicate that multiplicative Dropout reduces variance and can improve validation performance when finetuning budgets are small or datasets are limited.

Taken together, these ablations paint a picture in which BOFT’s hyperparameters offer predictable control over expressivity and regularization. Identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout are practical tools that improve robustness when operating in low-parameter regimes.

# Qualitative insights: weight interpolation behavior and learned transforms

Beyond scalar metrics, the paper emphasizes qualitative behaviors of BOFT that give insight into why it generalizes well.

- Weight interpolation: Because BOFT represents the finetuned weight as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is composed of orthogonal factors initialized at identity, there is a clean geometric interpretation of interpolation between the pretrained and finetuned models. Interpolating the parameters of BOFT (for example by scaling the skew-symmetric parameters inside the Cayley transforms) produces smooth, well-behaved trajectories in weight space that preserve norms and angles. This contrasts with additive low-rank updates where naive interpolation can change spectral properties abruptly. The paper suggests that such smooth interpolation reflects a more controlled adaptation of neural representations and may underlie improved transfer and stability.

- Learned transforms and connection to classical transforms: The butterfly parameterization can exactly represent many classic fast linear transforms (e.g., Fourier, Hadamard, discrete cosine–type transforms) via specific settings of its components. In practice, the learned orthogonal butterfly factors often resemble structured mixing operations that redistribute information across coordinates in ways that mirror these classical transforms. The paper argues that this affinity to known useful transforms provides an inductive bias well suited to many signal-processing and representation tasks encountered in vision and language models.

- Implicit structural priors: The sparse factorization introduces implicit priors that encourage structured information mixing across neuron coordinates, rather than completely unconstrained reweighting. The authors suggest that these priors favor solutions that reuse pretrained representations more effectively, which is consistent with BOFT’s favorable generalization performance when compared to both block-diagonal OFT (which is too localized) and unconstrained full finetuning (which can overfit).

- Visualization and interpretability: The paper indicates that inspecting the nonzero patterns and learned values inside butterfly components can be informative. For example, when BOFT is trained with small $m$ and larger $b$, learned transforms tend to focus on permuting and rotating subsets of coordinates; with larger $m$, the learned behavior is more global and mixing is deeper. Such qualitative observations align with the intended role of $(m,b)$ as knobs that tune locality vs. globality of the learned orthogonal transformation.

# Summary and practical recommendations

The empirical results and analyses in the paper lead to several practical conclusions for applying BOFT in real-world finetuning:

1. BOFT provides a favorable trade-off between parameter count and task performance across modalities (vision, language, and generative diffusion models). It often outperforms block-diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget and competes well with strong baselines like LoRA while preserving orthogonality benefits.

2. Tune $(m,b)$ to match your constraints: prefer smaller $b$ with larger $m$ when you want better parameter efficiency (more expressive mixing for a given parameter budget); increase $b$ or $m$ when you can afford more parameters and need higher accuracy.

3. Use identity initialization for safety and stability. Start finetuning from an exact copy of the pretrained model by initializing all butterfly components to identity via the Cayley transform.

4. Use multiplicative Dropout when overfitting is a concern, particularly in low-data or low-parameter-budget regimes. Randomly masking butterfly components to identity during training stabilizes performance.

5. Expect modest additional training-time cost due to repeated sparse matrix multiplications, but no added inference latency: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal factors can be composed with the pretrained weights into a single weight matrix for efficient inference.

Overall, BOFT demonstrates that carefully structured orthogonal parameterizations—motivated by information transmission and the butterfly topology—can yield practical, interpretable, and broadly applicable PEFT methods that balance expressivity, stability, and parameter efficiency.
