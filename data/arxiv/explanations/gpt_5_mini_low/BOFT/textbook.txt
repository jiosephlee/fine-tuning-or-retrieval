\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

From Full Finetuning to Parameter-Efficient Adaptation

# Scaling of foundation models and the cost of full finetuning

Over the last few years, foundation models—large pretrained models trained on massive uncurated datasets—have grown dramatically in both capability and size. Representative examples include GPT-3 in language and Stable Diffusion in vision: these models contain tens to hundreds of billions of parameters and exhibit strong generalization across many downstream tasks. This scale delivers impressive performance, but it also creates a practical barrier: training such models from scratch or even repeatedly fine-tuning all parameters for each downstream task is prohibitively expensive in compute, memory, and human time.

Full finetuning a foundation model means updating (and storing) the entire parameter set for each task. When models have on the order of $10^{10}$–$10^{11}$ parameters, that approach multiplies costs across tasks and users. Beyond raw monetary and energy costs, full finetuning is operationally cumbersome: cloning large checkpoints, performing lengthy optimization runs, and maintaining many task-specific parameter copies are all undesirable. Moreover, unrestricted changes to the full parameter set can destroy useful pretrained structure and sometimes harm generalization on small target datasets.

These limitations motivate an alternative paradigm: instead of modifying all parameters, adapt models in a parameter-efficient way that preserves most pretrained weights while allowing the model to specialize. Reducing the number of trainable parameters lowers storage and computational demands for adaptation, enables rapid experimentation, and often reduces the risk of overfitting by constraining how much the model can change.

# Three PEFT families: model finetuning, adapter tuning, prompt/prefix tuning

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods broadly fall into three families, each embodying a different strategy for where and how to introduce task-specific flexibility while keeping most of the pretrained model fixed.

The first family keeps the original architecture unchanged and selectively updates a subset of model parameters. This includes straightforward strategies such as tuning only biases, layer norms, or a small subset of weights, and using small learning rates so the model remains close to its pretrained state. The attraction of this family is that it avoids inference-time overhead (no extra modules) and remains simple to implement and reason about.

The second family augments the network with small trainable modules—so-called adapters—and leaves the pretrained weights fixed. Adapter tuning inserts lightweight learnable layers (for example, small MLPs) at various points in the network; only these adapter parameters are optimized for a downstream task. This approach is flexible: adapters can be placed where they are most effective, and their isolation from the pretrained weights makes it easy to compose multiple adapters or share them across tasks.

The third family is prompt or prefix tuning, where task adaptation is achieved by adding trainable tokens or prefixes to the model input. These extra tokens act like a learned prompt that steers the frozen model to produce task-specific behavior. Prompt tuning and related methods have the advantage of extreme parameter efficiency: only a small set of virtual tokens are learned. However, they are limited to architectures and tasks that can accept and exploit input prefixes effectively.

Each family has trade-offs. Adapter and prompt methods introduce extra inference components or rely on model interfaces, while updating a subset of weights retains the original architecture but can be limited in expressive power if the subset is too small. The remainder of this chapter focuses on a principled variant of the first family—reparameterization-based model finetuning—that explicitly constrains the form of weight updates to preserve desirable properties of the pretrained model.

# Reparameterization-based PEFT: LoRA and beyond

Reparameterization-based PEFT methods change how weights are updated by expressing the finetuned weight matrix as a function of the frozen pretrained weights plus a compact, learnable transformation. The key idea is to restrict the parameterization of the update so that it uses far fewer trainable parameters while still providing sufficient adaptation capacity.

A canonical example is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). LoRA replaces the additive full-rank update to a pretrained weight matrix with an additive low-rank decomposition: the finetuned weight is

z = (W^0 + \Delta W)^\top x, with \Delta W = A B,

where A and B are small matrices of rank r. By choosing r small, LoRA dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to full finetuning. It has proven effective for many language tasks and gained popularity because of its simplicity and low inference overhead: the low-rank updates can be merged into the pretrained weights after training.

Reparameterization approaches are attractive because they can be designed to reflect prior beliefs about which transformations are plausible or safe for a pretrained model. Low-rank updates assume that the task-specific changes lie in a low-dimensional subspace, which is often a reasonable approximation. However, low-rank structure is only one of several possible structured parameterizations. Other approaches explore diagonal, block-diagonal, or more exotic structured parameterizations to trade expressivity against parameter efficiency and regularity.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which we will discuss next, is another reparameterization approach—but it takes a multiplicative rather than additive view of adaptation and enforces orthogonality constraints on the learned transformation. This yields different theoretical and practical properties from additive low-rank updates.

# Motivation for orthogonal finetuning: preserving pretrained knowledge

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is motivated by a structural perspective on neural weights. Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix W^0. The columns of W^0 (the neuron weight vectors) encode relational information, including pairwise angles between neurons, which are meaningful because they reflect how different neurons partition or relate input features. Large-scale pretraining tends to arrange neurons and representations in structured geometric ways that capture useful semantic knowledge.

If finetuning arbitrarily changes the weight matrix, those relational structures can be distorted, potentially discarding useful pretrained structure and harming generalization. OFT addresses this concern by restricting the finetuning transformation to be an orthogonal matrix R applied multiplicatively to the rows (or equivalently, to the neuron vectors): the finetuned weights become R W^0. Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and lengths, applying R preserves pairwise angles between neuron vectors. Thus, OFT attempts to modify the model while maintaining the geometric relationships learned during pretraining, ensuring that pretrained knowledge is not destroyed.

Concretely, OFT parameterizes a learnable orthogonal matrix R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} and transforms the forward pass from z = (W^0)^\top x to z = (R W^0)^\top x, initializing R as the identity so the finetuning starts from the pretrained model. To maintain orthogonality during training, OFT uses parameterizations such as the Cayley transform, which maps skew-symmetric matrices Q (with Q = -Q^\top) to orthogonal matrices via R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}. The orthogonality constraint gives OFT desirable spectral properties: left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values of W^0, which helps retain stability and can aid generalization.

Despite these advantages, OFT in its unrestricted form requires learning a full d \times d orthogonal matrix, which consumes \mathcal{O}(d^2) parameters—too many to be parameter-efficient when d is large. OFT therefore originally introduced block-diagonal structure to reduce parameter count, but that introduces its own limitations, as we will summarize next.

# High-level overview of OFT vs. BOFT and their goals

OFT and BOFT share the core conceptual goal: enable stable, generalizable finetuning by transforming pretrained weights via an orthogonal matrix that preserves relational structure among neurons. They differ primarily in how that orthogonal matrix is parameterized to achieve parameter efficiency.

OFT in practice uses two main parameter regimes. The unconstrained OFT learns a full orthogonal R of size d \times d, preserving all expressivity at the cost of \mathcal{O}(d^2) parameters. To be more practical, OFT often restricts R to be block-diagonal, R = \text{diag}(R_1,\ldots,R_r), where each block R_i is of size b \times b and b r = d. This block-diagonal OFT reduces trainable parameters to \mathcal{O}(b d), which can be manageable. However, the block-diagonal assumption enforces that different contiguous groups of dimensions do not interact under the learned orthogonal transform. This hard sparsity pattern can limit expressivity: block-diagonal transforms cannot approximate many dense linear mappings or recover classic transforms like the discrete Fourier transform, and the particular grouping by contiguous indices has no principled justification.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) addresses this tension—achieving dense transform expressivity while keeping parameter counts low—by factorizing the dense orthogonal matrix R as a product of multiple sparse, structured orthogonal matrices. Instead of learning R directly, BOFT represents

R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1,

where each B_i is a sparse orthogonal factor structured according to a butterfly pattern inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. The butterfly structure is a layered sparse connectivity pattern that efficiently routes information between all input and output coordinates in O(d \log d) edges when d is a power of two. When each sparse factor is itself orthogonal, their product is orthogonal, so BOFT enforces orthogonality while dramatically reducing the number of free parameters.

There are several virtues to this approach. First, BOFT can produce dense orthogonal matrices (the product of sparse factors yields dense connectivity), avoiding the expressivity limitations of block-diagonal OFT. Second, the butterfly factorization reduces parameter complexity from \mathcal{O}(d^2) to \mathcal{O}(d \log d) when using radix-2 butterfly factors with block size b = 2 and m \approx \log d layers. Third, the butterfly structure is not an arbitrary sparsity pattern: it aligns with fast linear transform topologies (e.g., FFT, Hadamard) known to mix local and global information efficiently, which supplies an inductive bias that can help generalization.

BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases: if m = 1 and block size b varies, BOFT(m,b) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size b; if m = 1 and b = d, BOFT reduces to unconstrained OFT. At the other extreme, BOFT with m \approx \log d and small b yields a dense R with far fewer trainable parameters than the full OFT. Practically, BOFT initializes its butterfly factors to identity (so finetuning starts from the pretrained model) and uses an orthogonal parameterization (e.g., via Cayley transforms on small blocks) to preserve orthogonality during optimization. To mitigate overfitting BOFT can use multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity during training.

In short, OFT aims to preserve pretrained geometric structure via orthogonal multiplicative updates; BOFT refines this idea by finding a parameter-efficient factorization that yields dense orthogonal transformations with much lower parameter cost and an inductive bias aligned with fast mixing transforms. This combination yields a practical, principled PEFT method that balances expressivity and regularity and can be applied across modalities.

Mathematical Preliminaries: Orthogonality and Reparameterization

# Orthogonal matrices: definitions, properties, and geometric intuition

An orthogonal matrix is a square matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that satisfies $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}_d$, where $\bm{I}_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix. Equivalently, the columns (and rows) of an orthogonal matrix form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$. This algebraic definition immediately implies several useful algebraic properties: an orthogonal matrix is invertible with $\bm{R}^{-1}=\bm{R}^\top$, and its determinant is either $+1$ or $-1$.

Geometrically, an orthogonal matrix represents a rigid transformation of Euclidean space: it performs rotations and possibly reflections, but does not change lengths or angles. If you view $\bm{R}$ as acting on vectors $v\in\mathbb{R}^d$, then $\bm{R}v$ is the same vector after a rotation/reflection. This viewpoint is central when orthogonal matrices are used as model updates: multiplying a pretrained weight matrix from the left by an orthogonal matrix corresponds to applying such a rigid transformation to every neuron (column) of the weight matrix.

When we parameterize model updates with orthogonal matrices, two important practical consequences follow. First, the set of orthogonal matrices forms a compact manifold (the orthogonal group), which imposes structure and regularity on the allowed updates. Second, because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise relations among vectors (see next section), they provide a principled way to alter representations while retaining much of the pretrained geometry.

# Angle and norm preservation; impact on neuron similarity

A core geometric property of orthogonal matrices is that they preserve inner products, and hence both norms and angles. For any vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and any orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$,
\[
(\bm{R}u)^\top(\bm{R}v) \;=\; u^\top v.
\]
Setting $u=v$ shows norm preservation: $\|\bm{R}u\|_2 = \|u\|_2$ for all $u$. Dividing the inner product by vector norms gives cosine similarity, so angles between vectors are preserved as well. Concretely, if $\theta$ is the angle between $u$ and $v$, then the angle between $\bm{R}u$ and $\bm{R}v$ is the same $\theta$.

In the context of a linear layer with pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns are neuron vectors $\{\bm{w}^0_i\}_{i=1}^n$, left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ transforms every neuron to $\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$. Because orthogonal maps preserve inner products, the pairwise angles and relative lengths among neurons remain unchanged:
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j) = (\bm{w}^0_i)^\top(\bm{w}^0_j),\qquad\forall i,j.
\]
This preservation provides a principled way to finetune: the pretrained relational geometry among neurons—which encodes semantic information learned during pretraining—is kept intact. Thus, orthogonal finetuning aims to adapt the model for downstream tasks while maintaining the intrinsic similarity structure of neurons, which is argued to benefit generalization and training stability.

# Cayley transform: mapping skew-symmetric to orthogonal matrices

Enforcing orthogonality of a matrix during optimization is not trivial if one directly optimizes the entries. A practical reparameterization used for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in the paper is the Cayley transform. The Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices (except those orthogonal matrices with eigenvalue $-1$). Concretely, for a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, the Cayley transform defines
\[
\bm{R} \;=\; (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
By construction, when $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric and $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible, the resulting $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}_d$. Thus, instead of optimizing $\bm{R}$ directly with an orthogonality constraint, we can optimize the entries of a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ without constraints and obtain an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley map.

This reparameterization is practical because the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ has fewer degrees of freedom structured by the skew constraint ($\bm{Q}_{ij} = -\bm{Q}_{ji}$), and the Cayley transform yields an orthogonal matrix that can be initialized and updated smoothly during optimization. It is the specific parameterization adopted in OFT and BOFT to ensure orthogonality throughout the finetuning process.

# Skew-symmetric parameterization and identity initialization

The skew-symmetric parameterization central to the Cayley transform requires us to represent $\bm{Q}$ such that $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. In practice, one can parametrize only the strictly upper-triangular entries of $\bm{Q}$ and set the lower-triangular entries to their negatives, thereby ensuring skew-symmetry automatically. Optimizing these unconstrained parameters then yields a valid $\bm{Q}$ during training.

A common and useful initialization strategy—used in OFT and adopted in BOFT—is to set $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start of finetuning. The Cayley transform of $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ gives $\bm{R}=\bm{I}_d$, which means the finetuned model initially exactly matches the pretrained model. This "identity initialization" realizes zero initial deviation from the pretrained weights and mirrors the initialization strategies of other parameter-efficient finetuning methods (for example, LoRA uses zero initialization for the additive low-rank update). Starting from the identity promotes stability and ensures the adaptation process begins from the pretrained solution, moving only as the skew parameters change.

When BOFT uses butterfly-structured components, the same principle applies at the component/block level: each sparse orthogonal factor (the block or $2\times 2$ rotation blocks used in the butterfly factorization) is initialized to the identity (implemented by initializing its skew-symmetric generator to zero), so the overall product is the identity at the start of finetuning.

# Spectral norm preservation under left-orthogonal multiplication

An important algebraic consequence of left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix is preservation of singular values of the multiplied matrix, and in particular preservation of the spectral norm. Let the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ have singular value decomposition
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top,
\]
where $\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal (or appropriate-sized orthogonal embeddings) and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is the rectangular diagonal of singular values. If we left-multiply $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, the finetuned weight matrix becomes
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Since $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is orthogonal (product of two orthogonal matrices), the singular values of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ are exactly those of $\bm{W}^0$. In particular, the largest singular value, i.e. the spectral norm $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$, is preserved:
\[
\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2 = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2.
\]

Preserving the spectral norm has practical implications for training stability and generalization. Because the magnitude of linear operator norms relates to gradient magnitudes and Lipschitz properties of layers, maintaining the pretrained spectral properties avoids introducing sudden changes in scale that could destabilize optimization. This preservation is one of the theoretical motivations for preferring multiplicative orthogonal updates (OFT/BOFT) over unconstrained additive updates: orthogonal updates change directions and relative geometry but keep the global spectral scale unchanged.

Taken together, these mathematical preliminaries—definitions and geometry of orthogonal matrices, inner-product and norm preservation, Cayley reparameterization via skew-symmetric matrices with identity initialization, and spectral norm invariance under left-orthogonal multiplication—form the foundation for orthogonal finetuning approaches such as OFT and its parameter-efficient variant BOFT. They explain why orthogonal updates are attractive: they allow principled adaptation that preserves pretrained relational and spectral structure while constraining the hypothesis space through structured parameterizations.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Formulation, Strengths, and Limits

# OFT forward pass: W -> R W^0 and multiplicative update vs. LoRA’s additive low-rank

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) reparameterizes a pretrained linear layer by applying a learned orthogonal transformation on the neuron side, i.e., the finetuned weight matrix is obtained multiplicatively from the pretrained weights. Concretely, given a frozen pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and an input vector $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the original forward computation is $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. OFT replaces this by $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix. Because $\bm{R}$ multiplies on the left of $\bm{W}^0$, every neuron (column of $\bm{W}^0$) is transformed by the same linear isometry before producing outputs.

This multiplicative reparameterization contrasts with popular additive reparameterization approaches like LoRA. LoRA updates the pretrained weight by adding a low-rank correction: $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$ with $\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ and $r\ll d,n$. LoRA therefore modifies weights by additive low-rank perturbations, while OFT applies a global multiplicative orthogonal transform shared across neurons. The two approaches impose different structural constraints on the class of permissible finetuned models: additive low-rank changes allow rank-limited deviations from $\bm{W}^0$, whereas multiplicative orthogonal transforms preserve norms and relative geometry of neurons while permitting rotations and reflections in feature space.

Because OFT multiplies $\bm{W}^0$ by a single shared orthogonal matrix, it introduces no inference latency compared to the pretrained model: at inference time one can merge $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ into a single weight matrix. During training, the multiplicative nature means updates act globally across all neurons rather than being localized to a few low-rank directions. This global effect is central to OFT’s motivations and consequences covered in the next sections.

# Angle preservation and links to hyperspherical energy

A central motivation for OFT is to preserve relational structure among neurons learned during pretraining. Neurons in a layer are column vectors of $\bm{W}^0$; their pairwise geometry—angles between neuron vectors—encodes relational information that pretraining has organized. An orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ exactly preserves pairwise inner products and hence pairwise angles. If two neurons have columns $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$, then after transformation they become $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$, and inner products satisfy
$$
(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0) = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top\bm{w}_j^0,
$$
because $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Thus OFT guarantees exact preservation of the pairwise angular relationships among neurons irrespective of how $\bm{R}$ is trained (so long as orthogonality is maintained). This contrasts with additive updates which generally change angles.

The emphasis on preserving angular relationships connects to prior work that ties hyperspherical configuration of neurons to generalization. Measures like hyperspherical energy quantify how neuron directions are distributed on the unit sphere; well-spread configurations (low hyperspherical energy) are associated with desirable generalization properties. By constraining finetuning to orthogonal transformations, OFT keeps neuron directions on the same hypersphere arrangement up to a global isometry, maintaining the relational structure that may encode valuable pretraining inductive biases. In practice, this angle-preserving constraint is argued to improve stability and generalization during finetuning, particularly for large generative models, as it prevents arbitrary distortions of the pretrained geometric arrangement of features.

# Ensuring orthogonality during training via Cayley

Maintaining orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout optimization is essential for OFT to preserve angles. A common and practical parameterization used in OFT is the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix (i.e., $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$). The Cayley parameterization maps $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ via
$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
$$
This formula yields an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ whenever $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible (which holds for sufficiently small $\bm{Q}$, and in practice invertibility is managed numerically). Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric it has only $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ independent entries, and optimizing these entries implicitly enforces $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ without requiring constrained optimization or projection steps at every update.

The Cayley parameterization affords a natural identity initialization for OFT. Setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly. During optimization, updates to the skew-symmetric parameters smoothly move along the orthogonal manifold. This preserves desirable spectral properties discussed later and provides stability because the parameterization never leaves the orthogonal group (except transient numerical issues), unlike unconstrained optimization where one would have to add orthogonality regularization or employ projected gradient steps.

# Block-diagonal OFT: parameter count and sparsity pattern

Although a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (roughly $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom), finetuning with that many additional parameters is often impractical. To reduce parameter cost, the original OFT paper introduces a block-diagonal parameterization. Partition the $d$ neuron dimensions into $r$ contiguous groups of size $b$ each so that $br=d$. The orthogonal matrix is constrained to be
$$
\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),
$$
where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is an orthogonal matrix parameterized (for example) via Cayley transforms on skew-symmetric blocks. The number of trainable parameters in this block-diagonal OFT equals the sum of parameters in the blocks; asymptotically this is $\mathcal{O}(bd)=\mathcal{O}(d\cdot b)$, and more precisely each $b\times b$ orthogonal block contributes $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, so total degrees $\frac{r b(b-1)}{2}=\frac{d(b-1)}{2}$.

This sparsity pattern implies that the orthogonal transform is applied independently within each block: coordinates belonging to different blocks do not mix. Computationally this reduces parameter count dramatically when $b\ll d$. Initialization can again be identity by initializing each block as $\bm{I}_b$ (via zero skew-symmetric blocks), ensuring no initial deviation from pretrained weights.

# Inductive biases of block-diagonal structure: reduced expressivity, fixed sparsity, inability to approximate classic transforms

Constraining $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal introduces a strong structural inductive bias. The most immediate consequence is reduced expressivity: a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot rotate information between different blocks, so any finetuned feature that requires coordinated changes across blocks is impossible. This limits the set of representable orthogonal transformations to those that permute and rotate within the pre-specified index groups only.

Another aspect of this inductive bias is that the sparsity pattern is fixed and determined by the choice of partitioning. There is no mechanism within block-diagonal OFT to adapt which coordinates should interact; the grouping is based purely on fixed index ranges, which is generally arbitrary relative to semantic structure learned in pretraining. Because the sparsity pattern cannot change during finetuning, block-diagonal OFT may miss transformations that mix coordinates across groups even when those are crucial for a downstream task.

A concrete limitation is that block-diagonal OFT cannot represent many classic dense linear transforms that are useful in signal processing and neural architectures—transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform, Hadamard transform, or other global permutations and mixes. Those transforms require dense cross-coordinate interactions; a block-diagonal matrix with small blocks cannot approximate such global transforms regardless of block rotations. As a result, block-diagonal OFT may be unable to reach functionally useful neighborhoods in parameter space that full orthogonal transforms can access.

# Trade-offs: generalization gains vs. parameter count and expressivity constraints

OFT embodies a trade-off between preserving pretraining structure (which tends to improve generalization and training stability) and adding sufficient expressivity to adapt to downstream tasks. On one side, the orthogonality constraint preserves pairwise neuron angles and spectral norms, properties linked to stable optimization and better generalization, as it prevents explosive changes to singular values of $\bm{W}^0$. On the other side, a full orthogonal parameterization introduces many parameters ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$) which is often infeasible; the block-diagonal restriction reduces parameter count to a manageable level but at the cost of expressivity and flexibility.

Empirically and conceptually, block-diagonal OFT can outperform additive low-rank methods like LoRA in some settings because OFT maintains relational structure across neurons within each block and preserves spectral properties globally. However, because block-diagonal OFT enforces a rigid sparsity pattern and prohibits cross-block mixing, it may underperform when tasks require more global transformations. Thus the practitioner must choose block sizes $b$ that balance parameter budget and the amount of cross-coordinate mixing likely needed: larger $b$ increases expressivity (closer to full orthogonal) but also increases parameter count, while smaller $b$ reduces parameters but may harm adaptation capability.

The multiplicative nature of OFT also has ramifications: because orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves norms and angles, OFT cannot change the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$, only the left singular vectors. This spectral preservation helps training stability but restricts the class of reachable finetuned weight matrices compared to additive methods that can change singular values. Whether that restriction helps or hurts depends on the downstream task: maintaining pretraining spectral structure can prevent overfitting and keep generalization, but may also prevent the model from acquiring necessary representational changes that require altering singular values.

In summary, OFT provides a principled finetuning framework that preserves neuron geometry through orthogonal multiplicative updates and can be made parameter-efficient via block-diagonal structure. Its strengths lie in angle preservation, spectral stability, and potentially improved generalization. Its limits derive from the parameter cost of full orthogonal matrices and the reduced expressivity and fixed sparsity of block-diagonal parameterizations. Choosing how to trade off these factors is central to applying OFT in practice and motivates the development of more expressive yet parameter-efficient orthogonal factorizations such as the butterfly-based approaches introduced elsewhere in the paper.

An Information Transmission View of Matrix Factorization

# Dense matrix entries as end-to-end connectivity

A dense square matrix can be interpreted as specifying, for each output coordinate, which input coordinates can influence it and by how much. Concretely, consider a matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. The element $R_{ij}$ is nonzero precisely when information from the $j$‑th input node can reach the $i$‑th output node through the linear map induced by $\mathbf{R}$. Viewing a matrix this way turns a purely algebraic object into a connectivity pattern: nonzero entries are edges in a directed bipartite graph from $d$ source nodes (inputs) to $d$ sink nodes (outputs). The numerical values of the nonzero entries determine weights on those edges, while the sparsity pattern indicates which direct input→output transmissions are possible.

This interpretation emphasizes an end-to-end perspective: what matters for many downstream uses (including finetuning pretrained layers) is whether each input coordinate can ultimately influence each output coordinate, not necessarily the particular intermediate structure that realizes that connectivity. A single dense matrix provides direct connectivity for all $d^2$ possible input→output pairs. When we speak of generating a dense $\mathbf{R}$ via some factorization, we therefore mean producing a composition of intermediate maps whose overall connectivity graph from inputs to outputs has no missing edges. Framing dense matrix entries in this way makes it natural to reason about sparsity, expressivity, and parameter cost in terms of communications (paths) on a graph instead of raw matrix entries.

# Unrolling products of sparse matrices into layered graphs

When a dense matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is represented as a product of $m$ square matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
\]
each factor $\mathbf{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ can be viewed as connectivity between two adjacent layers of $d$ nodes. Unrolling the product into a layered graph produces $m+1$ layers of $d$ nodes each: layer~0 (the original inputs), layer~1 after $\mathbf{B}_1$, layer~2 after $\mathbf{B}_2$, and so on, up to layer $m$ (the final outputs). An entry $(\mathbf{B}_i)_{uv}$ indicates whether node $v$ in layer $i-1$ connects to node $u$ in layer $i$ (and with what weight).

Under this unrolled graphical view, an end-to-end entry $R_{ij}$ is nonzero precisely when there exists at least one directed path from input node $j$ in layer~0 to output node $i$ in layer~m that traverses edges present in the successive factors $\mathbf{B}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{B}_m$. Thus the composition of sparse adjacency patterns across layers can create dense overall connectivity even though each factor is sparse. This is the key mechanism by which factorization trades off between the number of trainable parameters (edges) and computation: repeated, structured exchanges across the layers can route every input to every output using far fewer total edges than a single dense bipartite graph would require.

A simple illustrative example: take $d=4$ and $m=5$ factors. Each factor corresponds to a bipartite connection between adjacent levels of 4 nodes. If, after composing the five sparse connectivity patterns, every node in layer~0 reaches every node in layer~5, then the product $\mathbf{B}_5\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$ is dense. Crucially, the edges that are present in individual $\mathbf{B}_i$ need not form dense matrices themselves; they only need to enable routing of information across the whole depth. The unrolled layered graph makes it straightforward to check which end‑to‑end entries are reachable and where the sparsity in intermediate factors produces zeros in the composed matrix.

# Desiderata: dense end-to-end connectivity with minimal trainable edges

From the information transmission perspective there are two competing desiderata when we seek parameter‑efficient factorizations of dense matrices. First, we require dense end-to-end connectivity: for the composed matrix to be dense, every source node in layer~0 must have at least one path to every sink node in layer~m. Without this property the composed matrix will have zero entries and will not be fully expressive as a dense transform. Second, we want to minimize the number of free (trainable) edges across the entire layered graph. The fewer the trainable edges, the fewer parameters must be optimized during finetuning; this is the "space" we trade for extra computation.

These desiderata must be balanced. A naive approach that connects every pair of nodes densely in each factor would satisfy the first desideratum trivially but violates the second by using $\mathcal{O}(d^2 m)$ edges. A better approach uses sparse $\mathbf{B}_i$, arranged so that the concatenation of sparse patterns still gives dense paths from every input to every output. Butterfly‑style factorizations (inspired by FFT networks) are canonical examples: they achieve full end‑to‑end connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges (for $m=\mathcal{O}(\log d)$), substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters compared to a single dense matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ entries.

When assessing candidate factorizations in this framework, counting edges in the layered graph gives an intuitive proxy for parameter cost. However, because some entries of orthogonal factors are constrained (see below), the effective count of free parameters is slightly different from the raw edge count; we must account for structural constraints that remove degrees of freedom.

# Orthogonality constraints as structured edge dependencies and bijections

Orthogonality imposes additional, nonlocal structure on the entries of each factor. Two principal consequences matter for the layered graph model.

First, each orthogonal factor must be full rank and invertible, which implies that between any two adjacent layers the factor must implement a bijection between the $d$ nodes (as a linear map). At minimum, therefore, there must exist $d$ independent connections that map the $d$ inputs at layer $i-1$ to the $d$ outputs at layer $i$. In graph terms this means there are at least $d$ disjoint edges (counting multiplicity with weights) needed so the factor achieves full column rank. In practice, for structured sparse orthogonal blocks (for example, made from $2\times 2$ rotations), each factor will have exactly $d$ rows and $d$ columns active in specific patterned ways that enforce invertibility. The paper highlights that these necessary $d$ edges are not free trainable degrees in the same way as arbitrary real entries: in many sparse orthogonal patterns the allowed nonzero entries take constrained forms (for example, $\pm 1$ in permutation-like blocks, or belong to low-dimensional parameterizations like Cayley transforms for $2\times 2$ rotations). Thus the count of trainable edges excludes these structural, non‑parametric connections.

Second, orthogonality induces dependencies among entries: a zero in a particular location often forces zeros or specific values elsewhere to maintain orthogonality. A simple example is a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix. If one entry is zero in a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix, orthogonality dictates another entry must be zero or fixed, eliminating certain independent parameters. In the layered graph, this means that removing one edge from a factor can create constraints on other edges; modeling factors purely as independent binary edges misses this coupling. Overall, orthogonality reduces the effective degrees of freedom relative to naive counts of nonzero locations, and the specific sparse patterns used (e.g., block rotations, butterfly structures built from $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks) encode which degrees of freedom remain trainable.

Because of these points, in designing parametric factorizations for orthogonal finetuning we should separate (a) the minimal connectivity needed for invertibility (the bijection-style edges that are structural) from (b) the remaining degrees of freedom that are actually learned. Good factorizations aim to minimize the number of learned degrees while respecting the structural orthogonality constraints that maintain invertibility and desirable spectral properties.

# Why naive dense matrices cost O(d^2) and how factorization trades time for space

A direct parameterization of a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (or any dense matrix) uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ independent scalar parameters (for orthogonal matrices, the dimension of the orthogonal group is $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$). This quadratic parameter cost becomes prohibitive for large $d$ when one wishes to finetune many layers in a foundation model. The core idea of matrix factorization is to represent the same end‑to‑end mapping as a composition of several simpler, structured maps so that the total number of free parameters across factors is much smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. The tradeoff is computation: evaluating and backpropagating through $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$ requires $m$ sparse matrix multiplications (and in training these are performed repeatedly), so we exchange extra per‑step computation for a much smaller parameter footprint.

From the unrolled graph view, rather than building a dense bipartite graph with $d^2$ edges, we build a layered sparse network where each edge participates in many end‑to‑end paths. Carefully designed topologies can realize all $d^2$ input→output connectivities using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ or $\mathcal{O}(d m)$ edges. The butterfly architecture from the Cooley‑Tukey FFT is a canonical example: with $m=\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages and $\mathcal{O}(d)$ connections per stage, the entire layered network uses only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ connections yet achieves dense end‑to‑end routing. Because each stage's nonzero pattern can be parameterized with a small number of learnable parameters per active block (for instance, using $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotations with just one scalar angle per block), the total number of trainable parameters is likewise $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$.

This is the practical meaning of trading time for space: we pay a multiplicative increase in per‑update computation (multiply through $m$ factors each forward and backward pass) to dramatically reduce the number of trainable scalars stored and optimized. For finetuning large pretrained models, where storing and communicating parameter updates is expensive while additional computation during training is often tolerable, this tradeoff is very attractive. The paper demonstrates that, by designing sparse orthogonal factors (e.g., blockwise $2\times 2$ rotations arranged in butterfly stages), one can retain desirable spectral properties (orthogonality preserves singular values) and expressivity sufficient for downstream tasks, but with parameter complexity reduced from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ or, more generally, $\mathcal{O}(dm)$ for $m$ stages.

In summary, the information transmission view reframes dense matrix factorization as a routing problem on layered graphs. This perspective clarifies the goals (dense end‑to‑end connectivity) and constraints (orthogonality forces bijections and structured dependencies) when designing sparse factor patterns, and it explains how factorization enables a controlled time‑for‑space tradeoff: more stages and repeated sparse exchanges produce dense effective transforms using far fewer trainable parameters than a naive dense representation.

Butterfly Structures: From FFT to Efficient Information Mixing

# Cooley–Tukey FFT recursion and the butterfly pattern

The Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) provides a classic example of how a global dense linear transform can be computed efficiently by composing a sequence of sparse, structured operations. At its heart is a divide-and-conquer recursion: an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform is reduced to smaller transforms on halves of the data, with a small interleaving/scrambling step between them. When this recursion is unrolled, the pattern of nonzero interactions between input indices and intermediate indices forms the characteristic “butterfly” pattern.

Viewed abstractly, the FFT recursion shows how to exchange information between all input positions and all output positions without ever forming an $N\times N$ dense matrix explicitly. Instead one performs $O(N\log N)$ scalar operations arranged into $O(\log N)$ stages; each stage only mixes nearby pairs of values. Each local mixing is a small structured operation (in radix‑2, a 2×2 operator), and the sequence of these local mixings routes information so that, after the last stage, every output has received contributions from every input. This is the key property: dense connectivity between source and target coordinates emerges from a small number of sparse stages.

In the language used in the paper, the FFT demonstrates an information transmission solution on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid (take $d=N$ and $m=\log_2 N$): a chain of sparse factor matrices whose product yields a dense transform. The Cooley–Tukey recursion therefore motivates searching for sparse factorization topologies that achieve dense end-to-end connectivity while using far fewer free parameters than a single dense matrix.

# Butterfly graphs as efficient broadcast/multicast networks

The unrolled stages of an FFT define a layered graph: nodes are positions at each stage, and edges connect positions that interact in a single sparse factor. That layered graph is precisely what the paper calls an information transmission graph. The butterfly graph is a particular layered topology that achieves the desiderata of dense end-to-end connectivity with very few inter-stage edges. Concretely, for radix‑2 and $d=2^N$ inputs, the butterfly network has $N=\log_2 d$ stages and each stage connects each node to only a constant number of nodes in the next stage (for radix‑2, typically two). Over all stages the total number of inter-stage edges is $O(d\log d)$, yet from any source node there exists a path to every sink node. Thus the butterfly graph achieves full broadcast/multicast capability across the $d$ inputs with only $O(d\log d)$ links.

From the perspective of parameter-efficiency in parameterizing linear maps, this graph implies that a dense $d\times d$ mapping can be implemented as a product of $m=O(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $O(d)$ nonzero blocks. If those stage matrices are restricted to be orthogonal, then the network realizes a dense orthogonal transform using only $O(d\log d)$ degrees of freedom rather than $O(d^2)$. The paper emphasizes this as a way to meet two transmission desiderata: (i) dense connectivity (every source can reach every sink), and (ii) minimum free edges (use as few trainable locations as possible subject to orthogonality constraints). The butterfly topology is a natural network that satisfies both.

# Butterfly matrices: factors, components, and recursive construction

A butterfly factor in the paper is the elemental block that mixes two neighboring groups of coordinates. For a block size $k$ that is a power of two, a single butterfly factor $\mathbf{B}^F(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$ is written as a 2×2 block matrix whose blocks are diagonal matrices:
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each $\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ parameterizes how corresponding coordinates mix. This representation captures the local pairwise interactions (or blockwise pairwise interactions) used at each stage.

Building up to dimension $d=2^N$, the paper defines butterfly components $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)$ as block-diagonal matrices composed of many copies of $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ arranged to cover all coordinates. The full $d\times d$ butterfly matrix is then the product of these components across multiple scales:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,\cdots\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
\]
Each component operates at a different granularity: the component with block size $2$ performs the finest-grained pairwise mixes, while the components with larger $k$ permute and mix larger groups. Importantly, the nonzero pattern of every component is a permutation of that of the smallest component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$, so one can parameterize orthogonality for each component by ensuring each small $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$ in the block case) block is orthogonal.

Because each component is block-sparse and the number of components is $m=O(\log d)$ for the full butterfly, the product gives an expressive dense matrix while exposing only $O(d\log d)$ trainable elements when the internal blocks are parameterized compactly (for example by Cayley transforms of skew-symmetric parameters). This yields the efficient orthogonal parameterization exploited in BOFT.

# Block butterfly generalization and relation to fast transforms (DFT, DCT, Hadamard)

The paper generalizes the pure scalar radix‑2 butterfly to block butterflies: each scalar entry of the elemental $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix. Formally, a block butterfly component is denoted $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each diagonal vector $\mathbf{d}_i$ becomes an array of $b\times b$ matrices. Taking $b>1$ allows the method to trade off granularity and parameter count: larger blocks reduce the depth $m$ required to reach dense connectivity while increasing the per-block parameter budget.

This block generalization also connects the butterfly factorization to many classical fast linear transforms. The paper points out that with appropriate choices and permutations, butterfly compositions can exactly recover transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), discrete cosine/sine transforms (DCT/DST), and the Hadamard transform. Those classic transforms are canonical examples where global mixing is implemented by a sequence of sparse, structured stages—the same structure the butterfly encodes. Because the block-butterfly family includes these fast transforms as special cases, it supplies an inductive bias aligned with many useful global transforms: the learned parameterization can express these transforms efficiently and can smoothly approximate a broader class of orthogonal maps by tuning the block entries.

This relation underpins the paper’s argument that butterfly parameterizations are well suited as a structured hypothesis class for orthogonal finetuning: they can represent known global mixing patterns while using far fewer degrees of freedom than an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix.

# Why butterfly topology fits the transmission desiderata better than blocks

Two practical desiderata for sparse factorization were highlighted in the information transmission view: (i) dense connectivity from all sources to all sinks; and (ii) minimum number of free edges (trainable parameters) consistent with orthogonality. The naive block-diagonal approach in earlier OFT satisfies (ii) in a trivial way—each small block is orthogonal and independent—but it fails on (i): block-diagonal structure prevents cross-block information flow entirely, so the composed mapping cannot be dense across all coordinates. That lack of dense mixing can artificially constrain the finetuned model and impede expressivity.

The butterfly topology remedies this by arranging sparsity across multiple stages so that local pairwise (or blockwise) mixes are repeatedly permuted and recombined. Over $m=O(\log d)$ stages, this routing ensures every input index influences every output index, satisfying dense connectivity. At the same time the total number of trainable elements remains $O(d\log d)$ rather than $O(d^2)$. Moreover, orthogonality can be enforced at the small-block level (for example by using Cayley parameterizations or 2×2 rotations), which both reduces the free parameter count and preserves desirable spectral properties (e.g., spectral norm preservation) of the pretrained weight.

The paper demonstrates this trade-off empirically and theoretically: for the same block size, BOFT (butterfly OFT) is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, and by choosing $(m,b)$ appropriately one can interpolate between the extremes of identity (maximum regularity) and full orthogonal group (maximum expressivity). Practically this yields a hypothesis class that is large enough to capture useful finetuning transformations but constrained enough to improve generalization and training stability. The butterfly therefore provides an information-efficient topology—high connectivity per parameter—that is well matched to the goals of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

# BOFT parameterization: R(m, b) as a product of orthogonal butterfly components

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) parameterizes the orthogonal update applied to a pretrained weight matrix as a product of structured, sparse orthogonal factors called butterfly components. Concretely, for a linear layer whose frozen pretrained weight matrix is \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), BOFT replaces the usual forward computation \(\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}\) with
\[
\mathbf{z} = \big(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\big)^\top \mathbf{x},
\]
where the learned matrix \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is constrained to be orthogonal and is factorized as a product of \(m\) block-butterfly components:
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]

Each component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is a block-diagonal matrix formed by repeating a small structured block (a butterfly factor) along the diagonal. The block size is \(b\) (or equivalently, blocks of size \(2b\times 2b\) when describing the smallest orthogonal parameterized unit), and the number of components \(m\) controls how many sequential sparse information-exchange steps are composed. Intuitively, each component implements a sparse orthogonal mixing across subspaces of the \(d\)-dimensional space; composing \(m\) such sparse mixes enables global information flow between every input and every output coordinate while keeping the number of trainable parameters far below \(O(d^2)\).

The butterfly product is designed so that the sparsity patterns in the successive components interleave and permute connections across coordinates; this is the same kind of connectivity exploited by the classical Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform. When chosen appropriately, the sequence of components can produce a dense orthogonal matrix even though each individual component is sparse. The BOFT parameterization therefore trades parameter count for additional repeated structured multiplications during training.

# Ensuring orthogonality: 2x2 (or 2b x 2b) Cayley-parameterized blocks and permutations

Maintaining orthogonality of \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is central to BOFT, since orthogonality preserves geometric relations (pairwise angles) among neurons and yields favorable spectral properties. The BOFT construction ensures orthogonality by enforcing that every factor \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is itself orthogonal. Because each component is block-diagonal (with many identical small blocks), orthogonality can be enforced blockwise.

At the smallest granular level, each repeating block is based on a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal building block (or a \(2b\times 2b\) block when operating with block-butterfly components). A convenient and numerically stable way to parameterize such small orthogonal blocks is via the Cayley transform. For a skew-symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) (so \(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\)), the Cayley map
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
\]
produces an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) (where defined), and initializing \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}\) yields \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\). In BOFT, each small \(2\times 2\) (or \(2b\times 2b\)) block is parameterized by a small skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) and converted to an orthogonal block by Cayley. Because the full component is a block-diagonal composition of these blocks (possibly after a fixed permutation of coordinates), the whole component is orthogonal if every block is.

Permutations appear naturally in the butterfly construction: the non-zero pattern of a higher-level butterfly component is a permuted arrangement of the \(2\times 2\) block pattern. Thus, to produce \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) one can parameterize a canonical block-diagonal orthogonal matrix (via Cayley on the small blocks) and then apply a fixed permutation of rows and columns to realize the butterfly connectivity at that level. Since permutation matrices are orthogonal, permuting an orthogonal block-diagonal matrix preserves orthogonality. This design makes it straightforward to guarantee orthogonality at every component while retaining the desired sparse, interleaving connectivity.

# Special cases: BOFT(1, b) = block-diagonal OFT; BOFT(1, d) = full OFT; BOFT(log(2d/b), b) = dense orthogonal

BOFT is a generalization of previously studied orthogonal finetuning variants and subsumes those as special cases through particular choices of \(m\) and \(b\).

If we set \(m=1\), there is a single component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,1)}\). Because this component is block-diagonal with block size \(b\), BOFT(1, b) is exactly the block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning used in prior OFT work. In that regime the orthogonal matrix is restricted to independent orthogonal transforms applied to contiguous groups of coordinates; this reduces parameter count but cannot generate a dense orthogonal transform across coordinates.

If we further set \(m=1\) and choose \(b=d\) (so there is only one block that spans all dimensions), BOFT(1, d) becomes an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. This recovers the original unconstrained OFT where a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) is learned (typically via Cayley parameterization on a full skew-symmetric matrix), requiring \(O(d^2)\) parameters.

At the other extreme, choosing \(m=\log (2d/b)\) and keeping \(b\) small (in the limit the classic radix-2 butterfly has \(b=2\)) yields a block-butterfly construction that achieves dense connectivity: successive sparse components interleave so that every source coordinate can influence every output coordinate in the composed matrix. In particular, the canonical butterfly case of \(m=\log d\) and \(b=2\) yields a construction whose product is capable of representing many dense linear transforms (and, in more general compositions, can approximate the whole orthogonal group). Thus BOFT(log(2d/b), b) is the regime in which the factorized sparse components collectively produce a dense orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) while using far fewer parameters than a single dense factor.

# Parameter counts: O((b-1) d m / 2); O(d log d) when m = log d, b = 2

A primary motivation for BOFT is parameter efficiency. Counting effective trainable parameters (i.e., degrees of freedom in the orthogonal factors), BOFT analysis in the paper shows that BOFT(m,b) requires
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
\]
effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size \(d\times n\). The factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\) arises because each \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal block has \(\frac{1}{2}(2b)(2b-1)\) skew-symmetric degrees, but when counted per dimension and normalized per component this simplifies to the stated expression for the effective free parameters contributed per component across all \(d\) rows.

Two particularly informative regimes follow directly from this formula. If \(m=\log d\) and \(b=2\) (the classical butterfly), then BOFT uses \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters, since
\[
\frac{1}{2}(2-1)\,d\,\log d = \frac{1}{2}d\log d = \mathcal{O}(d\log d).
\]
This is a dramatic reduction from the \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters required by a full orthogonal matrix. In the block-diagonal OFT case (which is BOFT(1,b)), the parameter count reduces to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\), which is recovered from the general formula by setting \(m=1\). Thus BOFT smoothly interpolates between the block-diagonal regime and the full orthogonal regime, while allowing constructions (small \(b\), larger \(m\)) that achieve dense transforms at parameter costs that grow near-linearly (up to logarithmic factors) in \(d\).

This parameter-count tradeoff is the core practical advantage: by composing a sequence of sparse, structured orthogonal factors, BOFT attains dense global transformations while dramatically limiting the number of trainable parameters, enabling orthogonal finetuning on very large models with modest finetuning budgets.

# Identity initialization for stability and compatibility with pretrained weights

When finetuning a pretrained foundation model, it is important that the finetuning update does not immediately alter the pretrained functionality in uncontrolled ways. BOFT addresses this by initializing every orthogonal butterfly component to the identity. Practically, this is achieved by initializing each small skew-symmetric Cayley parameter \(\mathbf{Q}\) to the zero matrix, which maps to the identity via the Cayley transform. With this initialization, \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) starts as the identity matrix, so the initial forward pass is exactly the pretrained model's computation.

Identity initialization yields two useful properties. First, it realizes a zero-effect start: early optimization steps make small, controlled deviations from the pretrained weights rather than abrupt changes, which helps preserve pretraining knowledge and stabilizes learning. Second, identity initialization enables direct comparison with other zero-initialized PEFT methods (for example LoRA initializes additive low-rank updates to zero), and it guarantees that any regularization or dropout designed for zero-initialized reparameterizations can be applied analogously. Because Cayley parameterization naturally supports zero initialization, it is well suited for BOFT's multiplicative, orthogonality-preserving setup.

# Multiplicative Dropout: blockwise identity replacement to regularize multiplicative updates

Standard Dropout, which stochastically zeros additive components, is not directly compatible with BOFT's multiplicative orthogonal update: simply zeroing entries inside an orthogonal factor breaks orthogonality and the structure of the update. To regularize BOFT's multiplicative composition, the paper introduces a multiplicative Dropout that respects the orthogonal factorization.

The multiplicative Dropout operates at the granularity of whole butterfly components and at block granularity within each component. At each training iteration it randomly selects a fraction \(p_1\) of the butterfly components to disable; for each selected component it also randomly selects a fraction \(p_2\) of its diagonal blocks to disable. Disabling a component or a block means replacing that component (or block) with an identity matrix for this forward/backward pass. Because identity matrices are orthogonal, this replacement preserves the orthogonality of the overall product. Conceptually, this procedure randomly removes some of the sparse mixing steps during training, which discourages over-reliance on any single component or sub-block and encourages the optimizer to build more robust, distributed updates across components.

Multiplicative Dropout thus provides a principled way to regularize the multiplicative orthogonal update without violating orthogonality constraints, and it aligns with the intuitive goal of preventing overfitting that ordinary Dropout provides in additive-update PEFT methods.

# Algorithmic steps for forward/backward passes and integration with existing layers

Integrating BOFT into an existing model is straightforward because BOFT applies a left-multiplicative orthogonal update to pre-existing linear layers. At high level, the forward pass for a finetuned linear layer proceeds as follows:

1. Assemble the current orthogonal components \(\{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\}_{i=1}^m\). Each component is constructed by applying the Cayley transform to its small skew-symmetric blocks and placing them on the block-diagonal, then applying the fixed permutation that realizes the butterfly connectivity for that level.

2. (Optional multiplicative Dropout) Randomly select a subset of components and blocks as described; for selected components/blocks, replace the corresponding block(s) with identity blocks for this pass.

3. Compute the composed orthogonal matrix (or apply the composition implicitly): \(\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,m)}\cdots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,1)}\). In practice, one should avoid materializing the full \(d\times d\) matrix when \(d\) is large, and instead apply successive sparse block operations to \(\mathbf{W}^0\) or to the activations.

4. Form the finetuned weight matrix \(\widehat{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\) and compute the layer output \(\mathbf{z} = \widehat{\mathbf{W}}^\top \mathbf{x}\).

For backward propagation, gradients flow through the product of orthogonal factors into the underlying small skew-symmetric Cayley parameters. Because every factor is orthogonal, gradient norms and conditioning on the weight side are better behaved than unconstrained multiplicative factorizations, and the Cayley parameterization provides straightforward differentiable formulas to map gradients on the orthogonal blocks to gradients on the skew-symmetric parameters. In implementation, one typically backpropagates through the sequence of sparse block multiplications; automatic differentiation frameworks handle the chain of operations and produce gradients for the small block parameters. If memory is a concern, the multiplication sequence can be re-computed in reverse during the backward pass (trading compute for memory), or efficient blockwise routines can be used to keep the per-step cost low.

Because BOFT composes with the existing frozen \(\mathbf{W}^0\) via a single left multiplication, it integrates cleanly into models without changing downstream interfaces. At inference time, a learned BOFT orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) can be multiplied into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) to produce a single static finetuned weight matrix, so there is no inference-time overhead: the multiplicative sequence is only used during training (unless one chooses to keep the factorized representation). This property preserves the inference efficiency of the base model while enabling parameter-efficient finetuning.

Together, these algorithmic steps and integration details make BOFT a practical, orthogonality-preserving, and parameter-efficient finetuning strategy that generalizes and subsumes OFT variants, while enabling dense orthogonal updates with dramatically fewer parameters when using butterfly factorization.

Expressivity, Kaleidoscopes, and the Structure–Regularity Trade-off

# Empirical approximation of random orthogonal matrices: error vs. parameters

A central empirical test used to characterize BOFT's expressive capacity compares how well structured parameterizations can approximate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ as a function of the number of effective trainable parameters. In the paper this experiment uses random orthogonal targets (sampled by standard procedures) and measures an approximation error (averaged over multiple random seeds) while varying BOFT's structural hyperparameters: the block size $b$ and the number of butterfly components $m$. Each configuration BOFT($m,b$) yields a particular number of effective trainable parameters, which is approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$ for a linear layer of dimension $d\times n$.

The observed learning curves show a clear trend: for a fixed parameter budget, BOFT configurations with smaller block size $b$ and larger depth $m$ (i.e., more butterfly components) achieve lower approximation error than configurations that use larger block blocks with fewer components. Concretely, BOFT($9,2$) attains better approximation of a $512\times 512$ random orthogonal matrix than BOFT($1,16$) despite using far fewer trainable parameters. Similarly, BOFT($6,4$) matches the approximation performance of BOFT($2,16$) while using substantially fewer parameters. These empirical findings demonstrate that structural factorization into many shallow, sparse components can be more parameter-efficient than a shallow factorization with large dense blocks.

This experiment is informative in two ways. First, it demonstrates that the butterfly factorization family provides a richer set of representable matrices per parameter than plain block-diagonal orthogonal matrices of comparable parameter count. Second, it suggests that the specific sparsity pattern imposed by the butterfly components helps propagate information more effectively across the $d$ coordinates when composing many factors, resulting in denser, more flexible transforms without quadratic parameter growth.

# Why BOFT with small b and larger m is more parameter-efficient

The intuition for why smaller block size $b$ combined with larger $m$ yields better parameter-efficiency rests on how information spreads through the factorization. Each BOFT component is a block-wise butterfly component: it has $d$ rows and columns but internal sparsity given by permuted $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$ in the block case) building blocks. With a small $b$, each component contains many small orthogonal sub-blocks that locally mix pairs (or small groups) of coordinates. When one composes many such components (large $m$), these local pairwise (or local-block) interactions cascade across components, allowing a single input coordinate to influence all output coordinates via multi-step paths through the grid of intermediate levels.

Viewed through the information transmission framework in the paper, the factorization R = B_m B_{m-1} ... B_1 corresponds to a layered graph with $d$ nodes per level and $m+1$ levels. Small $b$ components realize many simple local exchanges (edges) at each layer, and layering many of those components yields dense connectivity between source and sink levels with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ total edges in the canonical butterfly case. In contrast, taking large $b$ (i.e., larger block-diagonal components) gives each component more free parameters but restricts the connectivity pattern per component; fewer components then limit how many multi-step pathways can be formed, reducing the set of dense outputs reachable with the same parameter budget.

Another way to see the parameter-efficiency is to compare parameter counts to functional effect. A single large block gives the model the ability to perform wide local transformations within that block, but without composition across many layers it cannot move information between distant indices. In contrast, many small composable blocks can route information globally by sequentially connecting local interactions. Thus, for representing dense orthogonal transforms, compositions of many sparse components (small $b$, large $m$) provide a higher expressive payoff per parameter than few dense components.

# Theorem: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size

The paper states (and discusses) a formal relationship between BOFT and the original block-diagonal OFT in terms of expressivity. Intuitively, BOFT subsumes the block-diagonal parameterization as a special case (when $m=1$) and, for larger $m$, spans a strictly larger family of orthogonal matrices for the same block size $b$.

Informally restated from the paper: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. More concretely, by composing multiple appropriately chosen butterfly matrices one can realize transforms that block-diagonal OFT with the same per-component block size cannot. The paper sketches a constructive path: a sequence of butterfly matrices and their transposes can be multiplied to increase coverage of the orthogonal group. In particular, chains of the form

\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\,\bm{B}_{d-1,2}^\top(d)\,\cdots\,\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\,\bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d)
\]

where each $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ is a butterfly matrix, can approximate a richer set of orthogonal matrices than a single block-diagonal factor. The paper formalizes this by showing that repeated compositions of butterfly blocks (and their transposes) can generate arbitrary orthogonal matrices given sufficient depth and the appropriate block granularity (see the kaleidoscope discussion below).

The practical takeaway is that for fixed block size $b$, increasing the number of butterfly components $m$ strictly enlarges the hypothesis class compared to a single block-diagonal OFT($1,b$) while still retaining favorable parameter scaling. This explains the empirical approximation advantage observed earlier.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchies: alternating butterfly products to span the orthogonal group

To bridge from the structured but restricted family of butterfly-parameterized orthogonal matrices to the full orthogonal group, the paper describes a generalized composition often referred to as a kaleidoscope hierarchy. The idea is to alternate products of butterfly matrices and their transposes (or more generally different butterfly patterns) multiple times. Symbolically, this generalized orthogonal parameterization is written as

\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
= \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\,\cdots\,\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]

where each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(m,b)$ denotes an orthogonal matrix parameterized by a sequence of butterfly components as in BOFT. The kaleidoscope hierarchy stacks these blocks and alternates orientations to expand representation power.

The paper observes that if one takes $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$ and repeats this composition for $l=d-1$ levels, the resulting family can represent the entire orthogonal group in dimension $d$. Thus, in the limit of sufficient depth and alternation, the butterfly-based factorization becomes universal for orthogonal matrices. This construction matches previous work that used sequences of sparse structured transforms (kaleidoscope factorizations) to approximate arbitrary dense transforms.

It is important to note that while this provides a theoretical path to full expressivity, the number of parameters and depth required to reach universality scales up; the universality result primarily demonstrates that the butterfly family is not intrinsically limited to a tiny subset of orthogonals — with the right composition it can cover the whole group.

# Caveat: more expressivity is not always better for finetuning generalization

Although the kaleidoscope composition shows that BOFT can, in principle, reach full orthogonal expressivity, the paper emphasizes a crucial practical caveat: higher expressivity is not always desirable for finetuning. Full finetuning (unconstrained update of all parameters) is maximally expressive yet often leads to worse generalization and less stable training compared to constrained reparameterizations. Similarly, enlarging BOFT's representational capacity toward the full orthogonal group loosens the regularization enforced by orthogonality structure and can increase the risk of overfitting to the downstream task.

The core point is that finetuning success depends on finding a favorable trade-off between the hypothesis class size (expressivity) and inductive bias (regularity). Structured parameterizations like BOFT inherently restrict the space of permissible transformations, injecting an inductive bias that can preserve pretrained knowledge and improve generalization. Moving toward greater expressivity (for example by taking very large $m$ and complex kaleidoscopic compositions) reduces that inductive bias. In practice, finite-capacity structured parameterizations often hit a sweet spot where they are expressive enough to adapt to task-specific changes while remaining constrained enough to preserve useful pretrained structure and avoid overfitting.

The experiments and theoretical perspectives in the paper support this: BOFT with moderate $m$ and small $b$ improves parameter-efficiency and approximation power relative to block-diagonal OFT, but the authors caution that blindly pushing to universal expressivity (e.g., building very deep kaleidoscope hierarchies) may hurt finetuning generalization and stability.

# Selecting m and b to balance hypothesis class size and overfitting risk

Given the structure–regularity trade-off, choosing BOFT's hyperparameters $(m,b)$ is a practical exercise in balancing expressivity and regularity. The paper offers several guiding principles grounded in the experiments and the information transmission view.

First, smaller block size $b$ with larger $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient for approximating dense orthogonal transforms and provides better mixing connectivity per parameter. Thus, when the objective is to recover a dense transform with limited parameters, increasing $m$ while keeping $b$ small is an effective strategy.

Second, the effective hypothesis class size grows with $m$ (more composed components permit a larger family of orthogonals). Therefore, if overfitting is a concern— for example, with small downstream datasets or tasks that require only modest deviation from pretrained behavior—one should restrict $m$ (and possibly use a larger $b$ or even $m=1$, which reduces BOFT to block-diagonal OFT) to enforce stronger regularity. Identity initialization of all butterfly components and the Cayley parameterization further bias training toward small deviations initially, which helps stability and reduces early overfitting.

Third, the paper introduces multiplicative Dropout as a regularization mechanism tailored for multiplicative orthogonal updates. By stochastically replacing a fraction of butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices during training, multiplicative Dropout reduces effective depth and forces the model to be robust to missing components. This acts as an additional lever to control overfitting independent of static choices of $m$ and $b$.

In practice, the choice of $(m,b)$ should consider (i) the parameter budget, (ii) the downstream dataset size and risk of overfitting, and (iii) computational cost (larger $m$ increases training-time overhead because multiple matrix multiplications are composed during each forward/backward pass). The empirical results in the paper suggest starting with small $b$ (e.g., $b=2$ or $4$) and modest $m$ (enough to approach desired connectivity) and then tuning $m$ upward only if underfitting is observed. If overfitting appears, reducing $m$, increasing the block size $b$, or applying multiplicative Dropout are practical remedies.

# Closing perspective

BOFT provides a principled way to interpolate between the extremes of strict regularity (identity or small block-diagonal orthogonals) and full expressivity (dense orthogonal matrices). Its butterfly factorization gives a route to high parameter-efficiency: dense, expressive transforms can be composed from many sparse, orthogonal components with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters in the canonical case. The kaleidoscope hierarchy shows that, in principle, the family of compositions can span the entire orthogonal group, but the paper argues—both theoretically and empirically—that the most useful configurations for finetuning are those that strike a balance, providing enough flexibility to adapt while retaining structured regularity that preserves pretrained knowledge and improves generalization.

Stability, Inductive Bias, and Alternative Views

# Spectral norm preservation and its benefits for optimization stability

One of the central mathematical properties highlighted in the paper is that orthogonal finetuning (both OFT and BOFT) preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. If the pretrained linear layer has weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ with singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then left-multiplying by any orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ yields
$\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$.
Because $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is still orthogonal, the singular values (the diagonal entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$) remain unchanged. In particular the largest singular value, i.e. the spectral norm $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$, is preserved by the finetuning transformation.

This spectral-norm preservation has direct consequences for optimization stability during finetuning. The spectral norm governs how much a linear layer can amplify incoming signals: large singular values can magnify activations and gradients, and changes in singular values can alter the conditioning of the network. By ensuring that the singular-value profile of $\bm{W}^0$ is unchanged, orthogonal finetuning prevents accidental increases in amplification and keeps the layer’s conditioning close to the pretrained regime. Practically, this helps maintain the scale of forward activations and backward gradients, reducing the risk of exploding or vanishing signals that often destabilize training.

The paper emphasizes that this property distinguishes orthogonal multiplicative updates from many additive reparameterizations. For example, additive updates can change singular values directly and thus alter the spectral norm unless carefully constrained. By contrast, OFT and BOFT manipulate the layer with an orthogonal left multiplier, guaranteeing invariance of spectral norm by construction. The authors point out that such preservation has been linked to improved training stability and generalization in prior work; within the context of finetuning foundation models, maintaining spectral properties provides a principled way to keep the pretrained model behavior as a strong prior while allowing controlled adaptation.

# Inductive bias from butterfly structures: proximity to fast transforms

BOFT replaces a single dense orthogonal matrix with a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly pattern. This structure is not an arbitrary sparsity pattern: the butterfly graph is precisely the pattern underlying many classic fast linear transforms (for example, the Cooley–Tukey decomposition of the discrete Fourier transform and related fast transforms). What matters for inductive bias is that the hypothesis class of orthogonal matrices representable by the BOFT parameterization tends to be closer to transforms that have multi-scale, mixing behavior across coordinates, rather than isolated blockwise operations.

Because butterfly components permute and mix coordinates across a logarithmic number of stages, a BOFT parameterization can recover or approximate transforms that effect global re-distribution of information from local interactions. The paper stresses that such structured transforms—Fourier, cosine, Hadamard, and similar fast transforms—share a common multi-stage mixing topology that the butterfly factorization captures. Therefore, the BOFT constraint implicitly biases the finetuned orthogonal matrix toward representations that resemble or interpolate among these classical transforms. This bias is smoother and more global than the strong locality imposed by block-diagonal OFT.

From a practical viewpoint, this inductive bias is advantageous for generalization. The pretrained weights of large foundation models often encode distributed, multi-scale features; a finetuning family that is capable of coordinated, global reweighting via a small number of structured stages is well suited to adjust such distributed representations without overfitting to idiosyncratic patterns. The paper argues that the butterfly structure thus provides a favorable middle ground between the expressivity of full orthogonal transformations and the regularity of identity / near-identity transformations, enabling better trade-offs that help generalization.

# Implicit regularization from sparse factorization

Beyond the explicit structural constraint of orthogonality, BOFT’s sparse factorization introduces additional forms of implicit regularization. By decomposing a dense orthogonal transform into a product of sparse orthogonal factors, BOFT reduces the number of free parameters and imposes dependencies among entries of the final matrix. These dependencies function as a structured regularizer: not every dense orthogonal matrix is reachable with a small number of sparse factors, and the reachable set tends to exclude highly irregular or overly complex reparameterizations that would fit noise in a small dataset.

The paper frames this phenomenon through the information transmission view: representing $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ corresponds to designing paths on a layered grid so that information can flow from any source coordinate to any target coordinate. The butterfly topology achieves dense connectivity with relatively few edges (effective trainable entries). By minimizing the number of free edges subject to dense connectivity and orthogonality, BOFT implicitly selects orthogonal matrices that arise from efficient, staged information mixing. This selection produces a constrained hypothesis class that is less likely to overfit, particularly in low-data finetuning regimes.

In addition, the orthogonality constraint itself reduces parameter redundancy: elements are not independent because orthogonality enforces relationships among rows and columns. When combined with sparse factorization, these relationships become stronger and more structured. The authors note that this is qualitatively different from merely pruning or sparsifying a dense matrix after training; instead, BOFT builds the sparsity and dependencies into the parameterization at the outset, producing an implicit regularizer that guides optimization trajectories toward structured solutions.

# BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity R between neurons and inputs

A useful alternative viewpoint casts BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity between pretrained neurons and incoming inputs. Let $\bm{w}^0_i$ denote the $i$-th neuron (column) of $\bm{W}^0$. The finetuned pre-activation for neuron $i$ can be written as
$y_i = (\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{x} = \bm{w}^{0\top}_i \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}$.
Viewed this way, BOFT is learning the bilinear form $\bm{w}^{0\top}_i \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}$ where $\bm{R}$ controls how neuron directions and inputs are jointly compared. Since $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal, the bilinear similarity preserves norms of transformed vectors and enforces that the learned similarity is a rotation or reflection in the input (or neuron) space.

This bilinear-learning perspective connects BOFT to classical metric learning and bilinear modeling: instead of learning separate linear transforms for neurons and inputs, BOFT learns one shared orthogonal transform that reorients the pretrained neuron bank relative to the input space. The shared nature of $\bm{R}$ (applied to all neurons in a layer) is particularly important: it enforces that neurons are rotated coherently as a group, preserving pairwise angles among them and maintaining relational structure learned during pretraining. The paper emphasizes that this is precisely the motivation behind orthogonal finetuning: by preserving pairwise angles, OFT and BOFT retain semantic structure from pretraining while allowing controlled reorientation that adapts representation geometry to downstream tasks.

Viewing BOFT as bilinear similarity learning also clarifies why orthogonality is a natural constraint: it preserves the pretrained spectral and geometric properties, yielding a stable, norm-preserving reparameterization of the similarity space. The butterfly parameterization further restricts the space of permissible bilinear similarities to those arising from staged sparse orthogonal reorientations, aligning the learned bilinear form with structured transform priors.

# Comparisons to LoRA’s low-rank bias and OFT’s block sparsity bias

BOFT occupies a different point in the expressivity–regularity spectrum compared to two other prominent finetuning parameterizations discussed in the paper: LoRA’s low-rank additive updates and OFT’s block-diagonal orthogonal updates.

LoRA parameterizes updates as additive low-rank matrices, i.e. $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ with small intermediate rank. This low-rank bias concentrates parameter capacity into a small subspace of possible updates, effectively assuming that useful task-specific adjustments are compressible into a low-dimensional subspace. Such a bias can be very parameter-efficient and works well in many settings, but additive updates can change spectral properties of $\bm{W}^0$ unless explicitly regularized, and they do not directly preserve relational (angle) structure among neurons.

OFT with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices enforces that neurons are transformed by independent small orthogonal blocks. This block sparsity reduces parameter count while preserving pairwise angles within each block. However, because different coordinate groups are transformed independently (with no cross-block mixing), block-diagonal OFT imposes a strong locality bias: axes are grouped by index and cannot exchange information across blocks. As the paper notes, this makes block-diagonal OFT unable to approximate many classic global transforms and reduces its expressivity relative to full orthogonal updates.

BOFT generalizes and interpolates between these extremes. When BOFT uses a single block ($m=1$, large block size), it reduces to OFT with that block size; when it uses many stages with small blocks and sufficient depth, it can approximate dense orthogonal transforms. But crucially, for moderate values of $m$ and block-size $b$, BOFT yields a structured intermediate hypothesis class: it allows cross-coordinate mixing across stages (unlike block-diagonal OFT) while keeping a parameter count much smaller than dense OFT (unlike full orthogonal matrices). Relative to LoRA, BOFT changes weights multiplicatively and preserves spectral norms, promoting stability and preserving neuron relational geometry; relative to block-diagonal OFT, BOFT introduces multi-stage global mixing that can recover many fast transforms and thus provides richer expressivity per parameter.

The paper’s simulations support this tradeoff: with the same parameter budget, BOFT typically approximates random orthogonal matrices better than block-diagonal OFT. The authors therefore argue that BOFT’s inductive bias—structured multi-stage sparse mixing combined with orthogonality—yields a better parameter-efficiency vs. expressivity balance for finetuning large pretrained models.

In summary, the BOFT parameterization combines the stability benefits of orthogonality (spectral-norm preservation and angle preservation), the structured inductive bias of butterfly/topology-based transforms (proximity to classic fast transforms), and the implicit regularization provided by sparse factorization. These elements collectively explain why orthogonal finetuning with butterfly factorization can be both stable and generalize well in practical finetuning scenarios, and why it can outperform alternative PEFT approaches when the goal is to adapt large foundation models efficiently while preserving useful pretrained structure.

Complexity, Implementation, and Engineering Tips

# Time–space trade-off: repeated sparse multiplies vs. parameter savings
BOFT attains parameter efficiency by replacing a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal factors
$\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b$. This reparameterization reduces the number of trainable entries from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (full orthogonal) down to roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective parameters for BOFT($m$,$b$), and in the canonical butterfly case ($m=\log d$, $b=2$) to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. The cost of this space saving is that each forward (and backward) application of $\bm{R}$ requires sequentially applying the $m$ sparse factors rather than a single dense multiply. Practically this leads to a time–space trade-off that must be considered for engineering choices.

When evaluating this trade-off, keep three points in mind. First, each factor is very sparse and structured (blockwise $2\times 2$ rotations or their block generalizations), so the per-factor computation is much cheaper than a general dense multiply. Second, the cost scales linearly with $m$; choosing smaller $m$ (fewer factors, larger block size $b$) reduces runtime at the expense of parameter efficiency and expressivity. Third, the runtime overhead is most significant during training (where factors are repeatedly applied). After training, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ to remove any inference overhead. Thus, when inference latency is the dominant constraint, favor configurations with larger $m$ (to save parameters) during training because they do not penalize inference once merged.

Architecturally, exploit the structure of the factors to reduce constant overheads: implement the sparse multiplies as batched small-block operations (see next sections), fuse successive factors where possible, and avoid materializing full dense intermediate matrices. Empirically, the choice of $(m,b)$ should be guided by how much parameter savings are required vs. acceptable training-time cost: BOFT($1,b$) recovers block-diagonal OFT (fast, less expressive), while BOFT($\log d,2$) provides maximal parameter compression with higher training-time cost.

# Vectorized blockwise Cayley transforms and stable inverses
To maintain orthogonality of every factor during optimization, BOFT follows the Cayley parameterization used in OFT: for a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ we form
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. BOFT implements each factor as a block-structured orthogonal component built from many small orthogonal blocks (commonly $2\times 2$ rotations, or $2b\times 2b$ blocks for block-butterfly variants). This blockwise view affords two implementation advantages:

1) Vectorized parameterization. Instead of constructing full $d\times d$ skew-symmetric matrices, parameterize only the independent entries inside each small block. For a $2\times 2$ rotation block, a single scalar (or angle) suffices; for $2b\times 2b$ blocks, parameterize the skew-symmetric block at block resolution. Arrange these small blocks into tensors so that the Cayley transform is computed in parallel across blocks. This avoids dense linear-algebra on size $d$ and leverages efficient batched small-matrix kernels on modern accelerators.

2) Stable inverse computation. The Cayley formula requires computing $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ for each factor. Because each $\bm{Q}$ is block-sparse (and initialized to zero for identity initialization), the inverse reduces to many independent small inverses on the blocks. Those small inverses are stable to compute and can be implemented with closed-form formulas for $2\times 2$ blocks or with small LU solves for larger blocks. Working blockwise prevents numerical issues that might arise if one attempted to invert a dense $d\times d$ matrix for every factor.

Important engineering notes: initialize all skew-symmetric block parameters to zero so each Cayley-transformed factor starts as identity (zero deviation), which preserves the pretrained model initially. During backward passes, use automatic differentiation over the blockwise Cayley pipeline; because all operations are local to blocks, computational graphs remain small and memory friendly.

# Permutation-aware layout for efficient memory access
A key property of butterfly components is that their non-zero pattern is a structured permutation of the $2\times 2$ block interactions. Implementations should exploit this by storing data and parameters in a layout that matches the computation pattern, minimizing expensive gathers/scatters.

Concretely, each butterfly component can be viewed as a collection of disjoint small-block operations acting on specific pairs of indices. Arrange the input activation vectors so that pairs that will be jointly processed by the current layer of butterfly blocks are contiguous in memory. This permutation-aware layout lets you express each layer as a single batched block-matrix multiply over contiguous memory slices, eliminating many index indirections.

Two practical strategies:

- Precompute and cache the index permutations corresponding to each butterfly component. Use these permutations to apply reshapes and contiguous slices rather than scatter/gather kernels. On GPUs/accelerators, contiguous slices exploit coalesced memory access and give large speedups.

- Fuse permutation, block grouping, and small-block Cayley application into a single kernel when possible. For example, reshape the input as (batch, groups, block_size), apply one batched small-matrix multiply across groups, and then reshape/back-permute. This reduces kernel launches and intermediate memory writes.

Because BOFT factors are reused at every forward/backward step, the one-time cost of computing and encoding permutation metadata pays off quickly during training.

# Drop-in integration for linear/attention/MLP layers; handling non-power-of-two d
BOFT is designed to be a layer-wise multiplicative adapter: the finetuned weight for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0$ becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with a shared $\bm{R}$ per layer. This makes integration straightforward for different module types:

- Linear layers: replace the forward computation $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ by $((\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top)\bm{x}$. During training, implement this as applying $\bm{R}$ to the frozen $\bm{W}^0$ (or equivalently apply $\bm{R}^\top$ to inputs if that is cheaper), but avoid materializing $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ as dense unless merging for inference.

- Attention projections: apply BOFT to the key, query, value, and/or output projection matrices by left-multiplying each $\bm{W}^0$ with its layer-specific $\bm{R}$. Because BOFT uses a layer-shared orthogonal matrix that preserves spectral properties, it is directly compatible with attention heads and does not introduce head-specific state unless you choose to use distinct $\bm{R}$ per head.

- MLP (feedforward) layers: apply BOFT to the input-side linear weight $\bm{W}^0$ of each dense block so that neuron geometry is preserved across the layer.

Non-power-of-two dimensionalities: the canonical butterfly factorization assumes $d=2^N$ for neat recursive structure, but the BOFT paper explicitly discusses blockwise generalizations. In practice, handle non-power-of-two $d$ by using a block size $b>2$ and a suitable number of factors $m$ so that the block-partitioning covers $d$ (for example, partition into $\lceil d/b\rceil$ blocks and treat the last block specially). The block-butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ formalism supports $b>2$; parameterize the $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks in the smallest component and permute accordingly in higher components. If an exact tiling is not possible, pad the dimension to the next convenient multiple, initialize the padding to identity (so it has no effect), and ignore padded outputs when computing loss. These approaches preserve the BOFT conceptual framework without requiring strict powers of two.

# Initialization, learning rates, weight decay, and dropout schedules
Initialization. As the BOFT paper emphasizes, start with identity initialization: set the skew-symmetric block parameters to zero so every Cayley-transformed component is initially $\bm{I}$. This ensures the finetuned model begins identical to the pretrained model and prevents sudden departures during early optimization.

Learning rates and optimizer. BOFT is a multiplicative, structured reparameterization of the weight; avoid using the same large learning rates as used for training dense models from scratch. The paper highlights that orthogonal finetuning preserves spectral norms and tends to be stable; nevertheless, practical training should use modest learning rates for the BOFT parameters, often smaller than those applied to newly added linear heads. If you already use a small global finetuning rate for pretrained weights, treat BOFT parameters similarly or slightly larger depending on empirical behavior. Standard adaptive optimizers (Adam-family) or SGD can be used; because parameters are structured and few, monitor convergence and scale LR as needed.

Weight decay. Because BOFT parameters are not unconstrained dense weights but entries that represent skew-symmetric blocks feeding into Cayley transforms, weight decay interacts subtly with the induced orthogonal matrices. Use conservative weight decay or none initially; because orthogonality constrains the norm of the effective transform, aggressive weight decay is rarely necessary. If you apply standard weight decay schedules, apply them directly to the trainable block parameters (not to the frozen $\bm{W}^0$).

Dropout schedules (multiplicative dropout). The BOFT paper introduces multiplicative Dropout adapted to multiplicative updates: randomly replace a percentage $p_1$ of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of block-diagonal blocks within each component by identity matrices during training. This is different from conventional additive Dropout and is designed to regularize the multiplicative update. In practice, schedule these probabilities to be higher early in training and decay them as convergence nears, or keep them fixed if overfitting appears. Typical engineering strategy: start with small $p_1,p_2$ to avoid destabilizing early training (e.g., single-digit percentages), then increase if you observe overfitting. Because the paper does not prescribe exact values, tune on validation performance for your task.

Zero initialization + dropout synergy. Zero (identity) initialization coupled with multiplicative dropout provides a conservative starting point and regularizes the path away from the pretrained solution. This combination tends to promote stable fine-tuning, consistent with the paper’s motivation to preserve pretrained geometry.

# Merging learned R into W^0 after training for zero inference overhead
One of BOFT’s practical advantages is that after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ can be merged into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ by computing $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $\bm{W}^0$ is fixed, this is a one-time dense matrix multiply per finetuned layer and removes any inference-time cost associated with BOFT’s factorized representation.

Engineering checklist for merging:
- At the end of training, materialize $\bm{R}$ by composing the learned factors into a dense $d\times d$ matrix. Because $m$ is modest (e.g., $\log d$ factors), this composition is cheap compared to full training.
- Multiply $\bm{R}$ with $\bm{W}^0$ to produce $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ and replace the layer’s weights with $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ for deployment.
- Drop the BOFT factors and any auxiliary permutation metadata from the deployment artifact. The resulting model has identical inference behavior to a model that applied BOFT on-the-fly, but with no runtime overhead.
- Optionally, if storage is at a premium, compress $\bm{W}^{\text{merged}}$ in the same way you would any dense weight (quantization, pruning, etc.). Because BOFT does not change the rank or singular values of $\bm{W}^0$ (left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values), post-merge compression behavior will be similar to compressing $\bm{W}^0$.

This merge is the recommended production path: accept training-time overhead in exchange for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, then produce a normal dense model for inference.

# Practical recipe and debugging tips
Putting the above pieces together, a minimal practical implementation plan is:

1. Decide target parameter budget and runtime trade-off. Choose $(m,b)$ so that $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ fits your budget and training-time overhead is acceptable. For maximal compression choose $b=2$, $m\approx\log d$; for faster training choose larger $b$ and smaller $m$.

2. Implement blockwise Cayley parameterization per factor: store skew-symmetric block parameters (initialized to zero), compute the Cayley transform blockwise with batched small-matrix kernels, and assemble the factor as a block-diagonal or permuted-block structure.

3. Implement permutation-aware batched application of each factor to activations or to $\bm{W}^0$ via contiguous reshapes and batched small multiplies rather than scatter/gather.

4. Add multiplicative Dropout: during training, randomly replace selected factors/blocks with identity (controlled by $p_1,p_2$). Keep parameters for these probabilities configurable and schedulable.

5. Train with conservative learning rates for BOFT parameters, modest weight decay, and monitor validation performance. If training is unstable, reduce the BOFT learning rate or increase identity-preserving regularization (higher dropout).

6. After convergence, compose $m$ factors into dense $\bm{R}$, compute $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and replace weights for inference.

Common debugging signals and remedies:
- Training instability (loss spikes): ensure identity initialization and reduce BOFT learning rate; increase multiplicative dropout temporarily.
- Slow training throughput: fuse permutation + block multiply operations and reduce kernel launches; consider increasing $b$ (fewer factors) if parameter budget allows.
- Poor generalization (overfitting): increase multiplicative dropout or reduce the number of trainable parameters (smaller $b$ or fewer factors). The structured inductive bias of butterfly factorizations can help, but excessive expressivity may overfit.

# Limitations to keep in mind
BOFT deliberately trades training runtime for parameter efficiency; expect slightly higher per-step cost compared to block-diagonal OFT or simple additive adapters. Also, while the block-butterfly formalism supports non-power-of-two extensions, implementations must handle padding or uneven block tiling carefully. Finally, because BOFT factors are constrained to be orthogonal blocks via Cayley transforms, interactions with standard weight-decay and regularization schemes may behave differently than with unconstrained parameters; tune conservatively.

Summary
BOFT is a practical, parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method that is implementable with careful attention to blockwise Cayley transforms, permutation-aware data layout, and multiplicative dropout. Engineer the $(m,b)$ trade-off to meet your training budget, exploit batched small-block kernels and cached permutations to reduce overhead, and merge $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ after training to obtain zero-cost inference.

Applying BOFT to Vision, Language, and Generative Models

# Large Vision Transformers: attention and MLP layers to target; typical $b, m$
When adapting large vision transformers (ViTs) with BOFT, the natural places to insert orthogonal transformations are the large, layerwise linear maps: the projection matrices inside the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) modules (queries, keys, values, and the output projection) and the two large linear layers inside each feed-forward/MLP block. These matrices are often the dominant parameter and computation loci in ViTs, and they are exactly the kinds of pretrained weight matrices $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ that BOFT was designed to modify multiplicatively, i.e. producing $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$.

From the BOFT parameterization in the paper, each orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is formed as a product of $m$ block-butterfly components with block-size parameter $b$: $\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Two canonical regimes are helpful to keep in mind. The block-diagonal OFT corresponds to $m=1$ (BOFT$(1,b)$), which is the most parameter-efficient per-layer option but has the least cross-coordinate mixing; the “butterfly” dense regime corresponds to $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, which yields a dense orthogonal matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective parameters and much greater expressivity. In practice for ViTs you can trade off expressivity and parameter cost by choosing intermediate $(m,b)$ pairs: larger $m$ (more components) and smaller $b$ (finer blocks) increase expressivity and parameter count; smaller $m$ or larger $b$ reduce parameters but limit mixing across coordinates.

Practical guidance grounded in the BOFT framework is to prioritize more expressive BOFT (larger $m$, smaller $b$) for the largest linear maps you wish to adapt, and use smaller $(m,b)$ for smaller layers. Start experiments with BOFT$(\log d,2)$ as a reference for full dense behavior and BOFT$(1,b)$ for cheap, localized adaptation. Always use the identity-based initialization prescribed in the paper (each butterfly component initialized to identity via Cayley transform with zero skew-symmetric parameters) so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained behavior and only gradually deviates. For training regularization use BOFT’s multiplicative Dropout strategy (randomly set some butterfly components or blocks to identity during training) to reduce overfitting in high-capacity settings.

# Large Language Models: QKV/O projections vs. MLPs; stability considerations
In large language models (LLMs) the same architectural observations apply: the attention QKV and output projection matrices and the feed-forward MLP layers contain large linear maps where BOFT can be applied. Which of these to target will depend on the adaptation objective and the parameter budget. The QKV projections are small per-head but numerous (many heads across many layers), while the attention output projections and the MLP dense layers are often the largest single matrices. BOFT is most attractive for matrices with large embedding dimensionality $d$ because the asymptotic parameter saving of the butterfly parameterization ($\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a dense orthogonal) becomes significant.

An important advantage of orthogonal finetuning (and therefore BOFT) emphasized in the paper is improved spectral properties: left-multiplying a pretrained weight by an orthogonal matrix preserves the singular value spectrum of $\mathbf{W}^0$ (if $\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$, then $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ and the singular values remain $\mathbf{\Sigma}$). This preservation helps training stability compared to unconstrained additive updates because it avoids inflating or shrinking the spectral norm of pretrained layers. For LLMs—where stability is critical—this spectral invariance makes BOFT a compelling alternative to low-rank additive methods in regimes where multiplicative updates are acceptable.

Still, BOFT introduces some training overhead because the orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is implemented as a product of $m$ factors and each factor must be applied in the forward/backward passes. The paper notes this overhead is modest but nonzero during training; after finetuning, the learned $\mathbf{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight and there is no inference latency. For stability in LLM finetuning, use identity initialization, consider keeping $(m,b)$ conservative in the earliest experiments, and apply multiplicative Dropout to components/blocks if overfitting or instability appears. Because QKV weight shapes are often smaller in $d$, BOFT$(1,b)$ or BOFT with small $m$ may be preferable for per-head projections, while MLPs and attention output projections can get larger $m$ to capture more global mixing.

# Text-to-Image Diffusion Models: which modules benefit most; training stability
The motivating prior work on OFT in the paper applied orthogonal finetuning to text-to-image diffusion models; BOFT generalizes that approach with a parameter-efficient factorization. In diffusion models, the prominent large linear maps appear in U‑Net architectures (convolutions and linear projections in self-attention blocks) and conditioning pathways (text encoders to cross-attention matrices). BOFT is directly applicable to any linear projection or 1×1 convolution (viewed as a linear map with channel dimension $d$) where multiplicative orthogonal modification is appropriate.

Which modules benefit most depends on where you want to preserve pretrained relational structure. The paper’s motivation—preserving pairwise angles among neurons—suggests applying BOFT where the geometric relationships encoded in pretrained weights are important for generation quality (for example, the main residual blocks and attention projections that mediate global structure rather than very local convolutions). As with ViTs and LLMs, choose larger $(m,b)$ (more expressive BOFT) for the largest or most critical projections, and smaller options where you need strict parameter control. Again, identity initialization is essential to avoid distorting pretrained generation early in training.

Stability considerations are especially important in generative diffusion training because generation quality is sensitive to small spectral shifts; BOFT’s spectral-preserving property helps maintain stable generative behavior. The paper also notes that multiplicative Dropout can be applied to BOFT (turn some components or blocks to identity during training) to avoid overfitting and to improve generalization of the adapted generative model.

# Parameter budgeting across depth: heterogeneous $m$ and $b$ per layer
BOFT naturally lends itself to a heterogeneous allocation of budget across depth because $(m,b)$ can be chosen per-layer. The paper formalizes BOFT as BOFT$(m,b)$ for a layer of dimension $d$, and highlights that BOFT subsumes block-diagonal OFT as BOFT$(1,b)$ and the fully expressive butterfly as BOFT$(\log d,2)$. This parametrization lets you place more mixture capacity where it is most needed and save parameters elsewhere.

A practical budgeting strategy consistent with the paper’s principles is to (1) identify the layers with the largest $d$ (embedding dimensionality or channel width) and the layers most sensitive to downstream distributional shift (often later layers or transformer blocks closest to the output), (2) assign higher expressivity there by increasing $m$ and decreasing $b$ (move toward BOFT$(\log d,2)$), and (3) assign cheaper BOFT$(1,b)$ or BOFT with small $m$ to less critical layers. This produces a smaller hypothesis class in aggregate than unrestricted finetuning while allowing targeted flexibility where it helps the most. The paper also suggests that BOFT’s structured subset-of-orthogonal-group nature imposes a favorable inductive bias: smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal transforms, which can improve generalization when you constrain some layers more tightly.

Because the effective parameter count for BOFT$(m,b)$ on a $d\times n$ linear layer is approximately $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable parameters (as derived in the paper), you can budget globally by summing this over layers and then tuning per-layer $(m,b)$ to meet a total parameter budget. Start with a uniform budget allocation that scales with $d$ across layers, then refine by moving capacity toward layers that give the largest validation gains.

# Interplay with quantization and other PEFT methods (e.g., QLoRA)
The paper contrasts BOFT with additive low-rank PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA) and notes key differences: BOFT is multiplicative and orthogonality-constrained, preserving spectral norms and providing a distinct regularizing inductive bias. It also emphasizes that BOFT’s learned orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ can be absorbed into the pretrained weight after finetuning so that there is no inference-time overhead.

That absorbability implies compatibility with downstream inference optimizations such as weight quantization: because the final model after BOFT finetuning is just a set of standard weight matrices (the modified $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$), it can be quantized or further optimized with the same tooling used for fully-finetuned networks. Consequently, BOFT does not inherently prevent deploying a quantized model or using quantization-aware compression techniques; you should apply the usual post-processing (e.g., quantize after merging) and re-evaluate accuracy. The paper points out a training-time caveat: BOFT introduces extra computation during finetuning because of the factorized orthogonal components, so techniques that compress the training footprint (mixed precision, gradient checkpointing) remain useful.

Regarding combining BOFT with alternative PEFT methods (for example, LoRA or adapters), the paper frames BOFT as an alternative parameterization family (sparse-factorized orthogonal transforms) rather than an additive low-rank correction. While the paper does not experimentally explore hybrid schemes in detail, one clear pathway implied by the framework is orthogonal multiplicative adaptation for preserving spectral structure in some layers and additive low-rank or adapter modules in others—this would let you combine the strengths of both families (BOFT’s spectral preservation and structured inductive bias with LoRA’s straightforward low-rank flexibility). If you choose a hybrid approach, be mindful of interactions: multiplicative BOFT changes the left singular vectors of a layer while preserving singular values; additive updates change singular values and singular vectors. Empirical validation would be necessary to find complementary allocations.

Finally, for approaches like QLoRA (which use quantization during finetuning), the training-time overhead of BOFT’s factorized application must be considered: BOFT requires applying its factorized orthogonal transforms in forward/backward passes, so combine with training-time quantized or memory-saving PEFT pipelines only after ensuring the implementation supports the necessary operations efficiently. Post-finetuning, however, BOFT’s merged weights are fully compatible with quantization-based inference pipelines.

# Summary recommendations
- Target the largest linear maps (attention output projections, MLP dense layers, large 1×1 convolutions); use identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout as described for regularization.
- Use BOFT$(1,b)$ (block-diagonal OFT) as a low-cost baseline; use BOFT$(\log d,2)$ to reach dense expressivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. Sweep intermediate $(m,b)$ to trade capacity against budget.
- Allocate budget heterogeneously across layers by scaling $(m,b)$ with layer width $d$ and empirical sensitivity; use the approximate parameter formula $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ per layer to plan totals.
- Expect modest training-time overhead from factorized orthogonal components; after finetuning, merge $\mathbf{R}$ into pretrained weights for zero inference cost and compatibility with quantization and other inference optimizations.
- For LLMs and diffusion models, leverage BOFT’s spectral-preserving property to improve finetuning stability, and combine with multiplicative Dropout when overfitting is a concern.

These guidelines follow directly from BOFT’s design and properties in the paper: a factorized orthogonal parameterization that preserves singular values, can interpolate between identity and full orthogonal groups through $(m,b)$ choices, and yields substantial parameter savings (down to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$) while remaining expressive for many practical adaptation needs.

Empirical Results and Comparative Analysis

# Performance across ViTs, LLMs, and diffusion tasks

The paper reports an extensive empirical evaluation that applies BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) to three families of foundation models: large vision transformers (ViTs), large language models (LLMs), and text-to-image diffusion models. The goal of these experiments is to test whether the parameter-efficiency and inductive biases introduced by the butterfly orthogonal parameterization translate into consistent downstream improvements across modalities.

Across all three model families, BOFT is described as outperforming prior parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) baselines. The baselines specifically discussed in the paper include LoRA (low-rank additive updates) and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. The reported pattern is that BOFT attains better or comparable task accuracy while requiring fewer effective trainable parameters than full OFT and often matches or beats LoRA despite operating under the orthogonality constraint.

For ViTs, BOFT is applied to adapt pretrained vision transformers to downstream classification and image tasks. The claim is that BOFT yields favorable accuracy-vs-parameter trade-offs: it recovers much of the performance of full finetuning at a fraction of the trainable parameter cost, and it improves over OFT when the latter is constrained to parameter budgets comparable to BOFT’s.

For LLMs, BOFT is used to adapt large pretrained transformer-based language models to downstream NLP tasks. The reported result is that BOFT maintains model stability and generalization characteristics important for language fine-tuning, and achieves stronger parameter efficiency than OFT while remaining competitive with LoRA-type methods.

For diffusion models (text-to-image), BOFT inherits the strengths previously observed for orthogonal finetuning (preservation of pretrained structure and stable training) while reducing OFT’s parameter footprint. The paper highlights that BOFT performs well in controllable image generation adaptation problems where OFT had earlier shown advantages over low-rank approaches.

Across all modalities the unifying empirical observation is that BOFT’s structured orthogonal parameterization—via a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components—produces a compact hypothesis class that generalizes well across tasks, while allowing dense final orthogonal transforms when desired.

# Parameter efficiency vs. accuracy trade-offs

A central empirical theme is BOFT’s ability to trade off between parameter count and expressivity through two controllable hyperparameters: the number of butterfly layers (denoted $m$) and the block size $b$ inside butterfly components. Intuitively, $m$ controls how many sparse factors are multiplied to form the final orthogonal matrix, and $b$ controls the granularity of the smallest orthogonal blocks inside the factorization.

- Parameter scaling: BOFT with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ (the radix-2 butterfly) uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective trainable parameters for a $d$-dimensional layer. More generally, BOFT($m,b$) uses approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable scalar degrees of freedom for a $d\times n$ linear layer. This sits between the $\mathcal{O}(d)$ (very small block-diagonal OFT) and $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (full OFT) extremes and is typically much smaller than full finetuning.

- Accuracy behavior: Empirically, as BOFT increases $m$ or $b$ (thus increasing the number of effective parameters), model accuracy on downstream tasks generally improves, approaching the accuracy of full OFT or full finetuning. Importantly, BOFT typically achieves a target accuracy with far fewer trainable parameters than a naive block-diagonal OFT that matches the same parameter budget, demonstrating superior parameter-efficiency.

- Pareto frontier: The authors emphasize that BOFT moves the performance-versus-parameter Pareto frontier outwards compared to block-diagonal OFT. In other words, given a parameter budget, BOFT yields higher accuracy; conversely, given an accuracy target, BOFT requires fewer parameters. This improved frontier is attributed to the butterfly topology’s ability to create dense, expressive orthogonal transforms from many sparse factors while retaining structural regularization.

The practical consequence is that practitioners can tune $(m,b)$ to the desired accuracy vs. parameter trade-off in a predictable way: increase $m$ (more factor layers) or increase $b$ (larger block granularity) to gain expressivity when more parameters are acceptable; reduce them to push for extreme parameter efficiency.

# Stability and convergence characteristics compared to LoRA and OFT

Stability during finetuning and predictable convergence are recurring advantages associated with orthogonal finetuning approaches. BOFT preserves key spectral properties of the pretrained weights, and the paper highlights implications for training dynamics:

- Spectral preservation: Because BOFT multiplies the frozen pretrained weight matrix from the left by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ are preserved. Concretely, if $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the SVD of the pretrained weights, then the finetuned weights are $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which leaves $\bm{\Sigma}$ unchanged. Preserving $\bm{\Sigma}$ prevents uncontrolled growth or shrinkage of layer operator norms and thus promotes stable gradients.

- Improved training stability vs. LoRA: The paper notes that OFT already produced more stable training in previous work than additive low-rank updates like LoRA, and BOFT retains these stability benefits while being more parameter-efficient. In practical terms, this stability translates to less sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate and often more consistent convergence across random seeds.

- Convergence speed: BOFT introduces a small training-time overhead because the orthogonal matrix is represented as a product of multiple sparse factors that must be multiplied every iteration. Despite this extra computation, the paper reports that BOFT’s convergence (number of iterations to reach good validation performance) is similar to or better than block-OFT and competitive with LoRA in many experiments, owing to the improved inductive bias and regularization.

- Regularity vs. expressivity balance: The authors emphasize that better expressivity (e.g., full OFT or unconstrained finetuning) does not necessarily yield better generalization. BOFT’s controlled structure provides a balance that empirically leads to robust convergence and often superior generalization compared to both the highly constrained block-diagonal OFT and the unconstrained full orthogonal parameterization.

# Ablations: effect of m, b, identity init, multiplicative dropout

The paper reports several ablations that probe how BOFT’s components and hyperparameters influence empirical performance. While specific numerical values are not reproduced here, the qualitative conclusions are summarized below.

- Effect of $m$ (number of butterfly components): Increasing $m$—i.e., composing more sparse orthogonal blocks—improves expressivity and reduces approximation error to arbitrary dense orthogonal matrices. Empirically, going from small $m$ (few components) to larger $m$ typically raises downstream accuracy, up to diminishing returns. Very large $m$ tends toward the full-capacity orthogonal group (when combined with small $b$), but at the cost of more parameters and compute.

- Effect of $b$ (block size): Larger $b$ increases the per-component parameter budget because each basic orthogonal block grows. Increasing $b$ often yields similar qualitative effects as increasing $m$—more expressivity and higher accuracy—though the best way to allocate a fixed parameter budget between $m$ and $b depends on the task and layer size. The paper notes that configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ (deep factorization) are generally more parameter-efficient than the opposite, because the butterfly topology leverages depth to mix coordinates effectively.

- Identity initialization: BOFT initializes every butterfly component to identity (achieved by initializing the underlying skew-symmetric matrices to zero in the Cayley transform). This mirrors zero-initialization in additive PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA) and is reported to be important for stability and to ensure that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model. The identity initialization serves both as a safety mechanism (no initial perturbation of pretrained behavior) and as a regularizer that biases optimization toward minimal deviation unless task demands otherwise.

- Multiplicative Dropout: Because BOFT’s update is multiplicative (left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix), conventional additive dropout schemes are not directly applicable. The paper introduces multiplicative Dropout: at each iteration, a random subset of butterfly components and random diagonal blocks inside them are temporarily replaced by identity blocks. This form of dropout reduces overfitting and acts as structural stochastic regularization. Ablations indicate that multiplicative Dropout reduces variance and can improve validation performance when finetuning budgets are small or datasets are limited.

Taken together, these ablations paint a picture in which BOFT’s hyperparameters offer predictable control over expressivity and regularization. Identity initialization and multiplicative Dropout are practical tools that improve robustness when operating in low-parameter regimes.

# Qualitative insights: weight interpolation behavior and learned transforms

Beyond scalar metrics, the paper emphasizes qualitative behaviors of BOFT that give insight into why it generalizes well.

- Weight interpolation: Because BOFT represents the finetuned weight as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is composed of orthogonal factors initialized at identity, there is a clean geometric interpretation of interpolation between the pretrained and finetuned models. Interpolating the parameters of BOFT (for example by scaling the skew-symmetric parameters inside the Cayley transforms) produces smooth, well-behaved trajectories in weight space that preserve norms and angles. This contrasts with additive low-rank updates where naive interpolation can change spectral properties abruptly. The paper suggests that such smooth interpolation reflects a more controlled adaptation of neural representations and may underlie improved transfer and stability.

- Learned transforms and connection to classical transforms: The butterfly parameterization can exactly represent many classic fast linear transforms (e.g., Fourier, Hadamard, discrete cosine–type transforms) via specific settings of its components. In practice, the learned orthogonal butterfly factors often resemble structured mixing operations that redistribute information across coordinates in ways that mirror these classical transforms. The paper argues that this affinity to known useful transforms provides an inductive bias well suited to many signal-processing and representation tasks encountered in vision and language models.

- Implicit structural priors: The sparse factorization introduces implicit priors that encourage structured information mixing across neuron coordinates, rather than completely unconstrained reweighting. The authors suggest that these priors favor solutions that reuse pretrained representations more effectively, which is consistent with BOFT’s favorable generalization performance when compared to both block-diagonal OFT (which is too localized) and unconstrained full finetuning (which can overfit).

- Visualization and interpretability: The paper indicates that inspecting the nonzero patterns and learned values inside butterfly components can be informative. For example, when BOFT is trained with small $m$ and larger $b$, learned transforms tend to focus on permuting and rotating subsets of coordinates; with larger $m$, the learned behavior is more global and mixing is deeper. Such qualitative observations align with the intended role of $(m,b)$ as knobs that tune locality vs. globality of the learned orthogonal transformation.

# Summary and practical recommendations

The empirical results and analyses in the paper lead to several practical conclusions for applying BOFT in real-world finetuning:

1. BOFT provides a favorable trade-off between parameter count and task performance across modalities (vision, language, and generative diffusion models). It often outperforms block-diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget and competes well with strong baselines like LoRA while preserving orthogonality benefits.

2. Tune $(m,b)$ to match your constraints: prefer smaller $b$ with larger $m$ when you want better parameter efficiency (more expressive mixing for a given parameter budget); increase $b$ or $m$ when you can afford more parameters and need higher accuracy.

3. Use identity initialization for safety and stability. Start finetuning from an exact copy of the pretrained model by initializing all butterfly components to identity via the Cayley transform.

4. Use multiplicative Dropout when overfitting is a concern, particularly in low-data or low-parameter-budget regimes. Randomly masking butterfly components to identity during training stabilizes performance.

5. Expect modest additional training-time cost due to repeated sparse matrix multiplications, but no added inference latency: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal factors can be composed with the pretrained weights into a single weight matrix for efficient inference.

Overall, BOFT demonstrates that carefully structured orthogonal parameterizations—motivated by information transmission and the butterfly topology—can yield practical, interpretable, and broadly applicable PEFT methods that balance expressivity, stability, and parameter efficiency.

Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Training-time overhead due to multiple factors; avenues for kernel fusion

One clear limitation of BOFT emphasized in the paper is the increased training-time overhead compared to the original OFT. BOFT represents the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components,
$\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b$. Computing this product and applying it during each forward and backward pass requires repeatedly multiplying several sparse matrices instead of applying a single (possibly block-diagonal) transform. Even though each butterfly component is sparse and structured, the chained multiplicative updates add extra operations per layer and therefore a non-negligible runtime cost during finetuning.

The paper suggests a concrete direction to reduce this overhead: fuse the product of learned factors into a single dense matrix after training. Indeed, for inference the BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model so there is no additional inference latency. Extending that idea to the training phase leads to two distinct engineering and research opportunities. On the engineering side, kernel fusion or custom linear-algebra kernels could multiply multiple sparse orthogonal factors together on the fly, producing an intermediate dense or block-sparse representation that is reused for multiple micro-batches before being updated again. This amortizes factor-multiplication cost across several gradient steps at the expense of maintaining and updating the fused representation.

On the research side, one can ask how often it is safe to recompute the fused matrix without hurting convergence or generalization. That is, let $\bm{R}_t=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b(t)$ denote the factorized orthogonal matrix at training step $t$. A practical scheme could update a fused matrix $\hat{\bm{R}}$ every $T$ steps and apply $\hat{\bm{R}}$ for intermediate steps. Studying the trade-off between $T$, optimization stability, and final performance is an important open problem suggested by the paper. Another promising line is designing specialized GPU kernels that exploit the fixed sparsity and small block orthogonality (e.g., $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ rotations) so that the sequence of sparse multiplications is implemented with lower overhead than naïve sparse-dense multiplies. Any advances here will directly reduce the practical training-time penalty of BOFT while preserving its parameter efficiency.

# Beyond butterfly: searching network topologies for optimal transmission

A major conceptual contribution of the paper is framing dense orthogonal construction as an information transmission problem on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid. Within this framework, the butterfly graph from the Cooley–Tukey FFT is a natural, well-motivated topology: it achieves dense connectivity from source to receiver nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges and aligns with many fast transforms. However, the paper explicitly notes that whether the butterfly network is the most efficient topology for this transmission goal is unknown. This observation opens multiple research directions.

One direction is systematic exploration of alternative topologies from the networking and distributed-systems literature. The information transmission view invites metrics familiar in network design — for example, minimizing the number of trainable edges (free edges) subject to dense connectivity, minimizing diameter (longest path from source to sink), or optimizing redundancy and robustness to perturbations. Formally, given level count $m$ and node count $d$, one can pose a combinatorial optimization: choose adjacency patterns for $\bm{B}_1,\dots,\bm{B}_m$ to (i) ensure every source node can reach every sink node, (ii) minimize the total count of trainable entries (or a proxy like number of adjacency edges beyond the orthogonality-essential $d$ per layer), and (iii) respect orthogonality constraints on each factor. Exploring this space may reveal families of graphs with better parameter–expressivity–compute trade-offs than butterfly graphs.

A complementary direction is learning the topology jointly with the factor values. Under the information transmission picture we can parameterize potential adjacency choices and then use a differentiable relaxation (e.g., continuous gates on edges or learned permutations) to search for optimal sparsity patterns tailored to a dataset or a model family. The paper warns that orthogonality injects delicate dependencies among entries (so constraint-aware search is required), but this is precisely an interesting technical challenge: how to search over sparse orthogonal factorizations efficiently and reliably. Finally, because the paper connects to classical fast transforms, theoretical work might identify lower bounds on the number of factors $m$ or the number of nonzero entries required to approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices to specified accuracy, but such bounds for general topologies remain open.

# Automated selection of m, b, and per-layer sparsity patterns

BOFT introduces two important hyperparameters that control the trade-off between parameter count and expressivity: the number of factors $m$ (equivalently how many butterfly components are multiplied) and the block size $b$ used in block-butterfly components. The paper shows that BOFT($m,b$) interpolates between block-diagonal OFT ($m=1$) and full orthogonal OFT ($m=1, b=d$ or suitable generalization), and that choosing larger $m$ with smaller $b$ often yields better parameter efficiency empirically. Nevertheless, choosing $(m,b)$ is left as a manual design decision in the paper, and the authors explicitly point out that how to find good sparsity patterns remains a mystery.

Automating the selection of $(m,b)$ per layer is therefore a clear and practical research avenue. Several approaches are possible. One simple strategy is to budget the total number of trainable parameters and distribute this budget across layers according to some importance metric (layer sensitivity). For example, one could estimate layer-wise influence on validation loss via Fisher-information approximations and allocate larger $m$ or $b$ where layers are more sensitive. A more automated, adaptive strategy is to start from an overparameterized BOFT configuration and prune factors or block parameters during training according to learned importance scores; pruning could remove entire butterfly components or reduce block sizes locally. Differentiable architecture search could also be applied: introduce continuous relaxation variables that control the effective rank or activity of blocks and penalize parameter count in the objective to find a Pareto-optimal trade-off.

Per-layer heterogeneity is particularly promising: the same choice of $(m,b)$ for all layers is unlikely optimal because different layers have different dimensionalities, signal statistics, and roles in the pretrained model. The information transmission framework provides a natural interface for per-layer customization: each layer corresponds to a different grid and one can tailor the adjacency pattern and factorization depth to that layer’s needs. Empirical pipelines and principled criteria for selecting layerwise $(m,b)$ remain open work the paper motivates.

# Hybrid PEFT: combining butterfly with low-rank or adapters

The paper contrasts BOFT’s sparse multiplicative parameterization with other parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) families, most notably LoRA’s additive low-rank updates. Both low-rank and sparse-factorized orthogonal updates are structured ways to shrink the finetuning hypothesis class, and the paper highlights that they impose different inductive biases. This suggests a natural hybridization direction: combine BOFT with low-rank or adapter modules to merge complementary strengths.

A hybrid method could, for instance, update pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ with both a multiplicative orthogonal factor and an additive low-rank correction:
$\bm{W}^\text{new}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0+\bm{U}\bm{V}^\top$,
where $\bm{R}$ is parameterized by BOFT($m,b$) and $\bm{U}\bm{V}^\top$ is a low-rank LoRA-like term. The multiplicative orthogonal term preserves spectral properties and pairwise angles, while the additive low-rank term can provide directionally concentrated adjustments that BOFT alone might need many parameters to represent. Another hybrid is to interleave BOFT transforms with small bottleneck adapters inside layers; because BOFT is layer-shared while adapters are inserted per-position or per-head, such hybrids allow both global neuron rotations and local feature reweightings.

Designing and empirically validating hybrids raises many questions the paper points to: how to allocate a fixed parameter budget between multiplicative sparse factors and additive low-rank components for best generalization; whether additive and multiplicative terms interact benignly under standard optimizers; and whether hybrids preserve BOFT’s spectral advantages in practice. These are measurable, concrete experiments motivated directly by the comparison between sparse orthogonal and low-rank PEFTs in the paper.

# Theory: tighter bounds on expressivity, generalization, and implicit bias

The paper provides several theoretical insights: BOFT can be more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size (Theorem: BOFT more expressive than OFT with same block size), the kaleidoscope composition can represent the entire orthogonal group under sufficient factors, and orthogonal finetuning preserves spectral norms which benefits stability. These results are a beginning, but they also motivate deeper theoretical questions that remain open.

First, a principled characterization of expressivity versus parameter count for BOFT is missing. The paper empirically shows approximation curves (approximation error versus effective parameter count) and states that BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient. A next theoretical step is to derive approximation-error bounds: given target orthogonal $\bm{R}^\star$ and a BOFT architecture $(m,b)$, derive upper bounds on $\min_{\bm{R}\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)}} \|\bm{R}-\bm{R}^\star\|$ as a function of $m,b,d$. Such bounds could clarify how many factors or what block sizes are necessary to approximate arbitrary orthogonals to tolerance $\epsilon$ and would complement the kaleidoscope universality result with quantitative rates.

Second, the paper argues that BOFT’s structural prior induces an implicit inductive bias beneficial to generalization, but a tighter theory of this effect is lacking. One can formalize generalization differences between BOFT, block-diagonal OFT, and LoRA by studying complexity measures of the constrained hypothesis classes — for example Rademacher complexity or PAC-Bayes bounds adapted to orthogonality constraints and sparse factorization. Because orthogonality preserves singular values, spectral-norm-based bounds may be tractable and yield insights into why BOFT often generalizes better than less-structured finetuning. Another promising direction is to analyze optimization landscapes: how does the factorized orthogonal parametrization affect landscape smoothness, critical-point geometry, and implicit regularization of gradient-based methods? The paper hints at connections to implicit bias from sparse factorization; making these precise would deepen our theoretical understanding.

Finally, orthogonality imposes algebraic dependencies among factor entries (for instance, $2\times 2$ rotation blocks have one degree of freedom). Understanding how these dependencies interact with the information transmission graph to determine the set of representable transforms is an intriguing algebraic-combinatorial problem that connects matrix factorization, group theory (subgroups of $O(d)$), and graph connectivity. Tighter, perhaps layerwise, expressivity characterizations would directly inform practical hyperparameter choices.

# Concluding remarks on research agenda

The BOFT paper opens a structured and conceptually clean path to improve parameter-efficient finetuning by leveraging sparse orthogonal factorizations and an information transmission viewpoint. The limitations and open problems above are natural continuations of the paper’s contributions: reducing training overhead via fusion and kernels, exploring alternative transmission topologies beyond butterfly, automating the selection of factor depth and block sizes per layer, designing hybrid PEFT methods that combine multiplicative sparse rotations with additive low-rank or adapter modules, and developing tighter theoretical guarantees about expressivity and generalization.

Each direction is grounded in the framework and observations made by the paper: the information transmission graph formalism gives a language to pose combinatorial and optimization questions; the orthogonality constraint and its spectral preservation motivate both algorithmic and theoretical study; and the practical trade-offs between $m$, $b$, parameter count, and compute suggest engineering work on kernels and search. Pursuing these problems will clarify when and why structured multiplicative finetuning like BOFT outperforms other PEFT methods and will likely yield more efficient, robust, and theoretically principled finetuning algorithms for large foundation models.
