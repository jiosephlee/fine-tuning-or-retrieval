\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Why Orthogonality Helps: Preserving Angles and Spectral Norms for Safer Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning large pretrained models is now routine, but it can be fragile. Small changes to weights can disrupt internal geometries learned during pretraining, leading to instability or poor generalization on downstream tasks. Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and its parameter-efficient variant Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) offer a compelling remedy: update weights via orthogonal transforms so that the relative geometry of neurons and crucial spectral properties are preserved. This post explains, without heavy math, why those two properties matter and how BOFT delivers them while remaining parameter-efficient.

## What does “orthogonal update” mean in practice?

Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix \(W^0\). Traditional fine-tuning directly changes entries of \(W^0\). OFT instead reparameterizes the finetuned weight as
\(W = R\,W^0\),
where \(R\) is an orthogonal matrix (so \(R^\top R = I\)). That means we are rotating / reflecting the row space of the layer rather than arbitrarily stretching or skewing it.

Two practical design choices from the paper make this usable in large models:

- Orthogonality is enforced during optimization (e.g., using Cayley parameterization), so \(R\) stays orthogonal at all times.
- BOFT builds \(R\) not as a dense \(d\times d\) free matrix, but as a product of structured sparse orthogonal factors (butterfly components). That yields a dense \(R\) with far fewer trainable parameters (roughly \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) in the fully unconstrained case).

BOFT also initializes all factors to identity (so initial \(R=I\)) and can apply a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces some factors with identity during training to avoid overfitting.

Now let’s unpack why the orthogonality constraint is beneficial.

## 1) Preserving pairwise neuron relationships (angles)

Neurons in a layer can be viewed as vectors (columns or rows of \(W^0\)). The pairwise angles between these vectors encode geometric relationships developed during large-scale pretraining — relationships that likely capture important semantic structure.

An orthogonal transform \(R\) preserves angles and lengths: if two vectors \(u\) and \(v\) are transformed to \(Ru\) and \(Rv\), their dot product (and thus the cosine of the angle between them) remains the same. So applying \(R\) to \(W^0\) keeps the relative directions of neurons intact while permitting a global, rigid transformation.

Why does that help?

- It keeps pretraining-induced geometry stable. Small supervised datasets can otherwise nudge neurons into configurations that break those learned relationships, causing brittle behavior.
- By constraining updates to rotations/reflections, OFT/BOFT reduce the effective hypothesis space explored during fine-tuning. That acts as a regularizer which can improve generalization: we are searching only among models that keep the pretrained relational structure.

The paper emphasizes this angle-preserving view as a principled alternative to ad-hoc small learning rates. Instead of merely "moving weights a little," orthogonal updates guarantee a precise invariant (angles) that aligns with the intuition that pretrained models encode meaningful relational geometry.

## 2) Preserving spectral norms (stability)

Another important property: multiplying an orthogonal matrix on the left does not change the singular values of \(W^0\). If \(W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top\) is its SVD, then
\(R W^0 = (R U)\, \Sigma\, V^\top\),
and the diagonal singular-value matrix \(\Sigma\) is unchanged. The largest singular value (spectral norm) remains identical.

Why is spectral norm preservation useful?

- The spectral norm controls how much the layer can amplify inputs. Large increases can destabilize gradients, worsen optimization, and lead to brittle models.
- Methods that preserve the spectral properties of pretrained weights maintain the same input-output sensitivity as the pretrained model, providing a safer optimization landscape for downstream adaptation.

Empirically and theoretically, preserving or controlling spectral norms has been tied to training stability and better generalization; OFT/BOFT inherit those benefits by construction.

## 3) BOFT as learning a structured bilinear similarity under strong regularization

Viewed from another angle, OFT/BOFT are learning a bilinear similarity: each neuron response is of the form \(w_i^\top R x\), where \(w_i\) is a pretrained neuron and \(R\) is the learned orthogonal transform. So the learning problem reduces to finding a single (layer-shared) bilinear form \(R\) that adapts the pretrained features to the downstream task.

BOFT adds two important ingredients to this picture:

- Strong regularization: \(R\) must be orthogonal, which is a hard constraint rather than a soft penalty. This shrinks the hypothesis class to rotations/reflections and enforces stability.
- Structured parameter-efficiency: instead of a free dense orthogonal \(R\), BOFT parameterizes \(R\) as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly" factors. This imposes an inductive bias (a structured subset of the orthogonal group) that is expressive enough to recover many classical transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.) but far smaller in parameter count.

The result is that BOFT is learning a compact, structured bilinear similarity matrix that respects pretraining geometry and spectral behavior while being cheap to store and optimize.

## 4) The expressivity / regularity trade-off — why structure helps

Full fine-tuning is maximally expressive but often overfits or destabilizes training. Extremely constrained schemes (e.g., tiny block-diagonal orthogonal updates) are very stable but may lack expressivity needed for certain tasks. BOFT sits between these extremes.

- The butterfly factorization composes a small number of sparse orthogonal blocks so that, even with few parameters, the product can produce dense, globally mixing transforms.
- The paper shows BOFT can be substantially more expressive than simple block-diagonal OFT for the same parameter budget, yet still far more constrained than unconstrained dense orthogonal matrices.

This structured inductive bias—reflecting patterns common in fast linear transforms—appears to help generalization in experiments across vision, language, and generative models.

## 5) Practical touches: initialization and overfitting control

Two pragmatic choices in BOFT make the orthogonal approach work smoothly:

- Identity initialization. All butterfly factors are initialized as identity so the fine-tuned model starts exactly from the pretrained model (no sudden jump).
- Multiplicative dropout. Because the update is multiplicative (not additive), standard dropout doesn’t apply directly. BOFT randomly replaces some factors or blocks with identity during training, effectively regularizing the multiplicative transform.

These strategies help prevent overfitting and stabilize optimization.

## Limitations and trade-offs

BOFT reduces parameter count substantially, but it does add some runtime overhead during training because the orthogonal \(R\) is represented as a product of factors that must be multiplied each forward/backward pass. The paper notes this as an area for future improvement. Also, butterfly factorization is a principled and practical design choice, but whether it is the optimal sparse topology for every task is an open question.

## Takeaways

- Orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise neuron angles and the singular values of pretrained weights. Those invariants help stabilize training and improve generalization compared to unconstrained updates.
- BOFT achieves the same invariants while being parameter-efficient: it learns a structured bilinear similarity (a layer-shared orthogonal \(R\)) using butterfly factorization, which yields dense transforms from very few parameters.
- The combination of exact geometric invariants and a structured inductive bias gives a practical middle ground between full fine-tuning and overly rigid adapters—safer fine-tuning that still adapts effectively.

If you care about preserving pretrained geometry and spectral behavior while keeping the finetuning budget low, orthogonal finetuning—especially BOFT—offers a principled option worth trying.
