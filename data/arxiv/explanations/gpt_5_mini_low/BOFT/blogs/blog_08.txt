\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Beyond Butterflies: Future Network Topologies for Efficient Fine-Tuning

The Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) paper introduces a neat idea: factor a dense orthogonal update into a product of sparse orthogonal factors so you can finetune large pretrained layers with far fewer parameters. BOFT uses butterfly-structured factors (inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT) to get dense connectivity with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters instead of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). This is an elegant use of a classic network topology to meet two core desiderata for factorized orthogonal finetuning: dense connectivity (every input node can reach every output node after composing factors) and minimum free edges (few trainable non-structural degrees of freedom under orthogonality).

But the paper is explicit about open questions and limitations. Two that stand out are: (1) BOFT imposes runtime overhead because multiple factor matrices must be multiplied during training, and (2) butterfly is a compelling but not necessarily optimal topology for this information-transmission problem. This post digs into both issues and sketches principled directions for future work — drawing on the paper’s information-transmission view and the parallel literature on network topologies — without claiming results beyond the paper.

## Recap: why factorize an orthogonal update?

OFT reparameterizes a finetuned weight as \(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\) where \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal; this preserves pairwise angles and the spectral norm of \(\bm{W}^0\). Dense \(\bm{R}\) gives expressivity but costs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters. BOFT composes \(\bm{R}\) as a product of sparse orthogonal factors
\(\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i\),
where carefully chosen sparsity (the butterfly pattern) yields dense overall connectivity while keeping effective trainable parameters at roughly \(\mathcal{O}(dm)\) (for radix-2 butterfly with \(m\approx\log d\), this is \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\)). BOFT also preserves desirable spectral properties and starts factors at identity (Cayley parametrization with zero skew-symmetric initializers), plus multiplicative dropout that respects the multiplicative factorization.

So BOFT trades space (parameters) for time (additional factor multiplications per forward/backward). The paper frames this as an information transmission problem on a \(d \times (m+1)\) grid and shows butterflies are a topology that efficiently achieves the dense-transmission requirement.

## Two practical pain points the paper calls out

1. Training-time overhead. Because \(\bm{R}\) is built from \(m\) sparse orthogonal factors, each training iteration must apply (and backprop through) multiple factor multiplications. This increases runtime compared to directly applying a block-diagonal OFT or a LoRA-style additive update. The paper notes that BOFT has slightly larger training runtime and flags reducing that overhead as an open problem.

2. Is butterfly optimal? Butterflies are attractive because they give dense reachability with only \(O(d\log d)\) edges and match classic fast transforms. But the paper explicitly leaves open whether there exist other network topologies — informed by the computer networking literature — that transmit information even more efficiently under the orthogonality constraint and with better trade-offs between parameter count, expressivity, and runtime.

Both issues are fundamentally connected: a better topology could reduce the number of factor layers \(m\) (reducing runtime) or the per-factor block size \(b\) (reducing parameters), or both.

## How to think about alternative topologies (guided by the paper)

The paper provides an information-transmission abstraction that gives a clean way to evaluate any candidate topology. Any factorization corresponds to a directed layered graph on \(d\) nodes per layer; each sparse orthogonal factor encodes edges between consecutive layers. For a factorization to yield a dense final matrix \(\bm{R}\), every source node (layer 1) must have a path to every sink node (layer \(m+1\)). Under orthogonality, we should also respect structural constraints (e.g., each factor must be full-rank and orthogonal).

When evaluating or designing alternative topologies, the following metrics (implicit in the paper) are useful:

- Edge efficiency: total number of trainable degrees of freedom (roughly proportional to number of nontrivial edges that are not fixed by orthogonality). BOFT aims for \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).
- Depth \(m\): number of factor layers — smaller \(m\) generally reduces runtime overhead.
- Local block size \(b\) and block structure: larger blocks increase per-factor expressivity (and parameter count), smaller blocks reduce parameters but may require more layers.
- Connectivity/diameter: how many hops are needed for any source-to-sink path? Lower diameter often helps expressivity for a given depth.
- Orthogonality compatibility: can each factor be parameterized as orthogonal easily (e.g., via Cayley on small blocks or 2×2 rotations)?
- Recoverability of classic transforms: do the factors and permutations cover well-known transforms (implicit bias useful for generalization)?

Using these as evaluation axes keeps us faithful to the paper’s framing while opening the design space beyond butterflies.

## Candidate directions to explore (all consistent with the paper’s framework)

The paper points to the networking literature as a source of alternative topologies. Below are grounded, paper-consistent directions to investigate.

1. Search for sparser depth-vs-width trade-offs
   - The butterfly achieves dense connectivity with depth \(m\approx\log d\). But perhaps other layered topologies can achieve the same reachability with smaller \(m\) at the cost of slightly more edges, or reduce parameter coefficient constants (improving practical efficiency). The paper suggests combining ideas from networking; this invites systematic searches (analytical or learned) over layered graph families subject to orthogonality constraints.

2. Consider layered permutations and blockings (structured permutations)
   - BOFT already uses permutations implicit in butterfly components. Combining targeted permutations with small orthogonal blocks could reduce the number of layers required to reach dense connectivity. Since permutations are parameter-free, they can be used to trade parameter count vs depth without affecting orthogonality parametrization complexity.

3. Kaleidoscope / composite hierarchies
   - The paper notes kaleidoscope hierarchies (products of butterfly-like matrices and transposes) can represent the full orthogonal group when stacked appropriately. Studying shallow kaleidoscope instantiations (fewer blocks but richer interleavings) could hit sweet spots between expressivity and runtime.

4. Borrowing topology concepts (while respecting the paper’s caution)
   - The networking literature studies many topologies with different trade-offs (e.g., Benes networks, Clos/Fat-tree, expander-like constructions). The paper does not analyze these specifically, but its information-transmission view provides the bridge: each candidate topology must be cast as a layered factorization where each factor is orthogonal or decomposable into small orthogonal blocks.
   - For instance, expanders have excellent connectivity with low degree; if there is an orthogonality-preserving way to realize an expander-like layer decomposition, that could reduce parameter count while keeping small depth. Any such topology must also satisfy the orthogonality parameterization constraints described in the paper.

5. Learnable sparsity patterns within the information-transmission framework
   - Instead of fixing a topology (butterfly or otherwise), one can parameterize a family of sparse layered graphs with learnable binary structure (or sparsity masks) and optimize the structure jointly with the factor parameters, subject to constraints that permit orthogonal parametrization (e.g., blockwise Cayley transforms). The paper mentions multiplicative dropout and identity init; these mechanisms could be combined with structure learning to gradually reveal effective edges.

6. Hardware- and runtime-aware factor selection
   - The paper notes training-rate overhead as an engineering limitation. Practical follow-ups can explore:
     - Kernels that fuse the product of multiple sparse factors into a single batched operation to reduce kernel launches and memory traffic.
     - Mixed-precision or approximate compositions during training where frequent small factors are merged or approximated to cut cost.
     - Choosing topologies that map well to vectorized block operations on common hardware (GPUs/TPUs), e.g., preferring repeated small dense blocks versus many scalar-sparse operations.

7. Orthogonality relaxations and approximate constraints
   - The paper emphasizes strict orthogonality for spectral preservation and stability. Future work could explore controlled relaxations (e.g., near-orthogonality with a penalty) that allow sparser or shallower factorizations and cheaper training while retaining most spectral benefits.

## Practical experiments the paper suggests (and that follow logically)

The paper already performs expressivity simulations (approximating random orthogonal matrices) and finetuning experiments across modalities. To systematically compare topologies, the following experiments align with the paper’s methodology:

- Approximation experiments: measure how well different layered topologies (butterfly, alternatives from networking, learned sparse layers) approximate random orthogonal matrices as a function of effective trainable parameters and depth \(m\). This mirrors the paper’s Figure on parameter efficiency but extends the topology pool.
- Finetuning benchmarks: compare downstream performance (and runtime) of candidate topologies when finetuning large Transformers or diffusion model layers, controlling for parameter budgets and initialization protocols (identity/Cayley).
- Runtime profiling: measure forward/backward time per iteration for each topology and correlate with depth and block-size choices; experiment with fused kernels to reduce overhead, as the paper flags training-time cost as the main practical limitation.

All these are expressly consistent with the BOFT paper’s claims and its information-transmission evaluation framework.

## Closing thoughts

BOFT shows that thinking of factorized orthogonal finetuning as an information-transmission problem opens up a large and structured design space. The butterfly is an excellent first instantiation: principled, compact, and linked to many classic transforms which may serve as an implicit inductive bias for generalization. But the paper is careful to point out that butterflies are not proven optimal.

The next steps are thus clear and consonant with the paper: use the information-transmission graph abstraction to evaluate alternative layered topologies (from networking ideas and learned structures), balance depth \(m\), block size \(b\), and parameter count, and tackle runtime overhead with both algorithmic (fusion, efficient kernels) and architectural (topology selection) remedies. Doing so could reduce the multiplicative training cost while preserving BOFT’s spectral and geometric benefits, producing even more parameter-efficient and practical orthogonal finetuning schemes.
