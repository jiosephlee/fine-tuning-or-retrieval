\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# BOFT vs. OFT vs. LoRA: Trade-offs in Parameters, Expressivity, and Stability

Efficiently adapting giant pretrained models is now essential. Three popular parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) paradigms approach the problem differently: LoRA (low-rank additive updates), OFT (orthogonal multiplicative updates), and BOFT (orthogonal updates with butterfly factorization). This post breaks down how they compare along three axes you care about — parameter cost, expressivity, and stability/generalization — and offers practical guidance on when to pick each.

## The core ideas (short and precise)

- LoRA: adds a low-rank update to a frozen weight matrix. Concretely, a weight matrix $W^0$ is updated as $W = W^0 + A B$ where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ with small rank $r$. Very parameter-efficient when $r \ll d$.

- OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning): replaces the pretrained weight by a multiplicative orthogonal transform on the left: $W = R W^0$ with $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $R^\top R = I$. This preserves geometric relations (pairwise angles) between neurons and keeps singular values of $W^0$ unchanged. Full OFT requires $O(d^2)$ parameters; practical versions use block-diagonal $R$ to save parameters.

- BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning): parameterizes a dense orthogonal $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in butterfly structure (inspired by FFT). With block size $b$ and $m$ butterfly layers, BOFT uses about
  $$
  \text{params}\ \approx\ \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d m
  $$
  and the canonical butterfly choice ($b=2$, $m=\log d$) yields $O(d\log d)$ parameters — dramatically fewer than full OFT while still producing dense orthogonal transforms.

BOFT subsumes OFT as special cases: (i) BOFT$(1,b)$ equals block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and (ii) BOFT$(1,d)$ equals full OFT.

## Why orthogonality matters (angles and spectra)

Two properties explain why OFT and BOFT can produce more stable finetuning than simple additive updates:

- Angle preservation. Multiplying neuron vectors by the same orthogonal $R$ preserves pairwise angles between columns of $W^0$. Preserving these relational structures is argued to help retain pretrained semantic geometry.

- Spectral preservation. If $W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top$ is an SVD, then $R W^0 = (R U)\Sigma V^\top$. Because $R$ is orthogonal, the singular values (diagonal of $\Sigma$) are unchanged. Keeping the spectral norm of pretrained layers stable is known to improve training stability and generalization.

LoRA does not preserve angles or the singular values in general because it adds a low-rank perturbation.

## The expressivity — block-diagonal OFT can be too rigid

Full OFT (unconstrained orthogonal $R$) is maximally expressive among orthogonal multiplicative updates, but it costs $O(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce cost, OFT often uses block-diagonal $R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ with small block size $b$. That saves parameters (roughly $O(bd)$) but enforces that different coordinate groups are transformed independently. This induces a strong sparsity pattern that cannot represent many global linear transforms (for example, it cannot recover classic dense transforms such as FFT or Hadamard). In other words, block-diagonal OFT trades expressivity for parameter savings by imposing a rigid, index-based grouping.

BOFT offers a smoother trade-off. By composing multiple sparse factors in a butterfly topology, BOFT can produce dense orthogonal matrices while keeping parameter count low. The butterfly structure is known to efficiently transmit information across all coordinates; with $\log d$ sparse layers each having $O(d)$ nonzeros, you get dense connectivity with only $O(d\log d)$ free parameters. Practically this means BOFT can:

- Recover many classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT) because those transforms share butterfly-like structure.
- Be more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same local block size, while still being far more parameter-efficient than full OFT.

The paper also formalizes that BOFT with suitable compositions (kaleidoscope-style products of butterflies) can approximate the full orthogonal group — though, importantly, more expressivity is not always better for finetuning.

## Parameter costs (summary)

- Full OFT: O(d^2) trainable parameters (dense orthogonal).
- Block-diagonal OFT: O(b d) parameters when splitting into blocks of size $b$ (very cheap when $b\ll d$, but rigid).
- BOFT: about $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ parameters for BOFT$(m,b)$. The canonical dense-targeting butterfly BOFT with $b=2$, $m=\log d$ gives $O(d\log d)$ parameters.

Compare these to LoRA: LoRA uses $O(dr)$ parameters per finetuned layer (rank $r$); typically $r$ is small, so LoRA is also highly parameter-efficient and often cheaper than BOFT for small $r$.

## Training dynamics and engineering trade-offs

- Stability and generalization. Orthogonal updates (OFT/BOFT) preserve spectral norms and pairwise geometry, which empirically helps training stability and generalization on many tasks, according to the paper. LoRA, while effective, lacks these invariants.

- Initialization. BOFT (like OFT) initializes the orthogonal factors to identity (via Cayley parameterization with zero skew-symmetric factors), ensuring zero initial change and preserving pretrained behavior at start of finetuning.

- Regularization mechanisms. BOFT introduces a multiplicative dropout adapted to multiplicative updates: randomly replace selected butterfly component blocks with identity during training to prevent overfitting. LoRA typically uses conventional dropout on activations or on low-rank updates.

- Runtime. BOFT requires repeated multiplication of multiple sparse factors during training, so its runtime per update is higher than single-matrix block-diagonal OFT or simple LoRA updates. However, after finetuning the learned orthogonal $R$ can be folded into the base weights, so there is no inference overhead.

## When to use each method

- Use LoRA when your primary constraint is an extremely small tunable-parameter budget and you accept a low-rank inductive bias. LoRA is simple, fast, and very parameter-efficient; it is a good default when you know low-rank structure suffices.

- Use full OFT when you want the strongest orthogonal invariants (angle and spectrum preservation) and you can afford $O(d^2)$ parameters for the critical layers. This is rare for large models but might be viable for small/medium layers where you prioritize geometry preservation.

- Use block-diagonal OFT when you want orthogonal invariants but only modest parameter cost and you believe a per-block independent transform is acceptable. This is fast and cheap but can be overly restrictive if global mixing across coordinates matters.

- Use BOFT when you want a middle ground: orthogonality-based invariants plus much lower parameter cost than full OFT, while preserving expressivity far beyond block-diagonal OFT. BOFT is especially attractive when you need dense global transformations (so grouping-based sparsity would hurt) but cannot afford quadratic parameter counts. Expect a modest training-time overhead, but no extra inference cost after folding.

## Caveats and practical notes

- BOFT improves parameter-efficiency by trading computation for space: you do more (sparse) multiplications during training to represent a dense orthogonal $R$ compactly. The paper notes that training runtime overhead is higher and improving training speed is an open direction.

- More expressivity does not always equal better finetuning. Full expressivity (e.g., full OFT or extremely flexible BOFT compositions) can overfit; the structured constraint of BOFT can act as a beneficial inductive bias by resembling classic fast transforms.

- BOFT has been empirically validated across adaptations of vision transformers, language models, and text-to-image diffusion models in the paper; the authors report BOFT outperforms strong baselines across a range of tasks, demonstrating both parameter-efficiency and generalization gains.

## Takeaway

- LoRA = very cheap additive low-rank updates; good when low-rank is appropriate.
- OFT = orthogonal multiplicative updates; preserves neuron angles and spectra, which aids stability, but full OFT is expensive. Block-diagonal OFT saves parameters at the cost of expressivity.
- BOFT = orthogonal multiplicative updates with butterfly factorization; hits the sweet spot in many cases: preserves orthogonal invariants, yields dense transforms, and does so with O(d log d) (or tunable) parameters. Expect modest extra training cost but no inference cost.

If you care about preserving pretrained geometry and need a dense-like transform without quadratic parameters, BOFT is a compelling option — it smooths the expressivity/regularization trade-off that block-diagonal OFT enforces too abruptly.
