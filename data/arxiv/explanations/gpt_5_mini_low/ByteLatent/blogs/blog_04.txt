\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Scaling Without Tokens: FLOPs, Patch Size, and Why BLT Gets Cheaper at Inference

## TL;DR

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) shows you can train large, tokenizer-free models on raw bytes and get two important wins: (1) you can match the compute‑optimal training performance of tokenized models (Llama 3) up to 8B parameters and multi‑trillion bytes, and (2) you can make inference measurably cheaper—up to ~50% fewer FLOPs—by increasing the average patch size so the heavy global transformer runs fewer steps. The trick is dynamic patching (entropy-based) combined with a lightweight byte encoder/decoder and a large latent/global transformer that only runs per patch.

Below I unpack the core mechanisms, the flop accounting that matters, and the scaling experiments that show why patches scale better than tokens.

## The fundamental idea

Tokenizers fix a vocabulary and a step-size: each token is a unit the model must process, and the model spends roughly the same compute per token. BLT replaces fixed tokens with dynamically constructed "patches" of bytes. A patch is simply a contiguous group of bytes; its average size controls how many steps the main (heavy) transformer must take.

Key intuition:
- Many bytes in natural data are easy to predict (low entropy). Group them into longer patches so the global transformer is invoked less often.
- Hard, high‑entropy bytes (e.g., the first character of a new word/answer) become patch boundaries so the model allocates more compute where it matters.

This lets BLT change the cost per byte independently of model size. If you increase average patch size, the expensive global transformer runs fewer times per input length, saving inference FLOPs. Those saved FLOPs can be reallocated to grow the model (more parameters) while keeping the same inference budget.

## How BLT is structured (short version)

BLT splits the modeling work into three cooperating modules:

- Local encoder $\mathcal{E}$: a small transformer (few layers) that embeds raw bytes and pools them into initial patch representations. It uses byte embeddings plus hashed $n$-gram embeddings to capture local character patterns.

- Latent/global transformer $\mathcal{G}$: the large autoregressive transformer that consumes patch representations and models long-range dependencies across patches. This is the module that dominates FLOPs for typical transformer architectures.

- Local decoder $\mathcal{D}$: a small transformer that maps global patch outputs back to raw bytes.

Because $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ run on bytes but are lightweight, most compute cost is determined by how many patch steps $\mathcal{G}$ takes.

## Patching options and why entropy patching

BLT experiments with different patching schemes:

- Fixed stride (Megabyte-style): every $k$ bytes make a patch. Simple but non-adaptive: wastes steps on trivial bytes, and splits identical words inconsistently across contexts.

- Space/whitespace patching: create patches at word boundaries. Better for many natural language tasks, but not adaptive enough across languages/domains.

- Entropy patching (BLT's main dynamic method): use a small byte-level entropy model to estimate next-byte entropy $H(x_i)$ and place boundaries where entropy is high (globally above a threshold $\theta_g$ or high relative to the previous byte $\theta_r$). Formally,
  - Global boundary: $H(x_t) > \theta_g$
  - Approx. monotonicity: $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$

Entropy patching is incremental: it only needs the past context (so it can be used during generation) and produces contextually consistent patches.

## FLOP accounting — why patch size controls inference cost

Transformer compute scales roughly linearly with sequence length for the heavy feed‑forward layers and quadratic with attention window size; but for large models the FFN dominates. BLT’s per‑byte FLOPs can be expressed as the sum of contributions from the three modules and cross‑attention blocks. In the paper the per-byte FLOP estimate is written conceptually as:

FL_BLT = (Transf_FL for global on patches) / (patches per byte)
       + Transf_FL for local encoder (per byte)
       + Transf_FL for local decoder (per byte)
       + Cross‑attention costs (scaled per byte)

If you denote average patch size by $n_p$ (bytes per patch) and the byte context length by $n_{ctx}$, the dominant term (global transformer) scales inversely with $n_p$ because the global model runs once per patch, not per byte. Doubling $n_p$ roughly halves the global-transformer contribution to FLOPs.

Tokenizers are effectively fixed-patch-size systems where average token length is constrained by vocabulary design and embedding/output head costs. BLT decouples patch size from an embedding table growth, so you can increase $n_p$ without increasing output-vocabulary costs.

The net result: larger $n_p$ → fewer global steps → fewer inference FLOPs.

## What the scaling experiments show

The paper runs careful, flop-controlled experiments up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes. Two complementary studies are particularly important.

1. Parameter-matched, compute-optimal scaling (Llama‑2 data):
   - BLT models (with entropy patching and the local/global architecture) match or outperform BPE token models (Llama 3 baselines) at compute‑optimal training regimes across model sizes 1B–8B.
   - Simpler byte approaches (fixed stride) lag behind; space patching helps but still needs the full BLT architecture and hashed n‑gram embeddings to reach parity.

2. Fixed-inference-FLOP scaling (the key "patches scale better than tokens" experiment):
   - The authors build inference‑FLOP matched families: a baseline tokenizer model and BLT variants that have larger average patch sizes (e.g., ~6 or ~8 bytes per patch).
   - Because larger patches reduce the number of global steps, BLT can reallocate saved FLOPs into a bigger global transformer while holding inference FLOPs fixed.
   - Result: BLT models with larger patch sizes show steeper scaling curves and outperform token-based models at modestly larger training budgets. For some configurations, BLT achieves up to ~50% inference FLOP savings relative to Llama‑3-like baselines while preserving comparable evaluation performance (bits-per-byte and downstream tasks).

Additional results:
- BLT-Entropy (entropy patching) tends to perform better than BLT-Space on many downstream tasks, although BLT-Space offers larger patch sizes and thus more inference savings at the cost of some accuracy.
- BLT improves robustness on character-level tests, noisy inputs, grapheme-to-phoneme, and low-resource MT, showing byte modeling helps with long-tail sequences that tokenizers struggle with.

## Practical tradeoffs and caveats

- Encoder/decoder overhead: Local encoder/decoder and cross‑attention introduce byte-level costs that do not vanish as you increase patch size. These are relatively small and grow much slower than the global transformer, but they set a floor for how cheap inference can get.

- Entropy model cost: Entropy patching uses a small byte LM to compute patch boundaries. In training this is a preprocessing step; at inference you can tune the entropy threshold—lower thresholds mean more patches (better performance but higher inference cost), higher thresholds mean fewer patches (more savings). The paper also considers resetting entropy context to avoid drift in highly repetitive inputs.

- Matching context length: To compare methods fairly, BLT experiments keep the same expected number of bytes per batch across patch sizes. Models with larger patches use shorter sequence lengths in steps to keep byte context constant in expectation.

- Implementation complexity: Dynamic cross-attention masks and variable-length patches require attention implementations like FlexAttention. The paper notes production libraries are optimized for tokenizers and BLT may need further engineering to match wall-clock efficiency.

## Why this matters

Tokenizers have been a pragmatic compression trick that ties together model architecture, output heads, and efficiency. BLT breaks that coupling. By making the grouping of bytes dynamic and learnable (or data-driven via entropy), BLT:

- Removes a fixed vocabulary and its language/domain biases.
- Gives you a new knob—average patch size—to trade off inference cost vs. model size/performance without blowing up embedding/output costs.
- Enables growing model capacity under a fixed inference budget, which is a practical regime for deploying larger, more capable models.

In short: patches let you move compute where information is, and that flexibility lets BLT scale more favorably in inference-budget constrained settings.

## Bottom line

BLT demonstrates a viable path to scale large models from raw bytes without a fixed tokenizer. The central leverage is patching: larger patches mean fewer heavy global-transformer steps, which cuts inference FLOPs and lets you reallocate computation to grow model size. Empirically, BLT matches tokenized baselines at compute-optimal regimes up to 8B and shows better fixed-inference scaling trends, with up to ~50% inference FLOP savings in some configurations. For practitioners concerned about inference cost and robustness to noisy or long-tail inputs, BLT’s patch-based approach is worth following.

For implementation details (entropy thresholds, hash n‑gram sizes, cross‑attention design, and the exact FLOP formulas), the paper gives the equations and hyperparameters used for the reported runs.
