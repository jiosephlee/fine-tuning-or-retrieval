\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

From Tokens to Bytes: Why Patches Scale Better

# Limitations of fixed-vocabulary tokenizers: domain sensitivity, noise brittleness, multilingual inequity, orthographic blind spots

Modern large language models rely almost universally on a preprocessing step called tokenization: a fixed-vocabulary mapping that groups input bytes into a discrete set of tokens prior to model input. While tokenizers bring practical benefits—shorter effective sequence lengths and compact output softmaxes—they also impose structural biases that the rest of the model must live with. First, tokenizers compress strings according to heuristics optimized for an anticipated distribution of text. That compression is not neutral across domains or modalities: what compresses well for one domain may split or represent strings poorly in another, which creates domain sensitivity. The paper documents this phenomenon and cites shortcomings that emerge in downstream behavior when the compression heuristics mismatch the use case (Section 1). In other words, tokenization encodes a prior about linguistic units that may be inappropriate for many downstream tasks or language families.

Second, tokenizers produce brittleness to input noise. Because tokens are discrete and learned from clean corpora, small character-level perturbations can change token boundaries or produce rare/unseen tokens, dramatically shifting model behavior. The BLT study finds that models trained on raw bytes show marked robustness advantages under several character-level noising regimes (Section 7). These robustness gains directly trace to the fact that the model retains direct access to byte-level structure rather than depending on a fixed token inventory.

Third, tokenizers produce multilingual inequity. A fixed vocabulary learned from data mixtures will naturally favor languages and scripts that are frequent in the training mixture. Languages with different orthographies, richer morphology, or rare grapheme inventories will be represented with smaller or more fragmented tokens. This imbalance translates into performance gaps across languages. The paper highlights that raw byte modeling reduces certain long-tail generalization issues in low-resource translation and multilingual tasks (Section 7.1), because bytes provide a uniform substrate across scripts.

Fourth, token-based models are orthographically blind in subtle ways. Although tokenizers implicitly know about spellings (a token has a textual form), the trained model does not see the underlying bytes directly and cannot easily perform character-level manipulations or reason about spelling, phonology, and character composition. Empirically, BLT outperforms token-based models on the CUTE benchmark and grapheme-to-phoneme tasks, demonstrating that explicit byte representations enable character-level capabilities that tokenized models struggle to learn (Section 7.1).

Together, these limitations motivate exploring tokenizer-free approaches: by operating directly on bytes, a model can avoid some distributional biases of tokenization, be intrinsically robust to character-level noise, treat all scripts uniformly, and retain explicit orthographic information for tasks that demand it.

# Compute and modeling challenges of byte-level LMs (longer sequences, cost distribution)

The most immediate technical objection to byte-level modeling is computational cost. A byte-level representation increases sequence length relative to token-based inputs: average token lengths in the Llama tokenizers are on the order of several bytes (e.g., 3.7 to 4.4 bytes), whereas a byte-level model must process each byte explicitly. Naively applying a standard Transformer to raw bytes therefore multiplies the number of positions and leads to prohibitively large computational and memory requirements, a fact noted in prior work and summarized in Section 1.

Crucially, at scale the Transformer computation is dominated not by attention complexity with respect to sequence length, but by the cost of the feed-forward networks (FFNs) and the per-position MLPs inside each transformer layer. These dense layers are applied to every input position, so increased sequence length multiplies their computational burden directly. Earlier byte-level approaches tackled the attention bottleneck (e.g., more efficient attention or attention-free architectures), but that does not solve the dominant cost coming from FFNs across many byte positions. The paper emphasizes this point: attention optimizations help small models, but at scale they don't eliminate the per-byte cost of large FFN layers (Section 1).

Another modeling challenge is how compute should be allocated across positions. Tokenization imposes a uniform per-token compute budget: every token gets processed equivalently by the model's layers. But the information content (predictive difficulty) of bytes is not uniform—early bytes in a word or in a new semantic unit tend to be higher-entropy and more informative, while many following bytes are highly predictable. Uniform allocation can therefore waste compute on predictable bytes and under-allocate for hard predictions. This mismatch between information density and compute allocation is a central inefficiency that the BLT approach addresses.

Finally, there are practical constraints during generation: any segmentation method used by a byte-level model must be incremental—patching decisions for the prefix must not rely on future bytes that are not yet generated. Tokenizers like BPE are not incremental in this sense because the same prefix may tokenize differently depending on how it continues. This property complicates generating with a tokenizer and motivates a patching design that guarantees incremental segmentation (Section 2.4).

# Key idea: dynamic, learned grouping of bytes into patches as primary compute units

The core idea of BLT is to replace static tokens with dynamically-sized, learnable patches as the fundamental units the global model consumes. A patching function f_p maps a byte sequence x = {x_i}_{i=1}^n into a shorter sequence of patches p = {p_j}_{j=1}^m where m < n. The design goal is that m, the number of patches, controls how often the expensive global model is invoked: fewer patches mean fewer passes through the large latent transformer and hence lower overall flops. Importantly, patches are not drawn from a fixed vocabulary; instead, arbitrary groups of bytes are encoded into latent patch representations via lightweight encoder and decoder modules. This architecture avoids the embedding-table size vs. step-count trade-off inherent to tokenizers (Section 2 and 2.4).

There are several concrete patching strategies discussed in the paper. The simplest is strided patching: fixed-size groups of k bytes per patch. This is easy to implement and permits straightforward flop control by changing k, but it cannot adapt to contextual variation and can split meaningful linguistic units inconsistently (Section 2.1). Space patching creates patch boundaries at space-like bytes; it aligns with word boundaries and therefore often concentrates more global compute on word-initial (higher-entropy) bytes. Space patching equalizes the segmentation of the same words across contexts but cannot vary patch size continuously (Section 2.2). The most flexible approach is entropy patching: a small byte-level entropy model estimates next-byte uncertainty H(x_i) and identifies boundaries where uncertainty crosses a threshold (global) or increases relative to the previous byte (approximate monotonicity). Entropy patching yields variable patch sizes that tend to allocate more compute where the next-byte entropy is high—exactly the desideratum mentioned earlier (Section 2.3).

Entropy patching is computed in a lightweight preprocessing step during data loading. It respects incremental patching: boundary decisions for the prefix are made without access to future bytes, enabling generation. The entropy model used for patching can be small (e.g., 100M parameters with a sliding window), and the paper shows that varying the entropy-model capacity and receptive field affects performance with diminishing returns beyond certain sizes (Section 6).

By grouping bytes into patches that concentrate information more evenly across patch positions, BLT reduces the number of positions the expensive global transformer must process. Because patches are encoded into latent representations rather than chosen from a static vocabulary, BLT avoids large embedding tables and preserves access to byte-level signals via its encoder/decoder pathways.

# BLT’s three-block design: local byte models + global latent transformer

Architecturally, BLT splits work across three blocks: a lightweight local encoder, a large global latent transformer, and a lightweight local decoder (Section 3). This separation of roles is central to its efficiency and flexibility.

The Local Encoder \mathcal{E} is a small transformer stack (l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}) that maps raw byte embeddings into patch representations p_j. The encoder operates locally with a block-causal attention mask over a fixed window w_{\mathcal{E}} so that each byte attends to a limited preceding context. Crucially, the encoder interleaves transformer layers with a multi-headed cross-attention mechanism that pools byte representations into patch queries. For each patch p_j, an initial pooled query P_{0,j} is formed (for example by max-pooling or other pooling functions) and then refined via cross-attention to the bytes belonging to that patch. This cross-attention follows the Perceiver-style input-attention design but with variable-length latent queries that correspond to patches rather than a fixed latent set (Section 3.2 and 3.2.2). To enrich byte representations before pooling, the encoder uses hash n-gram embeddings: for each byte position i it constructs n-grams g_{i,n} for n=3..8, hashes them into a fixed-size embedding table E_n^{hash}, and adds these embeddings to the base byte embedding x_i. The combined embedding e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^8 E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})) provides richer local context that helps the encoder produce expressive patch latents (Section 3.2.1).

The Latent Global Transformer \mathcal{G} is the large autoregressive model (l_{\mathcal{G}} layers) that runs over the sequence of patch representations. It uses a block-causal attention mask that restricts attention to patches up to and including the current patch within a document, so the transformer models long-range dependencies across patches but at a reduced step count relative to raw bytes. This global model accounts for the majority of training and inference flops; therefore, reducing how often it is invoked (i.e., increasing patch size where possible) directly saves compute (Section 3.1).

The Local Decoder \mathcal{D} maps outputs o_j of the global transformer back to byte-level continuations. It is another lightweight transformer stack (l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}) that uses cross-attention where bytes are queries and patch representations are key/values. The decoder predicts raw bytes autoregressively and reconstructs continuations at the byte level. The encoder and decoder design ensures the model retains access to byte-level structure even though the global transformer operates on compressed patch latents (Section 3.3).

From a computational accounting standpoint, the paper computes FLOPs per byte for BLT by summing Transformer-FLOPs for the global transformer applied per patch, the local encoder and decoder transformers applied per their respective byte or window lengths, and the cross-attention FLOPs in both encoder and decoder. The key parameter that controls global-flop contribution is the average patch size n_p (Section 4.6). By increasing n_p, the per-byte cost attributable to the global transformer decreases, freeing compute budget to scale the global model depth/width instead.

# Main contributions: flop-controlled parity with token models, inference efficiency gains, fixed-inference scaling via patch size, robustness improvements

The BLT paper reports several concrete contributions that follow from the design above.

1) Flop-controlled parity with tokenization-based models. Through careful, flop-controlled experiments up to 8B parameters and training budgets spanning optimal and beyond-optimal regimes, BLT demonstrates that byte-level models can match the language-modeling scaling trends of tokenization-based baselines (notably Llama 3) when using its architecture and dynamic patching. This is the first time a byte-level Transformer architecture reaches matching performance at such scales (Section 5.1). Architectural elements (cross-attention, n-gram hashes) and dynamic entropy patching are essential to close the gap that simple static patching methods had left.

2) Inference efficiency gains. Because average patch size n_p is a tunable property of BLT and because the global transformer is the major contributor to flops, BLT can trade off patchiness against model capacity. The paper shows that with larger average patch sizes (e.g., 6 or 8 bytes per patch) BLT can reduce inference FLOPs by up to ~50% compared to tokenized models, while maintaining comparable performance by reallocating saved compute to increase global transformer size (Sections 5.1 and 5.3). This dynamic selection of longer patches in predictable contexts is what yields these runtime wins.

3) Fixed-inference scaling via patch size. BLT unlocks a new scaling axis: for a fixed inference-flop budget, one can increase the global model size while simultaneously increasing patch size so that the number of expensive global steps falls and the total inference cost remains constant. The paper demonstrates fixed-inference-flop scaling experiments (Section 5.3) that show BLT models achieve steeper scaling trends than token-based models when both model size and average patch length are increased. In essence, patches let you trade steps for parameters in a way that tokenizers cannot without paying steep embedding-table costs.

4) Robustness and improved long-tail modeling. Operating directly on bytes yields practical gains in robustness to character-level noise and in character-level tasks. Empirically, BLT outperforms tokenized baselines on noised prompt evaluations, grapheme-to-phoneme transcription, the CUTE orthographic benchmark, and low-resource machine translation (Section 7.1). These improvements reflect the model's access to native byte structure and its patching strategy that concentrates compute where prediction uncertainty is highest. Moreover, BLT shows better modeling of long-tail byte sequences, an advantage that improves generalization on many lower-resource language pairs.

A final contribution is a practical workflow to "byte-ify" an existing tokenizer-trained model: initializing BLT's global transformer with a pretrained Llama 3.1 checkpoint and fine-tuning with reduced learning rate for global weights. This approach can accelerate convergence and inherit strengths of large tokenizer-trained models while gaining byte-level robustness (Section 7.2).

Collectively, these contributions point to a new design point for large language models: discard the fixed-vocabulary token abstraction, represent raw bytes, and let a lightweight encoder/decoder plus a large latent transformer operate on dynamically-sized patches informed by byte-level uncertainty. The result is a model that can match tokenized baselines in modeling quality, while being more inference-efficient, more robust to noise and orthographic phenomena, and offering a new dimension of scaling via patch size.

Background: Tokenization, Bytes, and Compute

# Subword tokenization (BPE) vs bytes: vocabularies, compression bias, output head size tradeoffs

Modern large language models (LLMs) typically do not operate directly on raw bytes. Instead they first apply a subword tokenization step (commonly byte-pair encoding, or BPE) that maps variable-length byte sequences into discrete tokens drawn from a fixed vocabulary. This design introduces two important structural elements to the model pipeline. First, the tokenizer compresses byte sequences into fewer discrete tokens, which shortens the sequence length that the core Transformer must process. Second, the tokenizer defines a fixed vocabulary: every token corresponds to an entry in an embedding table and the final output projection maps model hidden states to logits over that finite vocabulary.

This design brings both benefits and costs. The benefit is efficiency through compression: larger average token sizes reduce the number of Transformer steps per input, saving compute in the recurrent processing performed by the model. The costs are several-fold. Because tokenization is a heuristic—constructed prior to training and fixed thereafter—it imposes an inductive bias on how text is grouped and compressed. The paper refers to this as compression bias: two identical byte substrings can be grouped differently depending on context or tokenizer vocabulary, and rare or multilingual orthography can be disadvantaged. Tokenization can therefore introduce sensitivity to domain and modality, reduce robustness to noisy or out-of-vocabulary inputs, and lead to inequities across languages with different orthographic properties.

There is also a tradeoff between vocabulary size and compute that is unique to tokenized models. Increasing vocabulary size tends to increase average token length (fewer tokens per byte sequence) and so reduces the number of steps the Transformer runs. But a larger vocabulary also increases the dimensionality of the model’s input and output layers: the embedding table grows and the final output projection must produce logits for more classes, which increases parameter count and some compute costs. The paper gives a concrete example: to increase average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes, Llama 3 increased its embedding table size by roughly 4× compared to Llama 2. This coupling constrains how much one can change token size without paying substantial costs elsewhere in the model, limiting a tokenization-based model’s flexibility to trade off step count versus projection/embedding overhead.

In contrast, byte-level approaches reason directly over individual bytes, avoiding a fixed vocabulary. Patching — grouping bytes dynamically into “patches” — decouples the unit of computation from a static discrete vocabulary. Arbitrary sequences of bytes are mapped into latent patch representations via learned encoder/decoder modules rather than a fixed token embedding. That removes the rigid vocabulary-vs-step tradeoff: average patch size can be varied to change the number of Transformer steps without enlarging an explicit output vocabulary. It also provides direct access to byte-level features, which the model can exploit for robustness and character-level reasoning.

# Why attention isn’t the only cost: FFN dominance at scale

A common intuition is that attention mechanisms dominate the compute cost of Transformers because attention complexity grows quadratically in sequence length. While attention cost matters at short-to-moderate scales or when sequence lengths are very large, the paper emphasizes an important practical point for large-scale LLMs: at scale, the dominant compute cost shifts to the large feed-forward network (FFN) layers (a.k.a. MLPs) inside each Transformer block. These FFN layers are applied per token (or per patch) and have large hidden dimensionalities; when model size grows, FFN parameters and their associated flops grow significantly and can dominate total compute.

Because FFNs are executed for every processing step (every token or patch), reducing the number of steps directly reduces the number of FFN evaluations and therefore yields substantial compute savings. This is the core motivation for patching: by grouping multiple bytes into a single patch, the global latent Transformer runs fewer FFN layers overall. Thus, optimizing the number of steps is an effective lever for reducing total computational cost, even if attention remains non-negligible. The asymmetry—attention can be optimized with sparse or approximate methods, but FFN layers remain dense per-step operations—means that reducing the step count (e.g., via patches) can yield larger practical gains than optimizations focused only on attention.

The paper uses this observation to justify why patching methods that reduce the number of steps sent to the large global Transformer are an effective strategy to make byte-level models practical at scale. Because FFN flops scale with the number of processed positions and the FFN widths, dynamic patching that reduces positions where the global model is invoked yields proportional reductions in the dominant compute component.

# Perplexity vs Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) for tokenizer-agnostic evaluation

Perplexity is the standard metric for evaluating autoregressive language models, but it assumes a fixed tokenization. Per-token cross-entropy and perplexity depend on the defined token units, so comparing perplexities across models that use different tokenizers (or between tokenized and byte-level models) is not meaningful. To evaluate tokenizer-agnostic models on a common scale, the paper adopts Bits-Per-Byte (BPB).

Bits-Per-Byte is computed by taking the sum of cross-entropy losses over the predicted units for a byte sequence, converting nats to bits via a factor of \( \ln 2 \), and normalizing by the number of bytes. Formally, for a byte sequence \( \mathbf{x} \) of length \( n_{\text{bytes}} \), they define
\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}},
\]
where \( \mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x}) \) is the sum of cross-entropy losses (in nats) assigned by the model over the sequence. BPB measures the average information content (in bits) per byte under the model distribution, providing a tokenizer-independent way to compare the uncertainty assigned to raw byte data by different architectures.

Using BPB allows fair comparison between BPE-tokenized models and byte-level (or patch-level) models because it normalizes for the underlying raw data unit (bytes) rather than the model’s internal tokenization. This metric is especially useful in the paper’s scaling experiments and ablations where models with different patching schemes and tokenizers are compared on the same raw byte data.

# FLOPs as a compute budget: forward/backward assumptions and components

To compare models under controlled compute budgets, the paper uses FLOPs (floating-point operations) as the primary measure of computational cost. FLOPs are counted across the core Transformer operations and related modules, and the authors make explicit assumptions about how forward and backward passes contribute to the total training cost.

They follow the standard approach from prior scaling-law work (e.g., Chinchilla) and compute flops for the following Transformer components: the FFN layers, the projection matrices used in self-attention (q, k, v, and output projections), the attention score computations, and the output projection for vocabulary logits. Importantly, they assume the input embedding lookup is implemented as a zero-flop operation (efficient lookup), rather than accounting for a dense multiplication for embeddings. For backward passes, they estimate the backwards pass to have twice the flops of the forward pass, in line with prior work that treats backward computation as a multiple of forward computation.

For BLT specifically, the per-byte flop accounting sums the contributions of several modules: the local encoder Transformer, the global latent Transformer, the local decoder Transformer, and the encoder/decoder cross-attention blocks. Because different modules operate over sequences of different lengths (bytes vs patches), the flops are adjusted for the context length each module sees. The paper uses notation where \(n_{ctx}\) is the sequence length in bytes and \(n_p\) is the average patch size (bytes per patch). The global Transformer is applied to the patch sequence and so its flops are roughly proportional to \(n_{ctx} / n_p\), whereas local encoder/decoder modules are applied over byte windows. Cross-attention blocks incur additional flops that depend on the number of queries and the number of key/value positions; the paper introduces a ratio \(r\) to denote the queries-to-key/values ratio used in cross-attention flop expressions.

Formally, the paper summarizes BLT flops (per byte) conceptually as a sum of terms like:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0)}{n_p}
+ \text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256)
+ \text{CrossAttn.FL}(\text{encoder params}) + \text{CrossAttn.FL}(\text{decoder params}).
\end{aligned}
\]
(See the paper Appendix for exact flop formulas for Transformer and Cross-Attention FLOPs.) This accounting makes explicit how changing patch size \(n_p\), encoder/decoder widths and layer counts, or average context \(n_{ctx}\) affects total FLOPs.

Using FLOPs as the currency enables flop-controlled comparisons: models can be trained or evaluated after consuming a fixed number of FLOPs, which is crucial to the paper’s claims that BLT can match or exceed performance of tokenized LLMs while sometimes using fewer inference FLOPs.

# Context length control and fairness in comparisons

When comparing different patching schemes or tokenizers, it is critical to control context length so that no model unintentionally benefits from having access to more raw bytes in its receptive field. The paper notes that patches change the effective number of processing steps and therefore can change how much raw-context a model actually ingests per batch. To avoid confounding results, they explicitly equalize the total number of bytes processed per batch across patching schemes when evaluating and training.

Concretely, if a patching scheme increases average patch size (fewer patches per fixed byte budget), they reduce the sequence length (in patches or tokens) so that the expected number of bytes per batch remains constant. For their Llama-2 experiments they used an 8k byte context; for the BLT-1T dataset experiments they used a 16k byte context while holding the average batch size to 16M bytes. Batches are packed by patches to avoid padding steps in the expensive latent transformer; this packing also ensures each batch has the same number of patches for efficiency. They additionally pad and truncate byte sequences in training to bounded lengths (e.g., pad/truncate to 12k/24k bytes on some datasets) to avoid memory spikes from unusually large patches.

This equalization ensures fairness: models compared under the same FLOP budget and the same bytes-per-batch are not advantaged simply because one architecture saw more raw data per update. It also isolates the effects of patching and model architecture from trivial context-size effects, allowing the paper’s scaling and robustness claims to be attributed to the design choices (dynamic patching, local encoders/decoders, cross-attention) rather than to differences in how much context the models used.

Taken together, these foundational considerations—how tokenization compresses and biases inputs, why FFN flops dominate large models, how to compare models via BPB, how to measure compute via FLOPs, and how to control context length—are the framework the paper uses to introduce the Byte Latent Transformer. They explain both why patching is necessary to make byte-level modeling practical at scale and how to evaluate architectures fairly across tokenization regimes.

The Patching Paradigm

# Formal definition of a patching function and patch size

A patching function converts a raw byte sequence into a shorter sequence of groups, or "patches", that serve as the primary units of computation for a model. Formally, let a byte sequence be $\pmb{x} = \{x_i \mid i = 1,\ldots,n\}$ where each $x_i$ is a byte and $n$ is the total number of bytes. A patching function $f_p$ maps this sequence to a sequence of $m$ patches $\pmb{p} = \{p_j \mid j = 1,\ldots,m\}$ with $m < n$. Concretely, $f_p$ can be represented as an assignment of a binary decision to each byte position indicating whether a new patch begins at that position. That is, $f_p$ determines for each index $i$ a bit $b_i \in \{0,1\}$ where $b_i=1$ marks the start of a new patch and the sequence of these decisions induces the patch segmentation.

Given this segmentation, each patch $p_j$ corresponds to a contiguous subsequence of bytes from the original sequence. The average size of a patch—what we will call the patch size—is the expected number of bytes per patch on the data distribution of interest. If on average there are $m$ patches in a sequence of $n$ bytes, the average patch size is approximately $n/m$. Throughout modeling and cost analysis, this average patch size is the key quantity: it controls how many steps the model's main Transformer (the "global" model in BLT) executes per input of fixed byte length.

Operationally, a patching function can be implemented in several ways. It might be a fixed stride that deterministically groups every $k$ bytes into a patch, it might be rule-based (for example, creating a boundary at whitespace-like bytes), or it might be data-driven and dynamic (for example, using estimated next-byte entropies to decide where boundaries should fall). Regardless of implementation, the patching function is a preprocessing step that reduces a byte-level sequence into a patch-level sequence on which most of the model's computation is performed.

# Patches vs tokens: dynamic groupings without a fixed vocabulary

Patches and tokens are both ways to reduce raw byte sequences to higher-level units, but they differ in two fundamental aspects that change modeling trade-offs.

First, tokens arise from a fixed vocabulary determined prior to training. A tokenizer like BPE constructs a finite set of byte-groups (the vocabulary) and every input is segmented into a sequence of vocabulary entries. Once chosen, the vocabulary is fixed: the model works entirely at the level of these discrete token identifiers and typically has no direct access to the original byte values inside each token. This fixed-vocabulary design couples average unit size to vocabulary size and to the model's output projection: increasing average token length implies increasing vocabulary size and hence a larger embedding/output matrix.

Patches, by contrast, are dynamic, contextual, and vocabulary-free in the BLT paradigm. A patching function can produce variable-length byte groups that are not limited to items from a predetermined finite set. In BLT, arbitrary byte sequences are mapped to latent patch representations via learned encoder and decoder modules; the latent representations are continuous vectors produced on the fly rather than discrete indices from a fixed table. The absence of a fixed vocabulary decouples the average size of a unit of computation (patch size) from the output projection size or embedding-table trade-offs that tokenizers must confront. This decoupling permits choosing much larger average units (patches) to reduce the number of Transformer steps while avoiding the large embedding/output matrices that a corresponding token vocabulary increase would require.

Second, patches preserve access to underlying byte features via the lightweight encoder and decoder: the model can inspect byte-level representations when constructing patch latents and when decoding back into bytes. Tokens typically hide constituent bytes from the model after tokenization. This direct byte access enables BLT to reason about sub-token structure, which improves character-level robustness and long-tail generalization on bytes that tokenizer vocabularies handle poorly.

# Incremental patching property and implications for generation

When generating autoregressively, decisions about whether the current position is a patch boundary must be made without knowing future bytes. This constraint is formalized by the incremental patching property. A patching scheme $f_p$ satisfies incremental patching if, for any position $i$,

$$
f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i},
$$

meaning the segmentation decisions up to position $i$ depend only on the prefix $\pmb{x}_{<i}$ and not on the future suffix. Incremental patching is required for causal generation because the model cannot condition on yet-to-be-generated bytes to decide whether a patch should end or continue.

Many rule-based patchers (fixed stride, whitespace-based) satisfy incremental patching. In contrast, many common tokenizers like BPE do not satisfy this property: a given prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation, so $f_p$ induced by BPE is not prefix-consistent; thus BPE is not an incremental patching scheme in the strict sense. (One could augment tokenization with explicit boundary markers to enforce incremental behavior, but that alters sequence length and the modeling trade-offs.)

The incremental requirement influences both model design and runtime generation. For BLT, the entropy-based patching approach (or whitespace-based patching) operates using information available at the prefix to decide boundaries. The practical implication is that at generation time BLT can determine whether to invoke the heavier global latent Transformer at a given step based solely on the bytes it has already generated, enabling correct causal generation and consistent compute scheduling.

# How patch size governs training and inference steps (and cost)

The core computation cost of an autoregressive Transformer-based model is proportional to the number of steps the main Transformer executes. In BLT, the main Transformer—the latent global Transformer—operates over patches, not bytes. Therefore, for a fixed byte-level sequence length, the number of Transformer steps equals the number of patches. Consequently, the patch size (average bytes per patch) directly controls the computational load: larger patch sizes imply fewer patches and fewer global Transformer steps per byte of input, lowering both training and inference FLOPs.

This relationship can be summarized intuitively: suppose a model must process $n_{\text{ctx}}$ bytes of context. If the average patch size is $s$ bytes, the model runs the global Transformer for about $n_{\text{ctx}}/s$ steps. The global Transformer typically dominates FLOPs for large models because its feed-forward networks are wide and expensive. Thus even modest increases in average patch size can produce substantial reductions in per-byte computational cost. The paper provides an explicit per-byte FLOPs accounting for BLT that sums contributions from the global Transformer, local encoder and decoder Transformers, and cross-attention blocks. In that accounting, terms that scale inversely with the patch size (i.e., those proportional to the number of patches) dominate when the global model is large, so patch size becomes the principal lever for inference and training cost.

However, the patch size is not a free optimization independent of model quality. Coarsening patches too aggressively (making them very large) risks losing granularity for hard prediction points and may make it harder for the model to represent fine-grained uncertainty and variation inside patches. Tokenizers face an analogous trade-off: increasing token granularity reduces sequence length but inflates vocabulary and output-projection costs. BLT's advantage is that patch size can be increased without inflating output layers, allowing a different point in the efficiency-quality trade space.

Practically, when comparing models with different patch sizes, the experiments ensure fair comparisons by equalizing the expected number of bytes per batch so that models see the same byte-context in expectation. This prevents larger-patch models from obtaining an unfair advantage through effectively larger context windows. It also reveals how changing patch size reallocates FLOPs between the global Transformer and the byte-level encoder/decoder: larger patches reduce global Transformer steps but leave encoder/decoder overhead largely unaffected, so there is a sweet spot where saved global FLOPs can be reallocated to grow the global Transformer's capacity while maintaining constant inference cost.

# Why dynamic allocation of compute aligns with information density

A motivating intuition behind BLT is that information content across a byte sequence is not uniform. Some bytes or local regions are high-entropy prediction points—places where the next byte is genuinely uncertain and therefore deserve more model capacity and compute—while many other bytes are highly predictable and can be handled with cheaper, local computation. Tokenization imposes a coarse, static allocation of compute: each token receives the same Transformer treatment regardless of its contextual difficulty. BLT aims to allocate compute adaptively by constructing patches so that the model's global (expensive) Transformer is invoked more often at high-information boundaries and less often in predictable regions, thereby aligning compute with information density.

Entropy patching operationalizes this idea. A small byte-level autoregressive entropy model estimates the next-byte predictive distribution $p_e(x_i \mid \pmb{x}_{<i})$ and computes its entropy:

$$
H(x_i) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}),
$$

where $\mathcal{V}$ is the byte vocabulary. Given these per-byte entropies, patch boundaries are placed at positions indicative of high uncertainty. The paper explores two thresholding strategies: a global threshold that places a boundary when $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, and an approximate monotonicity constraint that places a boundary when the entropy increases relative to the previous step, i.e., when $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$. Both strategies attempt to create patches whose internal bytes have relatively uniform, generally decreasing entropy, so that the initial bytes of a patch (which tend to be the high-entropy ones) prompt a global Transformer step while subsequent, easier bytes stay inside the patch and incur only local encoder/decoder work.

This dynamic allocation has several practical advantages. When data is predictable—for example, long runs of whitespace, repeated tokens, or repetitive structured content—the entropy model will produce lower entropy estimates, enabling larger patches and thus fewer global Transformer steps. Conversely, in complex regions (new sentences, code tokens with unusual characters, rare words), entropy patching will produce more, smaller patches, allocating the global Transformer more often where it matters. Empirically, BLT demonstrates that this adaptive scheme yields both computational savings (fewer inference FLOPs on predictable text) and qualitative benefits (improved robustness and better handling of long-tail byte phenomena) compared to static patching and tokenizer approaches.

In summary, a good patching scheme for autoregressive byte modeling should satisfy three practical desiderata: (1) incremental decisionability so generation can be causal, (2) the ability to vary patch size so that compute can be scaled with information density, and (3) contextual consistency so similar contexts are grouped similarly (avoiding splitting the same word inconsistently). Entropy-based dynamic patching in BLT is designed to meet these desiderata: it is incremental by construction, it produces variable-length, context-sensitive patches that adapt to local uncertainty, and it produces more consistent groupings than fixed stride methods because boundaries derive from learned distributional properties of the data rather than an arbitrary stride.

Collectively, the patching paradigm reframes the basic unit of autoregressive computation from a fixed discrete token to a flexible, continuous patch latent. This reframing allows a model to better align computation with the variable information content of byte sequences, enabling a new trade space for scaling models and inference under fixed compute budgets.

Patching Methods: From Heuristics to Entropy

# Strided patching (fixed K): simplicity vs misallocation of compute and inconsistent segmentations

Strided patching is the most straightforward patching scheme examined in the paper: the input byte sequence is partitioned into contiguous, fixed-length blocks of $k$ bytes each. Formally, the patching function $f_p$ simply marks every $k$-th byte as the start of a new patch, producing patches of exactly $k$ bytes (except possibly the last patch). This deterministic, stride-based approach is appealing because it is trivial to implement both at training and inference time, and it provides a direct knob for controlling average patch size and therefore computational cost. If you want to reduce inference flops you increase $k$; if you want to increase modeling granularity you decrease $k$. The correspondence between $k$ and flop cost is immediate and predictable.

However, the paper highlights important practical downsides that limit strided patching's effectiveness in large-scale byte-level language modeling. The fundamental problem is that strided patching does not take context or information content into account. Every patch receives the same amount of downstream compute from the global latent transformer, irrespective of the actual difficulty or entropy of the prediction that patch boundary implies. This uniform allocation of compute leads to two failure modes.

First, misallocation of compute: easy regions of the input—such as long runs of whitespace, indentation in code, or predictable suffixes in words—will still cause a full latent transformer step when they happen to align with a stride boundary. That wastes flops on low-information content. Conversely, highly informative regions (mathematical expressions, rare multi-byte sequences, code tokens with concentrated semantics) may be split across strides in a way that requires more latent transformer invocations to properly model the same amount of information, effectively under-allocating compute where it is most needed.

Second, inconsistent, non-contextual segmentation: a given linguistic unit such as a word or multi-byte symbol can be split differently depending on its position relative to the fixed stride. The same word occurring at different offsets will be decomposed into different patch boundaries, leading to inconsistent representations for semantically identical substrings. This harms model generalization because the encoder and global transformer cannot rely on stable boundaries for frequent constructs.

Empirically, the paper shows that static (strided) patching underperforms more context-aware schemes: while strided patching can match some baselines at small scales, it lags behind state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at compute-optimal regimes and larger scales. Thus, while strided patching is attractive for simplicity and clear flop control, its inability to adapt to data complexity makes it suboptimal for high-performance byte-level LLMs.

# Space patching: leveraging whitespace boundaries; pros and limitations across languages/domain

Space patching improves upon strided patching by using a simple, linguistically informed heuristic: create a new patch after any "space-like" byte. In the paper, space-like bytes are defined as bytes that are not a Latin character, not a digit, and not a UTF-8 continuation byte; additionally each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. This rule means the latent transformer is invoked approximately once per word (or other token-like unit defined by non-space-like runs), so the model naturally aligns compute steps with word boundaries in many languages and domains.

The benefits of this approach are intuitive. Linguistically, word boundaries are natural places where information density tends to spike: the first byte of a word (or the first few bytes) typically reduce a large set of possibilities for the continuation. By allocating a latent transformer step to each word, space patching concentrates compute on the harder predictions (e.g., selecting the initial character of a word or predicting the first character of a completion). It also yields more consistent segmentation of repeated words or substrings—unlike fixed-stride patching, the same word will usually be patched identically wherever it occurs, making it easier for the model to learn stable subword patterns.

Despite these advantages, the paper documents limitations that prevent space patching from being a general solution. First, the whitespace heuristic is language- and domain-dependent: some languages (or scripts) do not use space-like delimiters in the same way as Latin-script languages, and many domains (e.g., compact binary blobs, certain programming constructs, or markup with no whitespace separation) may not align well with space-based boundaries. Second, space patching lacks tunability: it cannot vary patch size continuously to trade off inference flops and modeling granularity because boundaries are determined by the data, not by a tunable threshold. As a result, space patching cannot easily control average patch size to meet a desired inference cost in the same direct way that strided patching can.

Empirically, space patching is a strong simple baseline and nearly matches more sophisticated dynamic schemes on some domains. But the paper shows that space patching still falls short of the best performance when compared with entropy-driven dynamic patching combined with architectural improvements (e.g., cross-attention and n-gram hash embeddings). Thus space patching is a robust heuristic that helps much more than fixed strides but cannot address all languages and domains nor provide the flexible compute-control that entropy patching enables.

# Entropy patching: training a small byte LM, computing next-byte entropy

Entropy patching is the paper's central innovation for determining patch boundaries in a data-driven way. The idea is to estimate the next-byte uncertainty (entropy) at each position using a small, efficient byte-level autoregressive language model, and to place patch boundaries where the entropy indicates a high amount of information or unpredictability. Intuitively, this concentrates latent transformer steps on the bytes that are hardest to predict given the preceding context, which should be where the model most benefits from extra compute and capacity.

Concretely, the authors train an entropy model (a compact byte-level transformer) on the same training distribution used for the full model. The entropy for the next byte at position $i$ is computed from the entropy model's predictive distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ as

\[
H(x_i) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]

where $H(x_i)$ is the Shannon entropy (the paper uses the standard negative-sum convention). Higher $H(x_i)$ means greater unpredictability for the next byte; lower $H(x_i)$ means the next byte is more predictable.

Using these entropy estimates, the patching function $f_p$ marks patch boundaries at positions where the entropy behavior indicates a new informational unit. This produces patches that are contextualized: a repeated or predictable sequence (for example, a long run of a repeated character or a repeated phrase) will be grouped into larger patches, while positions where unpredictability spikes (for example, the start of a new word, a punctuation-driven transition, or a rare symbol) will trigger a new patch. Because the entropy model operates on raw bytes, patch boundaries are learned in a tokenizer-free manner and can adapt to any script or domain.

The paper emphasizes that training the entropy model is lightweight relative to the full BLT training: they employ a compact transformer (e.g., 100M parameters, 14 layers, sliding-window attention of 512 bytes) in most experiments. This small model is sufficient to yield useful entropy estimates; the authors also explore smaller/ larger entropy models and find diminishing returns beyond a certain size (around 50M parameters in their ablations). When the entropy model's receptive field is small enough, they note it can even be encoded as an efficient lookup table for further speed.

Entropy patching addresses the two main shortcomings of strided patching: it dynamically allocates compute to high-information regions and produces consistent contextual groupings of bytes for the same logical units. Relative to space patching, entropy patching is domain- and language-agnostic and provides a tunable handle (via thresholds described below) to control patch sizes for flop budgeting.

# Global threshold vs approximate monotonicity constraints for boundary detection

The paper studies two practical algorithms for translating next-byte entropies into patch boundaries: a global threshold rule and an approximate monotonicity (relative) rule. Both are computed from the per-byte entropy sequence $H(x_t)$ produced by the entropy model, but they differ in how they detect "high" entropies indicative of boundary positions.

The Global Threshold rule is the simpler of the two. It marks a boundary at position $t$ whenever

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]

for some global threshold $\theta_g$ chosen so that the resulting average patch size meets a desired target on the training data mix. This rule is easy to implement and tune: by increasing or decreasing $\theta_g$ you increase or decrease the average patch size. The global threshold is particularly straightforward when the entropy model produces well-calibrated entropies and the underlying data distribution is relatively homogeneous.

The Approximate Monotonicity (relative) rule instead looks for local increases in entropy relative to the previous byte:

\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]

for a chosen relative threshold $\theta_r$. This form captures the insight that within a coherent patch, entropy often decreases as more context is observed—early bytes are more unpredictable and as you accumulate bytes within a patch predictions get easier. Therefore, a positive jump (or smaller-than-expected drop) in entropy signals that the current position breaks the approximate monotonic decrease and likely denotes the start of a new information-rich unit. The relative rule is less sensitive to absolute calibration of entropy across different contexts and more sensitive to local changes, which can be beneficial when entropy baselines drift with varying context or domain.

Each method has trade-offs. The global threshold offers simple, direct control of average patch size, which is useful when the goal is to meet an inference-flop budget. The approximate monotonicity rule is more robust to domain-specific entropy scales and less likely to produce very long patches in repetitive contexts where absolute entropies may be low but local structure still signals boundaries.

In practice, the paper uses both methods and reports that the approximate monotonicity constraint can be less susceptible to "entropy drift" in long contexts. For large-scale runs where entropy drift was observed due to repetitive structured content (which can create progressively larger patches than desired), they reset the entropy model's context at newlines and prefer the approximate monotonicity constraint to stabilize patch sizes. The choice between these two boundary detection rules is therefore an important practical knob.

# Practicalities: preprocessing during dataloading, context resets to avoid entropy drift

Entropy-based patching is implemented as a lightweight preprocessing step applied during data loading. The key practical requirement is incremental patching: patch boundaries must be decided without looking at future bytes beyond the current prefix, because during autoregressive generation the model cannot depend on future context to decide whether a boundary has been reached. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ must satisfy the property $f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}$ so that prefix segmentation matches segmentation derived from the full sequence truncated to that prefix. Entropy patching as described in the paper is computed from the entropy model in a way that satisfies this incremental requirement because entropies depend only on prior bytes.

During training, the paper enforces additional practical constraints to keep experiments comparable across patching schemes. Because different patching schemes change the ratio of bytes to patches, the authors control the expected number of bytes in each batch so that models do not inadvertently get larger context just because patch sizes are larger. They fix the average batch byte budget (for example, 16M bytes) and adjust sequence lengths so that models with larger patch sizes get fewer patches per batch but the same expected bytes. They also pack batches of patches to avoid padding the expensive latent transformer and pad/truncate sequences to fixed maximum byte lengths (12k or 24k bytes) to avoid memory spikes from unusually large patches.

Entropy drift is a practical issue that arises when the entropy model's estimates depend on long repetitive contexts. In repeated or highly structured content (for example, multi-choice questions with repetitive choice templates), the entropy model can become overly confident in a repeating pattern, causing patch sizes to inflate beyond expectations because entropies are low over long spans. To mitigate this, the authors reset the entropy model's context at newline characters for large-scale runs. This reset breaks the accumulation of drift across long contexts and yields more stable patch lengths. Additionally, switching to the approximate monotonicity rule reduces sensitivity to absolute entropy scale and thus reduces this drift effect.

Finally, although the entropy model is an extra component, it is inexpensive relative to the full BLT training and can be trained once and used for patching in preprocessing. When the entropy model has limited receptive field, it can even be compiled into a lookup table for maximum speed. All these practical engineering choices make entropy patching a feasible and scalable preprocessing step for training and inference with BLT.

# Why BPE tokenization is not incremental patching

The paper draws an important distinction between "patches" as used in BLT and "tokens" produced by subword tokenizers such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). The core difference that bears on patching is that patches in BLT are derived dynamically from the prefix context alone and the model retains access to raw byte features; tokens from a fixed BPE vocabulary are produced by a deterministic tokenizer that can (in general) depend on the entire sequence, including future bytes.

Formally, the incremental patching property requires that the segmentation of a prefix equals the prefix of the segmentation of the full sequence:

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]

BPE tokenization does not satisfy this property: the same byte prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation. For example, an English prefix that forms the beginning of two different frequent subwords might map to different token splits depending on what follows. Because the tokenizer can look ahead into the continuation to select the longest-match or most-beneficial subword splits, token boundaries are not solely a function of the prefix. This makes BPE tokenization non-incremental in general.

This non-incrementality has two implications for BLT-style autoregressive generation. First, a tokenizer-based autoregressive model cannot determine token boundaries in a strictly prefix-only generation loop without additional mechanisms (e.g., emitting delimiter tokens or running the tokenizer on full sequences), complicating generation and making it incompatible with a strict prefix-only patching assumption. Second, because tokenizers map groups of bytes to elements of a fixed, finite vocabulary and the model operates only on these vocabulary items (without direct access to the underlying bytes), tokenizer-based models lose direct byte-level signal and cannot dynamically vary "patch size" at inference time in a continuous, threshold-driven way. Increasing average token size in tokenizers requires enlarging the vocabulary, which increases embedding table size and the model's output dimension—this is an expensive structural change, not a tunable preprocessing parameter.

By contrast, BLT patches are vocabulary-free latent groups of bytes computed incrementally from prefixes via entropy or other incremental rules, and the model retains explicit byte representations in the local encoder/decoder pipeline. This allows BLT to choose patch size dynamically to control inference flops and to preserve direct byte-level information, enabling robustness to noisy inputs and better handling of long-tail or low-resource patterns.

In summary, BPE tokenization's dependence on full-sequence tokenization and its fixed-vocabulary nature prevent it from offering the incremental, tunable behavior that BLT patching provides, motivating the paper's shift toward dynamic, entropy-driven, tokenizer-free segmentation.

BLT Architecture: Mixing Byte and Patch Information

# Overall pipeline: bytes → local encoder → patch reps → latent transformer → local decoder → bytes

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) processes raw byte sequences end-to-end, replacing a fixed tokenizer with a learned patching and latent representation pipeline. At the input, the model receives a sequence of bytes b_i (where each byte is an integer in a 256-sized alphabet). These raw bytes are first embedded into byte-level vectors and then processed by a lightweight local encoder (denoted \mathcal{E}). The encoder's role is to convert contiguous runs of bytes into compact, expressive patch representations p_j that summarize the information content of each patch. Patches are variable-length groups of bytes determined by a patching function f_p; in the BLT paper the authors emphasize entropy-based dynamic patching (and also consider space-based and fixed-stride patching as alternatives), but the architecture is agnostic to the particular patching rule as long as patches can be identified incrementally during generation.

Once the local encoder has produced a sequence of patch representations \{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}, those are fed into the Latent Global Transformer (denoted \mathcal{G}). \mathcal{G} is an autoregressive transformer that operates at the patch level: it consumes the patch representations up to the current patch and produces contextually updated patch outputs o_j. The global transformer is the heavy-weight component of BLT and is where most of the model's parameters and pretraining FLOPs reside.

After the latent transformer produces its outputs, a lightweight local decoder (denoted \mathcal{D}) maps the patch-level outputs back to a byte sequence y_i. The decoder uses cross-attention from byte positions into patch outputs to reconstruct the sequence of bytes corresponding to each patch, predicting bytes autoregressively within a patch conditioned on previously generated bytes and the patch-level context. In training, encoder and decoder cooperate with the global transformer to minimize cross-entropy over bytes; at inference, the same pipeline is used but the decision of whether to run the global transformer at a byte position depends on patch boundaries determined incrementally.

This three-part flow—local encoder, latent transformer, local decoder—creates a hierarchical computation: heavy, expressive global reasoning is invoked only at patch boundaries, while surrounding byte-level processing is handled by light-weight local models. That separation enables BLT to scale the expensive global transformer while keeping per-byte costs lower than fully byte-level Transformers.

# Dynamic invocation of the global model at patch boundaries

A core design choice in BLT is that the global latent transformer should not run on every byte but only on patch steps. Patches mark points at which the model is expected to perform the more expensive, global computation. The patching function f_p maps each byte position to an indicator of whether that byte starts a new patch; invoking \mathcal{G} is tied to those indicators. Thus the global transformer is dynamically invoked at irregular intervals that depend on the input’s segmentation into patches.

The patch boundaries are chosen so that more compute is allocated where uncertainty is higher. In entropy patching, for example, a small byte autoregressive entropy model p_e estimates the next-byte distribution and computes next-byte entropy H(x_i) = \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v|\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v|\mathbf{x}_{<i}). Boundaries are placed where H(x_i) exceeds a global threshold \theta_g or where the change in entropy H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) exceeds a relative threshold \theta_r. Thus patches are contextual and data-driven: if the next-byte prediction is easy (low entropy), bytes are grouped into longer patches and the global transformer is called less frequently; if the next-byte prediction is uncertain, patches become shorter and the global transformer is invoked more often. Space-based patching uses space-like bytes as boundaries; fixed-stride patching uses a constant K. Regardless of the scheme, the decision whether to invoke \mathcal{G} at a given step must be incremental (i.e., based only on \mathbf{x}_{<i}), because generation cannot peek at future bytes.

By making the invocation of the global model dynamic and dependent on local uncertainty, BLT reallocates expensive computation to the sections of the sequence that need it most. This is in contrast to tokenized models that allocate a roughly constant amount of compute per token irrespective of local predictability.

# Block-causal attention in the global model and byte/patch-level masking

The Latent Global Transformer uses a block-causal attention mask: it is autoregressive at the patch level and, for each patch output o_j, attention can access the representations of all patches up to and including patch j within the same document. That is, attention in \mathcal{G} is causal across patches but treats each patch as an atomic step. This block-causal structure supports efficient autoregressive computation at the patch granularity while preserving causal conditioning on earlier content.

At the byte level, both the local encoder and local decoder use attention masks appropriate to their roles. The local encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask over bytes where each byte attends to a fixed window of w_{\mathcal{E}} preceding bytes (a fixed window may cross dynamic patch boundaries but not document boundaries). This choice keeps the encoder lightweight: it provides sufficient local context for encoding bytes into patch representations without incurring the quadratic cost of full-sequence attention on every byte.

The encoder and decoder cross-attention modules implement masked attention between bytes and patch latents. In the encoder cross-attention, each patch query attends only to the bytes that belong to that patch; keys and values are projections of byte-level hidden states h_{l-1,i} and the query is a pooled summary for the patch. In the decoder cross-attention the roles are reversed: byte positions (queries) attend to the patch outputs (keys/values) to reconstruct bytes. These attention masks are dynamic because patches vary in length: during cross-attention each query is explicitly constrained to the relevant set of keys/values (the bytes in the patch for encoder cross-attention, or the patches for decoder cross-attention). Implementationally, BLT uses Flex Attention to handle these dynamic, patch-dependent masks efficiently.

This combination—local windowed byte attention, patch-latent block-causal attention, and dynamic cross-attention between bytes and patches—creates a consistent causal hierarchy where byte-level local context informs patch formation and patch-level global context informs byte reconstruction, all while maintaining correct autoregressive conditioning.

# Where compute is spent and how patch size controls global steps

The BLT architecture is carefully balanced around where FLOPs are expended. The global latent transformer \mathcal{G} accounts for the majority of training and inference FLOPs because it has many layers and a large hidden size (l_{\mathcal{G}}, h_{\mathcal{G}}), similar to the bulk of modern LLMs. The local encoder \mathcal{E} and decoder \mathcal{D} are deliberately lightweight (l_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}} and smaller hidden sizes), and they include cross-attention blocks whose cost scales with the number and size of patches and the bytes per patch. Therefore, the expected FLOPs per byte for BLT can be decomposed into contributions from: (1) the global transformer applied per patch, (2) the local encoder transformer applied per byte (with a fixed local context window), (3) the local decoder transformer applied per byte, and (4) the encoder/decoder cross-attention cost.

Formally, the paper summarizes per-byte FLOPs as a sum of terms, where the global transformer's contribution is proportional to the number of patches n_p (or inversely to average patch size), while byte-level transformer costs are proportional to the number of bytes n_{ctx}. Thus average patch size (bytes per patch) is the single most important factor controlling how many global steps occur for a fixed byte context. Increasing average patch size reduces the number of times \mathcal{G} is run for a fixed number of bytes, directly decreasing global-transformer FLOPs and thereby the overall inference cost. The BLT authors exploit this by trading off average patch size against model size: with larger patches a model can use the saved compute to scale up \mathcal{G} (more parameters and layers) while keeping the inference FLOP budget fixed.

Because cross-attention and local transformers still incur costs per byte, there is a practical sweet spot: patches must be long enough to yield sizable reductions in global transformer steps but not so long that encoder/decoder overheads or information loss from over-aggregation hurt performance. Furthermore, dynamic patching makes this trade-off input-dependent: predictable regions produce long patches (fewer expensive global steps), while high-entropy regions produce short patches (more global steps and more capacity where needed).

# Design goals: efficiency, flexibility, and robustness

BLT is designed around three interrelated goals: to be efficient in FLOPs, flexible in representation, and robust to noisy or long-tail inputs.

Efficiency. By moving the bulk of computation to a patch-level global transformer and invoking it only at patch boundaries, BLT reduces per-byte FLOPs relative to a pure byte-level transformer that runs expensive feed-forward layers on every byte. The average patch size controls the number of global steps and hence the dominant inference cost. Entropy-based dynamic patching further improves efficiency by creating longer patches where the next-byte prediction is low-entropy, so the model spends fewer expensive steps on trivial or repetitive content and more on informative sections. The architecture also uses local transformers with fixed windows and cross-attention patterns that avoid quadratic blowup across entire byte sequences.

Flexibility. Removing a fixed vocabulary eliminates trade-offs imposed by tokenizers—such as increasing vocabulary size to get longer tokens at the cost of larger embedding/output projection matrices. Patches in BLT are not drawn from a fixed finite set; instead arbitrary byte groups are mapped into continuous latent patch representations via learned encoder/decoder modules. This means BLT can vary patch size freely and adapt compute allocation dynamically per input. It also allows simultaneous scaling along two axes: patch size and model size. The saved FLOPs from larger patches can be reallocated to grow the global transformer, yielding better performance under a fixed inference budget—a scaling dimension unavailable to tokenization-based models without inflating vocabulary costs.

Robustness. Operating directly on bytes gives BLT direct access to orthographic structure and character-level signals that token-based models do not see. The local encoder augments byte embeddings with hash n-gram embeddings (3- to 8-grams) to capture local character sequences, and the cross-attention mechanism pools encoder bytes into patches in a contextual way. These design choices improve BLT's handling of noisy inputs, character-level transformations, multilingual orthographies, and low-resource language phenomena. Empirically, BLT demonstrates better robustness to character-level noising strategies and improved performance on character/phonology tasks compared to tokenizer-based LLMs trained on similar data.

Together, these goals produce an architecture that can match tokenized LLMs at scale in terms of bits-per-byte, while offering an efficient inference tradeoff controlled by patch size and an intrinsic robustness advantage from direct byte modeling. The hierarchical mixing of byte and patch information—lightweight local processing to encode/decode bytes and heavy global processing on compact patch latents—realizes the intended balance between compute, flexibility, and fidelity to raw input structure.

Local Encoder: Building Expressive Patch Representations

# Lightweight transformer with local block-causal attention over bytes

The local encoder in the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is designed to convert a long sequence of raw bytes into a sequence of compact, expressive patch vectors that will be consumed by the much larger latent global transformer. Its primary design constraint is efficiency: it must produce rich contextualized byte representations while incurring a small fraction of the total model flops. To achieve this, the local encoder is implemented as a lightweight transformer stack with a small number of layers $l_{\mathcal{E}}$, where $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ (the number of layers in the global latent transformer). Despite being shallow, the encoder is carefully structured to capture local context and prepare byte features for pooling into patches.

A key architectural choice is the attention masking used inside local transformer layers: the encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask. Concretely, each byte position $i$ attends only to a fixed window of preceding bytes of width $w_{\mathcal{E}}$, so the self-attention computation for byte $i$ depends on bytes in the index range $[i-w_{\mathcal{E}}, i-1]$ (subject to document boundaries). This fixed-window, block-causal mask ensures that the encoder captures recent local context (which is most relevant for next-byte prediction and local structure) while bounding the quadratic attention cost so the encoder remains cheap. Importantly, these local attention windows are allowed to cross dynamic patch boundaries: the encoder is not constrained to respect patch segmentation. This gives the encoder the capacity to build byte representations that are informed by nearby bytes even when those bytes belong to different patches, improving continuity and reducing boundary artifacts. However, local attention does not cross document boundaries, preserving causality at the document level.

In practice the encoder's computational budget is deliberately small relative to the global model so that most flops are allocated to the latent transformer. This balance permits the encoder to be shallow but still effective at producing the intermediate byte-level features used by the cross-attention pooling module described below.

# Byte embeddings plus hash n-gram embeddings (3–8 grams): motivation and design

A single byte by itself carries limited signal; much of linguistic and orthographic structure emerges from sequences of bytes (n-grams). To make each byte input $b_i$ more informative, BLT augments the standard 256-way byte embedding with learned hash n-gram embeddings that summarize short byte sequences ending at position $i$. The encoder therefore represents the input at step $i$ as an augmented embedding
\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E^{\text{hash}}_n(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]
where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}}}$ is the dense byte embedding for $b_i$, $g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\}$ is the byte $n$-gram ending at $i$, and $E^{\text{hash}}_n$ denotes the n-gram hash embedding table for size $n$.

There are several motivations behind this design. First, many important signals (morphemes, syllables, common substrings, parts of identifiers in code, etc.) are captured by short n-grams; explicitly modeling those n-grams gives the local encoder richer initial features than single-byte embeddings alone. Second, using hash embeddings allows the model to represent a very large space of possible byte n-grams without a prohibitively large learned table: hash tables index a fixed-size embedding table that folds many distinct n-grams into shared slots, enabling parameter-efficient coverage of long-tail substrings. Third, n-gram features are especially helpful for byte-level modeling to recover higher-level sub-word and orthographic patterns that tokenizers normally expose as single token IDs.

Empirically, BLT uses n-grams of sizes $n\in\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$. These sizes strike a balance between capturing meaningful multi-byte units and keeping the hash computation and table management simple. The encoder adds the selected hash embeddings to the byte embedding and then normalizes by the count of added components (see the normalization subsection) before feeding the result into the local transformer layers.

# Rolling polynomial hash, table sizes, normalization, and trade-offs

The mapping from an n-gram $g_{i,n}$ to an index into the hash embedding table is implemented via a rolling polynomial hash (RollPolyHash). Formally,
\[
\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \bmod |E^{\text{hash}}_n|,
\]
where $|E^{\text{hash}}_n|$ is the fixed size of the embedding table for n-grams of length $n$. A rolling polynomial hash can be updated incrementally when advancing across positions $i$, making it efficient to compute for every step in a long sequence. Using a modulus with the table size bounds the index into a compact embedding table. BLT experiments use a single hash function and table sizes on the order of hundreds of thousands (e.g., 500,000 hashes in many runs), but smaller or larger sizes are explored in ablations.

Because different hash table sizes trade collision rate versus parameter cost, the choice of $|E^{\text{hash}}_n|$ matters. Larger tables reduce n-gram collisions and yield more distinct embeddings for common substrings, improving modeling especially on domains with repetitive or structured text (e.g., code, Wikipedia). The ablations in the paper show diminishing returns beyond a few hundred thousand hashes: going from 0 to a moderate number of hashes yields significant gains, but improvements taper off after roughly a few hundred thousand slots. Thus practitioners choose table sizes to balance memory/parameter budgets against collision-induced noise.

After summing the byte embedding and the selected n-gram embeddings, BLT normalizes the combined embedding by dividing by the number of components (the single byte embedding plus the number of n-gram sizes present at that position). Concretely, if the byte embedding and six n-gram embeddings are present, BLT computes
\[
e_i \leftarrow \frac{1}{1 + \#\{n\}} \left(x_i + \sum_{n} E^{\text{hash}}_n(\cdot)\right).
\]
This normalization prevents the scale of the input vectors from growing with the number of n-grams and stabilizes training. Practically, n-gram embeddings are omitted at early positions $i<n$ because those n-grams do not exist; the normalization accounts for these variable counts.

Trade-offs inherent in the design include: (1) memory: larger hash tables increase parameter count; (2) collision vs. expressivity: smaller tables increase collisions, which blends many distinct n-grams together and can hurt rare-substring modeling; (3) compute: computing multiple hash lookups per position adds modest overhead in the encoder pipeline, though this is small relative to global transformer flops. Ablations indicate that hash embeddings are especially beneficial on structured and long-tail domains (e.g., GitHub, Wikipedia) and that after a moderate table size the gains diminish.

# Cross-attention module to pool bytes into patch representations (Perceiver-style input CA)

Once the encoder has produced contextual byte representations $h_{l-1,i}$ (or $e_i$ for the first layer), these must be aggregated into a sequence of patch representations $P_{0,j}$—one per patch—that serve as the inputs to the latent global transformer $\mathcal{G}$. BLT adopts a Perceiver-style input cross-attention mechanism to perform this pooling, with the critical difference that the latents in BLT are variable-length patch representations rather than a fixed set of latents.

The cross-attention module operates in alternating steps with the encoder transformer layers. For each patch $p_j$, we initialize a query vector by pooling the byte representations that belong to that patch:
\[
P_{0,j} = \mathcal{E}_C\big(f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)\big),
\]
where $f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)$ denotes the pooling function (e.g., max-pool) applied to the encoder byte vectors corresponding to the bytes assigned to patch $p_j$, and $\mathcal{E}_C$ is a linear projection that maps pooled byte-level summaries into the query space. The pooled initialization provides a compact summary of the bytes in the patch and serves as the starting query for multi-headed cross-attention.

Subsequently, BLT applies a cross-attention update for each encoder layer \(l\):
\[
P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\Big(\text{softmax}\big(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\big)V\Big),
\]
where for patch queries $Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j})$ and the keys and values are projections of the per-byte hidden states $h_{l-1,i}$ via $K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i})$ and $V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$. The attention is computed only over bytes that belong to the corresponding patch (masking described next). After cross-attention, standard transformer-style processing continues (the encoder transformer layer updates the per-byte states $h_l$), and multiple alternating cross-attention steps help the patch vectors integrate progressively more contextualized byte information.

This Perceiver-like cross-attention design produces patch vectors that are both expressive (they attend to contextualized byte features produced by the local transformer) and compact (there is one vector per patch rather than per byte), enabling the global transformer to operate at a much shorter sequence length with rich inputs.

# Masking strategies: queries attend only to bytes within a patch

A crucial operational detail in the encoder cross-attention is the masking strategy used when computing attention for each patch query. BLT enforces a patch-specific mask so that the query corresponding to patch $p_j$ only attends to keys and values derived from the bytes that compose $p_j$. In other words, for each patch $j$, the attention matrix's allowed key/value positions are restricted to indices $i\in f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)$.

This design has several intentions. First, it preserves the semantic locality of patches: each patch representation is primarily a summary of its own bytes rather than a blended average of neighboring patches. Second, because patches may vary in byte length, the mask makes the cross-attention computation adaptive: patches with more bytes compute attention over more key/value entries, whereas short patches attend to fewer entries. Third, by restricting cross-attention in this way, BLT ensures that the global transformer receives patch representations that are localized summaries, while cross-patch dependencies are handled at the latent transformer level (where attention is cheaper due to the reduced sequence length).

Implementationally, the mask is dynamic during training and inference because patch boundaries are data-dependent (entropy patching or space patching). The cross-attention is therefore implemented with flexible attention kernels (e.g., FlexAttention) that accept per-query masks to restrict key/value positions efficiently.

# Design choices: heads, dimensions, residuals, pre-LN, no positional embeddings in cross-attn

Several concrete design choices in the local encoder cross-attention and transformer layers are important to understand both for implementation and for their effect on representation quality.

- Multi-headed attention and dimensionality splits. Patch representations $P_l$ are typically maintained at a higher dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ than the encoder byte dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$. During cross-attention, BLT represents $P_l$ as multiple sub-heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ so that the dot-product attention computations align with the per-byte key/value dimensions. After attention, these head representations are concatenated to form the full $h_{\mathcal{G}}$-dimensional patch vector expected by the global transformer. This split-and-concat design allows multi-headed interactions while keeping individual attention computations efficient.

- Residual connections. Each cross-attention block in the encoder uses a residual connection around the attention output:
  \[
  P_l \leftarrow P_{l-1} + \text{CrossAttn}(P_{l-1},\{h_{l-1,i}\}),
  \]
  which stabilizes training and preserves the initial pooled information while permitting the attention update to refine it.

- Pre-LayerNorm. BLT applies Layer Normalization before the attention projections (pre-LN), consistent with the transformer variants used in the global model. Pre-LN tends to stabilize optimization, especially in deeper stacks, and the encoder mirrors those conventions.

- No positional embeddings in cross-attention. The cross-attention module deliberately omits positional embeddings when attending from patches to bytes. The encoder's byte-level transformer layers already use local positional mechanisms (e.g., RoPE in self-attention) to impart order within the local window. The cross-attention is intended to pool content rather than encode precise byte offsets inside a patch, so positional embeddings are not added to the queries or the key/value byte representations within the cross-attention module. This keeps the cross-attention focused on content-based pooling and avoids doubling up positional signals.

- Query initialization and pooling function. The cross-attention queries are initialized by a pooling function applied to the encoder's byte embeddings for each patch. The default pooling used in BLT is max-pooling, which produced effective initialization in practice. After pooling, a linear projection $\mathcal{E}_C$ maps the pooled vectors into the appropriate query space. Alternative initializations (such as learned per-patch embeddings or hash embeddings of patch bytes) were ablated in the paper; pooling-based initialization provided the best trade-off between simplicity and performance.

- Attention head counts and projections. Standard multi-head parameters $W_q,W_k,W_v,W_o$ are used, where $W_q$ projects pooled patch queries into the head space, and $W_k,W_v$ project byte representations. The attention scalar uses the usual scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$. Because the cross-attention is applied for every encoder layer (interleaved with encoder transformer layers), these projections are kept lightweight so the encoder remains cheap.

Together, these design choices produce a compact, stable, and effective module that transforms byte-level features into patch-level latents while preserving efficiency. The encoder's architecture—small transformer depth, local block-causal self-attention, hash n-gram augmentation, and Perceiver-style cross-attention with per-patch masking and pooling—strikes a practical balance: it equips the global latent transformer with rich, contextual patch representations while keeping the encoder's compute footprint deliberately small so that most pretraining flops are spent learning global dependencies over patches.

Global Latent Transformer and Local Decoder

# Global latent transformer: depth, block-causal masking, role as primary compute consumer
The global latent transformer (denoted $\mathcal{G}$ in the paper) is the core autoregressive model in the BLT architecture. It operates on a sequence of latent patch representations $\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{n_p}$ and maps them to a sequence of output patch representations $\{o_j\}_{j=1}^{n_p}$. Concretely, $\mathcal{G}$ is a standard Transformer stack with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers and a hidden dimensionality $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, but its attention and autoregressive semantics are defined at the granularity of patches rather than raw bytes.

Two design choices about $\mathcal{G}$ are central to BLT's behavior. First, depth: the global transformer is intentionally large relative to the local models ($l_{\mathcal{G}} \gg l_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}$). This concentrates the majority of model parameters and the bulk of FLOPs in the latent-level computation. Second, masking: $\mathcal{G}$ uses a block-causal attention mask. That is, attention is restricted to include the current patch and earlier patches within the same document (or same autoregressive context). Block-causality enforces that the latent model cannot peek into future patches while still allowing full intra-patch communication of the patch representation. Formally, if patches are indexed by $j$, the attention mask for $\mathcal{G}$ permits attention from $o_{j}$ to $o_{k}$ only when $k \le j$ (and only within the same document boundary).

These two properties—large depth and block-causal attention—make $\mathcal{G}$ the primary consumer of compute during both training and inference. Because each patch is (on average) larger than a token in byte terms, invoking the global transformer fewer times (by having larger average patch sizes) saves FLOPs; those saved FLOPs can be reallocated to increase $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ or $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ to improve modeling capacity. Thus $\mathcal{G}$ is the knob through which BLT trades off step-count and model size: fewer latent steps (larger patches) but a bigger latent transformer, or more steps (smaller patches) but a smaller latent transformer.

# Local decoder: alternating cross-attention and transformer layers
The local decoder, denoted $\mathcal{D}$, reverses the encoder/global flow: it consumes the sequence of global output patch representations $\{o_j\}$ produced by $\mathcal{G}$ and decodes them into a byte-level output sequence $\{y_i\}$ (the predicted bytes). Architecturally, $\mathcal{D}$ is a lightweight Transformer stack with $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ layers and hidden size $h_{\mathcal{D}}$, but it interleaves cross-attention and local Transformer layers in a particular order.

Specifically, in each decoder layer the cross-attention sub-layer is applied first and pools information from the patch representations into byte-level queries; then a standard (local) Transformer sub-layer refines those byte representations. This alternating structure ensures the decoder can construct context-aware byte-level representations using high-capacity patch-level signals and then perform local sequence modeling and autoregressive conditioning over bytes. Because the decoder operates on byte positions, its Transformer layers use a local block-causal attention mask (a fixed window) to limit per-byte self-attention cost; the heavy lifting of long-range dependency modeling has already been handled by the global transformer operating on patches.

# Decoder cross-attention: byte queries attend to patch key/values
The decoder cross-attention is the central mechanism by which patch-level information is projected back into byte-space. In the decoder cross-attention module, byte-level vectors act as queries while the patch representations $\{o_j\}$ form the keys and values. The paper provides the formal computation used in the decoder cross-attention:

- Initialize the byte-level inputs to the decoder with the encoder's last-layer byte representations:
  $D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$.
- For layer $l$ the cross-attention output is
  \[
  B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V\right),
  \]
  where the queries are
  \[
  Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}),
  \]
  and the keys and values are computed from a linear transformation and split operation $\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)$ applied to patch outputs:
  \[
  K_j = W_k(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)),\qquad V_j = W_v(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)).
  \]
- After cross-attention, $B_l$ is passed through the decoder-transformer layer to produce $D_l$.

Two practical points follow from this formulation. First, the queries are indexed by bytes and therefore there is one query per output byte; keys/values are indexed by patches, so each byte's query attends only to the (usually small set of) patches that are relevant for that byte—typically the patch that contains that byte and possibly neighboring patch representations as allowed by the attention masking and implementation. Second, because patch representations live in a (typically) larger dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ than the decoder's byte-space $h_{\mathcal{D}}$, $\mathcal{D}_C$ and the multi-head attention projections bring patch vectors into the appropriate per-head dimensionality before attention, and $W_o$ projects the concatenated heads back to $h_{\mathcal{D}}$.

This arrangement lets the decoder combine detailed byte-local signals (from the encoder initialization and transformer sub-layer) with high-level, context-rich patch information (from $\mathcal{G}$) to produce accurate byte predictions.

# Decoder’s causal objective over bytes and 256-way output head
The decoder is trained with a standard autoregressive, per-byte causal objective: at each byte position $i$ it must predict the next byte $x_i$ conditioned on all previously emitted bytes. The modeling target is raw bytes, so the decoder's final projection maps to a 256-way categorical distribution (one logit per possible byte value). In the paper's notation, the cross-entropy loss computed over the predicted byte distribution is summed over bytes and used to obtain Bits-Per-Byte (BPB):

\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}},
\]

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})$ is the summed cross-entropy over all bytes in sample $\mathbf{x}$ and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the number of bytes. The 256-way output head is only present in the local decoder and is the only place where a byte vocabulary is explicitly enumerated; the global transformer works in latent patch space with no fixed external vocabulary of patches.

During generation, the decoder enforces autoregressive conditioning at byte-level: each decoded byte is produced conditioned on previously decoded bytes (via the decoder's local Transformer and cross-attention to patch outputs that themselves were computed autoregressively at patch-level by $\mathcal{G}$). The causality in the decoder is strict at byte resolution even though the global model is causal at patch resolution (via its block-causal mask).

# Interplay among encoder, global model, and decoder across training/inference
Training and inference in BLT involve a three-stage flow of information: (1) the local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ maps the raw input bytes to patch representations, (2) the global latent transformer $\mathcal{G}$ autoregressively models dependencies between patches, and (3) the local decoder $\mathcal{D}$ converts the global patch outputs back to bytes and computes the byte-level loss. Understanding how these three components coordinate is key to grasping BLT's efficiency and correctness.

During a forward pass on training data, the encoder first embeds each byte $b_i$ (augmented with hash n-gram embeddings) and applies a small number of transformer layers with local-window attention to produce rich byte representations $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}},i}$. Cross-attention pooling in the encoder then aggregates the byte representations belonging to each patch $p_j$ into an initial patch representation $P_{0,j}$; subsequent encoder layers continue to refine byte and pooled patch representations by alternating local transformer blocks and cross-attention pooling. The result is a sequence of patch embeddings $p_j$ that carry both local byte detail and some contextualized summary.

Those patch embeddings are fed to the global latent transformer $\mathcal{G}$, which models long-range and structural dependencies across patches under a block-causal mask. $\mathcal{G}$ produces output patch representations $o_j$ autoregressively. Because $\mathcal{G}$ is expensive, its invocation count is proportional to the number of patches. Hence BLT’s patching strategy (static or dynamic entropy-based) directly controls how many times $\mathcal{G}$ must run for a given byte sequence and therefore the dominant portion of FLOPs.

The decoder then takes the $o_j$ and, via its cross-attention and local transformer layers, reconstructs byte-level logits and computes the 256-way cross-entropy loss. Note the decoder is also causal at byte-level: it conditions each predicted byte on earlier bytes in the same sequence, while relying on the patch-level context provided by $o_j$.

At inference time the three modules interact similarly but with important incremental constraints. Patch boundaries must be determined without seeing future bytes (incremental patching property), so entropy-based or other incremental patchers must decide whether to end the current patch using only past bytes or an entropy predictor that itself is incremental. Once a patch boundary is chosen, the encoder pools the bytes into a patch representation which is then consumed by $\mathcal{G}$ to advance the patch-level autoregressive state. The decoder uses the latest patch outputs and the already-decoded bytes to produce next-byte probabilities. If the decoder emits bytes that do not end a patch (i.e., the patch continues), the global model is not re-invoked; when the decoder reaches a boundary that the patcher declares as a new patch, the encoder produces a new patch embedding and the global transformer is run to extend the latent sequence.

This separation—lightweight byte-level encoders/decoders plus an expensive latent transformer that is invoked per patch—enables BLT to dynamically allocate compute: predictable regions (long, low-entropy patches) cause fewer $\mathcal{G}$ invocations and thus save FLOPs, while unpredictable regions (patch boundaries where entropy is high) trigger more frequent $\mathcal{G}$ updates so model capacity is concentrated where it is most needed. During training the system is optimized end-to-end with the byte-level causal loss; during inference the incremental patching constraint governs when $\mathcal{G}$ must be stepped to preserve autoregressive correctness.

In sum, the global latent transformer supplies high-capacity, long-range modeling at patch granularity; the local decoder reconstructs bytes from patch outputs with per-byte causality and a 256-way output head; and the encoder-global-decoder pipeline together realizes an efficient, tokenizer-free autoregressive byte model in which step count and model size can be traded to optimize FLOPs and performance.

Training and Evaluation Setup

# Datasets: Llama 2 data (for compute-optimal scaling studies) and BLT-1T data mixture
The experiments in this work use two distinct pretraining datasets, each chosen to serve a particular experimental purpose. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, which consists of roughly 2 trillion tokens gathered from a variety of public sources, cleaned and filtered to improve quality. We use this dataset primarily for the compute-optimal scaling studies. By training both token-based baselines and BLT variants on the same Llama 2 mixture, we can compare performance under the same training distribution and under budgets tuned to known compute-optimal regimes for token models. This alignment is important when asking whether BLT can match or exceed token-model scaling behavior when the parameter-to-data ratio is chosen to be near-optimal.

The second dataset, which we call BLT-1T, is a distinct mixture of about 1 trillion tokens assembled from public sources and a subset of Datacomp-LM pretraining data. BLT-1T is used for a full pretraining run of larger BLT models (including the 8B experiments reported in evaluation tables). The BLT-1T mixture is used to measure downstream task performance beyond the controlled compute-optimal regime. Neither dataset contains data collected from Meta products or services. Using these two datasets allows the experiments to probe both controlled scaling behavior (with Llama 2 data) and more realistic, larger-scale pretraining effects (with BLT-1T).

# Token baselines: Llama 2 vs Llama 3 tokenizers (avg bytes/token and vocab sizes)
To obtain strong, fair baselines, the paper compares BLT models against tokenization-based LLMs that use subword tokenizers. Two tokenizer variants are considered: the Llama 2 tokenizer and the Llama 3 tokenizer. These differ in vocabulary size and resulting average bytes per token. Practically, larger tokenizer vocabularies tend to increase the average token length (bytes per token) but at the cost of larger embedding tables and output projection layers in tokenized models.

Concretely, the paper reports average token sizes of about 3.7 bytes for the Llama 2 tokenizer and 4.4 bytes for the Llama 3 tokenizer; the Llama 3 tokenizer uses a larger vocabulary (we use a 128K vocabulary for Llama 3 in baselines). The experiments show that the choice of tokenizer materially affects baseline performance: models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer outperform those trained with the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same training data. These tokenizer baselines serve two roles: (1) compute-optimal baselines when comparing training-flop vs performance curves on the Llama 2 dataset, and (2) realistic production-like baselines (Llama 3 tokenizer with 128K) for downstream task comparisons on BLT-1T.

# Entropy model: sizes, contexts, training, and when a lookup table becomes viable
Entropy patching is central to BLT's dynamic segmentation of bytes into patches. To obtain per-byte next-token uncertainty estimates, the authors train a small byte-level auto-regressive language model, called the entropy model, on the same training distribution as the full BLT model. The default entropy model configuration used in most experiments is a transformer with roughly 100M parameters, 14 layers, hidden dimension 512, and a sliding window (receptive field) of 512 bytes. Sliding-window attention of 512 bytes is used to estimate next-byte distributions efficiently.

The paper studies entropy-model ablations: varying the entropy model size from 1M up to 100M parameters and context windows from 64 to 512 bytes. Empirically, scaling the entropy model's size and receptive field improves patching quality and downstream BLT scaling, but returns diminish beyond about 50M parameters. A practical implementation note is that when the entropy model's receptive field is small enough, the trained entropy model can be encoded as an efficient lookup table, enabling extremely cheap entropy estimates at data-loading time instead of running a transformer. In short, the entropy model must be large and contextual enough to produce reliable next-byte entropies, but there is a regime (small receptive fields) where a lookup-table approximation becomes viable and desirable for efficiency.

# Equalizing context length in bytes; keeping bytes-per-batch constant across patch sizes
Because BLT operates on patches whose average size can vary (static strided patches, whitespace patches, entropy patches), a naive comparison could give models with larger patches more effective context in terms of processed bytes per update. To avoid this confound, the experimental protocol equalizes the number of bytes seen per batch in expectation across all patching schemes. That is, when the average patch size is increased, the sequence length (in patches) used during training is reduced so that the total bytes per batch remain constant. Concretely, on the Llama 2 dataset runs the authors use an average context of 8k bytes; for BLT-1T runs they use 16k bytes on average. The batch-size in bytes is fixed (roughly 16M bytes on average), which keeps the byte-level data exposure comparable between models.

This equalization is important because BLT changes the amount of latent-transformer steps via patch size; controlling the bytes-per-batch ensures that observed performance differences stem from architectural and patching advantages rather than simply from having seen more bytes per optimizer step.

# Packing patches to avoid latent-transformer padding; byte padding/truncation limits
Entropy and other dynamic patching schemes produce variable numbers of patches per sequence, so naive batching would require padding patch sequences to a common length for the global latent transformer, resulting in wasted computation. To avoid this, the BLT training implementation packs batches of patches so that the latent transformer receives tightly-packed patch sequences with minimal padding. The goal is to ensure that every batch has the same number of patches during training without incurring potentially large padding overheads in the expensive global transformer.

Despite packing patches at the patch-level, byte-level sequences must still be padded or truncated to control memory spikes from very long patches. In the experiments, the authors cap sequences during training by padding/truncating byte sequences to 12k bytes for Llama 2 runs and 24k bytes for BLT-1T runs. These caps prevent rare sequences with unusually large patches from causing excessive memory usage while keeping the average bytes-per-batch constraint intact. This combination—packing patches to avoid latent-transformer padding and bounding raw byte sequence length—enables efficient, stable training with dynamic patching.

# FLOPs accounting for local/global transformers and cross-attention; decoder output head
Accurate, comparable FLOPs accounting is central to the paper's flop-controlled evaluation. The paper follows a Transformer FLOPs accounting approach similar to Chinchilla-style estimates, summing flops for feed-forward layers, q/k/v/output projections in self-attention, and attention score computations. Two practical modifications are applied: (1) the input embedding lookup is treated as an efficient lookup (0 flops), and (2) backward pass flops are approximated as twice the forward pass flops.

For BLT, the per-byte FLOPs contribution is computed by summing the costs of the three model components and cross-attention modules, then normalizing appropriately by patch size or window lengths. The equation used in the paper (expressed here as in the text) is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p, V=0)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k)\times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)
\end{aligned}
\]

Here $n_{\text{ctx}}$ is the sequence length in bytes, $n_p$ is the average patch size (bytes per patch), $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the local encoder/decoder attention windows (in bytes), $h_{\mathcal{G}},h_{\mathcal{E}},h_{\mathcal{D}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{G}},l_{\mathcal{E}},l_{\mathcal{D}}$ are respective hidden dimensions and layer counts, $k$ is the ratio of patch-dimension to byte-dimension (number of encoder splits concatenated to form the global representation), and $V$ is the decoder vocabulary size (256 for bytes). The CrossAttn.FL terms adjust the attention flops according to the ratio of queries to key/values $r$ and are scaled to reflect whether they operate on patches or bytes. The paper provides exact derivations for Transformer-FLOPs and Cross-Attention FLOPs in an appendix.

This accounting lets the authors compare training and inference costs between BLT and tokenized models, compute flops-per-byte, and report inference savings (e.g., up to 50% fewer flops at fixed inference budgets when average patch size is larger).

# Transformer hyperparameters (SwiGLU, RoPE with high theta, RMSNorm); Flash/FlexAttention
Across all transformer blocks—global latent transformer, local encoder, and local decoder—the BLT models largely reuse the architectural choices from Llama 3. These include SwiGLU nonlinearity in feed-forward layers, rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) with a large $\theta = 500000$ (following the effective RoPE scaling adopted by prior work), and RMSNorm for normalization. RoPE positional embeddings are applied in the self-attention layers only. These shared choices make comparisons to Llama baselines more direct and leverage best-practice components for large transformer training.

Implementation details for attention kernels are also important. For self-attention layers that use fixed, standard attention masks (e.g., block-causal or fixed-window causal), the implementation uses FlashAttention for efficiency. For cross-attention layers that require dynamic masks tied to patch boundaries (so queries only attend to bytes in the corresponding patch), the experiments use Flex Attention implementations to produce fused kernels that handle dynamic masks efficiently. Using FlashAttention and Flex Attention allows the BLT implementation to keep wall-clock costs reasonable despite the added complexity of cross-attention to map between bytes and patch latents.

Together, these hyperparameter and implementation choices (SwiGLU, RoPE with large $\theta$, RMSNorm, Flash/FlexAttention) enable BLT to scale to the parameter sizes and training budgets reported, while keeping architectural decisions aligned with strong token-based baselines.

Scaling Results: Compute-Optimal Parity and Inference Efficiency

# Compute-optimal, parameter-matched scaling: BLT vs BPE up to 8B/4T bytes
A central empirical claim of the paper is that the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is the first byte-level Transformer architecture that attains compute-optimal parity with BPE (subword-token) based models at scale. To demonstrate this, the authors perform a flop-controlled, parameter-matched scaling study across model sizes from 1B up to 8B parameters. For each BPE baseline they train a compute-optimal model (i.e., matching the parameter-to-data tradeoffs recommended by Llama 3) and they train a BLT whose global latent Transformer matches the corresponding BPE Transformer in size and architecture. All models are trained on the same Llama 2 dataset mixture to avoid differences in data as confounders.

The measured metric is Bits-Per-Byte (BPB), a tokenizer-independent analogue of perplexity suitable for byte-level models. The results show that BLT models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across the scaling curve. This is significant because prior byte-level approaches either required much more compute or lagged behind token models as scale increased. The parity observed here indicates that the design choices in BLT — namely: dynamic patching, lightweight byte-level local encoders/decoders with cross-attention to patch latents, and n-gram hash embeddings — close the previous performance gap and allow byte-level models to enjoy the same compute-optimal training regime as token-based models up to 8B parameters and extensive training (up to 4T bytes in the larger experiments).

Two points make this result robust. First, BLT is evaluated in a flop-controlled way: training flops are accounted for and compared directly, so improvements are not attributed to simply spending more compute. Second, the experiments are parameter-matched: the global latent Transformer in BLT is directly comparable to the BPE Transformer, making the comparison meaningful in terms of model capacity rather than architectural idiosyncrasies.

# Space vs entropy patching and architectural components’ impact
Patching — grouping bytes into units processed by the expensive global Transformer — is the keystone enabling BLT efficiency. The paper compares different patching strategies and isolates architectural components to determine what drives the observed scaling improvements.

Space patching (creating patches at space-like bytes) is a simple rule-based approach that groups words or word-like units together. It yields larger average patch sizes than byte-stride patching, and it provides consistent boundaries across instances of the same word. Empirically, space patching improves over fixed stride schemes (e.g., Megabyte-style fixed k-byte patches) but still falls short of matching state-of-the-art BPE models when used alone.

Entropy patching is the data-driven alternative: a small byte-level next-byte language model is trained and used to compute per-byte next-byte entropy. Patches are formed where the entropy is high (absolute threshold) or where entropy rises relative to the previous position (approximate monotonicity threshold). Entropy patching dynamically identifies high-uncertainty bytes (e.g., the first byte of a word or other decision points) and thus allocates global Transformer steps where they matter most. The paper shows that entropy patching, combined with BLT’s architecture, delivers the best scaling behavior.

Architectural components are also crucial. BLT adds lightweight byte-level local encoder and decoder modules with multi-headed cross-attention between bytes and patch latents, and it augments byte embeddings with hash n-gram embeddings (3–8 grams hashed into fixed-size tables). Ablations indicate that: (1) cross-attention in the decoder is especially beneficial, (2) encoder cross-attention helps when initialized by pooling the encoder hidden representations for a patch, and (3) n-gram hash embeddings materially improve BPB across domains (with diminishing returns beyond a few hundred thousand hashes). Putting these components together — entropy patching, cross-attention, and n-gram hashes — is what allows BLT to match or surpass BPE scaling.

# Patch size effects: achieving parity with larger patches (e.g., 6–8 bytes) and ~50% inference FLOP savings
One of BLT’s distinctive advantages is the ability to trade patch size against other dimensions while avoiding the large embedding-table tradeoffs that constrain tokenizers. Tokenizer-based models face a tradeoff: increasing average token byte-size typically enlarges vocabulary and the output embedding matrix, increasing parameter cost and inference cost in different ways. In contrast, BLT forms patches without a fixed vocabulary, so it can increase average patch size directly and reduce the number of global Transformer steps without bloating an embedding table.

The experiments show that BLT attains parity with BPE models while using larger average patches. The paper reports that BLT can reach similar scaling trends with average patch sizes of 6 or even 8 bytes. Because inference FLOPs are roughly inversely proportional to average patch size (fewer patch steps means fewer expensive latent Transformer invocations), increasing patch size can yield substantial inference savings. The paper highlights cases where a patch size of 8 bytes corresponds to nearly 50% fewer inference FLOPs relative to the baseline tokenization settings. This is a practical, measurable gain: for fixed model and training budgets, larger patch sizes translate directly into lower per-inference compute.

However, the experiments also point out caveats. Very large patch sizes can hurt early points on the scaling curve (smaller models or low-training-flop regimes) and may push the model away from the compute-optimal data/parameter ratio because larger patch sizes effectively increase how much data is covered per forward step. Nonetheless, as model size and training compute increase, larger patch sizes tend to perform better — indicating a regime where BLT can both grow the model and improve inference efficiency simultaneously.

# Beyond compute-optimal: downstream evaluations (ARC, HellaSwag, PIQA, MMLU, MBPP, HumanEval)
To verify that BLT's favorable scaling on BPB translates into downstream capabilities, the authors pretrain an 8B BLT model beyond the compute-optimal ratio on the BLT-1T dataset (a 1T-token, higher-quality mixture) and evaluate on a suite of standard classification and code generation benchmarks. The evaluation battery includes ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, MMLU, MBPP, and HumanEval.

Three 8B models are compared: BPE Llama 3 (with the Llama 3 tokenizer), BLT-Space (space patching), and BLT-Entropy (entropy patching). All are trained with equivalent flop budgets. For BLT-Entropy an additional inference-time entropy threshold adjustment is sometimes used (reducing the threshold to increase the number of patch steps) to trade compute for performance.

The empirical takeaway is that BLT-Entropy outperforms the BPE Llama 3 model on 4 of 7 tasks when trained on the same number of bytes. This indicates that the benefits observed in flop-controlled BPB scaling also show up as real gains on reasoning and code-generation benchmarks, at least for the entropy-patched variant. BLT-Space, which enjoys a larger average patch size (about 6 bytes) and consequently inference-efficiency gains, underperforms BPE on most of these tasks, illustrating the tradeoff between efficiency and task-level performance when choosing a patching scheme.

# Task-level tradeoffs: BLT-Entropy gains vs BLT-Space efficiency
The evaluations isolate a clear tradeoff across BLT configurations. BLT-Entropy, which dynamically allocates patch boundaries guided by next-byte entropy, tends to match or exceed BPE performance on downstream tasks. It uses an average patch size similar to BPE in the reported runs (about 4.5 bytes) but benefits from smarter placement of patch boundaries, improved local representations, and the full BLT architecture. The dynamic allocation concentrates global compute on higher-uncertainty bytes and appears to yield better use of training compute for task-relevant modeling.

BLT-Space prioritizes efficiency: by cutting patches at space-like bytes, it produces larger average patches (about 6 bytes) and correspondingly reduces inference FLOPs substantially. This comes at the cost of some downstream performance: BLT-Space underperforms BPE on most benchmarks in the runs reported. The contrast encapsulates a practical design decision for deployment: if inference-flop budget is the binding constraint, BLT-Space can deliver substantial savings; if peak downstream performance is the goal (given a fixed training/inference budget), BLT-Entropy appears to be the better compromise.

The paper also notes that BLT-Space’s larger patches cover more data per training step and so, when trained with the same flop budget, it effectively sees more bytes. This moves it away from the compute-optimal point, which might partially explain performance gaps. Thus, some of the task-level tradeoff is due to differing effective exposure to data under equivalent flop budgets, not only the inherent expressivity of the patching rule.

# Fixed-inference scaling: growing model size while increasing patch size at constant inference budget
A distinctive experimental regime explored by the paper is fixed-inference scaling: holding the per-inference FLOP budget constant while reallocating saved inference FLOPs (from larger patch sizes) to increase model size. The hypothesis is that larger models that take fewer steps (because patches are larger) might outperform smaller models that take more steps, when inference cost is constrained.

To test this, the authors construct several flop-equivalent models across two inference-FLOP classes. Starting from baseline models (e.g., 400M and 3.6B parameter Llama 2 tokenizer models), they design BLT-Entropy variants with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes. For the patch-8 models they increase the number of encoder layers (e.g., using three encoder layers instead of one) to accommodate the larger patches. They then train each model at various training-flop budgets and compare downstream performance.

The results show that BLT models achieve superior scaling trends under a fixed-inference constraint. Initially, for small training budgets, BPE models perform better; but as the training budget grows, BLT models overtake. This validates the idea that the saved inference cost from larger patches can be reinvested into model capacity in a way that yields improved performance for a fixed per-query compute budget. It opens a new practical scaling axis: rather than only trading compute between width/depth and data, practitioners can trade patch granularity vs latent capacity while holding inference cost constant.

# Crossover behavior: when BLT surpasses BPE as training budget grows
Across the fixed-inference experiments, an important pattern emerges: BLT does not uniformly dominate BPE at all training budgets. Instead, there is a crossover point where BLT begins to outperform BPE. For the smaller inference-FLOP class, this crossover occurs at roughly 3x the compute-optimal budget for BPE models; for larger inference-FLOP classes the crossover shifts somewhat closer (about 2.5x the compute-optimal budget). Larger patch sizes (e.g., patch size 8) exhibit steeper scaling and therefore cross over earlier in training-flop budget.

Intuitively, early in training (low total flops), the simplicity and inductive bias of BPE tokenization can give smaller models an edge: BPE compresses information in a way that is already tuned to linguistic regularities, and with limited training compute this advantage manifests as better downstream performance. As training compute increases, BLT’s dynamic allocation and ability to scale latent capacity while saving inference cost become more influential. The cross-over therefore reflects a regime change: BLT excels once sufficient training compute allows the architecture to exploit its flexibility (dynamic patching, n-gram hashes, cross-attention mechanisms) and when the cost savings from patching can be reinvested into larger latent models.

This crossover behavior has two practical implications. First, it suggests BLT will be particularly attractive for scenarios where pretraining compute is plentiful (or where one is willing to train beyond the compute-optimal point) but inference costs must remain low. Second, it highlights that the design choices for patch size and the entropy-threshold should be tuned depending on the available training budget and the intended inference-FLOP target: smaller average patches (or lower entropy thresholds) help in low-flop training regimes, whereas larger patches become advantageous as training compute grows.

--- 

In summary, the experimental results show a nuanced but strong case for BLT. When equipped with entropy-based dynamic patching and the supporting architectural elements (cross-attention, hash n-gram embeddings, lightweight local models), BLT matches or exceeds BPE performance in flop-controlled, parameter-matched scaling up to 8B. Furthermore, because BLT can increase patch size without blowing up vocabulary-associated parameters, it unlocks a new practical scaling axis: for a fixed inference budget, one can grow the latent Transformer and thereby improve model capability. The tradeoffs between BLT-Entropy (performance-focused) and BLT-Space (efficiency-focused) give practitioners levers to choose between accuracy and inference cost, and the observed crossover points indicate when BLT is expected to overtake BPE as training compute increases.

Robustness and Byte-Level Strengths

# Noisy prompt/completion robustness: AntSpeak, Drop, RandomCase, Repeat, UpperCase on HellaSwag

One of the clearest practical advantages of modeling raw bytes is improved robustness to character-level noise. Tokenization-based models operate on a fixed vocabulary of subword units (tokens). Small perturbations at the byte/character level—case changes, insertions, deletions, or repeating characters—can change token boundaries or map the input to very different tokens. Because the model's entire input representation is mediated by those tokens, the downstream predictions can be highly sensitive to such surface-level noise. Byte-level models, by contrast, represent the input at the finest granularity and therefore can directly learn to be invariant or robust to such perturbations.

In the BLT paper, robustness is evaluated by applying five distinct character-level noising strategies to benchmark prompts and completions and measuring performance on a multiple-choice reasoning task (HellaSwag). The noising strategies are chosen to exercise different failure modes: AntSpeak (convert text to uppercase and space-separated characters), Drop (randomly remove characters), RandomCase (randomly change case for half the characters), Repeat (repeat some characters multiple times), and UpperCase (convert all characters to uppercase). The evaluation protocol applies each noising scheme to the prompt, to the completion, or to both, and reports averaged scores. This setup isolates whether models can recognize and reason from corrupted inputs or produce stable completions when the model's own output is noisy.

Empirically, BLT models outperform tokenizer-based baselines across these noise regimes. On noised HellaSwag, BLT shows an average advantage of around eight points over a BLT-trained tokenized baseline and even improves over a much larger Llama 3.1 tokenizer-based model trained on substantially more data. Intuitively, this gain arises because BLT's local encoder and decoder operate on bytes and thus can represent and aggregate noisy character evidence directly; the latent transformer then receives patch representations that are contextualized and less brittle to tokenization shifts. In addition, entropy- or space-derived patching lets BLT allocate fewer or more transformer steps depending on predictability, so predictable noisy patterns (like repeated uppercase characters) can be compressed into longer patches and treated efficiently while preserving robustness.

For a student, the important takeaway is that robustness to character-level corruption is not merely a training-data phenomenon; it is also architectural. Tokenizer-based models can be trained with noisy data to become more robust, but their representational bottleneck (fixed tokens) still makes them sensitive to small byte-level changes. Byte-level models like BLT remove this bottleneck, enabling direct learning of invariances and error-correcting patterns at the character/byte level.

# Grapheme-to-phoneme (Phonology Bench): advantages from subword awareness

Grapheme-to-phoneme mapping (G2P) is a classic subword-level task: given the written form (sequence of graphemes) of a word, predict its phonemic transcription. Success requires the model to reason about the relationship between characters and pronunciation rules, which often depend on local character sequences, orthographic patterns, and language-specific phonological phenomena. Tokenization can obscure these patterns by merging or splitting characters into subword tokens in inconsistent ways. In contrast, a byte-level model naturally has access to the full character sequence and can learn representations—and inductive biases—based on contiguous character n-grams.

In the BLT experiments, G2P performance is measured on Phonology Bench in a few-shot setting (5-shot). BLT outperforms a Llama 3 tokenizer-based baseline. This result demonstrates that BLT's explicit access to characters and its n-gram hash embeddings (which aggregate information from byte n-grams) enable better modeling of grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences. The local encoder's hash n-gram embeddings, which map 3–8 byte n-grams into hashed embeddings and add them to byte embeddings, give the model compact, dense signals about local orthographic context. These signals are particularly useful for G2P, where pronunciations are often determined by short character sequences (e.g., "ough", "tion", consonant clusters).

From a conceptual perspective, this shows that byte-level models can internalize subword knowledge (morphology and orthography) in a more direct way than token models, which must first learn token boundaries and then learn mappings from potentially inconsistent token spellings to pronunciations. Students should note that BLT combines local byte-level processing (with n-gram hashing and local transformer layers) and a global latent transformer operating on patches; this hybrid lets the model capture both fine-grained orthographic patterns needed for phonology and higher-level contextual cues needed for disambiguation.

# CUTE benchmark: composition, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation

The CUTE benchmark probes character-level understanding across three axes: composition (how characters form tokens and words), orthographic similarity (how similar words are by spelling), and sequence manipulation (editing operations such as deletion, insertion, and reordering). These tasks highlight deficiencies in token-based models: tokens obscure internal spelling and make character-level manipulations awkward because the model must implicitly reconstruct underlying characters from token embeddings, which is nontrivial when tokens are not atomic letters.

BLT shows substantial improvements on CUTE: the entropy-patched BLT model outperforms both BPE Llama 3 variants by more than 25 points overall, with near-perfect scores on spelling manipulation tasks. These gains are striking given that BLT was trained on far less data than the largest Llama model referenced. The reasons are architectural and representational. BLT's byte-level encoder and decoder give it direct access to characters and to localized n-gram features. The cross-attention-based pooling from bytes into patch latents and the decoder's reverse cross-attention ensure that the model can both compress and reconstruct character sequences with high fidelity. In sequence manipulation tasks, where the model must apply edits at the character level, this fidelity translates into near-perfect outcomes because the model does not need to infer character identity from a token representation.

Interestingly, where token models perform better—word-level deletion and insertion—BLT is slightly behind. This asymmetry reflects the natural inductive biases: token models are trained to reason in token units (words/subwords), which can sometimes make word-level edits more directly accessible; BLT, operating at characters, may need to aggregate characters into word-level structures for such operations. Nevertheless, the overall superior performance on composition, orthographic similarity, and character manipulation underscores that byte-level modeling supplies explicit, manipulable representations of orthography and composition that tokenized models struggle to recover.

For students, this emphasizes a tradeoff: token-level representations provide convenience for word-level reasoning, but they hide subword structure. BLT demonstrates that representing data at a lower level (bytes) and designing encoder/decoder mechanisms to compress and decompress into patch latents can yield practical advantages on tasks demanding subword awareness.

# Low-resource machine translation (FLORES-101): gains into/out of English across diverse scripts

An important domain where bytes pay off is low-resource machine translation. Many low-resource languages exhibit scripts or orthographies that are underrepresented in tokenizers' vocabularies. Subword tokenizers trained on large multilingual corpora tend to favor scripts and languages with higher prevalence, producing poor tokenization granularity or inconsistent splits for rarer scripts. This uneven tokenization creates learning difficulties and equity concerns. Byte-level models avoid these tokenization biases because every language is represented at the same atomic byte granularity.

BLT is evaluated on FLORES-101, translating both into and out of English across 21 lower-resource languages spanning diverse script families. The metric used is a SentencePiece BLEU measure over the benchmark. BLT outperforms a Llama 3 tokenizer-based model by about 2 BLEU points when translating into English and by about 0.5 BLEU points when translating from English. The improvements are not uniform across all popular language pairs—there BLT is comparable—but BLT demonstrates consistent gains on many low-resource pairs. The gains indicate better generalization to long-tail byte sequences: because BLT models characters and byte n-grams directly, it can compose known character patterns into novel word forms more effectively than a model forced to rely on pre-defined token vocabularies.

From a modeling standpoint, the n-gram hash embeddings and the local encoder equip BLT to detect and reuse orthographic subpatterns even when they occur in rare forms. The entropy-based patching mechanism further helps by allocating more computation to unpredictable byte regions—regions that often contain lexical or morphological information crucial for translation. Students should understand that in low-resource settings, two sources of advantage accrue to BLT: (1) the elimination of tokenizer-induced sparsity and bias, and (2) the ability to generalize across scripts and novel character sequences because the model learns at the byte level.

# Why token models struggle: lack of direct access to characters and phonology

The empirical gains across noise robustness, phonology, orthographic reasoning, and low-resource translation all trace back to a common shortcoming of token-based models: the loss of direct access to characters. Tokenizers compress byte sequences into atomic units with a finite vocabulary decided before training. This pre-processing choice imposes several constraints.

First, tokenization can be inconsistent: the same word or character sequence may be split differently depending on context or surrounding bytes, as in the incremental tokenization property discussed in BLT. This non-incrementality complicates generative use and weakens continuity of representation for prefixes. Second, subword vocabularies cause information hiding: a token embedding does not explicitly expose constituent characters; the model must implicitly infer them from distributional patterns, which is difficult for low-frequency or novel sequences. Third, tokenizers introduce linguistic and script biases because the vocabulary is learned from a training corpus that may overrepresent some languages or character sequences, disproportionately disadvantaging long-tail languages and scripts.

BLT addresses these limitations by preserving byte-level information through n-gram hash embeddings and local encoder/decoder architectures. The model does not commit to a fixed vocabulary of patches; instead, it forms patches dynamically (space-based or entropy-based) and represents them in latent space via learned encoders. This retains access to character composition while still reaping computational benefits: patches reduce the number of steps the global transformer needs to process, and entropy-based patching adaptively concentrates compute on high-uncertainty byte positions. The cross-attention pooling mechanism explicitly ties byte-level representations to patch latents and back, ensuring the model can both compress redundant patterns and reconstruct fine-grained byte outputs.

For learners, the conceptual lesson is that architecture and pre-processing choices together determine what inductive biases a model has. Tokenizers inject a bias toward subword atomicity that simplifies many tasks but harms others—especially those requiring direct subword introspection or robustness to surface noise. Byte-level modeling, when paired with efficient patching and encoder/decoder designs, changes that bias in ways that yield concrete benefits on long-tail languages, noisy inputs, and phonology.

# Concluding perspective

Overall, the BLT results make a compelling case that direct byte modeling—if done with architectures that manage compute through patches and intelligent local-global processing—can deliver substantial robustness and long-tail generalization benefits. The gains arise from having explicit access to characters and local n-gram structure, combined with dynamic compute allocation via entropy-based patching and cross-attentive encoder/decoder modules. For students preparing to read current literature, this suggests two directions to watch: (1) methods that preserve low-level signal (characters/bytes) while avoiding prohibitive per-byte compute, and (2) hybrid architectures that combine local fine-grained processing with global contextual modeling. BLT exemplifies both approaches and shows how they translate into measurable task-level improvements.

Bootstrapping from Token Models: Byte-ifying LLaMA

# Initialization strategy: copying global weights, reduced LR fine-tuning
One practical route to obtain a Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) without training every component from scratch is to reuse the high-capacity global transformer from an existing, pretrained token-based model such as LLaMA 3. The paper describes a straightforward initialization workflow: take the pretrained global transformer weights from a LLaMA 3.1 model and copy them into the BLT global transformer \mathcal{G}. The BLT architecture is then completed by randomly initializing the two byte-level local modules (the local encoder \mathcal{E} and the local decoder \mathcal{D}), plus any auxiliary embeddings (byte embeddings and n-gram hash tables) that BLT requires but the token model does not.

After initialization, training proceeds with different learning-rate treatments for the preloaded global transformer and for the freshly initialized local modules. Concretely, the global-transformer parameters are updated with a reduced learning rate, while the local encoder/decoder and byte-level embeddings use the standard (larger) learning rate used for training BLT from scratch. The paper reports using a learning-rate schedule where the global-transformer is trained at one-tenth of the learning rate used for the local modules; in symbolic form, if the local modules use learning rate \eta, then the global transformer is updated with learning rate \eta_{\mathcal{G}} = \frac{1}{10}\eta. Training is run for the same number of steps recommended for LLaMA 3’s optimal regime. This differential-rate fine-tuning stabilizes and preserves the semantic knowledge encoded in the pretrained global transformer while allowing the local encoder and decoder to learn how to interface bytes and patches with that global state.

Note that this bootstrapping does not alter the BLT inference-time patching procedure: patch boundaries, entropy models, and the local encoder/decoder architecture remain BLT-specific and are trained to operate with the copied global weights. The initialization is therefore a surgical replacement of only the global autoregressive block with a pretrained token-trained instantiation.

# Empirical outcomes vs training-from-scratch BLT and token baselines
Empirically, the “byte-ified” BLT (BLT initialized from LLaMA 3.1 global weights and fine-tuned with a reduced global learning rate) achieves markedly better performance than both (a) a BLT trained from random initialization on the same flop budget, and (b) a LLaMA 3 tokenizer-based model trained with the same amount of compute. In the experiments reported, BLT-from-LLaMA-3 significantly outperforms the LLaMA 3 and the randomly initialized BLT baselines on challenging evaluations such as MMLU and HumanEval when all models have been trained with the same number of flops. This demonstrates that transferring the high-level, patch-sequence modeling capability of a token-based global transformer into the BLT pipeline yields a strong empirical advantage during further fine-tuning on bytes.

However, the paper also benchmarks against the original LLaMA 3.1 model trained on a much larger pretraining corpus (15T tokens). Compared to that full-scale pretrained LLaMA 3.1, the BLT derived from LLaMA 3.1 shows a modest degradation on some tasks: a small drop on MMLU and HumanEval and larger drops on other benchmarks. These differences reflect the fact that the LLaMA 3.1 baseline benefited from a far larger data scale in its original pretraining run. Thus the bootstrapped BLT both (1) significantly closes the gap with token-trained models when compute budgets are equal and (2) does not fully supersede a very large-data token model that was pretrained on an order-of-magnitude more tokens.

# Interpretation: efficient transfer of high-level knowledge into a tokenizer-free system
The success of this bootstrapping workflow can be interpreted as an efficient transfer of high-level sequence modeling knowledge from a tokenization-based regime into a tokenizer-free byte regime. The global transformer \mathcal{G} is responsible for modeling long-range autoregressive dependencies between patch representations; those dependencies capture syntactic, semantic, and world-knowledge structure that is agnostic to the concrete byte-vs-token representation of local content. By copying pretrained \mathcal{G} weights, BLT inherits that structural capacity and only needs to learn two things during fine-tuning: (1) how to map raw bytes (and short byte n-gram context) into patch latents that are compatible with \mathcal{G}, and (2) how to decode \mathcal{G}’s patch outputs back into byte sequences.

Because the pretrained \mathcal{G} already organizes high-level predictive structure, the local encoder and decoder have a more constrained, easier learning problem: they must learn encoders/decoders that present the same kinds of predictive features to \mathcal{G} that the token-based tokenizer and embedding layers did during the original token-model pretraining. Reducing the global learning rate to \frac{1}{10}\eta during fine-tuning preserves that transferred structure while permitting minor adaptation where necessary. In this view, the bootstrapping acts as a “drop-in” conversion: it converts a token-aware global model into a tokenizer-free BLT by learning lightweight byte-level adapters around a largely fixed backbone.

This also explains why BLT-from-LLaMA 3.1 can outperform a BLT trained from scratch with the same flop budget: starting from a strong global prior short-circuits what would otherwise be a large portion of the training requirement needed to learn long-range abstractions purely from bytes and patches. In other words, the expensive part of learning—global structure and world knowledge—has already been acquired by LLaMA 3.1, and the BLT pipeline merely needs to learn the interface between bytes and that structure.

# Open questions: data mixtures, hyperparameters, and closing residual performance gaps
While the bootstrapping approach is promising, the paper highlights several open questions and practical gaps that merit future investigation.

First, data-mixture mismatch: the pretrained LLaMA 3.1 global transformer was trained with a particular data mixture and tokenizer. After copying into BLT, the byte-level fine-tuning may use a different dataset composition (for example, BLT-1T vs LLaMA 3.1’s 15T mixture). How best to choose or adapt the fine-tuning data mixture to get the most out of the transferred \mathcal{G} is unclear. A mismatch can leave some knowledge underutilized or encourage the model to drift away from useful priors.

Second, hyperparameter choices for fine-tuning require more systematic study. The paper uses a simple reduced-rate rule-of-thumb (\eta_{\mathcal{G}} = \frac{1}{10}\eta) and keeps the global block trainable rather than frozen entirely. But the optimal relative learning rates, schedule lengths, degree of freezing, and possible regularization to prevent catastrophic forgetting are open tuning knobs. It remains to be seen whether different schedules (for example, progressive unfreezing, per-layer learning-rate decay, or even parameter-efficient adapters) could retain transferred knowledge more effectively while enabling faster adaptation.

Third, architectural compatibility and local-module capacity matter. The local encoder/decoder must be expressive enough to produce patch latents that match the statistical substrate \mathcal{G} expects. The BLT experiments showed substantial gains from including n-gram hash embeddings and cross-attention in the encoder/decoder. Determining minimal local-module capacity for successful bootstrapping and whether increased capacity yields diminishing or improving returns are empirical questions.

Fourth, closing the residual performance gap relative to very large token pretrained models (e.g., LLaMA 3.1 trained on 15T tokens) is unresolved. The bootstrapped BLT performs well when budgets are equal but does not fully match models pretrained at far larger scale. It is not yet clear whether that remaining gap is primarily due to (a) missing data scale in the BLT fine-tuning stage, (b) imperfect data mixtures, (c) suboptimal adaptation of \mathcal{G} during fine-tuning, or (d) fundamental limits arising from the change in input granularity (bytes vs tokens). Each of these hypotheses suggests different remedies: longer fine-tuning with more data, reweighting mixtures to mimic token-pretraining domains, more aggressive/longer adaptation of \mathcal{G}, or hybrid strategies that gradually increase byte-to-patch abstraction.

Finally, the paper notes a broader question about whether one can design end-to-end training that learns patching jointly with the global model rather than relying on a separately trained entropy model and preprocessing. If bootstrapping is adopted widely, combining it with an end-to-end patch-boundary learner might further improve transfer and close remaining gaps.

In sum, initializing BLT’s global transformer from a pretrained token-based model is an effective and practical bootstrap: it reuses high-level knowledge, reduces total training flops needed to reach strong performance, and creates a pathway to convert existing tokenizer-dependent models into tokenizer-free BLT systems. At the same time, the approach raises concrete questions about optimal fine-tuning recipes, data mixtures, and the interplay between local adapter capacity and global-model adaptation that future work should systematically address.

Ablations, Practical Guidance, and Future Directions

# Entropy model ablations: size and context effects; diminishing returns beyond ~50M params
The entropy model is the lightweight byte-level autoregressive model used to signal where to break patches. In our ablations we varied both its parameter count (from 1M up to 100M) and its context window (64 to 512 bytes) to measure how these choices affect downstream BLT scaling behavior. The central observation is simple and actionable: larger and longer-context entropy models improve BLT's bits-per-byte (BPB) scaling performance, but the benefit saturates. Empirically we observed clear gains as we increased model size and context up to roughly 50M parameters; beyond that point the marginal returns diminish.

The intuition behind these results is that the entropy model must be good enough to distinguish high-uncertainty bytes (where additional compute is warranted) from predictable bytes (which can be grouped into larger patches). A too-small entropy model will make noisy or inconsistent boundary decisions, undercutting the purpose of dynamic patching; a very large entropy model that is only slightly more accurate still incurs engineering and runtime cost for little additional benefit. Practically, this means choosing an entropy model in the tens of millions of parameters with a receptive field that captures local disambiguating context (e.g., a few hundred bytes) is a good operating point: it yields significant scaling improvements for BLT without wasting resources on a near-plateaued component.

# Patching ablations: strided, space, tokenizer-derived, and entropy-based comparisons
We compared four patching families under controlled conditions where every model sees the same expected number of bytes per batch (so context advantages are neutralized): (1) fixed-stride (every k bytes), (2) space/whitespace-based patching, (3) tokenizer-derived (BPE from Llama 3), and (4) entropy-based dynamic patching.

Fixed-stride is the simplest to implement and makes the patch size explicit, but it lacks context sensitivity: identical substrings can be split inconsistently, and compute is wasted on steps that require little modeling capacity (e.g., long stretches of whitespace or trivial suffix bytes). Space patching fixes some of that by using natural linguistic delimiters: it produces consistent word-level boundaries and tends to allocate a latent Transformer step per word, which is often a good proxy for where difficult predictions occur. In our experiments, space patching substantially outperformed fixed-stride and was a strong baseline.

Tokenizer-derived patching (i.e., BPE tokens) is different in kind because tokens come from a finite vocabulary chosen prior to training; tokens do not give the model direct access to raw bytes. When evaluated in a flop-controlled regime, BPE models provide strong performance because the tokenizer encodes frequent byte patterns into compact tokens. However, tokens impose a hard tradeoff: increasing average token size reduces Transformer steps but forces a proportionately larger embedding/output projection and fixes the vocabulary-dependent inductive bias.

Entropy-based dynamic patching aims to capture where true uncertainty lies by using next-byte entropy estimates from a small LM. This method produced the best scaling behavior overall when coupled with the full BLT architecture. Compared to space patching, entropy patching can vary patch size adaptively, allocating fewer latent steps in predictable contexts and invoking more steps where the next-byte distribution is uncertain. In ablations, dynamic patching outperformed static striding and matched or exceeded tokenizer-based methods when architectural components (cross-attention, n-gram hashes, light-weight local models) were in place.

# Cross-attention placement: strongest gains from decoder CA; encoder CA helps with pooling
Cross-attention is the mechanism that connects byte-level representations and patch-level latents in both directions: encoder cross-attention pools bytes into patch latents, and decoder cross-attention expands patch latents back into bytes.

Our ablations show asymmetric contributions from encoder and decoder cross-attention. Incorporating cross-attention on the decoder side delivered the most consistent and substantial gains. The decoder cross-attention directly conditions byte predictions on the richer latent context produced by the global Transformer, improving final byte-level likelihoods and downstream task performance.

Encoder cross-attention yields smaller but noticeable benefits when the queries are initialized by a pooling operation over the patch bytes (instead of a constant learned vector or a hashed initialization). Pooling initialization produces more informative queries, enabling the encoder cross-attention to construct higher-quality patch latents. Practically, this suggests prioritizing decoder cross-attention in design and using pooled initialization for encoder queries when budget permits. Cross-attention also interacts with patch size: its benefits are more pronounced on datasets and settings where patches become larger, because then the pooling and pooling-to-latent mapping carries more information that the global Transformer can exploit.

# n-gram hash embeddings: optimal table sizes, domain-specific gains, complementarity with CA
Hash n-gram embeddings augment the byte embedding at each position with hashed embeddings of recent byte n-grams (we used n ∈ {3,...,8}). The effect of these embeddings is to inject compact, high-order local context into the local encoder input without inflating model-wide parameters.

Ablations varying the hash table size (0, 100k, 200k, 400k, and experiments up to 500k used in production runs) demonstrate that hash embeddings consistently help, with the largest gains on certain domains (notably Wikipedia and GitHub). Gains are measurable even at modest sizes (100k), and we observed diminishing returns beyond approximately 300k–500k hashed slots. At 8B scale, switching from 500k to 300k hashes changed BPB by about 0.001 on 15k steps — small but measurable.

Importantly, n-gram hashes and cross-attention are complementary. Hashes strengthen local byte-level signals (especially for repetitive or domain-specific patterns), while cross-attention enables effective pooling and global reasoning. Together they close the gap between raw-byte models and token-based baselines; omitting either degrades performance, particularly on structured or code-like domains.

# Local model depth: very light encoder + heavier decoder works well with hash embeddings
When selecting depths for the local encoder (which pools bytes into patches) and the local decoder (which reconstructs bytes), the most favorable tradeoff found in ablations is an extremely light encoder (often just a single transformer layer) paired with a heavier decoder. This design leverages hash n-gram embeddings to give the encoder rich local context with minimal computation, enabling it to produce adequate patch representations cheaply.

The decoder benefits from more capacity because reconstructing bytes from latent patch representations is a generative task that needs expressive local structure and conditioning on previously decoded bytes. Thus increasing decoder depth yields clearer gains than doing the same for the encoder, especially when hash embeddings are present. In practice, this implies designers can save compute by keeping the encoder shallow and allocating model capacity to the global latent Transformer and to the decoder.

# Engineering gaps: wall-clock optimizations for dynamic masking and cross-attn
Although the flop accounting in our experiments is carefully controlled, we explicitly note that existing transformer codebases and libraries are optimized for the static masks and fixed-vocabulary pipelines of token-based models. BLT introduces two implementation patterns that are less well optimized in current frameworks: (1) dynamic, patch-dependent attention masks (both in encoder and decoder cross-attention), and (2) frequent cross-attention blocks whose masks vary per batch and per patch.

We mitigated some of this by using flexible backends (e.g., FlexAttention) and fused implementations where possible. Nevertheless, BLT's real wall-clock performance can lag the theoretical flop advantage because of irregular memory access and mask handling. This is an engineering frontier: optimizing dynamic masking, fused cross-attention kernels for varying query/key/value sizes, and pack/unpack patterns for variable-length patches can materially close the wall-clock gap. The paper highlights these as current limitations and practical opportunities for future engineering work.

# Scaling laws for BLT: reevaluating compute/data/parameter ratios beyond token-based rules
Throughout experiments we used the compute-optimal training regime derived for token-based Llama models to select training budgets and parameter/data ratios. However, BLT changes key aspects of the modeling problem: it alters sequence length dynamics via patching, uses separate local/global modules, and embeds byte-level inductive biases that token-based scaling laws do not capture.

Consequently, relying on token-derived scaling laws can be suboptimal for BLT. The paper explicitly flags the need to recompute and validate scaling relationships (parameter count, training tokens, and flops) tailored to BLT architectures. Early signals in our experiments suggest BLT may allow different optimal tradeoffs — for example, larger patch sizes permit shifting compute from steps to model capacity — so a dedicated study of BLT scaling laws could reveal more favorable training regimes and better choices of model partitioning between local, latent, and decoder components.

# End-to-end learned patching vs separate entropy model; adaptive thresholds at inference
We used a separately trained entropy model to produce next-byte entropy estimates used for patch boundary decisions. This has pragmatic advantages: it is simple, stable, and decouples the patching signal from the main model's weights. The paper reports that this setup works well, but it also notes an important future direction: learning patching end-to-end.

End-to-end patching could enable the global and local models to shape patch boundaries jointly with downstream objectives, potentially yielding better overall performance. However, end-to-end approaches introduce their own challenges (training stability, discrete decisions, and credit assignment) that must be addressed.

In the interim, there is also a useful practical lever in the entropy-based approach: threshold tuning at inference time. For example, we found that reducing the entropy threshold from 0.6 (training-time value) to 0.1 at inference improved downstream task performance at the cost of more latent steps. This demonstrates an express tradeoff you can make on the deployment side: lower thresholds → finer-grained patching → higher compute but potentially better accuracy; higher thresholds → coarser patches → lower compute and possibly small performance degradation. Designing adaptive, task-specific threshold schedules or simple heuristics (e.g., lowering threshold when prompts indicate complex reasoning) is therefore a practical and effective way to manage the speed/quality tradeoff without retraining.

# Practical tips: choosing patch size, equalizing byte contexts, threshold tuning for tasks
The paper yields a number of concrete recommendations for practitioners implementing or experimenting with BLT.

- Choosing a target average patch size: Patch size is a primary knob for inference cost. Larger average patches reduce the number of latent Transformer steps and thus inference flops; the paper demonstrates that average patch sizes of 6–8 bytes can yield substantial inference savings (up to ~50% fewer flops) while still matching or exceeding token-based scaling trends at sufficiently large model sizes. However, very large patches can hurt early-stage or small-model performance. If your priority is inference cost, start around 6–8 bytes and validate downstream performance; if your priority is sample efficiency or smaller-scale models, prefer patch sizes closer to 4–5 bytes.

- Equalizing byte contexts: Because BLT operates on bytes, changing average patch size changes the number of patches per fixed byte budget. To make fair comparisons and to stabilize training, keep the expected number of bytes per batch constant when you vary patching schemes or patch size. This prevents giving larger patches an unfair advantage through effectively increased context window. In practice, we maintain a fixed bytes-per-batch (e.g., 16M bytes) and adjust the sequence length (in patches) so that the expected bytes seen remain constant.

- Threshold tuning for tasks and inference: The entropy threshold used to decide boundaries is an important tradeoff parameter. You can tune it at inference for a fixed pre-trained model: a lower threshold tends to increase patch granularity and improve accuracy at the cost of more compute. We observed that lowering the threshold at inference led to improved task accuracy in some settings (e.g., BLT-Entropy switching from 0.6 to 0.1). Use held-out task data to find a suitable inference threshold that matches your compute/accuracy requirements.

- Handling entropy-context drift: When the entropy model has a large receptive field, repeated or highly structured content (e.g., multiple-choice items or long repeated sequences) can produce ever-larger patches due to entropy drift. Mitigations include resetting the entropy model context at natural delimiters (e.g., newlines) or using monotonicity-based constraints that detect local increases in entropy rather than absolute thresholds. These small adjustments can prevent pathological growth of patches in certain structured content.

- Pack patches for latent batching: Because patches are variable-length, pack patches into batches to avoid padding the global latent Transformer and improve throughput. During training we pack patches so that every batch has the same (or similar) number of patches, avoiding unnecessary latent steps.

# Limitations called out and concrete research directions
The paper makes clear several current limitations and lays out promising directions to address them.

1. Recompute optimal scaling laws for BLT. The current experiments used Llama-derived compute-optimal points. A principled recomputation for BLT could enable more efficient training and different parameter/data tradeoffs.

2. End-to-end learned patching. Building differentiable or learned boundary predictors that integrate into BLT training is a natural next step. This may improve boundary decisions and overall performance but requires careful engineering to handle discrete decisions and stability.

3. Wall-clock optimizations. Engineering work to optimize dynamic masking, cross-attention kernels, and pack/unpack primitives for variable-length patches can make the theoretical flop advantages into practical latency and throughput wins.

4. Better distillation and conversion recipes. The paper reports promising initial results converting tokenized models into BLT by initializing the global Transformer from pre-trained token-based weights and training the local components with a reduced learning rate. Extending and refining this approach (e.g., better schedules, mixture adjustments, or soft alignment techniques) could allow reuse of very large token-trained checkpoints to bootstrap BLT models with fewer flops.

5. Adaptive inference policies. Investigating learned or heuristic policies that adapt entropy thresholds based on prompt characteristics, compute budgets, or intermediate confidence estimates could yield a practical, general-purpose speed/accuracy controller.

6. Domain- or language-specific tuning. Hash table sizing, entropy model sizing, and patch-size priors show domain sensitivity; more systematic studies per domain or language family could refine default settings for production deployments.

# Final recommendations summary
- Use an entropy model of order tens of millions of parameters (≈50M) with a few-hundred-byte context as a sweet spot between performance and cost.
- Prefer entropy-based dynamic patching or space patching over fixed-stride for better allocation of latent Transformer steps; entropy patching is most flexible but requires an entropy model and threshold management.
- Prioritize decoder cross-attention; add encoder cross-attention with pooling-initialized queries if budget allows.
- Include hash n-gram embeddings (hundreds of thousands of hash slots) to strengthen local byte context, especially for structured domains; expect diminishing returns past ~300k–500k.
- Design the local encoder to be minimal (1 layer) and allocate more capacity to the decoder and global latent Transformer.
- Equalize bytes-per-batch when comparing patching regimes; pack patches to avoid padding overhead in the latent Transformer.
- Tune entropy thresholds at inference to trade flops for quality; consider resetting entropy context on delimiters to avoid patch drift on repetitive content.
- Invest engineering effort into optimizing dynamic masks and cross-attention kernels to realize wall-clock gains.

Together, these ablation-driven recommendations provide a practical playbook for building and experimenting with BLT models today, and they point to clear research directions that will likely improve both the performance and deployability of byte-level latent-transformer architectures in the future.
