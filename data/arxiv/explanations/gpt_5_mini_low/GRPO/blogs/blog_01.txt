# How DeepSeekMath Found 120B Math Tokens in the Wild Web

DeepSeekMath’s biggest tactical win wasn’t a fancy model trick — it was getting the data right. The team turned the noisy Common Crawl into a focused, high-quality math corpus of 120B tokens (35.5M math pages) using a practical, repeatable pipeline. This post walks through the pipeline step-by-step: the fastText classifier, iterative domain mining, human-in-the-loop curation, aggressive deduplication, and benchmark decontamination. I’ll also note why the same approach generalizes to other domains such as code.

## Overview: goals and constraints

The objective was simple but hard in practice: extract large quantities of web text that look like math content (explanations, worked problems, proofs, code-based solutions) while avoiding low-quality pages and contamination of evaluation benchmarks. Key constraints influenced design choices:

- Scale: mine hundreds of billions of tokens from Common Crawl efficiently.
- Precision: prefer higher-quality math content rather than blindly increasing token counts.
- Practicality: use a fast, easily trainable classifier with lightweight iterations and human refinement.
- Safety: remove any overlap with evaluation benchmarks to avoid data contamination.

The result: an iterative pipeline that produced 120B math tokens from Common Crawl and an end model (DeepSeekMath-Base 7B) that benefited substantially from the data.

## Step 1 — Seed + fastText classifier

They started with a seed math corpus: OpenWebMath, a high-quality set of math web texts. From that, a fastText classifier was trained to recall “OpenWebMath-like” pages from Common Crawl.

Training details captured in the paper:

- Positive examples: 500,000 data points sampled from the seed (OpenWebMath).
- Negative examples: 500,000 random Common Crawl pages.
- fastText hyperparameters: vector dimension = 256, learning rate = 0.1, max word n-gram length = 3, min word occurrences = 3, epochs = 3.

Why fastText? It’s fast to train and cheap to run at the scale of Common Crawl, making it practical for multiple iterations over billions of pages.

## Step 2 — Aggressive deduping and recall

Before classification, the team reduced the Common Crawl set size using URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication, shrinking the crawl to about 40B HTML pages for initial recall. The classifier was run over this deduplicated corpus to retrieve math-like pages. For quality, they didn’t take everything: pages were ranked by classifier score and only top-ranked pages were kept for further consideration.

In the first iteration they experimented with keeping varying volumes (top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens) and initially retained the top 40B tokens for refinement.

## Step 3 — Iterative domain mining to broaden recall

A single classifier trained on limited positives misses lots of math content because the seed lacks diversity. To expand coverage without sacrificing precision, they used an iterative domain-mining trick:

- Partition Common Crawl by domain (pages sharing the same base URL).
- For each domain compute the percentage of pages that the classifier collected in iteration 1.
- Mark domains where over 10% of pages were collected as “math-related” candidates (examples: mathoverflow.net).
- Manually inspect and annotate math-relevant URL paths within those domains (for example, /questions on mathoverflow).
- Add uncollected pages from those annotated URLs into the positive set.
- Retrain the fastText classifier with the expanded positives and repeat recall.

This loop—recall, analyze domains, augment positives, retrain—was run multiple times. After four iterations they arrived at 35.5M math pages totaling 120B tokens. They also observed diminishing returns: by the fourth iteration nearly 98% of the data had already been captured in the third iteration, so collection halted.

## Step 4 — Human-in-the-loop refinement

Human annotation was used to improve classifier quality in later iterations. Specifically, once math-related domains and candidate URLs were identified automatically, humans annotated URLs and pages to enrich the positive set. The refreshed positives were used to retrain fastText, improving recall for diverse math content.

This human-in-the-loop step is important: it injects diversity into the positives (different site structures, content styles, languages) so the classifier generalizes beyond the initial seed.

## Step 5 — Benchmark decontamination

To avoid leaking evaluation data into pretraining, DeepSeekMath applied explicit decontamination rules. Any web page with substrings that matched evaluation benchmarks was removed. The rule:

- Remove any text segment containing a 10-gram substring that exactly matches any substring from evaluation benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.).
- For benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, use exact matching to filter out contaminated pages.

This conservative matching reduces the risk of benchmark contamination while being straightforward to implement.

## Why deduping and near-deduping matter

Repetition in web data causes smaller corpora to be seen many times during training, flattening gains. By applying URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication up front, the pipeline ensured that the eventual math pages were unique and diverse, which the authors show leads to a steeper learning curve during pretraining compared to smaller math corpora that quickly plateau.

## Multilingual coverage and corpus scale

Two practical outcomes of the approach:

- The resulting DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese being the most represented languages. This multilinguality improved Chinese math benchmarks compared to English-only math corpora.
- Scale: 35.5M math pages → 120B tokens. The paper emphasizes that this is multiple times larger than prior math corpora like MathPile (8.9B tokens) and OpenWebMath (13.6B tokens).

## How the team validated quality

Rather than assume bigger is better, the team trained models on different math corpora for controlled experiments. A 1.3B parameter model trained on their DeepSeekMath Corpus for 150B tokens outperformed models trained on other math corpora across a range of benchmarks. That empirical validation shows the selection pipeline achieved both scale and quality.

## Why this generalizes to other domains (e.g., code)

The pipeline is domain-agnostic in principle. Replace the seed corpus (OpenWebMath) with a small high-quality seed for the target domain (for instance, code samples or documentation), then:

- Train a fast classifier (fastText or similar) to recognize domain-like pages.
- Deduplicate and recall across Common Crawl.
- Identify domain-rich domains by the same >10% collection heuristic and expand positives via manual annotation or heuristics.
- Iterate, retrain, and decontaminate relevant evaluation benchmarks.

The paper explicitly notes the pipeline is applicable to other domains such as coding; empirically they initialized DeepSeekMath with a code-trained base model and saw benefits, implying a productive synergy between code data and math data when curated carefully.

## Practical takeaways and caveats

- Use a fast, scalable classifier (fastText worked well here): speed matters when scanning billions of pages.
- Iteration beats one-shot filtering: domain mining plus human annotation expands coverage without exploding noise.
- Deduplication up front reduces useless repetition and improves effective corpus quality.
- Conservative decontamination is essential to avoid evaluation leakage.
- Human annotation is small but high-leverage: focus it on adding diverse positive examples (site structures, URL patterns, languages).
- The approach finds lots of useful content on the open web; closed sources or specialized corpora are not the only path to high-quality domain data.

Limitations mentioned by the authors: some math subdomains (e.g., geometric problems) remained weaker — likely a sign of selection bias in the collected pages — and arXiv-only content did not give consistent improvements. So data choice and diversity matter even after large-scale collection.

## Final note

DeepSeekMath’s 120B-token corpus shows the payoff of an engineering-first approach to data: combine a lean classifier, sensible deduplication, iterative domain discovery, and focused human labeling. The result is a large, high-quality, and reusable math corpus that materially improves model reasoning — and the same pipeline is directly applicable to other domains where high-precision extraction from the web is needed.
