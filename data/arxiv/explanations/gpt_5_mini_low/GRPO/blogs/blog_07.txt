\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# The ArXiv Surprise: Why Research Papers Didn’t Move the Needle

DeepSeekMath is a striking example of engineering around data and learning algorithms to push mathematical reasoning in an open 7B model. One of the paper’s counter-intuitive findings deserves a standalone discussion: adding arXiv papers to math pre-training did not improve, and in some cases slightly degraded, downstream mathematical reasoning across the benchmarks they tested. This post summarizes what the authors did, the evidence, plausible explanations grounded in the paper, key caveats they raise, and concrete directions for better data curation and experiments.

## What the authors tested — the experimental setup

The DeepSeekMath team compared multiple math-oriented pre-training corpora. Two arXiv-centric sources they evaluated were:

- MathPile: an 8.9B-token corpus where over 85% of content comes from arXiv-style scientific papers (as used in prior work).
- ArXiv-RedPajama: a 28B-token extraction of arXiv LaTeX sources (with preambles, comments, macros, bibliographies removed).

They ran controlled training runs on two model families:

- DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B: trained for 150B tokens on each corpus in isolation.
- DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B: trained for 40B tokens on each corpus in isolation.

Evaluation covered a range of math and reasoning benchmarks used throughout the paper: GSM8K and MATH (quantitative reasoning), MMLU-STEM (multiple-choice STEM), and miniF2F (informal-to-formal theorem proving). The key reported outcome: when trained on an arXiv-only corpus, both model sizes showed no notable improvements and sometimes worse performance across these tasks.

## The result, in plain terms

Contrary to common practice (many prior efforts incorporate large arXiv subsets), these controlled experiments suggest that, for the models and training regimes tested, arXiv tokens alone did not help the model’s downstream mathematical reasoning and may even have been detrimental.

This is not a blanket condemnation of arXiv. The authors explicitly note limits and possible confounders (more on those below). But it does challenge the simple assumption “more arXiv = better math reasoning” under the tested conditions.

## Why might arXiv fail to help? (Grounded hypotheses from the paper)

The paper discusses—and the experiments suggest—several plausible reasons why arXiv-only pre-training underperformed. These are consistent with observations and prior intuitions, and they’re all mentioned or implied in the study:

1. Format and representation mismatch
   - arXiv content is LaTeX-heavy. Even after stripping macros and preambles, the remaining structure (formulas, display math, custom notation) may not map cleanly to the language-style reasoning that the benchmarks reward.
   - Much valuable math in arXiv is formal or terse: definitions, theorems, dense proof sketches. Benchmarks like GSM8K reward step-by-step, worked-out arithmetic reasoning that is stylistically different.

2. Domain and task mismatch
   - Research papers focus on new results, abstract theory, or terse proofs rather than pedagogical worked examples or stepwise numerical problem-solving typical of benchmarks such as GSM8K.
   - Models trained on arXiv may learn to represent high-level formal exposition rather than the procedural chain-of-thought style used in benchmark solutions.

3. Signal-to-noise and scale issues
   - ArXiv text includes a lot of domain-specific notation, citations, and non-expository content (e.g., tables, figures, macros) which can reduce the effective signal for the target tasks.
   - If the amount of arXiv training is large but not well-curated, the model may overfit to arXiv-style patterns or be distracted by irrelevant tokens.

4. Lack of worked examples and explicit reasoning traces
   - Benchmarks benefit from exposure to worked-out, stepwise solutions. Many arXiv papers do not contain such material in a form similar to benchmark chain-of-thought traces.

5. Model scale and training recipe interactions
   - The paper’s experiments use 1.3B and 7B models. It is possible (they explicitly note) that arXiv benefits might materialize only at larger scales or under different pre-training schedules.

## Caveats — important limitations the authors themselves call out

The authors carefully list limits to this conclusion; keep these in mind before generalizing:

- Tasks not studied: They did not evaluate whether arXiv helps other math-related tasks such as informalization of theorems, advanced theorem-proving behaviors, or specialized symbolic reasoning tasks where research-style exposition could be directly useful.
- Combination effects: They did not fully measure the effect of combining arXiv with other data distributions (e.g., mixing arXiv + web math + worked examples) at various ratios. Negative signal could be mitigated in mixed corpora.
- Model scale: Their models are mid-sized (1.3B, 7B). Benefits of arXiv-style content may show up only at larger parameter counts or with different compute budgets.
- Preprocessing choices: Different LaTeX parsing, equation normalization, or autoformalization pipelines might extract more useful signals from papers than the preprocessing used in their experiments.

Because of these caveats, the result should be interpreted as: “arXiv-only training did not help under these tested setups,” not “arXiv is useless for math models in general.”

## What better curation could look like (practical suggestions grounded in the paper’s reasoning)

If arXiv-style content is potentially valuable but underperforming in these experiments, the likely path forward is smarter extraction and mixing rather than raw inclusion. The paper suggests (implicitly via their focus on data engineering) several pragmatic directions:

1. Extract and emphasize worked examples and expository sections
   - Parse papers to separate introductions, examples, and explanatory paragraphs from dense theorem/proof statements. Prioritize passages that resemble step-by-step reasoning.

2. Convert LaTeX math into reasoning-friendly text
   - Render or translate display formulas and inline math into a linearized, human-readable representation that preserves semantics but is more consistent with natural-language reasoning traces.

3. Build paired informal/formal or sketch/full-proof examples
   - When possible, pair informal explanations with formal proofs (or vice versa) so the model learns mappings between reasoning styles. This is especially relevant for tasks like autoformalization.

4. Deduplicate, denoise, and normalize notation
   - Remove extraneous artifacts (figure captions, bibliography content, compilation remnants) and normalize recurring notation to reduce spurious token patterns.

5. Mix and balance data sources
   - Don’t rely on arXiv alone. Combine arXiv-derived content with web-sourced math pages, solved problem datasets, and stepwise solutions. The DeepSeekMath paper itself relies heavily on web math content (their highest gains came from a curated Common Crawl math corpus).

6. Targeted sampling and curriculum
   - Use a curriculum that starts from worked examples and pedagogical content, then gradually incorporates research-style text. Or sample papers selectively by section and by discipline relevance.

7. Evaluate preprocessing variants and scale
   - Systematically test different LaTeX-to-text transformations, and try the same experiments at larger model scales or with larger compute budgets.

## Concrete follow-up experiments to try (based on the paper’s methodology and limitations)

- Mixed-corpus ablations: train with varying arXiv:web-math ratios to find a sweet spot where arXiv adds value.
- Section-aware training: include only introductions and example sections from arXiv papers and compare to whole-document training.
- Preprocessing ablations: compare raw LaTeX tokens vs. rendered linearized math vs. symbolic placeholders vs. natural-language paraphrases for formulas.
- Scale sweep: repeat promising preprocessing + mixture experiments at larger parameter counts to test scale dependence.
- Task-specific curation: for theorem-proof tasks, extract theorem/proof triples and compare to generic arXiv inclusion.

## Bottom line

The DeepSeekMath paper provides a careful, controlled observation: arXiv-only pre-training did not improve—and sometimes slightly hurt—mathematical reasoning performance for the tested models and training recipes. This result is a useful corrective to the default assumption that more research-paper text is always better for math capabilities.

However, the finding is not an indictment of arXiv content per se. Rather, it highlights that raw research text is a different signal than the pedagogical, worked-out reasoning examples that many math benchmarks demand. The practical implication is that we should invest in better extraction, normalization, and mixing strategies for arXiv material (and systematically test model scale effects), instead of blindly scaling up raw research-paper tokens.

The authors’ broader message is clear and actionable: data curation matters, and careful engineering of what to include from research papers—and how to transform it—may be the difference between neutral/negative and positive gains for math reasoning models.
