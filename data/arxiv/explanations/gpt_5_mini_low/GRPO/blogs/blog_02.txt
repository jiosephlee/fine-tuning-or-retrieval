\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Small Model, Big Brains: Why DeepSeekMath-7B Rivals Giants

DeepSeekMath-7B is a striking example of how careful data curation and targeted training can let a relatively small model punch well above its weight on structured reasoning tasks. Trained from a code-initialized 7B model and continued with massive, math-focused web data, DeepSeekMath-7B reaches competition-level performance on math benchmarks that approaches that of much larger closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. This post walks through the three practical ideas that make this possible: (1) curated math pre-training at scale, (2) starting from a code model, and (3) reinforcement learning tailored for math (GRPO). I’ll keep the math light but precise.

## The headline results (so you don’t miss them)
- DeepSeekMath-RL 7B scores 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without tool use or voting tricks, and self-consistency over 64 samples reaches 60.9%.
- After RL, GSM8K accuracy reaches 88.2% (chain-of-thought setting).
- The base 7B model (after math pretraining) already outperforms many open-source models: e.g., DeepSeekMath-Base achieves 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH — comparable to much larger prior models.

If you expected these numbers only from huge models, that’s exactly the point: carefully engineered data and training choices matter a lot.

## 1) Data: quality and scale beat raw parameter count
One of the central ideas in DeepSeekMath is that mathematical reasoning benefits tremendously from high-quality, domain-focused pretraining data, even when the model is relatively small.

How they built the math corpus
- They iteratively mined Common Crawl using a fastText classifier seeded with OpenWebMath examples. The classifier was refined over multiple iterations by adding more positive examples found by domain analysis and human annotation.
- After deduplication and filtering to avoid benchmark contamination, the final DeepSeekMath Corpus contains about 35.5M math-related web pages, totaling 120B math tokens. This corpus is multilingual (notably English and Chinese).

Why this matters
- The DeepSeekMath Corpus is several times larger than previously released math web corpora (e.g., OpenWebMath and MathPile) and was shown empirically to be higher quality in downstream math tasks.
- Pretraining on this focused corpus produced strong few-shot chain-of-thought performance and delivered sustained improvements across benchmarks rather than plateauing quickly (a common issue when training on smaller or repeatedly sampled corpora).

Practical takeaway: if you want a model to get better at structured reasoning, invest in a large, carefully filtered, domain-specific corpus — scale plus relevance beats blind parameter scaling.

## 2) Start from code: code training helps math reasoning
Another key design decision: DeepSeekMath-Base was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (a code-trained 7B model) and then continued training on the math corpus.

What they found
- Code pretraining improved mathematical reasoning both with and without tool/program use. In particular, models that had gone through code training solved program-aided math problems more effectively and made the subsequent math training more efficient.
- Mixing code and math tokens in a single training stage can avoid catastrophic forgetting while preserving gains in both coding and math capabilities on the smaller model sizes used here.

Why code helps
- Code exposes models to precise, structured, and compositional patterns (variables, control flow, symbolic manipulation) that resemble the formal structure of mathematical solutions. This prior makes the model better at generating stepwise, executable reasoning (useful for program-of-thought and tool-aided solving).

Practical takeaway: for tasks that require structured stepwise reasoning, initializing from a code model is a surprisingly practical and effective shortcut.

## 3) RL for math: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
After supervised instruction tuning, DeepSeekMath used a reinforcement learning stage that significantly boosted performance. Instead of vanilla PPO, the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant tailored to reward-model-based fine-tuning for reasoning tasks.

What GRPO does differently
- Removes the learned critic/value function (which in PPO is expensive to train and memory-hungry).
- For each question, GRPO samples a group of G outputs from the old policy, scores them with a reward model, and uses the group statistics as the baseline for advantage estimation. Concretely, advantages are computed relative to the group mean/std, so the reward signal is inherently comparative across sampled responses for the same prompt.
- It also adds a KL regularizer between the policy and a reference model directly into the GRPO objective, instead of embedding the KL into per-token rewards.

A simplified view of the idea
- PPO needs a value model V to compute advantages A_t. GRPO replaces V with a group-based baseline: compare each sampled output to the group of outputs for the same question, and use those relative scores to shape the policy gradient.

Why this helps
- Reward models for reasoning are typically trained on comparisons (which output is better for the same question). GRPO’s group-relative estimation matches that comparative nature and removes the extra overhead of a value network.
- In experiments, GRPO improves top-of-distribution accuracy and increases the chance that a correct solution is elevated into the model’s Top-K outputs (see “Why RL works” below).

Practical takeaway: when you have a reliable comparative reward signal, using group-relative RL simplifies training and reduces resource cost while preserving (or improving) effectiveness.

## Why RL seems to help: Maj@K vs Pass@K
An important diagnostic result: reinforcement learning improved Maj@K (majority/top-K robustness) but not necessarily Pass@K (raw capability measured by single sample success). That is, RL makes correct answers more likely to appear among the top-K sampled outputs — it “sharpens” the distribution toward better outputs — without necessarily increasing the model’s underlying problem-solving capability on a per-sample basis.

Implication
- RL here acts to concentrate probability mass on already-possible correct reasoning traces. That is valuable in practice (self-consistency, majority voting, and sampling-based strategies get better), but it also means the RL gains are, to some extent, distributional rather than reflecting fundamental algorithmic advances in reasoning.

## Some additional practical findings
- ArXiv text: Counterintuitively, adding arXiv papers (LaTeX sources or arXiv-derived corpora) did not meaningfully improve math benchmark performance in their experiments and sometimes hurt it. That suggests not all “math text” is equally useful; web math pages mined and filtered for problem/solution style may be more directly helpful for solving benchmark-style problems.
- Multilingual data: because their corpus included substantial non-English math content (notably Chinese), the model improved on Chinese math benchmarks too.
- Instruction tuning and RL were done with problem/solution pairs including chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated formats — the diversity of formats likely helped.

## Limitations and where the model still falls short
- Geometry and some theorem-proof tasks remain weaker than top closed-source models. The authors note failures on certain triangle/ellipse problems, pointing to possible pretraining/fine-tuning data coverage gaps.
- Few-shot scaling: GPT-4 still benefits more from few-shot examples than DeepSeekMath does; the smaller model shows similar zero-shot and few-shot behavior.
- Reward-model dependence: GRPO and other RL-based methods rely heavily on the reward model’s quality and generalization; noisy or miscalibrated reward signals remain a risk.

## Bottom line
DeepSeekMath-7B demonstrates that careful engineering — a large, curated domain corpus, a code-initialized starting point, and a reward-aware, resource-efficient RL variant — can push a 7B model into performance territory that previously seemed reserved for much larger models. The work underlines a broader lesson: for structured problems like mathematical reasoning, data and training design can be as important as raw parameter count. If you want a practical, efficient route to strong reasoning performance, focus first on targeted data and training recipes that match the domain and the supervision signal.
