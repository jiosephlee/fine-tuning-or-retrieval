# GRPO, Explained: The Leaner, Meaner RL That Boosts Math Reasoning

DeepSeekMath introduced a neat trick for reinforcement tuning of language models that want to get better at math: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). It gives you most of PPO’s benefits while ditching the heavyweight critic, slashing memory cost, and aligning naturally with how reward models are trained (they compare answers on the same question). In DeepSeekMath’s experiments, GRPO helped their 7B model reach 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark (and 88.2% on GSM8K), using only chain-of-thought instruction data during RL.

This post breaks GRPO down into what it does, why it works, how it’s computed, and some practical takeaways — with minimal math and clear intuition.

## The idea in one sentence
Instead of learning a separate value function (the critic) to estimate a baseline for advantage computation, GRPO forms a baseline from a group of outputs sampled for the same question and computes advantages relative to that group. Use those group-relative advantages in a PPO-like clipped objective, and regularize with a KL term against a reference policy.

## Why that matters
- Memory & compute: No critic model means you don’t need to store/train an extra large network alongside the policy. For large LLMs this is a big win.
- Natural fit for preference-style rewards: Reward models are usually trained to compare different candidate answers to the same prompt. GRPO’s group baseline uses exactly that comparative information.
- Robust updates: By normalizing rewards inside each group, GRPO stabilizes the scale of advantages and therefore the policy updates.

## How GRPO works (intuitively)
1. For each question q, sample a group of G outputs {o1, o2, ..., oG} from the current (old) policy.
2. Score each output with a reward model, producing rewards r1, r2, ..., rG.
3. Convert these raw rewards into group-relative advantages. For outcome supervision, every token in oi gets the same normalized group reward; for process supervision, each reasoning step receives a reward and token-level advantages sum the (normalized) future step rewards.
4. Maximize a PPO-like surrogate objective across tokens, where the per-token advantage is the group-relative advantage. Also add an explicit KL regularizer between the current policy and a reference policy (usually the current policy snapshot), rather than embedding KL in each token reward.
5. Iterate: re-sample groups from the evolving policy and (optionally) periodically re-train the reward model on newer data.

That surrogate looks like a group-averaged PPO objective; symbolically the GRPO objective (compressed) is:

J_GRPO(θ) = E_{q, {o_i}∼π_{old}} [ (1/G) ∑_{i=1}^G (1/|o_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|o_i|} min( r^{ratio} · Â_{i,t}, clip(r^{ratio},1−ε,1+ε) · Â_{i,t} ) − β · D_{KL}(π_θ || π_ref) ]

where r^{ratio} denotes the probability-ratio term for that token and Â_{i,t} is the group-relative advantage for token t in output i. The paper gives the full formula and implementation details; the core idea is replacing a learned critic baseline with the within-group mean/std normalization.

## Group-relative advantage — two flavors

Outcome supervision (coarse):
- Each sampled output oi gets a single terminal reward ri.
- Normalize the group rewards: \tilde{r}_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r).
- Set token advantages equal to \tilde{r}_i: Â_{i,t} = \tilde{r}_i for all t in oi.

Process supervision (fine-grained):
- The reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., after each chain-of-thought step).
- Normalize all step rewards across the group.
- For a token at position t, its advantage is the sum of normalized rewards of steps that occur at or after t:
  Â_{i,t} = ∑_{index(j) ≥ t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.
- This produces token-level credit assignment aligned with the reasoning process.

Process supervision typically gives finer guidance and empirically improves performance over coarse outcome-only supervision.

## KL regularization: how GRPO keeps the policy grounded
PPO often includes a KL penalty via the reward shaping term. GRPO instead directly adds a KL term to the objective:

− β · D_{KL}(π_θ || π_ref)

and uses an unbiased estimator for per-token KL when needed. Treating KL this way simplifies advantage calculation and gives a clean regularizer that prevents runaway policy drift away from the reference policy.

## Why GRPO outperforms simpler alternatives
- Online vs offline sampling: GRPO uses online sampling from the live policy (not only SFT-generated offline outputs), which matters as the policy diverges during training. Online sampling captures the policy’s current failure modes and improvements.
- Graded feedback: Unlike rejection-based fine-tuning approaches that just keep or drop outputs (i.e., binary signals), GRPO leverages continuous reward magnitudes to produce graded advantages (penalize bad outputs more; reward good ones more).
- Process supervision: Where available, step-wise rewards help assign credit more precisely, improving learning efficiency.

## Practical configuration highlights (from the paper)
- Group size: In the DeepSeekMath RL runs, they sampled G = 64 outputs per question. Large groups make the group statistics (mean/std) more stable.
- Data used: They trained RL on chain-of-thought-format questions from GSM8K and MATH (about 144K questions), starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B.
- Learning details: policy LR ~ 1e-6, KL coefficient β around 0.04, max token length 1024, batch size 1024 for the RL stage in their setup.
- Reward model: initially trained from the base model; iterative RL includes periodically refreshing the reward model using new sampled outputs (replay with 10% historical data).

## Results (what it bought them)
Using GRPO on their 7B chain-of-thought instruction-tuned model:
- GSM8K (CoT): improved to 88.2%
- MATH (CoT): improved to 51.7%
- Self-consistency 64-sample result mentioned in the paper: 60.9% on MATH

Importantly, this RL boost came from only a subset of instruction tuning data (GSM8K and MATH CoT-format), yet GRPO improved performance on both in-domain and some out-of-domain math tasks.

## When to use GRPO
- You want RL improvements but lack resources to train a large critic alongside the policy.
- Your reward signal is comparative (preference/ranking-style) or you can get scores across multiple candidates per prompt.
- You can afford to sample many outputs per prompt (GRPO benefits from larger group sizes).
- You want a method that integrates well with process-level reward supervision (step-wise scoring).

## Limitations and cautions
- Sampling cost: GRPO needs many samples per prompt (they used 64), which raises inference cost during the exploration stage.
- Reward model quality matters: GRPO leans on the reward model; noisy or poorly-generalized reward models can mislead training. The paper highlights the need for better reward generalization and uncertainty estimation.
- Not a magic bullet: GRPO improves the distributional robustness (Maj@K) more than base reasoning capability (Pass@K); it tends to promote putting a correct answer higher in the output distribution rather than necessarily learning fundamentally new problem-solving skills.

## Bottom line
GRPO is a pragmatic, resource-friendly RL recipe for LLM fine-tuning: replace the critic with a group baseline, normalize rewards within groups, use graded advantages (optionally process-level), and regularize with KL to a reference policy. For math reasoning at the 7B scale, that combination produced substantial gains — tipping MATH to 51.7% and GSM8K to 88.2% in the DeepSeekMath experiments — while keeping the RL memory footprint much lower than PPO with a learned critic.

If you’re budget-limited on memory but can afford wide sampling per prompt, GRPO is an attractive option to try for preference-style reward problems.
