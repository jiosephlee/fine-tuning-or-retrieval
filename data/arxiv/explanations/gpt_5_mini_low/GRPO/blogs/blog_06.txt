\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Benchmarks, Decoded: What GSM8K, MATH, miniF2F, MMLU, and BBH Really Measure

DeepSeekMath is a focused attempt to push mathematical reasoning in an open 7B model. The paper reports wins across a range of benchmarks—some testing elementary arithmetic with words, some testing contest-style algebra and calculus, some testing formal theorem proving, and some testing general reasoning and code. Below I summarize what each benchmark measures, why it matters, and what DeepSeekMath achieved on it (numbers come from the paper).

## GSM8K — grade‑school multi‑step arithmetic word problems
What it measures: GSM8K targets grade‑school level quantitative reasoning. Problems are short word problems that typically require chaining a few straightforward arithmetic steps, and they’ve become the standard diagnostic for “chain‑of‑thought” style reasoning in LLMs.

Why it matters: Success on GSM8K shows a model can parse natural language, map it to basic mathematical operations, and keep a small stepwise reasoning trace.

DeepSeekMath results: 
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B reaches 64.2% using few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting, outperforming several larger or prior math models.
- After instruction tuning and reinforcement learning (GRPO), DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 88.2% on GSM8K (chain‑of‑thought), a very strong result for a 7B open model.

Implication: The pre‑training corpus and targeted tuning substantially improved robustness on simple multi‑step arithmetic reasoning—especially after RL, which concentrates probability mass on better solutions.

## MATH — competition‑level contest problems
What it measures: MATH is a collection of high‑school to early college level contest problems (algebra, number theory, calculus, combinatorics). Problems are longer, require deeper multi‑step reasoning, and often need more symbolic manipulation and strategy than GSM8K.

Why it matters: MATH is widely used to judge higher‑order mathematical reasoning beyond textbook arithmetic. Performance here reflects how well a model handles multi‑stage planning, algebraic manipulations, and nontrivial solution structure.

DeepSeekMath results:
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B scores 36.2% on MATH with chain‑of‑thought prompting, outperforming Minerva 540B (a much larger closed model) at the base model stage.
- After supervised instruction tuning (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) and then GRPO reinforcement learning (DeepSeekMath-RL), the model reaches 51.7% on MATH without external tool use or majority‑voting tricks.
- With self‑consistency sampling (64 samples), DeepSeekMath can push MATH accuracy to 60.9% (the paper reports self‑consistency over 64 samples gives 60.9%).

Implication: The combination of a large, targeted math corpus, instruction tuning with chain‑of‑thought/program‑of‑thought formats, and GRPO-style RL closes much of the gap to top closed models. The self‑consistency jump shows sampling and aggregation still matter a lot on harder math.

## miniF2F (Isabelle) — informal → formal theorem proving
What it measures: miniF2F tests autoformalization: convert an informal proof/statement into a formal proof script that a proof assistant (Isabelle here) can check. This is an extremely demanding task: the model must produce syntactically correct formal language and a logically complete proof that the assistant accepts.

Why it matters: Formal theorem proving exercises precise symbolic reasoning and programmatic syntax. Success means the model can bridge natural mathematical text and mechanized reasoning tools.

DeepSeekMath results:
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B shows strong few‑shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F with Isabelle, producing formal proofs where Sledgehammer (an automated prover) can fill in missing details in many cases.

Implication: Math‑focused pre‑training improved the model’s ability to write formal proof scripts and work with a proof assistant pipeline, not just free‑text reasoning.

## MMLU and BBH — general knowledge and hard reasoning benchmarks
What they measure:
- MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) is a broad multiple‑choice benchmark covering many subjects (including STEM). It tests factual and conceptual knowledge plus reasoning over diverse topics.
- BBH (BIG‑Bench Hard) is a set of difficult tasks designed to require multi‑step reasoning and sophisticated generalization.

Why they matter: Improvements here show that math pre‑training doesn’t only improve math; it can transfer to broader reasoning and understanding capabilities.

DeepSeekMath results:
- Math pre‑training improved DeepSeekMath-Base performance on both MMLU and BBH relative to the base (DeepSeek-Coder) initialization. The paper reports significant gains in these benchmarks after math pre‑training, indicating cross‑task transfer.
- Importantly, including code tokens in training maintained coding performance on HumanEval/MBPP—math training did not wreck programming ability because the model started from a code‑trained initialization and retained code data during continual training.

Implication: Focused math pre‑training can lift general reasoning benchmarks. Starting from a code‑trained model and continuing to include code during math training helps preserve (and in some cases synergize with) coding skills.

## What the differences between these benchmarks tell us
- GSM8K targets short multi‑step numerical reasoning; high scores suggest good chain‑of‑thought behavior on simple problems.
- MATH targets deeper, strategic mathematics; gains here reflect improved problem decomposition, algebraic manipulation, and multi‑stage planning.
- miniF2F (Isabelle) measures the ability to produce formal, machine‑checkable proofs—a qualitatively different kind of symbolic precision.
- MMLU and BBH evaluate transfer: can mathematical pre‑training improve broader knowledge and hard reasoning tasks?
- The presence of program‑of‑thought / tool‑integrated evaluations (where models write and execute Python) shows that mixing code into training and evaluating with tools raises practical problem‑solving performance.

## How DeepSeekMath achieved these gains (briefly)
The paper highlights two main drivers:
1. A large, carefully mined web math corpus (120B math tokens from Common Crawl) that is multilingual and higher quality than prior public math corpora. This corpus is the backbone for the pre‑training gains.
2. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a PPO variant that computes advantage within groups of sampled outputs (no separate value network), saving memory and aligning well with comparison‑style reward models. GRPO plus process supervision further raises performance on in‑domain tasks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) and even improves some out‑of‑domain math benchmarks (e.g., CMATH: 84.6% → 88.8% reported in the paper).

## Practical takeaways
- Different math benchmarks probe different capabilities: from surface‑level arithmetic chain‑of‑thought (GSM8K) to contest math strategy (MATH) to formalization (miniF2F).
- Large, targeted math pre‑training combined with instruction tuning and RL can substantially improve performance even in a compact 7B model.
- Math pre‑training can transfer to general reasoning tasks (MMLU, BBH) and preserve coding skill when code data and code‑initialized weights are used.
- Post‑training techniques (self‑consistency sampling, program execution) still give substantial gains on hard math problems.
