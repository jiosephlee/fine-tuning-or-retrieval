# A Unified Map of ‘Alignment’ Methods—and Why RL Actually Helped

## Introduction

There is a growing set of techniques used to push language models toward better, more aligned behavior: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), actor-critic methods like PPO, and new variants like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). They are often presented as separate ideas with different motivations. In DeepSeekMath, the authors show these methods can be understood under a compact, practical framework with three core components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm. Framing alignment this way makes it clearer why some techniques work better than others for mathematical reasoning, and it clarifies an important empirical finding from the paper: reinforcement learning (RL) improved the model’s robustness (Maj@K) rather than its base “Pass@K” capability.

This post walks through that unified view, highlights why online sampling and graded (continuous) rewards matter, summarizes GRPO at a high level, and explains the key takeaway about what RL actually changed in model behavior.

## The unified framework

At its heart, each of these methods updates model parameters by computing a gradient of the form

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\ \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\right],

where the three pieces determine everything practical:

- Data Source (\mathcal{D}): where (question, output) pairs come from. Is the data sampled from the currently trained policy (online) or from a fixed SFT model (offline)?
- Reward Function (\pi_{rf}): how we score or compare outputs. This can be a rule (correct/incorrect), a learned reward model that outputs continuous scores, or pairwise preferences.
- Algorithm (GC): how the method turns reward signals into per-token gradient coefficients (the multiplier in the formula above). This includes whether we use clipping (PPO-style), pairwise losses (DPO), or accept/reject logic (RFT).

Viewed this way, SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO are points in a 3-dimensional design space rather than unrelated islands.

- SFT: offline data source, no reward model, algorithm is standard supervised log-likelihood on human examples.
- RFT: typically offline — sample many outputs from the SFT model, filter those with correct answers, and fine-tune on the filtered set (effectively a binary rule-based reward).
- DPO: offline samples with pairwise preference loss that directly optimizes for higher pairwise likelihood of preferred outputs.
- PPO: online sampling (actor-based), uses a learned reward model, and computes per-token advantages via a learned value function (actor-critic).
- GRPO: online sampling too, uses a learned reward model, but replaces the critic/value network with a group-based baseline computed from several outputs sampled for the same question.

This abstraction makes patterns and trade-offs obvious: for example, whether training uses online or offline data strongly influences whether the policy can adapt to its own evolving distribution; and whether the reward is binary or continuous affects whether the algorithm can differentially strengthen partially-correct vs fully-correct outputs.

## Why online sampling matters

The paper compares offline and online variants (RFT vs Online RFT), and shows online sampling gives clear advantages during later stages of training. Intuitively:

- Offline approaches (sample once from SFT) can only correct biases present in the fixed sample distribution. As the trained policy diverges from the SFT model, the offline data becomes less representative.
- Online sampling continuously explores the current policy’s outputs. This lets the training loop react to the policy’s actual mistakes and modes of behavior, enabling larger and sustained gains as the model improves.

Empirically, Online RFT outperforms RFT: initially they are similar (policies are close), but online sampling yields more benefit in later iterations when the policy departs from the SFT distribution.

## Why graded (continuous) rewards matter

Another important axis is how rewards are used. Binary rule-based rewards (correct/incorrect) are simple and useful, but they collapse all correct outputs into the same signal and ignore gradations of quality. A learned reward model that scores outputs on a continuous scale lets an algorithm differentiate among outputs that are partially right, factually accurate in parts, or entirely correct.

This matters because:

- Binary reinforcement (e.g., RFT) treats all correct answers equally and does not penalize near-misses. It therefore can only push probability mass toward any correct-looking outputs without preference among them.
- Graded rewards let the optimizer assign stronger positive gradients to clearly superior outputs and negative gradients to worse ones. That more informative signal produces a richer gradient coefficient function GC in the unified formula and yields finer control over the policy distribution.

GRPO explicitly leverages graded rewards: it normalizes and uses the relative scores within a sampled group to compute advantages, allowing fine-grained reinforcement and penalization across outputs for the same question.

## GRPO in plain terms

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) is a lightweight variant of PPO designed to reduce memory and modelling overhead:

- Instead of training a separate critic/value network to estimate a per-token baseline (as in PPO), GRPO samples a group of G outputs for the same question and uses the group mean (and standard deviation) as the baseline. The reward for each sampled output is centered and scaled relative to its group.
- Advantages across tokens are then set from these normalized scores. For process supervision, stepwise rewards are normalized and summed forward into token-level advantages.
- GRPO still uses PPO-like clipping on per-token ratios and adds an explicit KL penalty between current policy and a reference model, but it removes the need for a learned value model and thus lowers memory/computation costs.

Because GRPO computes baselines from the sampled group, it aligns nicely with how reward models are trained (often on comparisons), and it benefits from online sampling: the group is drawn from the live policy, so baselines reflect the policy’s current output distribution.

## What RL actually changed: Maj@K vs Pass@K

A central diagnostic result in the paper distinguishes two different notions of model improvement:

- Pass@K: the probability that at least one sampled output among K samples (or a single top sample) is correct. This is a measure of the model’s raw capability to produce a correct answer in its distribution tail.
- Maj@K: the probability that a majority (or the top-ranked sample by a reward model) among K samples is correct — a measure of robustness and concentration of probability mass on correct answers.

DeepSeekMath’s experiments show a consistent pattern: RL (GRPO and other online, graded-reward methods) improves Maj@K substantially, but not Pass@K. Concretely, RL increases the model’s tendency to place higher probability mass on correct solutions, making correct answers more reliably appear near the mode of the policy distribution. It does not necessarily make rare, high-quality answers more likely to appear in the tail if they were already rare.

Put another way: RL made the model more robust and more likely to produce correct answers consistently (higher Maj@K), rather than increasing the absolute diversity or raw capability that would raise Pass@K. This is consistent with the observation that RL tends to “stabilize” or concentrate the output distribution toward preferred behaviors as judged by the reward model.

Why does this happen under the unified framework? Because:

- Online sampling lets the algorithm observe the policy’s current mistakes and tune the policy toward corrective behavior.
- Graded rewards let the algorithm differentially reinforce better outputs and penalize worse ones, sharpening the distribution.
- Algorithms that trust the reward signal (PPO/GRPO) apply these graded gradients across tokens so the policy’s conditional probabilities re-weight toward higher-reward trajectories.

The result is a redistribution of mass within the model’s existing capabilities rather than unlocking fundamentally new reasoning modes.

## Practical implications

- If your goal is to increase the model’s reliability and make correct answers more likely in typical decoding runs (higher Maj@K), then online RL with a good reward model (and group normalization like GRPO) is an efficient strategy.
- If your goal is to expand the model’s raw capability to find solutions that are currently rare (increase Pass@K), RL alone (especially with constrained SFT-question data and naive sampling) may be insufficient. You likely need richer data, stronger pretraining, or targeted fine-tuning that brings those rare modes into the policy support.
- Reward engineering and reward model quality remain central. RL methods largely “trust” the reward model — if the reward model is narrow or miscalibrated, RL will concentrate behavior toward the reward model’s preferences, which may not reflect real task correctness in some out-of-distribution regimes.
- Using group-based baselines (GRPO) is a practical trade-off: it reduces resource needs by eliminating a learned critic while preserving the benefits of comparative advantage estimation when you can sample multiple outputs per question.

## Conclusion

Reframing alignment and fine-tuning algorithms in terms of Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm (GC) clarifies how SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO relate and where their strengths come from. Two concrete takeaways from DeepSeekMath are especially useful: (1) online sampling matters because it keeps training firmly grounded in the policy’s evolving distribution, and (2) graded rewards matter because they let training differentiate among partially correct outputs. Together, these factors explain why RL (implemented as GRPO in the paper) improved robustness — making models more likely to output correct answers near their mode (Maj@K) — even if it did not raise the presence of rare correct outputs in the long tail (Pass@K).
