\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Math Beyond English: Why Multilingual Pretraining Pays Off

DeepSeekMath made a deliberate choice that many prior math-focused LLM efforts did not: the math pretraining corpus was explicitly multilingual. The result wasn’t just a novelty — it materially improved performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks and produced a model that generalizes reasoning better across languages. This post explains what DeepSeekMath did, what the paper’s evaluations show, and why multilingual math data matters for global education and research.

## What DeepSeekMath did differently

Most large-scale math pretraining efforts have focused on English-heavy sources (arXiv, English textbooks, English StackExchange, etc.). DeepSeekMath instead constructed the DeepSeekMath Corpus from Common Crawl using an iterative fastText-based mining pipeline and human-in-the-loop filtering, resulting in roughly 120B math tokens drawn from multilingual web pages. The paper highlights:

- The corpus is large (120B math tokens) and explicitly multilingual, with English and Chinese as the two largest languages represented.
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was trained with this corpus (plus code, arXiv, and general web data) and later instruction-tuned and RL-refined.
- Across evaluations, math pretraining on this multilingual corpus improved performance not only on English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) but also on Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, MGSM-zh, Gaokao subsets).

So the design choice was simple: don’t throw away non-English math; instead, mine and curate it. The empirical payoff was measurable.

## Evidence from the experiments

The paper reports several concrete signals that multilingual pretraining helped:

- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperformed comparable open-source base models across eight math benchmarks in both English and Chinese. The authors explicitly note stronger performance on Chinese benchmarks, which they tie to the inclusion of high-quality non-English data.
- The multilingual corpus produced gains even relative to other math corpora that are large but English-centric (e.g., MathPile, OpenWebMath). DeepSeekMath models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed a steeper and more lasting learning curve in experiments on a 1.3B model.
- After supervised instruction tuning and the GRPO reinforcement learning stage, the final models continued to show strong cross-lingual math performance (for instance, improvements on CMATH during RL).

These results indicate that the multilingual signal in pretraining yielded more than just localized gains: it concretely improved the model’s ability to reason about math problems posed in Chinese and retained strong English performance as well.

## Why multilingual math data helps reasoning (intuitions grounded in the paper)

The paper offers direct observations and plausible mechanisms to explain the multilingual gains. The core reasons are straightforward and supported by experimental outcomes in the study:

1. Data diversity increases robustness. Math problems are expressed in many linguistic forms. Multilingual pretraining exposes models to alternative syntactic structures, vocabulary, and rhetorical styles for describing the same mathematical concepts, which reduces overfitting to English phrasing and helps the model learn reasoning patterns that are language-agnostic.

2. Complementary formulations reveal structure. Different languages and regional education resources often present the same core idea with different intermediate steps, notations, or examples. Seeing multiple ways to decompose and explain a problem helps the model internalize reasoning patterns rather than memorize surface templates.

3. Cross-lingual transfer of reasoning skills. The paper’s evaluations show improvements on Chinese benchmarks without sacrificing English accuracy, implying that the model transferred reasoning skills learned in one language to another. In effect, mathematical problem solving acts as an interlingua: logical steps and formal problem structure can be learned and then applied across languages.

4. Better coverage for localized curricula and benchmarks. Some problems or problem styles appear more often in certain languages or educational systems. Including non-English sources increases the chance the model has seen relevant examples that match the style of Chinese benchmarks like CMATH or Gaokao-MathQA.

5. Multilingual pretraining mitigates dataset bias. The paper notes data selection bias (e.g., geometry weakness attributed to pretraining/fine-tuning biases). A multilingual corpus that draws from broader global resources reduces the risk that the model only learns English-dominant problem styles.

## Practical implications for education and research

- Models trained on multilingual math corpora can better serve students, educators, and researchers worldwide. For example, Chinese K–12 math syllabi and university-level problem styles differ from English curricula; a model exposed to both will produce better-targeted explanations and solutions.
- Open-source math models that want broad utility should consider multilingual data as a first-class component, not an afterthought. The DeepSeekMath results show that large, curated multilingual web data can be scaled effectively and yields measurable benefits.
- For cross-lingual research, multilingual models can act as bridges: they can convert patterns or solution styles found in one language’s resources into another’s, enabling comparative curriculum analysis or automated translation of math pedagogical materials.

## Caveats and open questions

The paper is careful to note limitations and areas for future work:

- The multilingual gains are empirically shown for English and Chinese (the two dominant languages in the corpus). It is an open question how well these findings generalize to low-resource languages where math web content is scarce.
- The authors did not exhaustively explore how different mixes of language proportions affect outcomes. The precise scaling laws of multilingual math pretraining remain to be characterized.
- Improvements are not uniform across all math subdomains. The model still exhibits weaknesses (e.g., certain geometry problems), which may reflect gaps in the pretraining distribution rather than a language issue per se.

## Takeaway

DeepSeekMath’s experience is a practical demonstration that multilingual pretraining for mathematical reasoning is not only feasible at scale but also valuable. By mining high-quality math pages across languages and including them in pretraining, the authors achieved stronger performance on Chinese benchmarks while retaining excellent English performance. For anyone building math-capable LLMs with global ambitions — whether to assist students, researchers, or educators — the message is clear: include non-English math data early and intentionally. The added linguistic variety doesn’t just broaden coverage — it helps the model learn reasoning patterns that generalize across languages.
