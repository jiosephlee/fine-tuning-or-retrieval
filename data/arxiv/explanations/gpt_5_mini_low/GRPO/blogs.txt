# How DeepSeekMath Found 120B Math Tokens in the Wild Web

DeepSeekMath’s biggest tactical win wasn’t a fancy model trick — it was getting the data right. The team turned the noisy Common Crawl into a focused, high-quality math corpus of 120B tokens (35.5M math pages) using a practical, repeatable pipeline. This post walks through the pipeline step-by-step: the fastText classifier, iterative domain mining, human-in-the-loop curation, aggressive deduplication, and benchmark decontamination. I’ll also note why the same approach generalizes to other domains such as code.

## Overview: goals and constraints

The objective was simple but hard in practice: extract large quantities of web text that look like math content (explanations, worked problems, proofs, code-based solutions) while avoiding low-quality pages and contamination of evaluation benchmarks. Key constraints influenced design choices:

- Scale: mine hundreds of billions of tokens from Common Crawl efficiently.
- Precision: prefer higher-quality math content rather than blindly increasing token counts.
- Practicality: use a fast, easily trainable classifier with lightweight iterations and human refinement.
- Safety: remove any overlap with evaluation benchmarks to avoid data contamination.

The result: an iterative pipeline that produced 120B math tokens from Common Crawl and an end model (DeepSeekMath-Base 7B) that benefited substantially from the data.

## Step 1 — Seed + fastText classifier

They started with a seed math corpus: OpenWebMath, a high-quality set of math web texts. From that, a fastText classifier was trained to recall “OpenWebMath-like” pages from Common Crawl.

Training details captured in the paper:

- Positive examples: 500,000 data points sampled from the seed (OpenWebMath).
- Negative examples: 500,000 random Common Crawl pages.
- fastText hyperparameters: vector dimension = 256, learning rate = 0.1, max word n-gram length = 3, min word occurrences = 3, epochs = 3.

Why fastText? It’s fast to train and cheap to run at the scale of Common Crawl, making it practical for multiple iterations over billions of pages.

## Step 2 — Aggressive deduping and recall

Before classification, the team reduced the Common Crawl set size using URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication, shrinking the crawl to about 40B HTML pages for initial recall. The classifier was run over this deduplicated corpus to retrieve math-like pages. For quality, they didn’t take everything: pages were ranked by classifier score and only top-ranked pages were kept for further consideration.

In the first iteration they experimented with keeping varying volumes (top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens) and initially retained the top 40B tokens for refinement.

## Step 3 — Iterative domain mining to broaden recall

A single classifier trained on limited positives misses lots of math content because the seed lacks diversity. To expand coverage without sacrificing precision, they used an iterative domain-mining trick:

- Partition Common Crawl by domain (pages sharing the same base URL).
- For each domain compute the percentage of pages that the classifier collected in iteration 1.
- Mark domains where over 10% of pages were collected as “math-related” candidates (examples: mathoverflow.net).
- Manually inspect and annotate math-relevant URL paths within those domains (for example, /questions on mathoverflow).
- Add uncollected pages from those annotated URLs into the positive set.
- Retrain the fastText classifier with the expanded positives and repeat recall.

This loop—recall, analyze domains, augment positives, retrain—was run multiple times. After four iterations they arrived at 35.5M math pages totaling 120B tokens. They also observed diminishing returns: by the fourth iteration nearly 98% of the data had already been captured in the third iteration, so collection halted.

## Step 4 — Human-in-the-loop refinement

Human annotation was used to improve classifier quality in later iterations. Specifically, once math-related domains and candidate URLs were identified automatically, humans annotated URLs and pages to enrich the positive set. The refreshed positives were used to retrain fastText, improving recall for diverse math content.

This human-in-the-loop step is important: it injects diversity into the positives (different site structures, content styles, languages) so the classifier generalizes beyond the initial seed.

## Step 5 — Benchmark decontamination

To avoid leaking evaluation data into pretraining, DeepSeekMath applied explicit decontamination rules. Any web page with substrings that matched evaluation benchmarks was removed. The rule:

- Remove any text segment containing a 10-gram substring that exactly matches any substring from evaluation benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.).
- For benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, use exact matching to filter out contaminated pages.

This conservative matching reduces the risk of benchmark contamination while being straightforward to implement.

## Why deduping and near-deduping matter

Repetition in web data causes smaller corpora to be seen many times during training, flattening gains. By applying URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication up front, the pipeline ensured that the eventual math pages were unique and diverse, which the authors show leads to a steeper learning curve during pretraining compared to smaller math corpora that quickly plateau.

## Multilingual coverage and corpus scale

Two practical outcomes of the approach:

- The resulting DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese being the most represented languages. This multilinguality improved Chinese math benchmarks compared to English-only math corpora.
- Scale: 35.5M math pages → 120B tokens. The paper emphasizes that this is multiple times larger than prior math corpora like MathPile (8.9B tokens) and OpenWebMath (13.6B tokens).

## How the team validated quality

Rather than assume bigger is better, the team trained models on different math corpora for controlled experiments. A 1.3B parameter model trained on their DeepSeekMath Corpus for 150B tokens outperformed models trained on other math corpora across a range of benchmarks. That empirical validation shows the selection pipeline achieved both scale and quality.

## Why this generalizes to other domains (e.g., code)

The pipeline is domain-agnostic in principle. Replace the seed corpus (OpenWebMath) with a small high-quality seed for the target domain (for instance, code samples or documentation), then:

- Train a fast classifier (fastText or similar) to recognize domain-like pages.
- Deduplicate and recall across Common Crawl.
- Identify domain-rich domains by the same >10% collection heuristic and expand positives via manual annotation or heuristics.
- Iterate, retrain, and decontaminate relevant evaluation benchmarks.

The paper explicitly notes the pipeline is applicable to other domains such as coding; empirically they initialized DeepSeekMath with a code-trained base model and saw benefits, implying a productive synergy between code data and math data when curated carefully.

## Practical takeaways and caveats

- Use a fast, scalable classifier (fastText worked well here): speed matters when scanning billions of pages.
- Iteration beats one-shot filtering: domain mining plus human annotation expands coverage without exploding noise.
- Deduplication up front reduces useless repetition and improves effective corpus quality.
- Conservative decontamination is essential to avoid evaluation leakage.
- Human annotation is small but high-leverage: focus it on adding diverse positive examples (site structures, URL patterns, languages).
- The approach finds lots of useful content on the open web; closed sources or specialized corpora are not the only path to high-quality domain data.

Limitations mentioned by the authors: some math subdomains (e.g., geometric problems) remained weaker — likely a sign of selection bias in the collected pages — and arXiv-only content did not give consistent improvements. So data choice and diversity matter even after large-scale collection.

## Final note

DeepSeekMath’s 120B-token corpus shows the payoff of an engineering-first approach to data: combine a lean classifier, sensible deduplication, iterative domain discovery, and focused human labeling. The result is a large, high-quality, and reusable math corpus that materially improves model reasoning — and the same pipeline is directly applicable to other domains where high-precision extraction from the web is needed.



# Small Model, Big Brains: Why DeepSeekMath-7B Rivals Giants

DeepSeekMath-7B is a striking example of how careful data curation and targeted training can let a relatively small model punch well above its weight on structured reasoning tasks. Trained from a code-initialized 7B model and continued with massive, math-focused web data, DeepSeekMath-7B reaches competition-level performance on math benchmarks that approaches that of much larger closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. This post walks through the three practical ideas that make this possible: (1) curated math pre-training at scale, (2) starting from a code model, and (3) reinforcement learning tailored for math (GRPO). I’ll keep the math light but precise.

## The headline results (so you don’t miss them)
- DeepSeekMath-RL 7B scores 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without tool use or voting tricks, and self-consistency over 64 samples reaches 60.9%.
- After RL, GSM8K accuracy reaches 88.2% (chain-of-thought setting).
- The base 7B model (after math pretraining) already outperforms many open-source models: e.g., DeepSeekMath-Base achieves 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH — comparable to much larger prior models.

If you expected these numbers only from huge models, that’s exactly the point: carefully engineered data and training choices matter a lot.

## 1) Data: quality and scale beat raw parameter count
One of the central ideas in DeepSeekMath is that mathematical reasoning benefits tremendously from high-quality, domain-focused pretraining data, even when the model is relatively small.

How they built the math corpus
- They iteratively mined Common Crawl using a fastText classifier seeded with OpenWebMath examples. The classifier was refined over multiple iterations by adding more positive examples found by domain analysis and human annotation.
- After deduplication and filtering to avoid benchmark contamination, the final DeepSeekMath Corpus contains about 35.5M math-related web pages, totaling 120B math tokens. This corpus is multilingual (notably English and Chinese).

Why this matters
- The DeepSeekMath Corpus is several times larger than previously released math web corpora (e.g., OpenWebMath and MathPile) and was shown empirically to be higher quality in downstream math tasks.
- Pretraining on this focused corpus produced strong few-shot chain-of-thought performance and delivered sustained improvements across benchmarks rather than plateauing quickly (a common issue when training on smaller or repeatedly sampled corpora).

Practical takeaway: if you want a model to get better at structured reasoning, invest in a large, carefully filtered, domain-specific corpus — scale plus relevance beats blind parameter scaling.

## 2) Start from code: code training helps math reasoning
Another key design decision: DeepSeekMath-Base was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (a code-trained 7B model) and then continued training on the math corpus.

What they found
- Code pretraining improved mathematical reasoning both with and without tool/program use. In particular, models that had gone through code training solved program-aided math problems more effectively and made the subsequent math training more efficient.
- Mixing code and math tokens in a single training stage can avoid catastrophic forgetting while preserving gains in both coding and math capabilities on the smaller model sizes used here.

Why code helps
- Code exposes models to precise, structured, and compositional patterns (variables, control flow, symbolic manipulation) that resemble the formal structure of mathematical solutions. This prior makes the model better at generating stepwise, executable reasoning (useful for program-of-thought and tool-aided solving).

Practical takeaway: for tasks that require structured stepwise reasoning, initializing from a code model is a surprisingly practical and effective shortcut.

## 3) RL for math: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
After supervised instruction tuning, DeepSeekMath used a reinforcement learning stage that significantly boosted performance. Instead of vanilla PPO, the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant tailored to reward-model-based fine-tuning for reasoning tasks.

What GRPO does differently
- Removes the learned critic/value function (which in PPO is expensive to train and memory-hungry).
- For each question, GRPO samples a group of G outputs from the old policy, scores them with a reward model, and uses the group statistics as the baseline for advantage estimation. Concretely, advantages are computed relative to the group mean/std, so the reward signal is inherently comparative across sampled responses for the same prompt.
- It also adds a KL regularizer between the policy and a reference model directly into the GRPO objective, instead of embedding the KL into per-token rewards.

A simplified view of the idea
- PPO needs a value model V to compute advantages A_t. GRPO replaces V with a group-based baseline: compare each sampled output to the group of outputs for the same question, and use those relative scores to shape the policy gradient.

Why this helps
- Reward models for reasoning are typically trained on comparisons (which output is better for the same question). GRPO’s group-relative estimation matches that comparative nature and removes the extra overhead of a value network.
- In experiments, GRPO improves top-of-distribution accuracy and increases the chance that a correct solution is elevated into the model’s Top-K outputs (see “Why RL works” below).

Practical takeaway: when you have a reliable comparative reward signal, using group-relative RL simplifies training and reduces resource cost while preserving (or improving) effectiveness.

## Why RL seems to help: Maj@K vs Pass@K
An important diagnostic result: reinforcement learning improved Maj@K (majority/top-K robustness) but not necessarily Pass@K (raw capability measured by single sample success). That is, RL makes correct answers more likely to appear among the top-K sampled outputs — it “sharpens” the distribution toward better outputs — without necessarily increasing the model’s underlying problem-solving capability on a per-sample basis.

Implication
- RL here acts to concentrate probability mass on already-possible correct reasoning traces. That is valuable in practice (self-consistency, majority voting, and sampling-based strategies get better), but it also means the RL gains are, to some extent, distributional rather than reflecting fundamental algorithmic advances in reasoning.

## Some additional practical findings
- ArXiv text: Counterintuitively, adding arXiv papers (LaTeX sources or arXiv-derived corpora) did not meaningfully improve math benchmark performance in their experiments and sometimes hurt it. That suggests not all “math text” is equally useful; web math pages mined and filtered for problem/solution style may be more directly helpful for solving benchmark-style problems.
- Multilingual data: because their corpus included substantial non-English math content (notably Chinese), the model improved on Chinese math benchmarks too.
- Instruction tuning and RL were done with problem/solution pairs including chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated formats — the diversity of formats likely helped.

## Limitations and where the model still falls short
- Geometry and some theorem-proof tasks remain weaker than top closed-source models. The authors note failures on certain triangle/ellipse problems, pointing to possible pretraining/fine-tuning data coverage gaps.
- Few-shot scaling: GPT-4 still benefits more from few-shot examples than DeepSeekMath does; the smaller model shows similar zero-shot and few-shot behavior.
- Reward-model dependence: GRPO and other RL-based methods rely heavily on the reward model’s quality and generalization; noisy or miscalibrated reward signals remain a risk.

## Bottom line
DeepSeekMath-7B demonstrates that careful engineering — a large, curated domain corpus, a code-initialized starting point, and a reward-aware, resource-efficient RL variant — can push a 7B model into performance territory that previously seemed reserved for much larger models. The work underlines a broader lesson: for structured problems like mathematical reasoning, data and training design can be as important as raw parameter count. If you want a practical, efficient route to strong reasoning performance, focus first on targeted data and training recipes that match the domain and the supervision signal.

References and technical details are in the DeepSeekMath paper; the key numbers and methods above are taken directly from that work.



# GRPO, Explained: The Leaner, Meaner RL That Boosts Math Reasoning

DeepSeekMath introduced a neat trick for reinforcement tuning of language models that want to get better at math: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). It gives you most of PPO’s benefits while ditching the heavyweight critic, slashing memory cost, and aligning naturally with how reward models are trained (they compare answers on the same question). In DeepSeekMath’s experiments, GRPO helped their 7B model reach 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark (and 88.2% on GSM8K), using only chain-of-thought instruction data during RL.

This post breaks GRPO down into what it does, why it works, how it’s computed, and some practical takeaways — with minimal math and clear intuition.

## The idea in one sentence
Instead of learning a separate value function (the critic) to estimate a baseline for advantage computation, GRPO forms a baseline from a group of outputs sampled for the same question and computes advantages relative to that group. Use those group-relative advantages in a PPO-like clipped objective, and regularize with a KL term against a reference policy.

## Why that matters
- Memory & compute: No critic model means you don’t need to store/train an extra large network alongside the policy. For large LLMs this is a big win.
- Natural fit for preference-style rewards: Reward models are usually trained to compare different candidate answers to the same prompt. GRPO’s group baseline uses exactly that comparative information.
- Robust updates: By normalizing rewards inside each group, GRPO stabilizes the scale of advantages and therefore the policy updates.

## How GRPO works (intuitively)
1. For each question q, sample a group of G outputs {o1, o2, ..., oG} from the current (old) policy.
2. Score each output with a reward model, producing rewards r1, r2, ..., rG.
3. Convert these raw rewards into group-relative advantages. For outcome supervision, every token in oi gets the same normalized group reward; for process supervision, each reasoning step receives a reward and token-level advantages sum the (normalized) future step rewards.
4. Maximize a PPO-like surrogate objective across tokens, where the per-token advantage is the group-relative advantage. Also add an explicit KL regularizer between the current policy and a reference policy (usually the current policy snapshot), rather than embedding KL in each token reward.
5. Iterate: re-sample groups from the evolving policy and (optionally) periodically re-train the reward model on newer data.

That surrogate looks like a group-averaged PPO objective; symbolically the GRPO objective (compressed) is:

J_GRPO(θ) = E_{q, {o_i}∼π_{old}} [ (1/G) ∑_{i=1}^G (1/|o_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|o_i|} min( r^{ratio} · Â_{i,t}, clip(r^{ratio},1−ε,1+ε) · Â_{i,t} ) − β · D_{KL}(π_θ || π_ref) ]

where r^{ratio} denotes the probability-ratio term for that token and Â_{i,t} is the group-relative advantage for token t in output i. The paper gives the full formula and implementation details; the core idea is replacing a learned critic baseline with the within-group mean/std normalization.

## Group-relative advantage — two flavors

Outcome supervision (coarse):
- Each sampled output oi gets a single terminal reward ri.
- Normalize the group rewards: \tilde{r}_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r).
- Set token advantages equal to \tilde{r}_i: Â_{i,t} = \tilde{r}_i for all t in oi.

Process supervision (fine-grained):
- The reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., after each chain-of-thought step).
- Normalize all step rewards across the group.
- For a token at position t, its advantage is the sum of normalized rewards of steps that occur at or after t:
  Â_{i,t} = ∑_{index(j) ≥ t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.
- This produces token-level credit assignment aligned with the reasoning process.

Process supervision typically gives finer guidance and empirically improves performance over coarse outcome-only supervision.

## KL regularization: how GRPO keeps the policy grounded
PPO often includes a KL penalty via the reward shaping term. GRPO instead directly adds a KL term to the objective:

− β · D_{KL}(π_θ || π_ref)

and uses an unbiased estimator for per-token KL when needed. Treating KL this way simplifies advantage calculation and gives a clean regularizer that prevents runaway policy drift away from the reference policy.

## Why GRPO outperforms simpler alternatives
- Online vs offline sampling: GRPO uses online sampling from the live policy (not only SFT-generated offline outputs), which matters as the policy diverges during training. Online sampling captures the policy’s current failure modes and improvements.
- Graded feedback: Unlike rejection-based fine-tuning approaches that just keep or drop outputs (i.e., binary signals), GRPO leverages continuous reward magnitudes to produce graded advantages (penalize bad outputs more; reward good ones more).
- Process supervision: Where available, step-wise rewards help assign credit more precisely, improving learning efficiency.

## Practical configuration highlights (from the paper)
- Group size: In the DeepSeekMath RL runs, they sampled G = 64 outputs per question. Large groups make the group statistics (mean/std) more stable.
- Data used: They trained RL on chain-of-thought-format questions from GSM8K and MATH (about 144K questions), starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B.
- Learning details: policy LR ~ 1e-6, KL coefficient β around 0.04, max token length 1024, batch size 1024 for the RL stage in their setup.
- Reward model: initially trained from the base model; iterative RL includes periodically refreshing the reward model using new sampled outputs (replay with 10% historical data).

## Results (what it bought them)
Using GRPO on their 7B chain-of-thought instruction-tuned model:
- GSM8K (CoT): improved to 88.2%
- MATH (CoT): improved to 51.7%
- Self-consistency 64-sample result mentioned in the paper: 60.9% on MATH

Importantly, this RL boost came from only a subset of instruction tuning data (GSM8K and MATH CoT-format), yet GRPO improved performance on both in-domain and some out-of-domain math tasks.

## When to use GRPO
- You want RL improvements but lack resources to train a large critic alongside the policy.
- Your reward signal is comparative (preference/ranking-style) or you can get scores across multiple candidates per prompt.
- You can afford to sample many outputs per prompt (GRPO benefits from larger group sizes).
- You want a method that integrates well with process-level reward supervision (step-wise scoring).

## Limitations and cautions
- Sampling cost: GRPO needs many samples per prompt (they used 64), which raises inference cost during the exploration stage.
- Reward model quality matters: GRPO leans on the reward model; noisy or poorly-generalized reward models can mislead training. The paper highlights the need for better reward generalization and uncertainty estimation.
- Not a magic bullet: GRPO improves the distributional robustness (Maj@K) more than base reasoning capability (Pass@K); it tends to promote putting a correct answer higher in the output distribution rather than necessarily learning fundamentally new problem-solving skills.

## Bottom line
GRPO is a pragmatic, resource-friendly RL recipe for LLM fine-tuning: replace the critic with a group baseline, normalize rewards within groups, use graded advantages (optionally process-level), and regularize with KL to a reference policy. For math reasoning at the 7B scale, that combination produced substantial gains — tipping MATH to 51.7% and GSM8K to 88.2% in the DeepSeekMath experiments — while keeping the RL memory footprint much lower than PPO with a learned critic.

If you’re budget-limited on memory but can afford wide sampling per prompt, GRPO is an attractive option to try for preference-style reward problems.



# From Chain-of-Thought to Program-of-Thought: Teaching Models to Show Their Work

## Introduction

One of the clearest trends in improving language models on hard math is that "showing your work" helps. DeepSeekMath demonstrates this by combining multiple ways for the model to expose its reasoning: chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning. Together these instruction-tuning styles let a 7B model punch well above its parameter count on benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K, and—when paired with tools—push the MATH score toward roughly 60%.

This post walks through what each of these formats is, why stepwise reasoning matters, when generating code and executing it helps, and how DeepSeekMath stitches these formats into a single instruction-tuning pipeline to produce stronger, more reliable math solutions.

## Three ways to "show work"

- Chain-of-Thought (CoT): The model writes natural-language intermediate steps that explain the reasoning. CoT is designed to emulate how humans break complex problems into logical steps and has been shown to improve multi-step reasoning by making the chain of inference explicit during generation.

- Program-of-Thought (PoT): Instead of natural-language steps, the model generates a small program (in DeepSeekMath's case, Python). The program implements the logical steps and computations required to solve the problem. The program can then be executed to produce a numerical or symbolic answer.

- Tool-integrated reasoning: This is PoT plus actual execution (and possibly use of libraries like math or sympy). The model emits code and a runtime executes it; the execution result is used as the final answer. Tool integration turns subjective or brittle intermediate reasoning into deterministic computations where appropriate.

DeepSeekMath uses all three during supervised fine-tuning: problems are paired with solutions in CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated formats. The SFT dataset contains problems from both English and Chinese sources, annotated with these stepwise solution styles.

## Why showing steps helps

1. Better credit assignment during learning. When the model produces intermediate steps, the training signal can reinforce correct sub-steps, not just final answers. This reduces reliance on lucky final-token prediction and guides internal reasoning patterns.

2. More robust generation. Models that reason internally (versus jumping directly to answers) tend to produce more interpretable and stable outputs. DeepSeekMath shows that math pre-training plus instruction tuning with CoT/PoT improves not only math benchmarks but also other reasoning tasks (MMLU, BBH).

3. Enables verification and tooling. CoT is interpretable, PoT is executable. Executable steps enable deterministic verification (run the code), which reduces errors from numeric slip-ups or mental arithmetic mistakes in the model's text. This is especially helpful for problems that require precise arithmetic, algebra, or calculus manipulations.

4. Amplifies top-K robustness. The paper observes that reinforcement fine-tuning made the model's output distribution "more robust" by increasing Maj@K (majority across samples) rather than Pass@K. Producing many candidate stepwise solutions (and checking them) benefits from explicit step structure that can be validated or executed.

## When code execution helps (PoT + tools)

PoT becomes especially valuable when:

- The problem requires nontrivial numeric computation, symbolic manipulation, or repeated arithmetic that is tedious and error-prone in free text.
- There is a well-known library that can perform the task reliably (e.g., sympy for algebraic simplification, math for standard functions).
- Deterministic verification of intermediate results reduces uncertainty (you can run the code and check the output).

DeepSeekMath explicitly trains models to write Python programs as part of PoT and to rely on execution in tool-integrated evaluations. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, when prompted to write runnable Python solutions (PoT + execution), outperforms other prior open-source models on GSM8K and MATH in the program-aided setting. Mixing code tokens into pretraining also helps program-aided reasoning: code training prior to math training yielded improvements in solving math problems via Python.

Caveats: mixing code and math data together can be a double-edged sword for smaller models. For instance, in DeepSeekMath experiments, combining code tokens and math tokens in a single-stage training sometimes reduced performance on purely text-based reasoning relative to a two-stage approach. The model scale and training schedule matter.

## How DeepSeekMath blends the approaches

DeepSeekMath builds a supervised instruction-tuning dataset of roughly 776K examples that mixes CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated solutions across English and Chinese math problems. During SFT:

- Examples are concatenated into 4K-context sequences and used to fine-tune DeepSeekMath-Base 7B.
- The model learns to produce both natural-language step-by-step solutions and runnable Python programs, depending on the prompt and solution style used in training.
- Tool-integrated examples explicitly model program execution as part of the reasoning loop.

This blended instruction tuning makes the model flexible: it can output an explanatory chain, a program, or a program plus execution scaffold depending on the task and prompt.

## Impact on benchmarks

The combined strategy materially improves performance:

- After instruction tuning with CoT/PoT/tool-integrated formats, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B significantly outperforms other open-source 7B models on step-by-step reasoning. In the no-tool setting on the competition-level MATH benchmark, it surpasses many open and some proprietary models by large margins.

- When tool use (program execution) is allowed, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B "approaches an accuracy of 60% on MATH", surpassing all open-source models in the evaluation and becoming competitive with much larger models on several benchmarks.

- Further reinforcement learning (GRPO) atop the instruction-tuned model improves chain-of-thought performance further (e.g., DeepSeekMath-RL reached 88.2% on GSM8K and 51.7% on MATH using CoT). Self-consistency sampling from DeepSeekMath (64 samples) achieves about 60.9% on MATH, illustrating how sampling + voting over stepwise outputs can raise final accuracy.

The takeaway: mixing explicit step formats and leveraging execution during evaluation enables a 7B model to attain performance previously seen only in much larger models, especially when execution is available to validate computations.

## Practical recommendations

- Use CoT when interpretability and human-readable reasoning are important. It helps training align reasoning steps and supports human-in-the-loop verification.

- Use PoT when the problem involves precise computations or symbolic steps; emitting runnable code reduces arithmetic errors and enables deterministic checking.

- Use tool-integrated evaluation (execute generated code) when correctness depends on exact numeric/symbolic outputs. Tool integration converts otherwise fuzzy reasoning into concrete outputs.

- For training, include a mix of CoT and PoT examples so the model learns both explanatory and executable styles. Be mindful of model size: small models may need staged training (code pretraining then math fine-tuning) to avoid capacity conflicts.

- Combine sampling strategies (e.g., many PoT/CoT samples) with self-consistency or voting to further boost accuracy; DeepSeekMath’s experiments show large gains from sampling and aggregation.

## Limitations and open points

DeepSeekMath notes that some math areas (e.g., geometry and certain theorem-proof tasks) remain weaker than closed models, suggesting that data selection biases or gaps remain. Also, the interactions between code pretraining, math pretraining, and instruction formats depend on model scale and training mix; these are active areas for further research.

## Conclusion

Teaching models to "show their work" via chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning is an effective, practical strategy to improve mathematical reasoning. DeepSeekMath demonstrates that a careful blend of these formats in instruction tuning—combined with targeted pretraining and RL—enables a 7B model to reach performance levels on par with much larger systems when execution is allowed. For practitioners, the lesson is clear: make models explain and, when feasible, compute.



# Math Beyond English: Why Multilingual Pretraining Pays Off

DeepSeekMath made a deliberate choice that many prior math-focused LLM efforts did not: the math pretraining corpus was explicitly multilingual. The result wasn’t just a novelty — it materially improved performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks and produced a model that generalizes reasoning better across languages. This post explains what DeepSeekMath did, what the paper’s evaluations show, and why multilingual math data matters for global education and research.

## What DeepSeekMath did differently

Most large-scale math pretraining efforts have focused on English-heavy sources (arXiv, English textbooks, English StackExchange, etc.). DeepSeekMath instead constructed the DeepSeekMath Corpus from Common Crawl using an iterative fastText-based mining pipeline and human-in-the-loop filtering, resulting in roughly 120B math tokens drawn from multilingual web pages. The paper highlights:

- The corpus is large (120B math tokens) and explicitly multilingual, with English and Chinese as the two largest languages represented.
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was trained with this corpus (plus code, arXiv, and general web data) and later instruction-tuned and RL-refined.
- Across evaluations, math pretraining on this multilingual corpus improved performance not only on English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) but also on Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, MGSM-zh, Gaokao subsets).

So the design choice was simple: don’t throw away non-English math; instead, mine and curate it. The empirical payoff was measurable.

## Evidence from the experiments

The paper reports several concrete signals that multilingual pretraining helped:

- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperformed comparable open-source base models across eight math benchmarks in both English and Chinese. The authors explicitly note stronger performance on Chinese benchmarks, which they tie to the inclusion of high-quality non-English data.
- The multilingual corpus produced gains even relative to other math corpora that are large but English-centric (e.g., MathPile, OpenWebMath). DeepSeekMath models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed a steeper and more lasting learning curve in experiments on a 1.3B model.
- After supervised instruction tuning and the GRPO reinforcement learning stage, the final models continued to show strong cross-lingual math performance (for instance, improvements on CMATH during RL).

These results indicate that the multilingual signal in pretraining yielded more than just localized gains: it concretely improved the model’s ability to reason about math problems posed in Chinese and retained strong English performance as well.

## Why multilingual math data helps reasoning (intuitions grounded in the paper)

The paper offers direct observations and plausible mechanisms to explain the multilingual gains. The core reasons are straightforward and supported by experimental outcomes in the study:

1. Data diversity increases robustness. Math problems are expressed in many linguistic forms. Multilingual pretraining exposes models to alternative syntactic structures, vocabulary, and rhetorical styles for describing the same mathematical concepts, which reduces overfitting to English phrasing and helps the model learn reasoning patterns that are language-agnostic.

2. Complementary formulations reveal structure. Different languages and regional education resources often present the same core idea with different intermediate steps, notations, or examples. Seeing multiple ways to decompose and explain a problem helps the model internalize reasoning patterns rather than memorize surface templates.

3. Cross-lingual transfer of reasoning skills. The paper’s evaluations show improvements on Chinese benchmarks without sacrificing English accuracy, implying that the model transferred reasoning skills learned in one language to another. In effect, mathematical problem solving acts as an interlingua: logical steps and formal problem structure can be learned and then applied across languages.

4. Better coverage for localized curricula and benchmarks. Some problems or problem styles appear more often in certain languages or educational systems. Including non-English sources increases the chance the model has seen relevant examples that match the style of Chinese benchmarks like CMATH or Gaokao-MathQA.

5. Multilingual pretraining mitigates dataset bias. The paper notes data selection bias (e.g., geometry weakness attributed to pretraining/fine-tuning biases). A multilingual corpus that draws from broader global resources reduces the risk that the model only learns English-dominant problem styles.

## Practical implications for education and research

- Models trained on multilingual math corpora can better serve students, educators, and researchers worldwide. For example, Chinese K–12 math syllabi and university-level problem styles differ from English curricula; a model exposed to both will produce better-targeted explanations and solutions.
- Open-source math models that want broad utility should consider multilingual data as a first-class component, not an afterthought. The DeepSeekMath results show that large, curated multilingual web data can be scaled effectively and yields measurable benefits.
- For cross-lingual research, multilingual models can act as bridges: they can convert patterns or solution styles found in one language’s resources into another’s, enabling comparative curriculum analysis or automated translation of math pedagogical materials.

## Caveats and open questions

The paper is careful to note limitations and areas for future work:

- The multilingual gains are empirically shown for English and Chinese (the two dominant languages in the corpus). It is an open question how well these findings generalize to low-resource languages where math web content is scarce.
- The authors did not exhaustively explore how different mixes of language proportions affect outcomes. The precise scaling laws of multilingual math pretraining remain to be characterized.
- Improvements are not uniform across all math subdomains. The model still exhibits weaknesses (e.g., certain geometry problems), which may reflect gaps in the pretraining distribution rather than a language issue per se.

## Takeaway

DeepSeekMath’s experience is a practical demonstration that multilingual pretraining for mathematical reasoning is not only feasible at scale but also valuable. By mining high-quality math pages across languages and including them in pretraining, the authors achieved stronger performance on Chinese benchmarks while retaining excellent English performance. For anyone building math-capable LLMs with global ambitions — whether to assist students, researchers, or educators — the message is clear: include non-English math data early and intentionally. The added linguistic variety doesn’t just broaden coverage — it helps the model learn reasoning patterns that generalize across languages.



# Benchmarks, Decoded: What GSM8K, MATH, miniF2F, MMLU, and BBH Really Measure

DeepSeekMath is a focused attempt to push mathematical reasoning in an open 7B model. The paper reports wins across a range of benchmarks—some testing elementary arithmetic with words, some testing contest-style algebra and calculus, some testing formal theorem proving, and some testing general reasoning and code. Below I summarize what each benchmark measures, why it matters, and what DeepSeekMath achieved on it (numbers come from the paper).

## GSM8K — grade‑school multi‑step arithmetic word problems
What it measures: GSM8K targets grade‑school level quantitative reasoning. Problems are short word problems that typically require chaining a few straightforward arithmetic steps, and they’ve become the standard diagnostic for “chain‑of‑thought” style reasoning in LLMs.

Why it matters: Success on GSM8K shows a model can parse natural language, map it to basic mathematical operations, and keep a small stepwise reasoning trace.

DeepSeekMath results: 
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B reaches 64.2% using few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting, outperforming several larger or prior math models.
- After instruction tuning and reinforcement learning (GRPO), DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 88.2% on GSM8K (chain‑of‑thought), a very strong result for a 7B open model.

Implication: The pre‑training corpus and targeted tuning substantially improved robustness on simple multi‑step arithmetic reasoning—especially after RL, which concentrates probability mass on better solutions.

## MATH — competition‑level contest problems
What it measures: MATH is a collection of high‑school to early college level contest problems (algebra, number theory, calculus, combinatorics). Problems are longer, require deeper multi‑step reasoning, and often need more symbolic manipulation and strategy than GSM8K.

Why it matters: MATH is widely used to judge higher‑order mathematical reasoning beyond textbook arithmetic. Performance here reflects how well a model handles multi‑stage planning, algebraic manipulations, and nontrivial solution structure.

DeepSeekMath results:
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B scores 36.2% on MATH with chain‑of‑thought prompting, outperforming Minerva 540B (a much larger closed model) at the base model stage.
- After supervised instruction tuning (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) and then GRPO reinforcement learning (DeepSeekMath-RL), the model reaches 51.7% on MATH without external tool use or majority‑voting tricks.
- With self‑consistency sampling (64 samples), DeepSeekMath can push MATH accuracy to 60.9% (the paper reports self‑consistency over 64 samples gives 60.9%).

Implication: The combination of a large, targeted math corpus, instruction tuning with chain‑of‑thought/program‑of‑thought formats, and GRPO-style RL closes much of the gap to top closed models. The self‑consistency jump shows sampling and aggregation still matter a lot on harder math.

## miniF2F (Isabelle) — informal → formal theorem proving
What it measures: miniF2F tests autoformalization: convert an informal proof/statement into a formal proof script that a proof assistant (Isabelle here) can check. This is an extremely demanding task: the model must produce syntactically correct formal language and a logically complete proof that the assistant accepts.

Why it matters: Formal theorem proving exercises precise symbolic reasoning and programmatic syntax. Success means the model can bridge natural mathematical text and mechanized reasoning tools.

DeepSeekMath results:
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B shows strong few‑shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F with Isabelle, producing formal proofs where Sledgehammer (an automated prover) can fill in missing details in many cases.

Implication: Math‑focused pre‑training improved the model’s ability to write formal proof scripts and work with a proof assistant pipeline, not just free‑text reasoning.

## MMLU and BBH — general knowledge and hard reasoning benchmarks
What they measure:
- MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) is a broad multiple‑choice benchmark covering many subjects (including STEM). It tests factual and conceptual knowledge plus reasoning over diverse topics.
- BBH (BIG‑Bench Hard) is a set of difficult tasks designed to require multi‑step reasoning and sophisticated generalization.

Why they matter: Improvements here show that math pre‑training doesn’t only improve math; it can transfer to broader reasoning and understanding capabilities.

DeepSeekMath results:
- Math pre‑training improved DeepSeekMath-Base performance on both MMLU and BBH relative to the base (DeepSeek-Coder) initialization. The paper reports significant gains in these benchmarks after math pre‑training, indicating cross‑task transfer.
- Importantly, including code tokens in training maintained coding performance on HumanEval/MBPP—math training did not wreck programming ability because the model started from a code‑trained initialization and retained code data during continual training.

Implication: Focused math pre‑training can lift general reasoning benchmarks. Starting from a code‑trained model and continuing to include code during math training helps preserve (and in some cases synergize with) coding skills.

## What the differences between these benchmarks tell us
- GSM8K targets short multi‑step numerical reasoning; high scores suggest good chain‑of‑thought behavior on simple problems.
- MATH targets deeper, strategic mathematics; gains here reflect improved problem decomposition, algebraic manipulation, and multi‑stage planning.
- miniF2F (Isabelle) measures the ability to produce formal, machine‑checkable proofs—a qualitatively different kind of symbolic precision.
- MMLU and BBH evaluate transfer: can mathematical pre‑training improve broader knowledge and hard reasoning tasks?
- The presence of program‑of‑thought / tool‑integrated evaluations (where models write and execute Python) shows that mixing code into training and evaluating with tools raises practical problem‑solving performance.

## How DeepSeekMath achieved these gains (briefly)
The paper highlights two main drivers:
1. A large, carefully mined web math corpus (120B math tokens from Common Crawl) that is multilingual and higher quality than prior public math corpora. This corpus is the backbone for the pre‑training gains.
2. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a PPO variant that computes advantage within groups of sampled outputs (no separate value network), saving memory and aligning well with comparison‑style reward models. GRPO plus process supervision further raises performance on in‑domain tasks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) and even improves some out‑of‑domain math benchmarks (e.g., CMATH: 84.6% → 88.8% reported in the paper).

## Practical takeaways
- Different math benchmarks probe different capabilities: from surface‑level arithmetic chain‑of‑thought (GSM8K) to contest math strategy (MATH) to formalization (miniF2F).
- Large, targeted math pre‑training combined with instruction tuning and RL can substantially improve performance even in a compact 7B model.
- Math pre‑training can transfer to general reasoning tasks (MMLU, BBH) and preserve coding skill when code data and code‑initialized weights are used.
- Post‑training techniques (self‑consistency sampling, program execution) still give substantial gains on hard math problems.

# The ArXiv Surprise: Why Research Papers Didn’t Move the Needle

DeepSeekMath is a striking example of engineering around data and learning algorithms to push mathematical reasoning in an open 7B model. One of the paper’s counter-intuitive findings deserves a standalone discussion: adding arXiv papers to math pre-training did not improve, and in some cases slightly degraded, downstream mathematical reasoning across the benchmarks they tested. This post summarizes what the authors did, the evidence, plausible explanations grounded in the paper, key caveats they raise, and concrete directions for better data curation and experiments.

## What the authors tested — the experimental setup

The DeepSeekMath team compared multiple math-oriented pre-training corpora. Two arXiv-centric sources they evaluated were:

- MathPile: an 8.9B-token corpus where over 85% of content comes from arXiv-style scientific papers (as used in prior work).
- ArXiv-RedPajama: a 28B-token extraction of arXiv LaTeX sources (with preambles, comments, macros, bibliographies removed).

They ran controlled training runs on two model families:

- DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B: trained for 150B tokens on each corpus in isolation.
- DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B: trained for 40B tokens on each corpus in isolation.

Evaluation covered a range of math and reasoning benchmarks used throughout the paper: GSM8K and MATH (quantitative reasoning), MMLU-STEM (multiple-choice STEM), and miniF2F (informal-to-formal theorem proving). The key reported outcome: when trained on an arXiv-only corpus, both model sizes showed no notable improvements and sometimes worse performance across these tasks.

## The result, in plain terms

Contrary to common practice (many prior efforts incorporate large arXiv subsets), these controlled experiments suggest that, for the models and training regimes tested, arXiv tokens alone did not help the model’s downstream mathematical reasoning and may even have been detrimental.

This is not a blanket condemnation of arXiv. The authors explicitly note limits and possible confounders (more on those below). But it does challenge the simple assumption “more arXiv = better math reasoning” under the tested conditions.

## Why might arXiv fail to help? (Grounded hypotheses from the paper)

The paper discusses—and the experiments suggest—several plausible reasons why arXiv-only pre-training underperformed. These are consistent with observations and prior intuitions, and they’re all mentioned or implied in the study:

1. Format and representation mismatch
   - arXiv content is LaTeX-heavy. Even after stripping macros and preambles, the remaining structure (formulas, display math, custom notation) may not map cleanly to the language-style reasoning that the benchmarks reward.
   - Much valuable math in arXiv is formal or terse: definitions, theorems, dense proof sketches. Benchmarks like GSM8K reward step-by-step, worked-out arithmetic reasoning that is stylistically different.

2. Domain and task mismatch
   - Research papers focus on new results, abstract theory, or terse proofs rather than pedagogical worked examples or stepwise numerical problem-solving typical of benchmarks such as GSM8K.
   - Models trained on arXiv may learn to represent high-level formal exposition rather than the procedural chain-of-thought style used in benchmark solutions.

3. Signal-to-noise and scale issues
   - ArXiv text includes a lot of domain-specific notation, citations, and non-expository content (e.g., tables, figures, macros) which can reduce the effective signal for the target tasks.
   - If the amount of arXiv training is large but not well-curated, the model may overfit to arXiv-style patterns or be distracted by irrelevant tokens.

4. Lack of worked examples and explicit reasoning traces
   - Benchmarks benefit from exposure to worked-out, stepwise solutions. Many arXiv papers do not contain such material in a form similar to benchmark chain-of-thought traces.

5. Model scale and training recipe interactions
   - The paper’s experiments use 1.3B and 7B models. It is possible (they explicitly note) that arXiv benefits might materialize only at larger scales or under different pre-training schedules.

## Caveats — important limitations the authors themselves call out

The authors carefully list limits to this conclusion; keep these in mind before generalizing:

- Tasks not studied: They did not evaluate whether arXiv helps other math-related tasks such as informalization of theorems, advanced theorem-proving behaviors, or specialized symbolic reasoning tasks where research-style exposition could be directly useful.
- Combination effects: They did not fully measure the effect of combining arXiv with other data distributions (e.g., mixing arXiv + web math + worked examples) at various ratios. Negative signal could be mitigated in mixed corpora.
- Model scale: Their models are mid-sized (1.3B, 7B). Benefits of arXiv-style content may show up only at larger parameter counts or with different compute budgets.
- Preprocessing choices: Different LaTeX parsing, equation normalization, or autoformalization pipelines might extract more useful signals from papers than the preprocessing used in their experiments.

Because of these caveats, the result should be interpreted as: “arXiv-only training did not help under these tested setups,” not “arXiv is useless for math models in general.”

## What better curation could look like (practical suggestions grounded in the paper’s reasoning)

If arXiv-style content is potentially valuable but underperforming in these experiments, the likely path forward is smarter extraction and mixing rather than raw inclusion. The paper suggests (implicitly via their focus on data engineering) several pragmatic directions:

1. Extract and emphasize worked examples and expository sections
   - Parse papers to separate introductions, examples, and explanatory paragraphs from dense theorem/proof statements. Prioritize passages that resemble step-by-step reasoning.

2. Convert LaTeX math into reasoning-friendly text
   - Render or translate display formulas and inline math into a linearized, human-readable representation that preserves semantics but is more consistent with natural-language reasoning traces.

3. Build paired informal/formal or sketch/full-proof examples
   - When possible, pair informal explanations with formal proofs (or vice versa) so the model learns mappings between reasoning styles. This is especially relevant for tasks like autoformalization.

4. Deduplicate, denoise, and normalize notation
   - Remove extraneous artifacts (figure captions, bibliography content, compilation remnants) and normalize recurring notation to reduce spurious token patterns.

5. Mix and balance data sources
   - Don’t rely on arXiv alone. Combine arXiv-derived content with web-sourced math pages, solved problem datasets, and stepwise solutions. The DeepSeekMath paper itself relies heavily on web math content (their highest gains came from a curated Common Crawl math corpus).

6. Targeted sampling and curriculum
   - Use a curriculum that starts from worked examples and pedagogical content, then gradually incorporates research-style text. Or sample papers selectively by section and by discipline relevance.

7. Evaluate preprocessing variants and scale
   - Systematically test different LaTeX-to-text transformations, and try the same experiments at larger model scales or with larger compute budgets.

## Concrete follow-up experiments to try (based on the paper’s methodology and limitations)

- Mixed-corpus ablations: train with varying arXiv:web-math ratios to find a sweet spot where arXiv adds value.
- Section-aware training: include only introductions and example sections from arXiv papers and compare to whole-document training.
- Preprocessing ablations: compare raw LaTeX tokens vs. rendered linearized math vs. symbolic placeholders vs. natural-language paraphrases for formulas.
- Scale sweep: repeat promising preprocessing + mixture experiments at larger parameter counts to test scale dependence.
- Task-specific curation: for theorem-proof tasks, extract theorem/proof triples and compare to generic arXiv inclusion.

## Bottom line

The DeepSeekMath paper provides a careful, controlled observation: arXiv-only pre-training did not improve—and sometimes slightly hurt—mathematical reasoning performance for the tested models and training recipes. This result is a useful corrective to the default assumption that more research-paper text is always better for math capabilities.

However, the finding is not an indictment of arXiv content per se. Rather, it highlights that raw research text is a different signal than the pedagogical, worked-out reasoning examples that many math benchmarks demand. The practical implication is that we should invest in better extraction, normalization, and mixing strategies for arXiv material (and systematically test model scale effects), instead of blindly scaling up raw research-paper tokens.

The authors’ broader message is clear and actionable: data curation matters, and careful engineering of what to include from research papers—and how to transform it—may be the difference between neutral/negative and positive gains for math reasoning models.



# A Unified Map of ‘Alignment’ Methods—and Why RL Actually Helped

## Introduction

There is a growing set of techniques used to push language models toward better, more aligned behavior: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), actor-critic methods like PPO, and new variants like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). They are often presented as separate ideas with different motivations. In DeepSeekMath, the authors show these methods can be understood under a compact, practical framework with three core components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm. Framing alignment this way makes it clearer why some techniques work better than others for mathematical reasoning, and it clarifies an important empirical finding from the paper: reinforcement learning (RL) improved the model’s robustness (Maj@K) rather than its base “Pass@K” capability.

This post walks through that unified view, highlights why online sampling and graded (continuous) rewards matter, summarizes GRPO at a high level, and explains the key takeaway about what RL actually changed in model behavior.

## The unified framework

At its heart, each of these methods updates model parameters by computing a gradient of the form

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\ \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\right],

where the three pieces determine everything practical:

- Data Source (\mathcal{D}): where (question, output) pairs come from. Is the data sampled from the currently trained policy (online) or from a fixed SFT model (offline)?
- Reward Function (\pi_{rf}): how we score or compare outputs. This can be a rule (correct/incorrect), a learned reward model that outputs continuous scores, or pairwise preferences.
- Algorithm (GC): how the method turns reward signals into per-token gradient coefficients (the multiplier in the formula above). This includes whether we use clipping (PPO-style), pairwise losses (DPO), or accept/reject logic (RFT).

Viewed this way, SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO are points in a 3-dimensional design space rather than unrelated islands.

- SFT: offline data source, no reward model, algorithm is standard supervised log-likelihood on human examples.
- RFT: typically offline — sample many outputs from the SFT model, filter those with correct answers, and fine-tune on the filtered set (effectively a binary rule-based reward).
- DPO: offline samples with pairwise preference loss that directly optimizes for higher pairwise likelihood of preferred outputs.
- PPO: online sampling (actor-based), uses a learned reward model, and computes per-token advantages via a learned value function (actor-critic).
- GRPO: online sampling too, uses a learned reward model, but replaces the critic/value network with a group-based baseline computed from several outputs sampled for the same question.

This abstraction makes patterns and trade-offs obvious: for example, whether training uses online or offline data strongly influences whether the policy can adapt to its own evolving distribution; and whether the reward is binary or continuous affects whether the algorithm can differentially strengthen partially-correct vs fully-correct outputs.

## Why online sampling matters

The paper compares offline and online variants (RFT vs Online RFT), and shows online sampling gives clear advantages during later stages of training. Intuitively:

- Offline approaches (sample once from SFT) can only correct biases present in the fixed sample distribution. As the trained policy diverges from the SFT model, the offline data becomes less representative.
- Online sampling continuously explores the current policy’s outputs. This lets the training loop react to the policy’s actual mistakes and modes of behavior, enabling larger and sustained gains as the model improves.

Empirically, Online RFT outperforms RFT: initially they are similar (policies are close), but online sampling yields more benefit in later iterations when the policy departs from the SFT distribution.

## Why graded (continuous) rewards matter

Another important axis is how rewards are used. Binary rule-based rewards (correct/incorrect) are simple and useful, but they collapse all correct outputs into the same signal and ignore gradations of quality. A learned reward model that scores outputs on a continuous scale lets an algorithm differentiate among outputs that are partially right, factually accurate in parts, or entirely correct.

This matters because:

- Binary reinforcement (e.g., RFT) treats all correct answers equally and does not penalize near-misses. It therefore can only push probability mass toward any correct-looking outputs without preference among them.
- Graded rewards let the optimizer assign stronger positive gradients to clearly superior outputs and negative gradients to worse ones. That more informative signal produces a richer gradient coefficient function GC in the unified formula and yields finer control over the policy distribution.

GRPO explicitly leverages graded rewards: it normalizes and uses the relative scores within a sampled group to compute advantages, allowing fine-grained reinforcement and penalization across outputs for the same question.

## GRPO in plain terms

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) is a lightweight variant of PPO designed to reduce memory and modelling overhead:

- Instead of training a separate critic/value network to estimate a per-token baseline (as in PPO), GRPO samples a group of G outputs for the same question and uses the group mean (and standard deviation) as the baseline. The reward for each sampled output is centered and scaled relative to its group.
- Advantages across tokens are then set from these normalized scores. For process supervision, stepwise rewards are normalized and summed forward into token-level advantages.
- GRPO still uses PPO-like clipping on per-token ratios and adds an explicit KL penalty between current policy and a reference model, but it removes the need for a learned value model and thus lowers memory/computation costs.

Because GRPO computes baselines from the sampled group, it aligns nicely with how reward models are trained (often on comparisons), and it benefits from online sampling: the group is drawn from the live policy, so baselines reflect the policy’s current output distribution.

## What RL actually changed: Maj@K vs Pass@K

A central diagnostic result in the paper distinguishes two different notions of model improvement:

- Pass@K: the probability that at least one sampled output among K samples (or a single top sample) is correct. This is a measure of the model’s raw capability to produce a correct answer in its distribution tail.
- Maj@K: the probability that a majority (or the top-ranked sample by a reward model) among K samples is correct — a measure of robustness and concentration of probability mass on correct answers.

DeepSeekMath’s experiments show a consistent pattern: RL (GRPO and other online, graded-reward methods) improves Maj@K substantially, but not Pass@K. Concretely, RL increases the model’s tendency to place higher probability mass on correct solutions, making correct answers more reliably appear near the mode of the policy distribution. It does not necessarily make rare, high-quality answers more likely to appear in the tail if they were already rare.

Put another way: RL made the model more robust and more likely to produce correct answers consistently (higher Maj@K), rather than increasing the absolute diversity or raw capability that would raise Pass@K. This is consistent with the observation that RL tends to “stabilize” or concentrate the output distribution toward preferred behaviors as judged by the reward model.

Why does this happen under the unified framework? Because:

- Online sampling lets the algorithm observe the policy’s current mistakes and tune the policy toward corrective behavior.
- Graded rewards let the algorithm differentially reinforce better outputs and penalize worse ones, sharpening the distribution.
- Algorithms that trust the reward signal (PPO/GRPO) apply these graded gradients across tokens so the policy’s conditional probabilities re-weight toward higher-reward trajectories.

The result is a redistribution of mass within the model’s existing capabilities rather than unlocking fundamentally new reasoning modes.

## Practical implications

- If your goal is to increase the model’s reliability and make correct answers more likely in typical decoding runs (higher Maj@K), then online RL with a good reward model (and group normalization like GRPO) is an efficient strategy.
- If your goal is to expand the model’s raw capability to find solutions that are currently rare (increase Pass@K), RL alone (especially with constrained SFT-question data and naive sampling) may be insufficient. You likely need richer data, stronger pretraining, or targeted fine-tuning that brings those rare modes into the policy support.
- Reward engineering and reward model quality remain central. RL methods largely “trust” the reward model — if the reward model is narrow or miscalibrated, RL will concentrate behavior toward the reward model’s preferences, which may not reflect real task correctness in some out-of-distribution regimes.
- Using group-based baselines (GRPO) is a practical trade-off: it reduces resource needs by eliminating a learned critic while preserving the benefits of comparative advantage estimation when you can sample multiple outputs per question.

## Conclusion

Reframing alignment and fine-tuning algorithms in terms of Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm (GC) clarifies how SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO relate and where their strengths come from. Two concrete takeaways from DeepSeekMath are especially useful: (1) online sampling matters because it keeps training firmly grounded in the policy’s evolving distribution, and (2) graded rewards matter because they let training differentiate among partially correct outputs. Together, these factors explain why RL (implemented as GRPO in the paper) improved robustness — making models more likely to output correct answers near their mode (Maj@K) — even if it did not raise the presence of rare correct outputs in the long tail (Pass@K).
