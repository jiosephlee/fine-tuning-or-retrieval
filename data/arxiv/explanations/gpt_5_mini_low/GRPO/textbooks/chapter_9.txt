\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 9: DeepSeekMath-RL 7B: Training Setup and Results

# RL data scope: CoT-format subsets of GSM8K and MATH (~144K Qs)
The reinforcement learning (RL) phase for DeepSeekMath-RL 7B was deliberately scoped to a compact, focused dataset: chain-of-thought (CoT) formatted questions drawn from GSM8K and MATH, totaling roughly \(1.44\times 10^{5}\) questions. These questions are the same type of problems used during the instruction-tuning (SFT) stage but restricted to CoT-format examples so that the RL optimization concentrates on multi-step reasoning trajectories rather than on a heterogeneous mix of formats.

Two points motivated this constrained data choice. First, concentrating on CoT-format problems allows the reward model and the policy updates to leverage structured intermediate reasoning steps (when available) instead of only terminal correctness signals. Second, using a focused subset (GSM8K + MATH) yields a clear in-domain vs out-of-domain distinction for evaluation: GSM8K and MATH are treated as in-domain (the RL data sources), while other benchmarks—English and Chinese mathematical sets not used in RL—serve as out-of-domain tests of generalization.

# Reward model training data and initialization
The reward model used during GRPO was trained beginning from the base DeepSeekMath-Base 7B checkpoint. Training data for the reward model was constructed following the procedures used in prior CoT-focused RL work: sampled outputs from the SFT/policy model are paired, compared or annotated according to correctness/quality, and used to train a neural reward scorer. The paper reports that the initial reward model is trained with learning rate \(2\times 10^{-5}\) and that iterative updates to the reward model are performed during RL via a replay mechanism (which includes approximately \(10\%\) historical data in each update). This setup produces a reward model capable of scoring whole outputs (outcome supervision) and, where available, stepwise reasoning progress (process supervision).

# GRPO settings: LR, KL coeff, group size (64), batch and length limits
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was the RL algorithm used to fine-tune the instruction-tuned policy. The key hyperparameters and engineering choices used in the reported run are:

- Policy learning rate: \(1\times 10^{-6}\). This conservative step size is applied when updating the policy network during GRPO iterations.
- Reward-model learning rate (for iterative updates): \(2\times 10^{-5}\).
- KL regularization coefficient: \(\beta = 0.04\). GRPO regularizes by explicitly adding a KL divergence term between the trained policy and the current reference policy, rather than embedding KL into the per-token reward.
- Group size (number of outputs sampled per question): \(G = 64\). For each question in a batch, \(64\) candidate outputs are sampled from the old policy; their rewards are computed and the group statistics are used to form relative advantages.
- Maximum sequence length used during sampling/training: \(1024\) tokens.
- Batch size (questions per training batch): \(1024\).
- Policy update cadence: the policy receives a single update following each exploration stage (i.e., one update per batch of sampled outputs). The algorithm then repeats sampling and updating throughout RL.
- Iterative reward-model updates: the reward model is continually refined using new sampled data; the replay buffer retains about \(10\%\) historical samples when retraining the reward model.

These settings reflect the design goals of GRPO: reduce memory/computation by removing an explicit critic model, exploit within-group relative reward statistics to compute advantages, and stabilize training via an explicit KL regularizer and small learning rates.

# Results: 88.2% GSM8K, 51.7% MATH without tools; gains on Chinese OOD
GRPO produced substantial in-domain improvements. Starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B and applying GRPO over the CoT subset, the final DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieved:

- GSM8K (CoT): \(88.2\%\) accuracy.
- MATH (CoT, competition-level): \(51.7\%\) accuracy.

For reference, the instruction-tuned model prior to RL (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) had lower scores (e.g., MATH increased from \(46.8\%\) to \(51.7\%\) during the RL phase). These gains demonstrate that GRPO produced meaningful improvements in both a grade-school quantitative reasoning benchmark (GSM8K) and a more challenging competition-level benchmark (MATH).

Although the RL data were only GSM8K and MATH CoT questions, the RL fine-tuning also yielded improvements on out-of-domain tasks, including Chinese mathematical benchmarks such as CMATH and other held-out datasets. For example, the paper reports improvements on CMATH from \(84.6\%\) (SFT) to \(88.8\%\) after RL. These out-of-domain gains indicate that GRPO did not merely overfit to GSM8K and MATH: the policy’s distributional changes improved robustness on related mathematical tasks in other languages and formats.

# Self-consistency improvements; comparison vs. SFT-only Instruct
One notable effect of GRPO fine-tuning was the improvement of self-consistency under sampling. The paper states that DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches \(51.7\%\) on MATH without tools, and that self-consistency over \(64\) samples achieves \(60.9\%\). (Here, self-consistency refers to sampling multiple chain-of-thought answers and aggregating them—e.g., majority voting over final answers.) This jump from single-sample accuracy to majority-aggregated accuracy illustrates that RL shaped the output distribution so that correct solutions occupy a larger mass among top-\(K\) samples.

Comparing RL to SFT-only Instruct, GRPO produced consistent improvements across metrics and benchmarks. On GSM8K, the model improved from \(82.9\%\) (SFT Instruct) to \(88.2\%\) (GRPO RL). On MATH, the model improved from \(46.8\%\) to \(51.7\%\). Importantly, these improvements occurred even though RL training used only the CoT-format subset of SFT data (i.e., the RL data were narrower than the SFT data), showing that focused RL can increase peak performance and the probability mass of correct answers without requiring broader or more labeled data.

A further diagnostic mentioned in the paper separates Pass@K from Maj@K behaviors. The authors observed that RL improved Maj@K (majority-based aggregation) but not necessarily Pass@K. This pattern suggests that GRPO’s primary benefit is increasing the proportion of correct outputs among top samples (concentrating probability mass toward better answers), rather than changing the model’s ability to produce any correct answer at least once under diverse decodings.

# Ablations: online vs. offline, outcome vs. process supervision
The paper includes several ablation-style comparisons that illuminate why GRPO works and which design choices matter.

- Online sampling vs. offline sampling (data source): The authors contrast offline approaches (e.g., RFT/DPO using outputs sampled from a fixed SFT model) with online methods that sample from the continually evolving policy. Results indicate that online sampling substantially outperforms offline sampling in the long run. Early in training both styles are similar, but as the policy diverges from the initial SFT model, online-sampled data better reflect the model’s actual errors and potentials and yield greater final gains. This supports the use of on-policy or near-on-policy exploration in RL fine-tuning.

- Outcome supervision (OS) vs. process supervision (PS): Outcome supervision supplies a single terminal reward per output and assigns that normalized scalar as the advantage for all tokens in the output. Process supervision supplies stepwise rewards (scored per reasoning step) and forms token-wise advantages from the sum of future stepwise rewards. The paper reports that GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS). The fine-grained, step-aware signals in PS yield better shaped advantages and better guide policy updates for multi-step reasoning, which is intuitive for chain-of-thought problems where intermediate steps matter.

- GRPO vs. Online RFT / other algorithms (gradient coefficient design): GRPO differs from Online RFT in that GRPO uses the full scalar reward values from a learned reward model to shape gradient coefficients (i.e., graded encouragement/penalty), whereas Online RFT typically treats correct/incorrect judgments more coarsely (reward as a binary filter). Empirically, GRPO outperforms Online RFT, demonstrating the value of graded reward magnitudes and the group-relative advantage normalization used by GRPO.

- Iterative RL and reward-model refinement: The experiments show iterative RL—where sampled outputs from the current policy are used to expand and refresh the reward-model training set, and the reward model is updated with a replay buffer—yields additional improvements. A two-round iterative protocol produced significant gains, particularly after the first iteration, supporting the practice of alternating policy improvement and reward-model retraining rather than a one-shot reward model.

# Interpretations and practical takeaways
The empirical pattern across ablations suggests several mechanistic conclusions relevant for applying RL to reasoning-focused LLMs:

1. Online data collection (sampling from the current policy) is crucial for sustained improvement: it supplies constructive, up-to-date examples that reveal current weaknesses and promising generations.

2. Using graded reward magnitudes (rather than binary accept/reject signals) and forming advantages relative to a sampled group improves learning stability and efficacy. GRPO’s use of group normalization (subtracting group mean and dividing by group standard deviation) aligns well with how reward models are trained (comparative judgments).

3. Providing process-level supervision (stepwise rewards) helps the policy learn to structure intermediate reasoning steps, not just produce a correct final line, and yields better downstream accuracy.

4. RL with GRPO primarily improves the concentration of correct answers in the top-\(K\) sample space (hence improving Maj@K/self-consistency), rather than necessarily increasing the diversity of potential solutions that pass at least once (Pass@K). This implies RL is especially valuable when combined with majority-vote aggregation or other ensemble decoding strategies.

# Efficiency and generalization highlights
GRPO was designed to be more resource-efficient than vanilla PPO by removing the explicit learned critic (value function). Instead, GRPO computes advantages using within-group relative reward statistics from \(G\) sampled outputs (here \(G=64\)). This design reduces memory and compute overhead while still producing stable policy updates via clipped ratios and an explicit KL regularizer to the reference policy.

Despite the constrained RL data scope, GRPO delivered both strong in-domain performance (GSM8K, MATH) and measurable out-of-domain gains (e.g., Chinese benchmarks). This generalization is encouraging: with a focused, high-quality CoT RL dataset and a reliable reward model, GRPO can shape the model’s distribution in ways that transfer beyond the directly trained tasks.

# Summary
DeepSeekMath-RL 7B demonstrates that targeted GRPO-based RL over a CoT-formatted subset of instruction data can substantially increase both absolute accuracy (e.g., GSM8K \(88.2\%\), MATH \(51.7\%\)) and the probability mass of correct answers among top samples (self-consistency up to \(60.9\%\) on MATH with 64 samples). Key elements driving these gains are: (1) group-relative advantage estimation with \(G=64\), (2) graded reward magnitudes from a continuously updated reward model, (3) process-level supervision when possible, and (4) online sampling plus iterative reward-model retraining. GRPO achieves these improvements while being more memory-efficient than PPO by eliminating a learned critic and leveraging within-group baselines.
