\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Orientation: What DeepSeekMath Tries to Achieve

# The challenge of mathematical reasoning for LLMs
Mathematical reasoning differs from many other natural language tasks in that it requires both precise symbolic manipulation and long, structured chains of logical thought. Language models are trained to predict the next token in text; this objective does not by itself guarantee that a model will learn to plan multistep deductions, avoid subtle arithmetic mistakes, or represent intermediate symbolic states in a reliably compositional way. As the DeepSeekMath paper frames it, mathematical problems often combine hierarchical structure (subgoals that must be solved in order), symbolic computation (algebraic manipulation, arithmetic, calculus operations), and verification (ensuring a derived result is exact). These attributes make mathematical reasoning especially sensitive to the quality and domain-specificity of training data, to how the model is fine-tuned for multi-step solution generation, and to whether training uses supervision that reflects intermediate reasoning steps.

Empirically, the community has observed a wide performance gap between closed-source state-of-the-art models and accessible open-source models on benchmarks that test competition-level mathematics. This gap highlights two failure modes: models that can produce plausible-sounding but incorrect derivations, and models that lack robustness across problem types or languages. The DeepSeekMath work begins from this challenge: can we push an open, efficiently sized model (7B parameters) to competition-level mathematical reasoning by focusing on two main axes—large-scale, high-quality math pre-training and an efficient reinforcement learning stage that improves answer reliability?

# DeepSeekMath 7B at a glance: 51.7\% MATH, 60.9\% with self-consistency
DeepSeekMath presents a family of models culminating in DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, a 7-billion parameter model that attains strong results on established mathematics benchmarks. The headline results reported are an accuracy of 51.7\% on the competition-level MATH benchmark in the single-run evaluation, and an improved 60.9\% when using self-consistency over 64 samples. Self-consistency here refers to sampling multiple solution trajectories from the model and selecting an answer by majority (or another aggregation) to reduce variability and surface the most robust solution among the sampled outputs.

These numbers are notable for two reasons emphasized in the paper. First, they are achieved without external tool use (for the single-run MATH score) and without ensemble voting techniques in the core reported single-run result; the self-consistency result is an explicit multi-sample method. Second, DeepSeekMath 7B approaches the performance of large closed models such as Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on the same benchmark family, despite being much smaller in parameter count. The paper frames this as evidence that model size alone is not the sole determinant of mathematical capability: careful data selection and targeted training procedures can yield substantial gains.

# Two pillars: high-quality math corpus and GRPO reinforcement learning
DeepSeekMath builds its results on two central contributions.

The first pillar is large-scale math pre-training using the DeepSeekMath Corpus. The team mined Common Crawl to extract math-focused web pages and assembled a training corpus of about 120B math-related tokens. The corpus construction uses an iterative pipeline starting from a seed dataset (OpenWebMath), training a fastText classifier to retrieve similar pages from Common Crawl, ranking and selecting high-scoring pages, and iteratively expanding the seed with domain-level mining and human annotation. To prevent benchmark contamination, they filtered pages that contained n-gram overlaps (10-gram exact matches, or exact matches for shorter segments of length 3–9) with evaluation benchmarks such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval. The resulting corpus is multilingual (predominantly English and Chinese) and is substantially larger than previously released math-focused corpora; the authors report it is roughly 7× the size used by Minerva and 9× the size of OpenWebMath.

Building on that corpus, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was trained for 500B tokens with a mixture of 56\% DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4\% AlgebraicStack, 10\% arXiv, 20\% GitHub code, and 10\% multilingual Common Crawl. The paper reports that this base model already outperforms other open-source base models and even competes with much larger closed models on several math benchmarks (for example, reaching 36.2\% on MATH from the base model and 64.2\% on GSM8K).

The second pillar is an efficient reinforcement learning algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO is designed as a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) but removes the need for an auxiliary critic (value function) by estimating baselines from a group of sampled outputs for the same prompt. Concretely, for each question the policy samples a group of $G$ outputs, the reward model scores each output, and advantages are computed relative to the group's average and standard deviation. GRPO’s objective applies a clipped likelihood ratio per-token (as in PPO) but uses group-relative advantages and directly adds a KL regularizer against a reference policy. There are two forms of supervision compatible with GRPO: outcome supervision (a single normalized reward assigned equally to every token of an output) and process supervision (rewards assigned at step boundaries so the advantage at a token sums normalized future step rewards). Iterative GRPO further updates the reward model by replaying samples from the evolving policy. Using GRPO and only a subset of English instruction-tuning data, the paper reports GRPO improved DeepSeekMath-Instruct to reach 88.2\% on GSM8K and 51.7\% on MATH.

Together, the corpus and GRPO implement a training pipeline that emphasizes domain-relevant data and an RL stage tuned for comparative reward signals (reward models trained on pairwise comparisons). The design choices reduce RL memory cost (by removing the critic) and align with how reward models are typically trained.

# Positioning vs. GPT-4/Gemini and open-source landscape
The DeepSeekMath paper positions its contributions relative to both closed-source and open-source models. On the closed-source side, models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra represent current high-water marks in reasoning benchmarks, but they are proprietary and inaccessible for community research. DeepSeekMath aims to narrow the gap between open and closed models: a carefully pre-trained 7B model plus specialized fine-tuning and GRPO achieves performance that approaches those larger closed models on the competition-level MATH benchmark.

Compared with open-source alternatives, DeepSeekMath reports substantial improvements. DeepSeekMath-Base already outperforms open-source base models (Mistral 7B, Llama variants, etc.) on multiple mathematics and reasoning tasks. After instruction tuning (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) and reinforcement learning (DeepSeekMath-RL with GRPO), the final 7B model surpasses many larger open-source instruction-tuned models (including some 70B models) on math-specific evaluations, while also improving multilingual math capability—because the corpus includes high-quality non-English math content, the model shows stronger performance on Chinese math benchmarks than prior English-centric math-trained models.

The paper carefully notes limitations: DeepSeekMath does not uniformly match closed-source models across all domains. Geometry and theorem-proof tasks remain relative weaknesses, and few-shot scaling behavior is not as strong as in much larger models like GPT-4. The authors point to possible causes such as pre-training selection bias and model scale constraints.

# Textbook roadmap and prerequisites
This chapter has given an orientation to what DeepSeekMath aims to achieve: high-quality, scalable math pre-training combined with an efficient RL algorithm (GRPO) yields a compact model (7B) with strong competition-level mathematical reasoning. For a student preparing to study the rest of the DeepSeekMath material and related research papers, the following roadmap and prerequisites will help make subsequent chapters accessible.

First, prerequisites in modeling and machine learning: familiarity with transformer-based language models, maximum likelihood pre-training, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and the basics of policy optimization in reinforcement learning, particularly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Understanding likelihood ratios, KL divergence, and the role of a value function (critic) in actor-critic methods will be important to appreciate GRPO’s design choices.

Second, background in dataset construction and evaluation: knowledge of web-scale data sources like Common Crawl, techniques for domain-specific data mining (classifiers like fastText), deduplication, and benchmark contamination avoidance methods (exact n-gram filtering) will be necessary to follow the corpus construction chapters. Also, familiarity with chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, program-of-thought (PoT), and program-aided evaluation paradigms will be useful to understand how instruction tuning and evaluation are structured.

Third, reading roadmap for the textbook: after this orientation chapter, the reader should expect chapters that dive into (a) the iterative data mining and decontamination pipeline used to assemble the DeepSeekMath Corpus, (b) the pre-training experiments and ablations showing the corpus’s effects relative to other math corpora, (c) supervised fine-tuning strategies for CoT/PoT/tool-integrated formats, (d) the GRPO algorithm, its derivation from PPO, practical implementation details (group sampling, normalized group advantages, KL regularization), and empirical RL ablations (outcome vs. process supervision, online vs. offline sampling, iterative reward-model updates), and (e) evaluation analyses including self-consistency, Pass@K vs. Maj@K effects, and limitations observed on geometry and theorem proof tasks.

Finally, suggested practical skills: the student will benefit from hands-on experience with training and evaluating language models (using tokenized corpora and supervised fine-tuning), implementing reward models trained from preference data, and running controlled RL experiments that sample multiple outputs per prompt to compute group statistics. These practical exercises will make the conceptual descriptions of GRPO and the DeepSeekMath pipeline concrete and easier to reproduce or extend.

Concluding this orientation, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that focused, large-scale domain data plus an RL algorithm designed to work with group-comparative reward signals can push a modestly sized model toward competition-level math performance. The coming chapters unpack each element—corpus construction, pre-training experiments, instruction tuning, GRPO, and evaluations—so you can understand how each design choice contributes to the reported results.
