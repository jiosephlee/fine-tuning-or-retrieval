\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: Data Pipeline: Building the 120B-Token DeepSeekMath Corpus

# Seed corpus choice: OpenWebMath and rationale
The first and foundational decision in assembling the DeepSeekMath Corpus was the selection of a seed corpus to guide large-scale mining. The team chose OpenWebMath as the seed. OpenWebMath is described in the paper as a high-quality collection of mathematical web texts; it therefore serves as a focused exemplar of the kinds of web content that should be recalled from a noisy, massive source like Common Crawl. The rationale is straightforward and practical: when mining billions of web pages, an initial labeled set of high-precision positive examples enables training a retrieval or classification model that preferentially selects pages with mathematical content similar in style and substance to that seed.

Using OpenWebMath as the seed achieves two goals simultaneously. First, it anchors mining to human-curated, domain-relevant material so that the downstream collected corpus contains the kinds of mathematical expressions, expositions, and problem-answer formats that are useful for training reasoning models. Second, it enables an iterative expansion strategy: a classifier trained on the seed can recall additional candidate pages from Common Crawl, and the mining process can then add newly discovered high-quality pages back into the seed set to improve recall and diversity. This bootstrapping approach avoids relying on any single static source and allows the process to adaptively discover domains and page patterns that OpenWebMath alone might not cover.

# FastText classifier training: positives/negatives, hyperparameters, iterations
To scale from a seed to tens of millions of pages, the DeepSeekMath pipeline used a fastText classifier as the primary recall model. The fastText approach was chosen for its speed and scalability across the many terabytes of Common Crawl HTML. Training used a balanced set of positive and negative examples in the first iteration: 500,000 positives sampled from OpenWebMath and 500,000 negatives drawn from Common Crawl pages that were not known to be math-related.

Key hyperparameters of the fastText configuration are specified explicitly in the paper. The vector dimension was set to 256, the learning rate to 0.1, word n-gram length to 3, minimum word occurrence to 3, and the number of training epochs to 3. These settings favor a compact but expressive representation, capture short phrase structure via n-grams, and prioritize broad recall by keeping the minimum occurrence threshold low.

The classifier was then applied to deduplicated Common Crawl pages to score each page’s likelihood of containing mathematical content. In the first iteration the team retained the top-ranked pages by classifier score, with a first-choice volume set empirically (experiments compared top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B token cutoffs; the initial iteration retained the top 40B tokens). After human-in-the-loop augmentation (described below) and iterative retraining, the classifier was improved and used for subsequent larger recalls, ultimately yielding a 120B-token math corpus after four iterations.

# Deduplication (URL and near-dup) and scale reduction
Common Crawl contains enormous redundancy at multiple levels: page mirrors, boilerplate, and scraped copies. Before the fastText scoring stage, the pipeline applied URL-based deduplication and near-duplicate removal to reduce the working set from the unprocessed Common Crawl to a much smaller set of candidate HTML pages. The paper reports reducing the original crawl to approximately 40B HTML web pages after these deduplication steps.

URL-based deduplication removes exact duplicate URL records and consolidates canonical host forms, while near-duplication handles pages that are substantially identical even if their URLs differ (for instance, cached copies or pages differing only by tracking parameters). This scale reduction is essential for two reasons. First, it makes the subsequent fastText recall computationally feasible; scoring tens of billions of pages is expensive even for a lightweight classifier. Second, deduplication increases the diversity of the retained corpus: without it, certain pages or domains could dominate the dataset and bias the math corpus toward specific styles or repetitive content.

# Domain-level mining and human-in-the-loop URL annotation
A critical innovation in the pipeline is the domain-level analysis and selective human annotation to expand the diversity of positive examples. After the first iteration of fastText recall, many math-related pages still remained uncollected. To find them, the authors partitioned Common Crawl by domain (a domain here means pages sharing the same base URL) and computed, for each domain, the fraction of pages that had been collected in the first pass.

Domains where more than 10% of pages were retrieved were flagged as math-related candidates. Examples include dedicated math sites such as mathoverflow.net. For these flagged domains, human annotators inspected domain URL patterns and manually annotated the specific URLs (or URL patterns) that corresponded to mathematical content (for instance, pages under mathoverflow.net/questions). Any previously uncollected pages linked to these annotated patterns were then added to the positive set.

This human-in-the-loop URL annotation accomplishes two things. First, it injects domain-specific structural knowledge that the initial classifier — trained primarily on the seed — may not have captured. Many domain sites have characteristic URL structures or templates that strongly indicate math-related content; capturing these patterns expands recall of high-quality pages. Second, it provides additional positive examples for retraining the fastText classifier, increasing its subsequent ability to generalize beyond the initial seed distribution.

# Iteration dynamics: coverage growth, stopping after diminishing returns
The DeepSeekMath pipeline was iterative: train a fastText classifier on current positives and negatives, recall and rank pages, add high-quality pages to the seed via automated and manual procedures, retrain the classifier, and repeat. Over four such iterations, the pipeline collected 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120B tokens.

The paper reports that by the fourth iteration, nearly 98% of the data collected in that iteration had already been collected in the third iteration. This observation of diminishing marginal returns was used as an empirical stopping criterion: when consecutive iterations yield negligible additional unique high-quality pages, further mining is unlikely to improve corpus diversity and may only add redundant tokens. Thus the iterative process was halted after the fourth iteration, having converged to a stable set of math-relevant pages.

This iterative, data-driven stopping rule is important. It balances the goal of maximizing useful coverage against the computational and annotation cost of further mining. The pipeline thus demonstrates a practical approach: iterate until coverage growth plateaus, then stop and move to downstream decontamination and model training.

# Decontamination protocol: n-gram exact matching against benchmarks
To avoid contaminating evaluation benchmarks, the DeepSeekMath pipeline applied a rigorous decontamination protocol modeled after prior work. The goal was to remove any training text that reproduces problems or answers appearing in the English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks used for evaluation (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval).

The decontamination criterion was based on exact n-gram matching. Specifically, any text segment in the candidate corpus that contains a 10-gram string exactly matching any substring from the evaluation benchmarks was removed. For shorter benchmark texts — those between 3 and 9 grams — exact matching on those shorter n-grams was also used to identify contaminated web pages. This conservative filter ensures that verbatim problems or direct answer text from benchmarks are unlikely to remain in the training corpus.

This exact-match approach is simple to implement and conservative in practice. It prevents overfitting to benchmark items while retaining the broader mathematical content and style that make the corpus useful for training reasoning capabilities. By removing pages with exact long n-gram overlap, the pipeline reduces the chance that downstream evaluation scores reflect memorization rather than generalization.

# Multilingual collection: English and Chinese emphasis
An important design decision was to build the DeepSeekMath Corpus as multilingual rather than English-only. While many prior math corpora are heavily English-centric, DeepSeekMath purposely included substantial non-English content, with English and Chinese being the two most represented languages. The fastText classifier and the iterative domain mining naturally discovered and collected multilingual math pages within Common Crawl because the seed and subsequent positive examples included multilingual material.

The multilingual emphasis yields measurable benefits: models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed improvements on Chinese mathematical benchmarks in addition to English ones. This result highlights an operational advantage of web-scale Common Crawl mining: by engineering a selection pipeline (seed, classifier, domain mining, human annotation) that does not artificially restrict language, one can assemble a multilingual math corpus that supports improved performance across languages.

# Quality vs. volume trade-offs and selection thresholds
Throughout the pipeline the authors explicitly grapple with the classic quality-versus-volume trade-off. The fastText classifier ranks candidate pages, and the team must decide how many top-ranked pages to keep at each iteration. Early choices (top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B token thresholds) were evaluated empirically via downstream pretraining experiments to determine the sweet spot where additional tokens remain beneficial rather than redundant or noisy. The initial iteration retained the top 40B tokens; later iterations and refinements produced the final 120B-token corpus.

Several points summarize how the pipeline balances quality and volume:

- Conservative retention by score: ranking candidate pages by classifier score and keeping only top-ranked pages at each iteration prioritizes precision over blind recall. This reduces the inclusion of low-quality or tangential pages.

- Human-in-the-loop augmentation: domain-level human annotation offsets classifier blind spots and increases the diversity and precision of added pages rather than expanding volume with noisier candidates.

- Iteration-based stopping: by monitoring coverage growth and stopping when new iterations add negligible unique material (98% redundancy from iteration three to four), the pipeline prevents wasteful accumulation of near-duplicate or low-information tokens.

- Decontamination: exact n-gram filtering further improves corpus quality by removing potential benchmark contamination, even if that reduces volume.

- Empirical validation: the paper reports pretraining experiments comparing models trained on different corpora and corpus sizes; DeepSeekMath-trained models performed better than alternatives, indicating that the adopted thresholds resulted in a high-quality dataset rather than merely a larger one.

In short, selection thresholds (how many top-ranked tokens to retain) were chosen not by an arbitrary quota but through iterative empirical evaluation: tune the classifier, expand positives with domain annotation, retrain, and test downstream performance to ensure added volume yields genuine model improvements.

# Concluding remarks on transferability
The DeepSeekMath pipeline emphasizes methods that are portable to other domains. The combination of a high-quality seed corpus, a scalable fastText recall model with well-chosen hyperparameters, deduplication to reduce processing scale, domain-level mining enhanced by human URL annotation, iterative retraining until diminishing returns, conservative decontamination by n-gram filtering, and a multilingual openness together form a repeatable recipe. The paper explicitly notes that the same approach could be applied to other domains (for example code), and that the key is the careful engineering of selection and validation criteria tuned by downstream empirical evaluation.

By documenting precise hyperparameters, iteration counts, decision rules (such as the 10% domain flagging threshold and 10-gram decontamination cutoff), and stopping heuristics (convergence in newly discovered pages), the authors provide a reproducible and practical path for constructing large-scale, high-precision domain corpora from Common Crawl while limiting contamination of evaluation datasets.
