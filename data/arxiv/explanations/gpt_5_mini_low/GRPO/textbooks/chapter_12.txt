\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 12: Practical Implementation Guide

# Data mining checklist: classifier bootstrapping, domain mining, human labeling
Reproducing DeepSeekMath’s large-scale math corpus begins with a focused approach to mining Common Crawl (CC) for math-related web pages. The paper’s pipeline is iterative and hinges on a classifier that is bootstrapped from a small high-quality seed. Start by assembling a seed corpus of high precision math pages (DeepSeek used OpenWebMath). From that seed, train a lightweight text classifier (the paper uses fastText) to recall pages with similar characteristics. In DeepSeekMath, they sampled 500k positive examples from the seed and 500k negative examples from CC to train a 256-d fastText model with n-grams up to 3, learning rate 0.1, minimum word frequency 3, and 3 epochs. These concrete settings provide a robust starting point for bootstrapping.

After the initial classifier run, partition Common Crawl into deduplicated and near-deduplicated pages (their pre-filter produced about 40B HTML pages). Use URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication before classification so your candidate set is manageable. Score CC pages with the classifier and retain top-ranked pages; DeepSeekMath experimented with preserving top tokens at different cutoffs (e.g., top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens) to choose a final scale. They kept the top 40B tokens in the first iteration, and after iterative enrichment ended up with 35.5M pages (~120B tokens).

Domain mining is crucial to expand recall beyond the initial seed. For each domain (base URL), compute the fraction of pages that the classifier already retrieved. Domains where more than 10% of pages are retrieved are likely math-related (e.g., mathoverflow.net). Manually inspect and annotate the most relevant URL patterns (for instance, paths like /questions) and add uncollected pages from those URLs to the positive seed set. Retrain the classifier with the enriched seed and re-run retrieval. Repeat this loop a few times; DeepSeekMath performed four iterations, and observed diminishing returns by the fourth (98% of the data was already captured by the third), so iterate until marginal gains are negligible.

Human labeling is used for quality control and to expand positive examples. After the classifier retrieves candidate pages, samples from different score deciles should be human-annotated to estimate precision and guide where to adjust score thresholds. Use the human-labeled examples to further fine-tune the classifier and to build a high-quality held-out validation set for corpus quality checks. DeepSeekMath also used human annotation to refine the classifier between iterations.

# Corpus hygiene: dedup, decontam, multilingual balance
Corpus hygiene requires careful deduplication and strict benchmark decontamination. Start with URL-based deduplication and near-duplication filters before classification, as in the paper, to reduce redundant crawl volume. After selecting math pages, apply text-level deduplication across the mined set to avoid repeating long passages during pre-training. The paper’s initial CC reduction (to ~40B HTML pages) demonstrates that dedup before selection reduces compute and improves diversity.

Benchmark decontamination is explicit and rule-based in DeepSeekMath. Remove any training segments that match evaluation benchmark content by exact n-gram matching: remove text segments containing any exact 10-gram substring that appears in evaluation benchmark texts. For benchmarks shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens long, remove exact matches at that shorter n-gram length. Apply this decontamination across English and Chinese benchmarks used in evaluation (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval). This conservative exact-match filter helps prevent leakage of evaluation items into the pre-training data.

Multilingual balance matters. DeepSeekMath intentionally preserved multilingual mathematical pages (not English-only) and observed gains on Chinese benchmarks as a result. When mining, track language statistics and ensure the retained set matches your target evaluation languages. If you aim to reproduce English-only behavior, you can filter by language; however, the paper found including high-quality non-English data improved multilingual benchmarks, so include language-aware selection when adapting to new domains (e.g., physics or finance in multiple languages).

Finally, keep careful provenance metadata (domain, URL, classifier score, language) per document. That enables targeted ablations (e.g., remove arXiv-derived examples) and is essential if you later want to iterate or decontaminate further.

# Training stacks: optimization schedules, batch sizes, context lengths
DeepSeekMath’s pre-training experiments establish repeatable training hyperparameters across model sizes. The core optimization recipe used for math-pretraining runs (for 1.3B and 7B models) includes AdamW with parameters $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and weight decay $=0.1$. Use a multi-step learning rate schedule: linear warmup to peak over the first 2,000 steps, then decay so that at 80% of training steps the learning rate is 31.6% of peak, and at 90% it is 10% of peak. For 1.3B experiments, they used a peak learning rate of $5.3\times 10^{-4}$; for the 7B continual training, they used peak learning rate $4.2\times 10^{-4}$. These specific numbers are practical defaults for models in this parameter regime.

Batch sizing and context lengths: DeepSeekMath used very large effective batch sizes measured in tokens. For the 1.3B training experiments a batch of 4M tokens and 4K context length were used. For DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, they used batch size 10M tokens with the same 4K context length, and maximum learning rate as mentioned above. These token-based batch sizes are important for stable large-scale training on long-context math passages. If your compute budget differs, scale the batch (in tokens) and learning rate proportionally.

Total pre-training volume: DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was trained for 500B tokens, composed roughly as: 56% DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4% AlgebraicStack, 10% arXiv, 20% GitHub code, and 10% general Common Crawl natural language (English and Chinese). For initial corpus-quality experiments, 150B tokens per corpus were used to compare different math corpora. Use such token-level budgets as targets when evaluating corpus choices.

When doing two-stage vs. one-stage mixes (code then math or simultaneous mixing), follow the paper’s experimental setups: code-first training for hundreds of billions of tokens can be followed by math training; alternatively mixing code and math tokens in a single-stage run was explored to mitigate catastrophic forgetting of coding ability.

# SFT recipe: format unification (CoT, PoT, tool integration), sampling and mixing
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) in DeepSeekMath focuses on unifying multiple problem–solution formats: Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning. To reproduce this, build an SFT dataset where each example contains the problem and a solution in one of these formats. DeepSeekMath collected 776K instruction-tuning examples, combining English and Chinese problems, across CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated formats. English sources included GSM8K and MATH (annotated with tool-integrated solutions), MathInstruct subsets, and Lila-OOD; Chinese sources included K–12 problems spanning many subtopics annotated in CoT and tool-integrated formats.

Format unification implies consistent prompt/response templates so that the model sees regular structure. Concatenate examples end-to-end up to a maximum context length (DeepSeekMath used 4K) during training. They trained the instruct-tuned model for 500 steps with batch size 256 and constant learning rate $5\times 10^{-5}$. This short fine-tuning regime reflects that the starting point is a well-pretrained base and that instruction tuning is relatively light.

Sampling and mixing: keep a balanced mixture of formats and languages according to your target evaluation needs. DeepSeekMath included both CoT and PoT examples and specific tool-integrated examples (e.g., Python programs using math and sympy) for program-aided reasoning. If you plan to support tool execution, include PoT examples that emit executable code and tool-integration examples that show how to call external computations.

Quality control: ensure CoT solutions are correct and demonstrative of desirable step-by-step reasoning; PoT/code examples should be executable or at least syntactically plausible; tool integrated examples should include the prompt, the program, and the expected output pattern. Maintain separate validation sets for CoT and PoT to monitor format-specific performance during SFT.

# RL recipe: reward modeling (rule-derived labels → reward), GRPO default settings
DeepSeekMath obtained substantial gains via reinforcement learning, introducing Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as an efficient alternative to PPO. The RL pipeline has three concrete parts: reward modeling, sampling/exploration, and policy updates via GRPO.

Reward modeling: prepare a dataset of pairwise or stepwise comparisons. The initial reward model training followed the paper’s approach used in prior work: construct training data from rule-derived labels (e.g., whether the answer is correct) and human judgments where available, then train a reward model to score entire outputs (outcome supervision) or intermediate steps (process supervision). DeepSeekMath trained the initial reward model starting from the DeepSeekMath-Base 7B and used a learning rate of $2\times 10^{-5}$ for the reward model.

GRPO specifics: GRPO avoids training a separate value-function critic by using group baselines computed from multiple outputs sampled per question. For each question $q$ sample a group $\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}$ under the current old policy, score each output with the reward model to get $\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}$, then normalize within the group (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation). Two supervision modes are supported:

- Outcome supervision: each $o_i$ receives a single normalized group reward $\widetilde{r}_i$, and set token-level advantages as $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i$ for all tokens in $o_i$.

- Process supervision: score each reasoning step; normalize all step-level rewards across the group to get $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$, and set token-level advantages $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ (sum of subsequent normalized step rewards).

GRPO objective (see paper) resembles PPO’s clipped surrogate but replaces critic-derived advantages with group-relative $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ and places an explicit KL regularizer $\beta \mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta||\pi_{ref}]$ in the loss. GRPO uses an unbiased KL estimator (the paper cites an estimator) to compute the KL term per token.

Default hyperparameters and practical settings from the paper: sample $G=64$ outputs per question; policy learning rate $1\times 10^{-6}$; KL coefficient $\beta=0.04$; max generation length 1024 tokens; training batch size 1024 (questions per batch); perform a single policy update per exploration stage. Iterative training improves performance: after an exploration phase collect new labeled pairs for the reward model and retrain the reward model with a replay buffer (the paper used a 10% historical data replay). Repeat iterations (they found the first iteration yields large gains).

Why GRPO: it reduces memory and compute compared to PPO by removing the critic and leveraging group baselines (advantage computed from relative group scores). It naturally aligns with comparison-style reward datasets since reward models are often trained on same-question comparisons. For practical reproduction, implement the group sampling, within-group normalization, and the GRPO clipped surrogate with the KL regularizer; tune $G$, learning rates, and $\beta$ to your compute budget and dataset.

# Evaluation protocols: zero-/few-shot, CoT vs. PoT, self-consistency
Use consistent evaluation protocols that mirror the training formats and target abilities. DeepSeekMath assessed models across multiple axes:

- Zero-shot vs. few-shot: evaluate both zero-shot and few-shot prompts. The paper notes DeepSeekMath showed similar zero- and few-shot performance due to model scale—still report both to detect few-shot gains.

- CoT vs. PoT: evaluate step-by-step natural language chain-of-thought (CoT) and program-based PoT/tool-integrated outputs separately. For CoT evaluation, use few-shot CoT prompts when appropriate; for PoT, prompt the model to emit Python code that, when executed, yields the final answer. Evaluate execution results as answers in the program-aided setting. Maintain separate benchmarks for no-tool (CoT) and tool-augmented (PoT) evaluations to measure both reasoning and program-aided capabilities.

- Self-consistency (maj/majority voting): when applicable, use self-consistency over multiple samples to improve final answer stability. DeepSeekMath reported that self-consistency with 64 samples increased their MATH score (e.g., from 51.7% to 60.9% for DeepSeekMath-RL 7B). For reproduction, sample $K$ generations (e.g., 64) per question using the same decoding scheme as during RL exploration, collect final answers, then take majority vote; report both single-sample and majority-aggregated metrics.

- Pass@K vs. Maj@K diagnostics: analyze both Pass@K (if using program outputs where multiple executions or retries are possible) and Maj@K (majority among K samples). DeepSeekMath observed RL improved Maj@K rather than Pass@K, indicating RL stabilized the distribution to boost the chance that the correct response appears among top-K samples. Track both metrics to determine whether improvements come from capability gains or from distributional sharpening.

- In-domain vs. out-of-domain splits: when RL is trained on a subset of tasks, measure both in-domain (used during RL) and out-of-domain tasks to test generalization. The paper trained RL on GSM8K and MATH CoT-format examples and observed improvements on both in-domain and several out-of-domain benchmarks.

- Multiple benchmarks and languages: evaluate on a diverse set of benchmarks covering difficulty levels and languages (DeepSeekMath evaluated GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW Courses, MMLU-STEM for English; MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao subsets for Chinese). Ensure you include problem sets that reflect practical demands in your target domain (e.g., physics or finance) and decontaminate them from training data.

Practical evaluation tips: decontaminate training corpora against the evaluation benchmarks (see earlier section), store per-sample metadata (format, language, source), and report both single-sample and aggregated (self-consistency) results. Also separate CoT and PoT evaluations so you can distinguish natural-language reasoning improvements from program-based solving gains.

# Final notes and domain adaptation
When adapting this blueprint to other structured domains (physics, finance), follow the same overall pipeline but replace seed corpora and domain-specific validators. For classifier bootstrapping, use authoritative domain seed sets (textbooks, curated corpora). For reward modeling, construct rule-derived correctness signals reflective of domain standards (e.g., equation correctness, conserved quantities, balance sheets) and supplement with human-annotated comparisons or step-scores if available. For SFT, design CoT-style and PoT-style exemplars relevant to domain workflows (derivations in physics, spreadsheet/formula code in finance). For RL, the GRPO strategy—group sampling, group normalization, outcome and process supervision, iterative reward-model retraining—remains applicable and resource-efficient for domains where training a full critic is costly.

By following the classifier bootstrapping, rigorous corpus hygiene, the training and SFT recipes, and the GRPO-based RL pipeline with the evaluation protocols above, you can reproduce the core components of DeepSeekMath or adapt them to other specialized reasoning domains while maintaining scalable, reproducible practices.
