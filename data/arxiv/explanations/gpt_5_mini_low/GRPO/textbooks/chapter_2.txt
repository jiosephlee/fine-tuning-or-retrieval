\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Background: Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models

# Nature of math reasoning: symbolic, multi-step, verifiable

Mathematical reasoning differs from most natural language tasks in three interrelated ways: it is symbolic, it is inherently multi-step, and its results are, at least in principle, verifiable. Symbolic aspects arise because mathematical problems often require manipulating numbers, expressions, and structured objects (for example, algebraic formulas, integrals, or geometric constructions) rather than merely producing fluent natural language. When a language model answers a math question, it must coordinate natural language with operations that resemble symbolic computation: arithmetic, algebraic transformation, equation solving, and programmatic evaluation. In the DeepSeekMath work, this symbolic character motivated treating code and computational artifacts as useful pre-training signals (for example, including code tokens and program-of-thought examples), because code provides explicit syntax and operations that align with symbolic manipulation.

Multi-step reasoning is central to complex math problems. Many competition-level problems cannot be solved in a single inference step; they require planning, intermediate calculations, and the chaining of lemmas or sub-results. This is why method families such as chain-of-thought (CoT) and program-of-thought (PoT) are emphasized: they encourage models to produce intermediate reasoning steps so that multi-step dependencies are explicit. The paper demonstrates that providing and training on multi-step solutions (both in pre-training and in supervised fine-tuning) improves performance on multi-hop mathematical benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K.

Finally, mathematical solutions are verifiable: once a candidate solution or program is produced, one can often check correctness deterministically (e.g., evaluate a Python program or verify an equation). This verifiability creates two important opportunities for model training. First, it enables tool-integrated reasoning where models are allowed to write code that is executed and whose results are used to form final answers. Second, it enables outcome- and process-level reward signals for reinforcement learning: a reward model can compare multiple candidate solutions for the same problem and produce preference signals that guide methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). DeepSeekMath explicitly leverages verifiability by evaluating program outputs (tool use) and by using reward models trained to compare candidate solutions for RL.

# Benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW, MMLU-STEM, BBH, CMATH, MGSM-zh, Gaokao variants

Benchmarks provide the standardized tasks used in the paper to measure progress and to compare models. DeepSeekMath evaluates extensively across both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks and several wider reasoning and code benchmarks. The central English quantitative-reasoning datasets highlighted are GSM8K and MATH. GSM8K targets grade-school arithmetic-style multi-step problems and is commonly used to test chain-of-thought competence; DeepSeekMath reports strong improvements on GSM8K (e.g., DeepSeekMath-Base reached 64.2\% under certain settings, and after RL the model achieved 88.2\%). MATH is a competition-level dataset representing harder contest-style problems; achieving over 50\% on MATH is a notable milestone cited in the paper (DeepSeekMath-RL attained 51.7\% without external toolkits or voting).

Other English benchmarks include SAT and OCW Courses (OpenCourseWare problems used by Minerva), which represent standardized tests and course-level problems respectively, and MMLU-STEM which is the STEM subset of MMLU used to probe subject-matter knowledge through multiple-choice queries. BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) appears as a reasoning benchmark that includes challenging tasks requiring multi-step thought; the paper uses BBH to profile general reasoning capabilities beyond pure math.

For Chinese evaluation, DeepSeekMath uses CMATH, MGSM-zh, and Gaokao variants such as Gaokao-MathCloze and Gaokao-MathQA. The DeepSeekMath Corpus explicitly includes multilingual material (primarily English and Chinese), and the paper reports improvements on Chinese mathematical benchmarks as a consequence. Reporting performance across both languages emphasizes the multilingual coverage of the pre-training data and demonstrates that math pre-training can enhance reasoning in multiple languages.

Finally, the paper evaluates code and formal-math tasks using HumanEval and MBPP (code generation), and formal theorem proving (miniF2F with Isabelle). These additional benchmarks give a fuller picture: improvements on HumanEval and MBPP show preservation or gains in code ability, and performance on miniF2F shows the model’s capability for autoformalization (informal-to-formal translation and theorem proving).

# Prompting paradigms: chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), tool-integrated reasoning

Prompting paradigms are methods for eliciting multi-step reasoning from models by shaping the format of their outputs during training and inference. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompts ask the model to produce explicit intermediate steps in natural language before providing a final answer. CoT has been used throughout the paper for both evaluation and supervised fine-tuning; DeepSeekMath trains on CoT-style data so the model learns to produce step-by-step natural-language derivations, which aids performance on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.

Program-of-thought (PoT) extends the idea of intermediate steps into executable code: rather than writing a chain of natural-language steps, the model emits a short program (for example, Python) that computes the required quantities, possibly using libraries such as math or sympy. The paper uses PoT both during instruction-tuning and evaluation: models are prompted to write Python programs for GSM8K and MATH problems and the programs are executed to generate final answers. This approach exploits the verifiability of program execution and often yields more reliable numerical computations than purely verbal reasoning.

Tool-integrated reasoning refers to allowing the model to call external tools (for instance, executing Python or using symbolic math libraries) during problem solving. DeepSeekMath constructs SFT data that includes tool-integrated solutions and evaluates models both when tool use is disallowed and when it is allowed. The paper reports that instruction tuning with such formats and subsequent reinforcement learning that leverages outcomes of execution improves results substantially, bringing DeepSeekMath-Instruct close to 60\% accuracy on MATH when tool use is permitted.

Together, these paradigms form a continuum: CoT emphasizes natural-language intermediate steps; PoT makes intermediate steps executable; tool-integrated reasoning closes the loop by actually running programs and using their outputs as part of evaluation and training. DeepSeekMath uses all three paradigms in data curation and training to exploit both the expressive benefits of structured reasoning and the practical benefits of execution-based verification.

# Formal math and theorem proving: Isabelle, miniF2F

Formal mathematics and theorem proving are distinct from typical problem-solving benchmarks because they require producing machine-checkable formal proofs rather than informal natural-language solutions. The paper evaluates informal-to-formal proof generation: given an informal statement and proof sketch, the model must produce a formal proof that can be checked by a proof assistant. DeepSeekMath uses miniF2F—a benchmark designed for formal Olympiad-level mathematics—and targets Isabelle as the proof assistant for autoformalization experiments.

The methodology employed in the paper follows a few-shot prompting approach: the model generates a formal proof sketch that is then fed into automated tools (for example, the off-the-shelf Sledgehammer prover integrated with Isabelle) to fill in missing details and attempt to obtain a machine-verified proof. DeepSeekMath-Base shows "strong few-shot autoformalization performance" on miniF2F, suggesting that pre-training on a large math-focused corpus improves the model’s ability to bridge informal reasoning and formal verification. This task differs from standard CoT or PoT in that it demands the model produce syntactically and semantically precise formal constructs that conform to the logic and tactics of a proof assistant, a requirement that closely aligns with symbolic rigor and verifiability mentioned earlier.

# Prior models and corpora: Minerva, Llemma, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2, MathPile

Understanding prior work contextualizes DeepSeekMath’s contributions in both data and modeling. The paper contrasts its approach against several existing models and corpora:

- Minerva is a closed-source model built on PaLM and further trained on mathematical texts and code; despite being much larger (e.g., 540B parameters), Minerva is cited as a reference that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B can outperform on some benchmarks, indicating the importance of targeted pre-training data quality over sheer parameter count.

- Llemma refers to a recent effort (Llemma family) which used Proof-Pile-2 as a mathematical pre-training corpus and produced models such as Llemma-34B. Proof-Pile-2 itself is a composite math corpus that includes OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack, and arXiv papers with a specified ratio between web, code, and arXiv content used in training. DeepSeekMath compares to Llemma models, noting that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforms Llemma-34B on program-aided evaluations such as PoT-driven GSM8K and MATH.

- OpenWebMath is a publicly released Common Crawl-derived corpus filtered for mathematical content; it contains 13.6B tokens. DeepSeekMath uses OpenWebMath as an initial seed dataset in its iterative data collection pipeline but goes much further in scale and iteration.

- Proof-Pile-2 (used by Llemma) and MathPile are other multi-source math corpora. MathPile is an 8.9B-token corpus aggregated from textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, Common Crawl, StackExchange, and arXiv, with over 85\% from arXiv. The paper uses these corpora as baselines in pre-training quality comparisons and finds that models trained on DeepSeekMath Corpus (120B math tokens) achieve stronger downstream performance, suggesting that larger, well-selected web-derived math data can outperform corpora heavily biased toward arXiv.

The DeepSeekMath Corpus is a central contribution: by iteratively mining Common Crawl with a fastText classifier seeded from OpenWebMath and enriched with human annotations, the authors assemble 120B math tokens across roughly 35.5M math web pages. The paper reports that, in controlled pre-training experiments using the same model architecture, training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus yields higher few-shot CoT performance across eight math benchmarks compared with MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. The corpus is also multilingual (English and Chinese predominating), which helps improve performance on Chinese benchmarks where prior corpora tended to be English-centric.

Taken together, the comparisons show two lessons emphasized by the paper: first, the selection and scale of math-relevant web data matter substantially; second, a smaller model (7B) trained on a high-quality, math-focused corpus and followed by SFT and RL can approach performance levels of much larger models that rely on different data mixes.

# Closing remarks

This chapter summarized how mathematical reasoning differs from other NLP tasks, which benchmarks are used to assess progress, the prompting paradigms that encourage multi-step and executable reasoning, formal-theorem-proving evaluation with proof assistants like Isabelle, and the key prior corpora and models against which DeepSeekMath is compared. The DeepSeekMath paper builds on these foundations by constructing a very large math-focused corpus from Common Crawl, leveraging CoT/PoT/tool-integrated SFT, and introducing an RL method (GRPO) that exploits group-based advantage estimation to further improve math reasoning — all pieces aimed at bridging the gap between open-source models and proprietary state-of-the-art systems on challenging mathematical benchmarks.
