\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 13: Limitations, Ethics, and Future Directions

# Current weaknesses: geometry, theorem-proving depth, few-shot limitations

DeepSeekMath achieves strong results on many quantitative reasoning benchmarks, yet the paper explicitly documents areas where the model remains weak. The most salient empirical weaknesses are in geometry and certain theorem-proof tasks. During internal evaluation ("dry run") the model struggled with geometry problems involving triangles and ellipses, and more generally its geometry and theorem-proof capabilities lag behind closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. This suggests two linked shortcomings: (1) gaps in the pre-training and fine-tuning data coverage for geometric content and formal reasoning styles, and (2) limitations of the current model architecture and training recipe at the 7B scale for capturing the representational structure needed in those domains.

The weakness in formal theorem-proving depth also shows up in comparisons on autoformalization and proof automation benchmarks. DeepSeekMath-Base performed strongly on some informal-to-formal tasks (e.g., miniF2F) with few-shot prompting plus automated prover assistance, but the paper acknowledges that its theorem-proving and geometry skills remain comparatively weaker than state-of-the-art closed models. This indicates that while the model can often produce useful proof sketches and leverage toolchains like Sledgehammer to complete proofs, it still fails on a nontrivial fraction of Olympiad-style or geometry-specific proofs that require delicate symbolic reasoning, diagrammatic understanding, or long, structured inference chains.

Another limitation is few-shot capability. The authors note that, constrained by model scale, DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation, whereas GPT-4 improves with few-shot prompts. This suggests that simply exposing a 7B model to additional exemplars at test time does not yield the same jump in capability that larger models enjoy. The consequence is that practical gains from few-shot prompting are limited for DeepSeekMath at its current size, and deployment scenarios that rely on few-shot adaptation will not see the same benefit as with much larger models.

These weaknesses should be read together: some are data-related (insufficient domain coverage for geometry/theorem styles), some are algorithmic (training objectives and RL choices), and some are scale-related (model capacity interacts with data and RL signal). Understanding which factor dominates for each failure mode is central to the research directions discussed below.

# Data bias risks and benchmark contamination safeguards

Collecting high-quality mathematical text from web-scale corpora like Common Crawl enables substantial gains, but it also brings data bias and contamination risks that must be addressed carefully. The paper documents an explicit decontamination strategy: any text segment containing an exact 10-gram match to evaluation benchmark substrings (or exact matches for shorter segments of length 3–9) is removed from the math training corpus to avoid leaking evaluation examples. This is an important safeguard; exact-match filtering is straightforward and effective at removing verbatim benchmark items but does not eliminate all possible contamination modes (e.g., rephrasings, derivations, or other near-duplicates).

Beyond benchmark leakage, data selection from web pages can introduce content bias. The DeepSeekMath Corpus was built by iteratively training a fastText classifier seeded with OpenWebMath and then expanding via domain-level mining and human annotation. While this pipeline increased recall for math-like pages and produced a multilingual corpus, it can still reflect biases present in the seed and annotation choices. For example, if the seed or annotated domains over-represent certain mathematical subfields (algebra, number theory) relative to others (geometry, diagram-heavy topics), the final corpus will be skewed. The paper's observation that geometry and some theorem-proof problem types remain weak suggests such distributional imbalances.

Ethically, model behavior trained on web-sourced mathematical text must be considered for safety and misuse. While mathematical content is less directly hazardous than some other domains, incorrect proofs or confidently wrong solutions can mislead students, researchers, or automated systems that depend on provable correctness. The authors mitigate some risk by emphasizing benchmark decontamination and by evaluating formalization with proof assistants, but additional safeguards (e.g., uncertainty calibration, provenance tracking, conservative defaults in downstream tools) are necessary for responsible release and use.

Finally, multilingual composition introduces disparate quality across languages. The DeepSeekMath Corpus contains significant English and Chinese content and the paper reports improved Chinese benchmark performance versus prior, English-centric corpora. Still, uneven multilingual coverage can propagate fairness issues and capability gaps for under-represented languages. Documenting language breakdowns, release-time metadata, and targeted evaluation sets are practical steps to reduce those biases.

# Scaling questions: model size vs. data quality vs. RL signal

A central finding in the work is that a relatively small model (7B) pre-trained on a large, high-quality, domain-specific dataset can match or surpass much larger models on mathematical tasks. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforms Minerva 540B on certain benchmarks, suggesting that data quality and domain relevance can compensate for parameter count to a substantial extent. This raises nuanced questions about where to allocate compute: scale up model parameters, scale up targeted data, or invest in stronger supervision (SFT/RL)?

The paper's experiments speak to this tradeoff in several ways. First, the DeepSeekMath Corpus (120B math tokens) is far larger than comparable public math corpora and yields a strong base model after continual training. Second, instruction tuning and a focused RL phase (GRPO) applied to the 7B policy produce large gains: GSM8K moves from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% to 51.7% under GRPO. These gains indicate that a high-quality RL signal, even when applied to a small model, can significantly shape the output distribution and recovery of correct answers within top-K generations.

However, the few-shot limitation highlights a scale-sensitive behavior: larger models can better absorb and leverage few-shot exemplars at evaluation time. The paper suggests that at 7B, some capabilities do not scale in the same way. Thus, there is a multi-dimensional budget decision: increasing model size tends to improve representational capacity and few-shot adaptability, whereas increasing curated, domain-specific data and targeted RL can more economically improve performance on specific tasks. The proper balance likely depends on the target applications; for broadly general-purpose reasoning and robust few-shot use, parameter scaling remains important, whereas for specialized mathematical systems that must be efficient and deployable, data and RL may offer better returns.

The role of RL signal strength and quality is also critical. GRPO demonstrates that carefully designed RL algorithms that align with the structure of reward data can provide marked improvements without the resource overhead of training a separate value network. But the paper also uncovers that RL's main effect is to shape the output distribution to push correct answers into the top-K rather than to fundamentally change underlying reasoning competence. This means that while RL can be highly effective at improving empirical benchmark scores, the gains may not generalize in the same way as truly improved underlying capability would.

# Future RL: robust to reward noise, weak-to-strong, uncertainty-aware rewards

The unified paradigm developed in the paper expresses update gradients in terms of three components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm. Using this lens, the authors identify major directions for future RL research that aim to make RL both more effective and more reliable for LLMs in mathematical domains.

One pressing direction is robustness to reward noise. Reward models are imperfect; even carefully curated datasets contain mislabels. The paper cites evidence that quality of reward annotations can be noisy (the PRM800K discussion is a motivating example). If RL algorithms fully trust reward signals, spurious or noisy supervision can push the policy model in undesirable directions. Therefore, an important research avenue is to design RL algorithms that mitigate the impact of noisy rewards—e.g., via robust objective formulations, conservative update rules, statistical filtering of outlier rewards, or hybrid targets that combine weak and strong supervision.

A related program is the weak-to-strong learning paradigm. The idea is to bootstrap from weak or noisy supervisory signals toward progressively stronger, more reliable signals. Concretely, one could first use coarse outcome-level comparisons (weak supervision), then leverage process supervision (step-level rewards) as the reward model improves, and finally incorporate verifier-based or tool-based checks (strong signals) such as symbolic solvers or proof assistants. This staged approach helps to avoid catastrophic policy drift due to early noise and to gradually shape policies toward higher-fidelity behaviors.

Uncertainty-aware reward modeling is another vital direction. Current reward models produce point estimates of quality, but not calibrated uncertainty. If reward models can report predictive uncertainty or confidence, RL algorithms can down-weight low-confidence signals and focus learning where the reward model is reliable. Uncertainty estimates can be used to define trust regions (only apply strong updates when confidence exceeds a threshold), to prioritize data collection for reward-model improvement, or as input to Bayesian RL formulations.

Finally, the paper advocates for developing more powerful process reward models. Outcome-only rewards (single scalar for the end result) are weaker signals for complex, multi-step reasoning; process supervision that scores intermediate steps provides richer gradients and allows the algorithm to credit or blame partial reasoning structures. Efficiently collecting and training such process reward models—possibly using mix of automated checks, human annotations, and self-supervision—is an important practical and theoretical challenge.

# Data-side improvements: broader math domains, better multilingual coverage

On the data side, the work shows that Common Crawl hides a substantial amount of useful mathematical content and that careful iterative selection can produce a very large math corpus. Yet the observed performance gaps (geometry, certain theorems) reveal that coverage is incomplete in important subdomains. Future data improvements should aim to broaden the domain breadth and depth. Specific directions include targeted mining for geometry-intensive resources (lecture notes, problem libraries with diagrams, solution expositions), curated collections of formal proofs and theorem statements, and datasets that emphasize diagrammatic or visual reasoning.

Another axis is multilingual balance. DeepSeekMath's inclusion of Chinese material improved Chinese benchmark performance relative to English-only corpora. Extending this approach to a wider set of languages is important for equitable capability and for discovering mathematical reasoning patterns that vary across linguistic and pedagogical traditions. Careful annotation and validation across languages will also help ensure reward models and evaluation metrics generalize well.

Data cleaning and deduplication practices should continue to be refined. Exact 10-gram decontamination is valuable but imperfect; future pipelines can incorporate semantic deduplication and paraphrase-aware detection to further reduce contamination risk. Additionally, assembling diverse seed corpora rather than relying on a single seed (OpenWebMath) can reduce selection bias in the fastText classifier and improve recall across subfields. Finally, including more machine-checkable content—e.g., LaTeX sources paired with verified computations or proof assistant developments—can support stronger supervision downstream.

# Advanced sampling/decoding and efficient inference for exploration efficiency

The paper identifies exploration strategy and decoding as limiting factors for RL, particularly because RL improvements concentrated on Maj@K rather than Pass@K. Advanced sampling and decoding strategies can make exploration both more efficient and more informative for RL. Tree-structured search methods (e.g., sampled tree search or search guided by value/policy) can reveal diverse, high-quality solution paths that simple nucleus sampling may miss. These richer candidate sets help the reward model and policy to find better positive examples and informative negative contrasts.

Efficiency of inference is also crucial. Sampling large groups of outputs per prompt is expensive; GRPO requires sampling G outputs per question (the experiments set G=64), which is computationally heavy. Efficient decoding methods—speculative decoding, caching of intermediate states, multi-token parallel sampling, and distilled samplers—can reduce compute while preserving diversity. The paper cites speculative and other efficient inference techniques as important enablers for better RL; combining those approaches with algorithmic improvements like GRPO's group-based baseline can substantially reduce the wall-clock cost of RL.

Finally, the choice of sampling strategy interacts with the reward model and data source. If the reward model generalizes poorly, advanced decoding might produce many out-of-distribution candidates that are mis-scored. Thus, iterative cycles that co-evolve the sampling policy, reward model, and policy updates (the "iterative RL" strategy in the paper) are recommended: sample, score, retrain reward model with replay, and repeat. This tight loop benefits from efficient inference to be practical.

# Concluding practical recommendations and ethical considerations

The paper's empirical and conceptual contributions suggest practical recommendations for near-term research and deployment. On the pre-training front, prioritize high-quality, diverse mathematical content and invest in iterative, human-in-the-loop selection to reduce selection bias and to fill coverage holes (geometry, formal proofs). For fine-tuning and RL, adopt algorithms that reflect the structure of reward data: group-relative baselines (as in GRPO) align well with comparison-trained reward models, process supervision can provide denser gradients, and iterative retraining of reward models reduces misalignment over training.

Ethically, releases should document data provenance, decontamination procedures, language composition, known failure modes, and intended use cases. Because RL tends to reshape output distributions to prefer higher-scoring but not necessarily more verifiable answers, systems combining DeepSeekMath-like models with automated verifiers, provenance tracing, and uncertainty signaling will better serve users who need reliable mathematical correctness.

Research directions arising from the unified paradigm include robust RL algorithms that tolerate noisy rewards, weak-to-strong supervision pipelines, uncertainty-aware reward modeling, richer sampling strategies for exploration, and continued attention to the data curation problem—both for domain breadth and multilingual equity. Pursuing these directions can close the remaining gaps identified in geometry, theorem-proving depth, and few-shot adaptability while improving the reliability and safety of deployed mathematical reasoning systems.
