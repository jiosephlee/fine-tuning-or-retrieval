\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: Validating Corpus Quality and Scale

# Controlled 1.3B-parameter experiments and training setup

To assess the intrinsic quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus relative to other available math-focused corpora, the authors designed controlled pretraining experiments using a medium-sized model so that differences in downstream performance could be attributed primarily to the data rather than model capacity. The chosen experimental vehicle is DeepSeek-LLM with $1.3\,$B parameters. Training each model on a single corpus for a fixed number of tokens offers a controlled comparison: all models share architecture, hyperparameters, and optimization schedules, varying only in the pretraining data.

The training recipe is described carefully and remains consistent across corpora. Optimizer choice is AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and weight decay $=0.1$. The learning rate follows a multi-step schedule: a linear warmup to peak after $2{,}000$ warmup steps, then decay to $31.6\%$ of the peak value at $80\%$ of total training, and further decay to $10\%$ of the peak after $90\%$ of training. The peak learning rate is set to $5.3\times 10^{-4}$. Models are trained with a large batch of $4\,$M tokens per update and a context length of $4{,}000$ tokens. Each run trains for $150\,$B tokens, providing the same exposure budget to each corpus so that final differences reflect data content and distribution rather than unequal training effort.

This consistent setup isolates the corpus as the principal independent variable. Because the $1.3\,$B model is small enough to train many variants while still expressive enough to show differences in reasoning capacity, it is a good choice for comparative evaluation. The training environment and hyperparameters are intentionally those used elsewhere in the project (the HAI-LLM framework), ensuring that the comparison reflects realistic pretraining practice rather than toy settings.

# Baselines: MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2 (with Web/Code/arXiv mix)

Three prior corpora are chosen as baselines because they represent the principal approaches in prior math-oriented pretraining: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. MathPile is a multi-source aggregation dominated by processed arXiv material (over $85\%$ of its tokens), alongside textbooks, ProofWiki, StackExchange, and some Common Crawl fragments. OpenWebMath is a Common Crawl–derived filtered subset explicitly curated for mathematical content, and Proof-Pile-2 is a composite that mixes web math, mathematical code from AlgebraicStack, and arXiv in a fixed ratio (the paper follows the $2:4:1$ arXiv:Web:Code ratio used by the Proof-Pile-2 authors).

Each baseline represents a different hypothesis about what data drives math reasoning capability: MathPile prioritizes formal scientific writing (arXiv), OpenWebMath prioritizes web-native mathematical exposition, and Proof-Pile-2 combines modalities including code. By training the identical $1.3\,$B model separately on each corpus with the same $150\,$B-token budget, the experiments directly compare which source or mixture yields the strongest downstream reasoning.

# Findings: higher average quality, steeper learning curves, longer scaling regime

Across the downstream mathematical benchmarks, models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperform those trained on the competing corpora under the controlled $1.3\,$B experiments. The paper presents aggregated few-shot chain-of-thought evaluations over eight benchmarks; in those evaluations, the DeepSeekMath-trained model exhibits a clear lead. The authors interpret this lead as evidence that the DeepSeekMath Corpus has higher average usefulness per token for mathematical reasoning than the other corpora under test.

Two empirical characteristics in the learning dynamics support this interpretation. First, the learning curve (performance as a function of tokens seen during training) is steeper for DeepSeekMath: performance rises faster early in training, indicating that each token provides more immediately useful signal for the downstream tasks. Second, the performance improvements persist longer with DeepSeekMath, producing a longer effective scaling regime before plateauing. By contrast, models trained on smaller or less-targeted corpora showed earlier saturation: because those corpora are both smaller and more repetitive, the $150\,$B-token schedule results in many repeated passes over the same material, which produces diminishing returns and a flatter long-term curve.

The paper notes a concrete comparison point: when Proof-Pile-2 was run for one full epoch at $50\,$B tokens, the DeepSeekMath-trained model already surpassed the Proof-Pile-2 model at that same token exposure, implying that DeepSeekMath's per-token quality is higher. This is the operational meaning of “higher average quality”: given equal token budget, DeepSeekMath yields stronger downstream mathematical reasoning.

# Multilingual benefits and cross-lingual transfer (English↔Chinese)

A distinctive property of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its multilingual composition: although English is dominant, a substantial fraction of tokens are in Chinese and other languages. The controlled experiments demonstrate that this multilinguality translates into improved performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks as well as English ones. When the $1.3\,$B model is trained on DeepSeekMath tokens, it improves both English benchmark scores (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese benchmark scores (e.g., CMATH, Gaokao-style tasks) relative to models trained on primarily English corpora.

This result highlights two points about cross-lingual transfer. First, mathematical content carries structural and conceptual regularities that can be shared across languages; exposure to high-quality math examples in multiple languages helps the model learn reasoning patterns that generalize across linguistic forms. Second, restricting pretraining to English-only math sources can harm non-English performance: some prior corpora are strongly English-centric, and models trained on them either show limited improvement or regress on Chinese tasks. Therefore, the DeepSeekMath Corpus's inclusion of multilingual web math appears to produce desirable cross-lingual transfer effects, improving the model's ability to reason in both English and Chinese.

# Interpreting performance vs. tokens seen (avoid over-epoching small datasets)

A methodological insight stressed by the paper is that comparing models trained on different-size corpora requires care to avoid confounding by over-epoching. If a corpus is small and the training schedule visits it many times, models will see repeated identical or near-identical material; that repetition can artificially flatten learning curves or lead to premature saturation. In the experiments, baseline corpora such as MathPile and OpenWebMath are much smaller (e.g., under $15\,$B tokens), so a $150\,$B-token training budget implies many repeated passes. DeepSeekMath, by construction, provides $120\,$B tokens of curated math material, substantially increasing the variety of tokens available.

The key practical consequence is that per-token usefulness must be read together with corpus size: a small but high-quality dataset may give quick early gains but hit saturation quickly when repeatedly epochs are forced; a larger but noisier dataset may continue to provide marginal gains longer. The DeepSeekMath experiments show both favorable per-token quality and sufficient scale to sustain learning across the large $150\,$B-token schedule. Thus, the measured advantages are not merely a byproduct of having fewer repeats; instead, they reflect both higher-quality examples and a larger, less-redundant token pool, which together produce steeper initial gains and longer-lasting improvement before plateauing.

In summary, the controlled $1.3\,$B-parameter experiments isolate the effects of data and reveal three empirical facts about DeepSeekMath: (1) it yields higher downstream mathematical performance per token compared to the tested baselines; (2) its learning curves are both steeper and sustain improvement longer, indicating better per-token signal plus less redundancy; and (3) its multilingual composition produces tangible cross-lingual benefits, improving both English and Chinese mathematical reasoning. The experiments also underscore the importance of matching token budgets to corpus size to avoid misinterpreting results from over-epoched small datasets.
