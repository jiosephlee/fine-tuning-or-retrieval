\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 11: Empirical Insights: What Really Helped (and What Didn’t)

# Code training improves math reasoning (with and without tools)

One of the clearest experimental findings in the paper is that pre-training on code materially benefits mathematical reasoning, both when the model solves problems unaided (pure language chain-of-thought) and when it is allowed to use programmatic tools (program-of-thought or writing Python). The authors measured this by comparing several training regimens where models received code tokens either before math training (two-stage) or mixed together with math tokens (one-stage). Across these controlled experiments, models that saw a substantial amount of code tokens showed consistent gains in program-aided mathematical tasks, and also obtained improvements on reasoning tasks that did not use tools.

Intuitively, code provides structured, symbolic, and procedural patterns that overlap with mathematical problem solving: clear variable manipulations, stepwise computations, and precise expression of algorithms. In the experiments reported, code training for large amounts of tokens (e.g., 400B code tokens) followed by math training produced the best program-aided reasoning performance when compared to models that received general text in place of code. This demonstrates that the representation and procedural patterns learned from code transfer to the task of writing correct programs to solve math problems.

Beyond program-aided tasks, code training also improved unaided chain-of-thought reasoning under the two-stage protocol. The initial code phase yields moderate gains in subsequent math-only fine-tuning, suggesting that code-derived inductive biases (such as modeling discrete operations and deterministic computation sequences) help the later acquisition of stepwise mathematical reasoning. The authors therefore conclude that, at least within their experiments and model scales, code training is a useful preparatory stage for improving mathematical reasoning.

# Two-stage vs. one-stage mixes: catastrophic forgetting and mitigation

The experiments compared two general approaches to combining code and math data: (1) two-stage training, where a model is trained on code tokens for a long phase and then fine-tuned on math tokens; and (2) one-stage mixed training, where code and math tokens are interleaved or mixed in a single training run. These settings have distinct trade-offs that practitioners should consider.

Under two-stage training, although the first (code) phase brings strong programmatic capabilities and benefits program-aided math, the subsequent math-only phase can lead to catastrophic forgetting of coding abilities. That is, focusing exclusively on math tokens after extensive code pre-training may erode some of the coding competence. Empirically, the authors found that two-stage schedules gave strong math-with-tool performance, but at a cost to retained coding performance unless special effort is made.

One-stage mixed training—where code and math tokens are trained together—was found to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. Mixing preserved coding performance better and still delivered strong program-aided mathematical reasoning. In some experiments, this mixed approach produced synergistic gains: it both maintained coding metrics and improved program-aided problem solving. However, the mixed single-stage setup did not always match the highest unaided math performance obtained by two-stage training followed by math fine-tuning. The authors conjecture that smaller models (e.g., 1.3B) may lack capacity to fully absorb both code and math signals simultaneously, which can explain why one-stage mixing sometimes underperformed on unaided chain-of-thought reasoning.

The practical takeaway is that if the priority is sustained code capabilities plus program-aided math, mixing code and math in one-stage training is an effective mitigation for catastrophic forgetting. If the top priority is raw unaided math reasoning and coding performance can be sacrificed or reintroduced later, a two-stage schedule (code then math) can produce strong final math performance.

# ArXiv pretraining appears ineffective for targeted math reasoning (with caveats)

A surprising experimental result reported is that arXiv papers—despite their prevalence in many prior math pre-training datasets—did not yield clear benefits for the math reasoning benchmarks used in this study. The authors directly evaluated models trained on arXiv-dominated corpora (e.g., MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama) and measured downstream performance on diverse mathematical tasks, including quantitative reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), multiple-choice STEM (MMLU-STEM), and formal mathematics (miniF2F). Models trained only on arXiv-style collections showed no notable improvements and in some cases degraded performance relative to other math-focused corpora.

There are several cautionary points attached to this finding. First, the authors are explicit that their conclusion is limited to the benchmarks and model scales they tested; different downstream tasks (for example, informalization of formal proofs) might benefit from arXiv content. Second, the experiments did not exhaustively explore combinations of arXiv tokens with other data types; it remains possible that arXiv content could complement other sources when mixed appropriately. Third, model scale may matter: benefits of scientific papers might emerge only at larger parameter counts than those evaluated. Thus, the practical implication is not to discard arXiv wholesale, but to treat it as a non-obvious win: for the targeted benchmarks and settings in the paper, Common Crawl–derived and web-filtered math tokens (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) yielded more consistent gains than arXiv-heavy collections.

In short, practitioners should validate the value of arXiv material for their particular evaluation tasks and model sizes. The paper suggests prioritizing diverse, web-mined math content and validating arXiv additions empirically rather than assuming they will help.

# Why RL works here: boosting robustness of the output distribution (Maj@K vs. Pass@K)

The reinforcement learning experiments (GRPO and related comparisons) revealed an insightful explanation for how RL improves math performance in practice. The authors measured both Pass@K (whether at least one of K sampled outputs is correct) and Maj@K (whether the majority of K sampled outputs are correct). They observed that RL substantially improved Maj@K but not Pass@K. In other words, RL made the model’s high-probability output mass more concentrated on correct answers (raising the chance that the majority of sampled outputs are correct), yet it did not significantly increase the chance that some correct answer exists in the tail.

This pattern supports the hypothesis that RL is primarily boosting the robustness and reliability of the model's output distribution rather than directly increasing fundamental reasoning capability. By adjusting the conditional probabilities, RL pushes already-correct responses to higher likelihood so they appear more frequently among top samples. Consequently, sampling-based strategies such as self-consistency or majority voting over multiple samples (the Maj@K regime) benefit significantly after RL. The authors frame this as RL improving the chance of retrieving a correct response from the model's TopK mass, rather than improving the model’s underlying problem-solving competence as measured by whether it can ever produce a correct solution (Pass@K).

This insight explains why RL-derived gains translate well to evaluation methods that aggregate multiple samples (e.g., majority voting across many chain-of-thought samples) and why even limited RL training—when it improves the distribution of outputs—can lead to large apparent performance boosts in practice.

# Practical guidance on dataset diversity, multilinguality, and contamination control

The paper’s experiments provide several practical recommendations on constructing pre-training and fine-tuning corpora for improving mathematical reasoning.

First, prioritize scalable, high-quality web-mined math content. The DeepSeekMath Corpus was built by iteratively mining Common Crawl using a fastText classifier seeded from OpenWebMath and expanded through domain-level heuristics and human annotation. Empirically, this large, diverse, web-derived corpus (120B math tokens) produced stronger downstream performance than smaller or arXiv-heavy corpora. The iterative collection—selecting domains where a high fraction of pages scored as math and then manually annotating math-specific URLs—helped broaden positive examples and improved classifier recall. This suggests that carefully engineered web selection pipelines can yield large amounts of useful math data.

Second, include multilingual content. The DeepSeekMath Corpus contained substantial English and Chinese math data, and the authors report that having non-English math pages helps performance on Chinese benchmarks (e.g., CMATH, Gaokao tasks). If your target users or evaluation tasks are multilingual, explicitly include and balance math content across those languages rather than assuming English-only data suffices.

Third, handle benchmark contamination explicitly. The authors removed any web page segments that contained exact 10-gram matches to evaluation benchmarks; for shorter benchmark texts (3–9 grams) they used exact matching to avoid contamination. This conservative decontamination is crucial to ensure reported benchmark gains reflect true generalization rather than memorization of evaluation items.

Fourth, curate instruction-tuning and reward-training sets with diverse reasoning formats. The paper’s SFT mixture included chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning examples in both English and Chinese. This diversity allowed downstream models to perform well across both pure language proofs and program-executed solutions. For RL, the reward models were trained from these question-output collections and benefited from process-level supervision (i.e., scoring intermediate steps) in addition to outcome supervision. Incorporating stepwise process signals can produce finer-grained advantages during RL.

Finally, iterate and validate: the authors show that iterative pipelines—both in data mining (multiple classifier-training iterations) and in RL (iteratively updating reward models and re-sampling)—improve final quality. Practical workflows should therefore include cycles of mining, filtering, human annotation, and re-training rather than one-shot collection.

Overall, the empirical message is concrete: invest in carefully engineered, diverse, and contamination-controlled math data (including structured code examples and multilingual pages); prefer mixed or staged schedules mindful of catastrophic forgetting trade-offs; and exploit RL not primarily to grant new reasoning capabilities, but to make correct solutions occupy more of the model’s high-probability mass, especially when combined with sampling-based aggregation techniques.
