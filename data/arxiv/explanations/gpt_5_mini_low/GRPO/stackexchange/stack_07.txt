\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Does GRPO reduce variance relative to PPO’s learned baseline, and how does group size G affect bias/variance?
Question:
In PPO, a learned value function reduces variance but can be mis-specified when token-level rewards are sparse. In GRPO, you use the within-group statistics as a baseline. Intuitively, larger \(G\) should reduce the variance of the baseline estimate but increase sampling cost. Do you have empirical curves of performance vs. \(G\) (e.g., \(G=8,16,32,64\))? Also, is the group baseline biased with respect to the true value function, and if so, is there any theoretical or empirical evidence that this bias is benign?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO does reduce variance compared to using no baseline, and increasing group size $G$ reduces the variance of the group-baseline estimate (roughly as $1/G$) at the expected cost of more sampling. The paper uses $G = 64$ in all reported RL experiments but does not include empirical performance-vs-$G$ curves, so there is no direct reported sweep (e.g., $G = 8,16,32,64$) in the paper. Regarding bias: the group baseline is intended to approximate a question-level (state) value and therefore plays the role of a baseline that preserves the policy-gradient signal; any bias introduced by using a finite-sample group statistic is small in practice (and the paper treats it as benign), but there are known caveats and small-sample corrections you can use (e.g., leave-one-out) if you want to eliminate even small bias.

Explanation and technical grounding in the paper

How GRPO forms the baseline and why it reduces variance
GRPO replaces an explicit learned critic (the per-token/value model used in PPO) with a group-based baseline: for each question $q$ the algorithm samples a group of outputs $o_1,\dots,o_G$ from the old policy, scores them with the reward model to get $r_1,\dots,r_G$, then normalizes rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. In outcome supervision each token’s advantage is set to the normalized scalar reward for that output, i.e. $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \dfrac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r)}{\mathrm{std}(r)}$. In process supervision the per-token advantages are sums of normalized step rewards.

Intuitively this reduces variance because the group mean is a sample-based estimate of the “question-level” expected reward (the baseline). The variance of a sample mean of independent draws scales like $1/G$, so as $G$ increases the baseline’s estimation noise shrinks and the residual variance in advantages goes down. The paper also divides by the group standard deviation (normalization), which stabilizes the scale of gradient coefficients across groups and training iterations.

What the paper reports empirically
The paper’s GRPO experiments (and the reported DeepSeekMath-RL 7B results) use $G = 64$ and show large practical gains over SFT and other simpler methods. However, the paper does not present empirical curves that sweep $G$ (e.g., $G = 8,16,32,64$) and plot performance or bias/variance metrics versus $G$. So there is no direct empirical answer in the paper about the marginal gains vs sampling cost for different $G$ values beyond the choice $G = 64$ used in the main RL runs.

Bias considerations (theoretical + practical)
Policy-gradient theory: subtracting a baseline that depends only on the state (here, the question $q$) does not change the expectation of the policy gradient estimator, so it does not introduce bias in the policy gradient. The group mean is intended to be an empirical approximation of that state value $V(q)$. If the baseline truly depends only on $q$ and not on the particular action (output) being evaluated, the estimator remains unbiased.

Finite-sample caveat: the group baseline computed from the same set of sampled outputs contains contributions from each output in that group. If you compute advantages as $r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r)$ where $\mathrm{mean}(r)$ includes $r_i$ itself, you are using a baseline that depends weakly on the action’s return, which can introduce a small bias in finite samples. In the large $G$ limit this bias vanishes; for moderate $G$ the bias is usually negligible. The paper does not explicitly discuss leave-one-out corrections, but this is a standard remedy: use a leave-one-out baseline $\mathrm{mean}_{j \neq i} r_j$ which removes the self-dependence and recovers an unbiased baseline even at finite $G$.

Practical assessment in the paper: “benign” bias
The paper treats the group baseline approach as an effective substitute for training a separate value model: it removes the cost of a value network, aligns with the comparative nature of the reward model (which is trained on comparisons of outputs for the same question), and performs well in practice (the reported RL model with $G = 64$ achieves substantial benchmark improvements). That practical success is the paper’s empirical evidence that any finite-sample bias from using group statistics is not harmful in their regime; they do not provide a formal proof that the bias is benign, but the large empirical gains and use of normalization implicitly argue that the approach works robustly.

Trade-offs and recommendations (derived from the paper and standard practice)
Larger $G$ reduces estimation variance of the baseline (and thus of the advantage), but increases sample-generation cost and compute per batch. The paper used $G = 64$, which indicates that the authors found that cost acceptable and effective. If you want to explore the bias/variance trade-off empirically (the paper does not provide this sweep), try a small ablation that measures (1) downstream performance (accuracy), (2) variance of the estimated advantages, and (3) wall-clock/sample cost for $G$ in $\{8,16,32,64\}$. If you see diminishing returns on performance as $G$ grows, pick the smallest $G$ that achieves near-peak performance.

If you want to be cautious about any finite-sample bias, implement leave-one-out baselines (baseline$_i = \mathrm{mean}_{j \neq i} r_j$) or use the group mean but correct for self-inclusion; both are straightforward and keep the baseline’s variance low while removing the self-dependence bias. The paper’s normalization by group std is also helpful to stabilize learning across questions and exploration stages.

Summary
- The paper’s GRPO uses within-group mean/std as the baseline and advantage normalization; larger $G$ reduces baseline estimation variance roughly as $1/G$, improving variance reduction for the policy gradient.  
- The paper reports experiments with $G = 64$ (no sweep across $G$ is reported), and shows strong empirical gains using this setting.  
- The group baseline approximates a state-dependent baseline and is effectively unbiased in the large-$G$ limit; finite-sample self-dependence can introduce tiny bias, but the paper’s results indicate that bias is not harmful in practice. If you want to eliminate even that small bias, use leave-one-out baselines or increase $G$.
