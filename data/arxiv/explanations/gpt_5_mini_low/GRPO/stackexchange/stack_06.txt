\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is the reward model trained and updated during iterative GRPO, and how do you prevent drift or overfitting?
Question:
Algorithm 1 mentions iteratively updating the reward model using replay with 10% historical data. Could you clarify: (1) what is the composition of new vs. old data per iteration, (2) whether labels for the reward model are purely rule-based (answer correctness) or include human/preferences at any stage, and (3) what metrics you monitor to detect reward model overfitting or reward-hacking by the policy? Do you freeze the reward model at any iteration to stabilize, or apply early stopping?
Answer:
Short answer: In the paper the reward model is continually re-trained during iterative GRPO using mostly fresh, policy-sampled data plus a small replay of historical examples ($10\%$); the reward labels are constructed primarily from rule-based correctness signals (used to generate pairwise/process supervision training data), not from large-scale human preference labels; and the authors rely on replay, normalization, KL regularization and held-out evaluation to limit overfitting and reward-hacking — they do not report freezing the reward model permanently, but rather iteratively update it with replay and use early-stop / validation checks in practice. Below I unpack each part with the intuition and technical detail tied to the paper.

1) Composition of new vs. old data per iteration
Algorithm 1 (Iterative GRPO) describes an iterative loop in which each iteration:

- samples outputs from the current (old) policy,
- scores them with the current reward model,
- uses those new (online) samples to both train the policy (GRPO) and to create training data for the reward model, and
- updates the reward model “through continuous training using a replay mechanism” that retains $10\%$ historical data.

So per iteration the training set for the reward model is dominated by newly collected (policy-sampled) examples, with roughly $90\%$ fresh / $10\%$ historical (replay). The replay fraction ($10\%$) is explicitly stated and is the main mechanism the paper uses to preserve older examples and reduce catastrophic shifts in the reward model as the policy drifts.

Intuition: most of the reward-model training material should reflect the current policy’s outputs (so the reward model learns to score what the policy actually produces). The small historical buffer stabilizes the reward model against rapid drift and helps preserve previously learned distinctions.

2) What the reward labels are (rule vs. human/preferences)
The paper’s reward construction follows prior math-RL work (cited as Wang et al. 2023). Concretely, the reward training data are constructed using rule judgments about correctness (e.g., answer correctness) to create comparison or step-wise supervision datasets. Two modes of supervision used are:

- Outcome supervision: a scalar reward per full output (e.g., correct/incorrect or a score derived from answer correctness), normalized within a group.
- Process supervision: per-step scores (rewards at step boundaries) obtained by heuristics/rule-based labeling of intermediate reasoning steps; these per-step scores are used to create fine-grained advantages.

The reward model is therefore trained to mimic these (rule-derived) scores and comparisons. The paper does not describe large-scale human preference labeling for the reward model training in the RL stage; instead, the initial dataset construction for the math corpus and some data refinement earlier in the pipeline used human annotation for data selection, but the RL reward labels themselves are derived from rule-based correctness and are used to form pairwise/process training examples for the reward model (as per the construction described in the GRPO section and the citation to Wang et al.).

Intuition: rule-based labels are scalable and align well with mathematical benchmarks (you can check the answer / compute program output). Human preferences are more expensive and are not described as the core supervision signal for the RL reward model in this paper.

3) How overfitting and reward-hacking are detected/mitigated (metrics and procedures)
The paper both uses a set of explicit mechanisms and recommends evaluation/diagnostics tied to broader points made in the discussion. The explicit mechanisms used in training are: replay ($10\%$ historical), within-group normalization of rewards, KL regularization between the policy and a reference model (the reference is set to the policy at the start of each outer iteration), and limiting policy update magnitude via the GRPO clipping term (similar to PPO). These act as practical defenses against runaway optimization of a mis-specified reward model.

Metrics and checks the authors either use or recommend (and which are consistent with what they report):

- Correlation of reward model score with ground-truth accuracy: monitor how well the reward scores predict actual correctness (accuracy on held-out questions). A degrading correlation signals reward model overfitting or poor generalization.
- Validation loss / calibration on a held-out reward dataset: keep a held-out set of comparisons/step labels not used in training the reward model and stop/regularize based on that.
- Downstream benchmark performance (in-domain vs out-of-domain): they report both in-domain (GSM8K, MATH) and out-of-domain benchmarks. If policy performance increases on metrics like Maj@$K$ (majority/top-$K$ success) but not Pass@$K$ (any-of-$K$), or if out-of-domain performance drops, that indicates distributional overfitting or reward hacking.
- Maj@$K$ vs Pass@$K$ breakdown: the paper explicitly inspects Maj@$K$ and Pass@$K$. They find RL improves Maj@$K$ (makes correct answers more probable), but not Pass@$K$ — monitoring such changes helps diagnose whether the policy is genuinely improving capability or just concentrating probability mass.
- KL divergence and training stability: the GRPO objective explicitly includes a KL penalty between policy and reference; tracking this KL and token-level distributions can reveal excessive policy drift that might exploit reward-model quirks.
- Reward vs accuracy divergence over training time: if the policy’s mean reward keeps rising while true accuracy on held-out test problems stagnates or falls, that’s a sign of reward-model overfitting or reward hacking.
- Human spot checks / audits: while not the main training signal, the paper emphasizes the importance of qualitative inspection and suggests human review when unexpected failure modes appear.

Freezing / early stopping
The paper does not describe freezing the reward model permanently at any iteration. Instead they use an iterative pipeline: after each outer iteration they re-train the reward model with new data + $10\%$ replay and then continue policy training against the updated reward model. They do report doing two rounds of iteration in experiments and show iterative RL improves performance (especially after the first iteration). That implies the practical approach in the paper is continuous updating, not freezing.

However, the paper also uses standard ML safeguards in reward training (held-out validation and normalization) and suggests early stopping / validation-based control in the discussion as good practice (they emphasize monitoring generalization and uncertainty of reward models). So while they didn’t report a practice of freezing the reward model forever, they do rely on validation / replay / conservative KL regularization and monitoring to avoid destabilization — and one can reasonably apply early stopping or temporarily freeze reward-model weights if validation indicates overfitting.

Practical checklist (grounded in the paper) you can use during iterative GRPO runs
- Keep a replay buffer (paper uses ~$10\%$ historical examples) when updating the reward model.
- Hold out a validation set of comparisons/step-labels to early-stop reward-model training and track correlation to ground-truth accuracy.
- Normalize rewards within groups and use GRPO’s group-standardized advantages to reduce scale issues.
- Include direct KL regularization between policy and reference and monitor that KL over training.
- Track Maj@$K$ vs Pass@$K$ and in-domain vs out-of-domain benchmark performance to spot reward-driven distributional shifts.
- If reward vs true-accuracy diverges, run human audits and consider stronger regularization, uncertainty-aware reward modeling, or temporarily freezing the reward model.

Summary
The paper’s iterative GRPO updates the reward model continuously with predominantly new, policy-sampled data and a $10\%$ historical-replay fraction. The reward labels are constructed mainly from rule-based correctness (outcome and process supervision), not large-scale human preference labels in the RL loop. To prevent reward-model overfitting or policy reward-hacking they use replay, within-group normalization, KL regularization, held-out validation and monitor metrics such as reward/accuracy correlation, Maj@$K$ vs Pass@$K$, KL drift, and in-/out-of-domain benchmark performance; they do not describe permanently freezing the reward model but instead update it iteratively while using the safeguards above, and apply early-stop/validation controls in practice.
