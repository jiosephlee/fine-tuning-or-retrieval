\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does prior code training help non-tool CoT reasoning, and why does mixed one-stage training hurt CoT?
Question:
You find code pretraining improves both PoT (tool use) and non-tool CoT reasoning under two-stage training, but mixing 400B code tokens and 150B math tokens in one stage compromises non-tool CoT for the 1.3B model. What evidence supports the capacity-saturation hypothesis vs. data-ordering effects? Did you try interleaving schedules, smaller code fractions, or curriculum (code-first then mix) to see if non-tool CoT still degrades? Any analysis of attention head specialization before/after mixing?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s empirical pattern (two-stage $\text{code}\to\text{math}$ helps non-tool CoT, whereas a one-stage mixture of large $\text{code}+\text{math}$ hurts non-tool CoT for the $1.3\text{B}$ model) motivates the capacity-saturation hypothesis, but the paper does not fully rule out data-ordering effects because it did not run the specific interleaving / fraction / curriculum / attention-head analyses you ask about. The authors report the observed phenomena, conjecture limited model capacity as a likely cause, and point to additional experiments as future work.

What the paper actually shows (evidence in favor of capacity saturation)
The key experimental facts reported are these. Training a $1.3\text{B}$ DeepSeek-LLM variant with a two-stage schedule (first $400\text{B}$ code tokens, then $150\text{B}$ math tokens) improves both program-aided (PoT) and non-tool chain-of-thought (CoT) mathematical reasoning. By contrast, training for the same total exposure but in one stage on a mixture of $400\text{B}$ code + $150\text{B}$ math tokens degrades non-tool CoT performance for the $1.3\text{B}$ model (while mixed one-stage training still helps program-aided reasoning and mitigates catastrophic forgetting in code). This asymmetric outcome — that separating code and math (sequentially) helps non-tool CoT but simultaneous exposure hurts it at small scale — is what the authors interpret as consistent with capacity saturation: the $1.3\text{B}$ model may not have enough representational capacity to “absorb” both modalities’ learning signals concurrently, so the model cannot form the structures used for human-like stepwise reasoning when trained on the interleaved mixture.

Why this pattern points to capacity saturation rather than (only) a data-ordering artifact
The reasoning in the paper is: if data-ordering alone were the story, mixing and two-stage training should not produce such a systematic difference in emergent non-tool CoT capability (or at least the effect would be more obviously about forgetting dependent on the second-stage data). Instead, the benefit from doing code first and then math suggests the model can first learn structures from code that later math training can refine; but if both signals are presented at once the smaller model can’t form both representations simultaneously, so math-like CoT reasoning fails to emerge. Additional supportive hints in the paper: (1) larger models ($7\text{B}$ initialized from a code-trained checkpoint) show strong math CoT, suggesting scale/capacity matters; (2) program-aided reasoning benefits more robustly from code exposure even in mixed regimes, which fits the idea that code knowledge supports tool use even when representations for internal chain-of-thought are under capacity pressure. These observations collectively favor a capacity-limited interpretation.

What the paper does NOT provide (so data-ordering is not excluded)
The authors explicitly note they have not exhaustively explored alternative training schedules and ablations that would decisively separate capacity-saturation from pure data-ordering effects. In particular, the paper does not report experiments with: interleaving schedules (e.g., block-wise alternation, periodic mixing), smaller fractions of code in the mixture, a curriculum where code-first is then followed by mixing, or graduated curricula. Because those experiments were not run (or at least not reported), a data-ordering explanation — e.g., that presenting math later or in clearer blocks is what matters rather than total capacity — cannot be ruled out.

What was tried and what remains undone (from the paper)
The paper reports the core comparisons (two-stage: $\text{code}\to\text{math}$ vs one-stage: mixed $\text{code}+\text{math}$) across model sizes and notes that the harmful effect of mixing appears for the $1.3\text{B}$ model. It also reports positive results when initializing $7\text{B}$ models from code-trained checkpoints (DeepSeek-Coder$\to$DeepSeekMath-Base $7\text{B}$). However, the paper does not report experiments that you asked about: it does not show interleaved schedules, progressive curricula (code-first then gradual mixing), or fine-grained ablations on the fraction of code tokens in the mixture. It also does not present analysis of attention-head specialization before vs after mixing.

What I would recommend (experiments to distinguish hypotheses)
To decide whether capacity saturation or data ordering is the dominant cause, run these controlled experiments:

1) Interleaved / block schedules: compare (a) pure two-stage $\text{code}\to\text{math}$, (b) one-stage uniform random mixing, (c) block-interleaving (e.g., $10\text{B}$ code then $5\text{B}$ math repeating), and (d) periodic alternation (e.g., switch modality every $N$ steps). If the harm persists even under many block/alternation schedules, capacity saturation is more plausible.

2) Code-fraction sweep: fix total tokens and sweep fractions ($0\%$, $10\%$, $25\%$, $50\%$, $100\%$ code) in a one-stage mixed regime. If degradation appears only above a certain code fraction and that threshold scales with model size, that’s direct capacity evidence.

3) Curriculum experiments: run code-first then gradually introduce math (code-heavy early, slowly decreasing code proportion) and compare to strict two-stage. If a gentle curriculum recovers CoT ability, ordering/curriculum matters; if only pure separation helps, capacity likely dominates.

4) Scaling test: repeat the mixed vs two-stage comparisons across at least three model sizes (e.g., $1.3\text{B}$, $7\text{B}$, $13\text{B}$). If larger models tolerate mixing better, that supports capacity saturation.

Suggested analyses on internal representations (attention / head specialization)
The paper does not perform these, but such probes would be useful. Suggested analyses you can run:

- Attention-pattern clustering: measure whether certain attention heads become strongly preferential to code tokens vs math tokens in two-stage vs mixed models. A loss of specialization in the mixed $1.3\text{B}$ model would support interference/capacity arguments.

- Head importance / ablation: ablate top-$k$ attention heads (or zero-out) to see if math CoT behavior collapses in the mixed model but not in the two-stage model, indicating fragile specialized circuitry.

- SVCCA / CCA or centered kernel alignment between activations on math vs code inputs before and after mixing: high alignment in the mixed model could indicate conflation; low alignment after two-stage training could indicate distinct modules formed.

- Probing classifiers: train small probes to predict token type (code vs math) from layer activations; easier separability in two-stage models suggests the model learned disentangled features.

Concluding summary
The paper’s empirical pattern (two-stage helps CoT; one-stage heavy mixing hurts CoT at small scale) supports a capacity-saturation interpretation, and larger-model results further point to capacity being relevant. However, because the paper did not run interleaving/curriculum/fraction sweeps or attention-specialization probes, it cannot definitively exclude data-ordering or schedule effects. The natural next steps are the controlled ablations and internal analyses I outlined; those will decisively separate capacity limits from ordering/interference phenomena.
