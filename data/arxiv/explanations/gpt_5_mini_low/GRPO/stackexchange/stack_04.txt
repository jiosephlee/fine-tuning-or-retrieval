\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is the per-token advantage defined for outcome vs. process supervision in GRPO, and why sum future step rewards?
Question:
For outcome supervision, you set \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i\) for all tokens. For process supervision, you sum normalized step rewards after token \(t\): \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}\). What is the rationale for using a simple cumulative sum (akin to undiscounted returns) with no discount factor \(\gamma\in(0,1)\)? Did you experiment with discounting or GAE-like smoothing on step rewards to reduce variance or better align credit with local decisions?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO uses the simple cumulative (undiscounted) sum of normalized future step rewards for process supervision mainly for simplicity, interpretability, and to align training with how the process reward model scores later reasoning steps. The paper does not report experiments using an explicit discount factor or GAE smoothing for these per-step process rewards; instead variance reduction and credit assignment are handled via group normalization and the group-baseline advantage, and the authors point to reward-model improvements and algorithmic robustness (rather than value-function/GAE machinery) as the more promising directions.

Why a plain cumulative sum (undiscounted)?
The process reward model in the paper provides scalar scores at the end of each reasoning step, so treating those step scores as immediate rewards and summing them forward is the most direct way to assign credit to earlier tokens that causally precede later beneficial steps. Formally they set, for output i and token position t,
$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$
where $\widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$ are the step rewards normalized across the sampled group. The benefits of this choice are:

- Simplicity and transparency: an undiscounted sum is easy to compute and reason about; each token is credited by all subsequent step evaluations, which matches the intuition that earlier reasoning choices enable later good steps.
- Alignment with the process reward semantics: the process reward model scores discrete reasoning steps (e.g., sub-answers or CoT segments). Summing future step scores directly maps those signals into per-token supervision without introducing extra learned components (value network) or hyperparameters that could change the intended meaning of the reward model.
- Avoiding extra modeling complexity and memory: GRPO’s design purposefully removes the learned critic/value network (unlike PPO). Introducing GAE or a learned value would reintroduce that memory and instability overhead that GRPO seeks to avoid.
- Normalization + group baseline already reduces scale/variance: step rewards are normalized over the group and GRPO uses the group-average as the baseline. This comparative normalization helps stabilize learning even without discounting or GAE.

Did the authors try discounting or GAE-like smoothing?
The paper does not report experiments that add a discount factor $\gamma$ or GAE smoothing on process step rewards. Instead, variance reduction is accomplished by two design choices in GRPO: (1) computing advantages relative to the group (group normalization / group baseline), and (2) using normalized step rewards (subtract group mean, divide by group std). The Discussion section explicitly emphasizes that GRPO intentionally forgoes a value function baseline (and thus GAE) to save memory and leverage the comparative nature of reward models. The authors also mention future directions for RL: making reward models better (uncertainty, generalization) and exploring algorithms robust to noisy rewards — which implicitly suggests they see more promise in improving the reward signal or algorithmic robustness than in reintroducing a critic for smoothing.

Would discounting or GAE be sensible to try?
Yes — conceptually discounting ($\gamma<1$) or GAE-style temporal smoothing could be helpful in some settings. Discounting reduces the influence of far-future step rewards on early tokens, which might better align credit when later steps are only weakly dependent on earlier token-level choices. GAE uses a learned value to trade bias and variance, often lowering variance of advantage estimates. But both bring trade-offs: they add hyperparameters ($\gamma$, $\lambda$), and (in the GAE case) require a value function to be trained, which is precisely what GRPO avoids for memory and stability reasons. If you suspect that (a) later step scores are noisy or weakly related to early choices, or (b) variance from long-sum advantages hurts optimization, then experiments with discounting or with lightweight smoothing (e.g., exponentially-weighted sums of normalized step rewards) are reasonable next steps — but they were not explored in this paper.

Practical takeaway
GRPO chooses the cumulative sum of normalized future step rewards because it maps process supervision directly into per-token advantages while keeping the RL pipeline lightweight (no critic) and leveraging group-based normalization for variance control. If you want to further reduce variance or tweak temporal crediting, you can try discounting or GAE-like approaches, but expect to reintroduce either extra hyperparameters or the need for a value model (and the memory/cost trade-offs that come with it). The authors explicitly flag improving reward models and building algorithms robust to noisy rewards as higher-priority avenues going forward.
