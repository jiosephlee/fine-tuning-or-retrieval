\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does fastText filtering avoid topic bias while scaling to 120B tokens, and what recall/precision trade-off did you target?
Question:
The data pipeline iteratively trains a fastText classifier to mine math pages from Common Crawl, culminating in 120B tokens. What operating point on the classifier ROC did you select in each iteration (e.g., a fixed precision target), and how did you validate that the selection did not skew topic distribution (e.g., over-representing contest-style algebra vs. geometry)? Did you quantify recall against a manually labeled holdout of domain URLs beyond those above the 10% math-page threshold?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report a single fixed ROC operating point (e.g., “choose threshold for X% precision”) for the fastText classifier. Instead the authors used a score-ranking + budget-driven cutoff (keep the top-ranked pages until a pre-set token budget: top 40B then expanded in later iterations up to 120B tokens), and they validated and broadened the seed positives by a domain-level analysis plus manual URL annotation. The paper does not publish a numeric precision/recall trade-off or an out‑of-domain manual-holdout recall measurement beyond the domain $10\%$ rule.

Why this matters and what the paper actually did
The fastText model was trained initially on OpenWebMath positives and random CC negatives with the stated training hyperparameters. Pages in the deduplicated Common Crawl were scored by that classifier and then ranked; rather than choosing a target precision or recall on a labeled validation set, the pipeline retained the top-ranked pages until the desired volume of math tokens was reached (first iteration kept the top 40B tokens, later iterations expanded to 120B tokens). This is effectively an operating point that varies with the chosen token budget rather than a fixed ROC cutoff.

To reduce topical and sampling bias and to expand recall, the authors iterated the pipeline: they grouped pages by domain, computed for each domain the fraction of pages collected in the first pass, and flagged domains where more than $10\%$ of pages were collected as “math-related” (the paper gives the $10\%$ rule). They then manually annotated math-related URL paths inside those domains (for example, adding pages under /questions on mathoverflow.net) and added uncollected pages from those annotated URLs back into the positive set for the next training round. After four iterations this procedure produced 35.5M math pages totaling 120B tokens. Validation of corpus quality was mainly empirical: models trained on the resulting corpus outperformed alternatives on multiple downstream math benchmarks, and the authors also applied benchmark-decontamination rules.

What the paper does not provide
The paper does not state an explicit ROC operating point (no fixed precision target), nor does it report per-iteration precision and recall numbers on a held-out labeled test set of pages. There is no published quantified recall against a manually labeled holdout of domain URLs beyond the $10\%$ domain-selection rule, and no per-topic recall/precision breakdown (e.g., algebra vs. geometry). The authors do acknowledge in the Discussion that geometry and certain theorem-proof areas remained weaker and that data selection bias is a plausible contributor.

Intuition about the practical trade-offs made
Ranking-and-budget selection (take top-ranked pages until you hit N tokens) is a pragmatic choice for large-scale corpus construction: it maximizes average classifier confidence under a strict volume target, but it does not guarantee fixed precision or equal topic coverage. The iterative domain-expansion step (10\% rule + manual URL annotation) is a practical way to increase recall for underrepresented classes that share domain structure (e.g., many math pages live under a few domain paths). However, that approach can still leave fine-grained topical imbalance: if a classifier scores contest-algebra pages higher than geometry pages, the top-N selection will over-represent algebra unless geometry pages are explicitly discovered and added via the domain URL annotation.

Recommended analyses (what I would do if you want the missing numbers)
If you want to quantify and control topic bias and report a precision/recall operating point, the following targeted checks are the minimal, practical ones:

1) Construct a stratified labeled holdout of web pages (random CC pages plus targeted samples from domains flagged as math-related) and measure classifier ROC / precision@k for each iteration. Report precision and recall at the budget-driven cutoffs (e.g., precision@token-budget).  
2) Create topical strata (algebra, geometry, number theory, problem-solving/contest style, proofs, applied math) and measure per-topic recall/precision to reveal any skew.  
3) For domains discovered by the $10\%$ rule, measure how many additional pages were recovered by URL-path annotation and how that changed per-topic coverage.  
4) If you need a fixed operating point, pick a precision target (e.g., $95\%$) on the labeled holdout and measure the token yield; or pick a token budget and report the empirical precision/recall at that budget.

Conclusion
The pipeline in the paper implicitly set operating points by token-budget (keep the top-ranked pages to reach 40B then 120B tokens) and used iterative domain-level discovery plus manual URL annotation to expand recall. They validated quality indirectly by downstream performance and by manual annotation of math URLs in domains exceeding the $10\%$ collection rate. The paper does not provide explicit ROC thresholds, nor does it publish a manual-holdout recall/precision breakdown or topic-wise recall numbers—so if you need those concrete trade-offs, you’ll need to run the additional labeled evaluations suggested above.
