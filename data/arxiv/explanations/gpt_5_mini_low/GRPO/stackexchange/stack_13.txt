\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why do arXiv-heavy corpora underperform for mathematical reasoning in your experiments?
Question:
You report that training on MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama alone did not improve, and sometimes hurt, math reasoning. Could you hypothesize mechanisms for this, such as (1) style mismatch between formal academic text and step-by-step solution style needed for MATH/GSM8K, (2) noisy or complex LaTeX that hurts tokenizer efficiency, or (3) insufficient coverage of problem-solution pairs? Did you try curriculum mixing (e.g., small fraction of arXiv with web math) or formatting normalization of LaTeX to improve outcomes?
Answer:
Short answer: in our experiments arXiv-heavy data (MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama alone) did not help and sometimes hurt math reasoning because its distribution and surface form differ from the “problem + step-by-step solution” style that the GSM8K/MATH benchmarks require. Concretely, arXiv content is largely formal, notation-dense, and not composed of worked-out pedagogical solutions, so models trained on it learn different conditional generation patterns and a noisier token representation for math. We list the most plausible mechanisms below, grounded in the paper’s findings and limitations, and then give practical mitigations that follow naturally.

Why arXiv-heavy corpora can underperform (mechanisms)
1) Style / task mismatch (primary effect). arXiv papers are written as compressed, formal exposition (definitions, theorems, terse proofs) rather than step-by-step worked solutions. GSM8K/MATH require chain-of-thought-style derivations and explicit intermediate steps. Training heavily on arXiv biases the model’s conditional distributions toward terse formal statements and towards producing symbolic/theorem style text, which does not transfer well to the multi-step explanatory outputs those benchmarks measure. The paper observed that arXiv-only training showed “no notable improvements or even deterioration” on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM and miniF2F.

2) Lack of problem–solution pairs. Benchmarks reward producing worked solutions; arXiv rarely contains many explicit “problem $\to$ solution (step-by-step)” examples in the pedagogical sense. Without paired prompts and their solved chains, the model gains less from arXiv for the *process* supervision needed to produce chain-of-thought outputs.

3) LaTeX and tokenization inefficiency / noisy structure. ArXiv text contains heavy LaTeX, macros, inline/display math, and bibliographic/comment blocks. If not normalized, these lead to:
   - Token fragmentation: mathematical symbols and macros break into many tokens, making learning less sample-efficient.
   - Spurious patterns: macros and preambles introduce idiosyncratic tokens and noise that don’t reflect the target inference patterns.
The paper used an “ArXiv-RedPajama” cleaned dump (preambles, comments, macros and bibliographies removed) but still found poor utility, suggesting residual structure/noise or that structural differences remain important.

4) Distributional mismatch and catastrophic specialization. The model capacity is finite; heavy exposure to arXiv-style content can shift model priors away from conversational/pedagogical reasoning and toward terse formalism. The paper reports that mixing strategies can matter (they found code $\to$ math training helps), and also notes that combining arXiv with other data or scaling effects were not fully explored — so specialization effects are plausible.

5) Difficulty of learning reasoning signals from arXiv. arXiv may contain correct mathematics but not the explicit reasoning traces (intermediate steps labeled as helpful). Supervised or reward signals that target chain-of-thought or program-of-thought are scarce in arXiv, so the model doesn’t learn the stepwise process that RL/SFT exploit.

What the paper did and did not try
The paper explicitly reports experiments training models on MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama and observes no improvement or degradation across several benchmarks (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base 7B). It also admits limitations: it did not study (a) the impact of arXiv tokens on tasks not in their benchmarks (e.g., informalization), (b) arXiv combined with other data (how mixing affects outcomes), or (c) whether benefits appear at larger model scale. In short, the authors did not report experiments specifically testing curriculum mixing (e.g., a small fraction of arXiv mixed with web math) nor detailed LaTeX normalization beyond the RedPajama-style cleaning; they leave those as future work.

Practical mitigations and hypotheses to test (guided by the paper)
To make arXiv useful for chain-of-thought style benchmarks, try approaches that directly address the mechanisms above. Suggested strategies are consistent with the paper’s observations about beneficial pretraining mixes (e.g., code + math) and the importance of data selection:

- Extract and align worked examples: mine arXiv for pedagogical sections (exercises, solved examples, appendices) and extract problem–solution pairs. Converting theorem proofs into stepwise chains (via heuristics or human annotation) can make arXiv more useful for process-style tasks.

- LaTeX normalization and macro expansion: canonicalize macros, convert math expressions to more tokenizable forms, or translate formulas into textual descriptions for portions where natural-language reasoning is desired. Also consider training or adapting the tokenizer/vocabulary to better capture LaTeX atoms so symbols are not fragmented.

- Curriculum / fractional mixing: instead of arXiv-only pretraining, mix a modest fraction of arXiv with web math that already exhibits worked solutions (DeepSeekMath used mostly web math plus 10% arXiv in the final 7B training). A plausible curriculum is: (1) pretrain on conversational/web math and code to learn stepwise and algorithmic patterns, then (2) fine-tune or continue-training with some arXiv to inject formal knowledge without overwhelming style. The paper’s results hint that mixing code and math helped; similarly, a careful mix of arXiv might help if not used alone.

- Convert formal proofs into intermediate reasoning steps for process supervision: train reward/process models that can score partial derivations extracted from arXiv (or autoformalized substeps) so the model can learn to produce stepwise reasoning.

- Use selective filtering or domain weighting: prioritize arXiv subsections that are closer to tutorial/lecture style (lecture notes, introductions) rather than dense theorem statements. The DeepSeekMath pipeline used iterative domain selection and manual URL annotation—similar curation could filter useful arXiv content.

- Leverage code training and tool-based reasoning: the paper found code training improves mathematical reasoning and program-aided solving. If arXiv contains algorithmic pseudocode, mapping that to code-like token distributions might make it more informative when combined with code tokens.

Broader conceptual point
This is largely a distributional-transfer problem: pretraining on a source distribution (arXiv) that lacks the predictive structure required by the target evaluation (worked-out, chain-of-thought solutions) will not transfer well and can even harm performance. The remedy is either to alter arXiv into a form closer to the target (normalize, extract worked examples), or to use training curricula and mixtures that preserve the signal needed for stepwise reasoning.

Summary
The paper’s experiments show arXiv-only or arXiv-heavy pretraining did not improve chain-of-thought style benchmarks and sometimes hurt them. The most plausible causes are style/task mismatch (arXiv lacks step-by-step worked solutions), LaTeX/tokenization noise, and lack of paired problem–solution signals. The paper did not report curriculum-mixing or aggressive LaTeX normalization experiments (these are left for future work), but it did find that mixing math web data, code pretraining, and careful selection of web math yielded the strong gains seen in DeepSeekMath. If you want to experiment further, focus first on extracting aligned problem–solution chains from arXiv or normalizing formulas/tokenization before large-scale arXiv-heavy pretraining.
