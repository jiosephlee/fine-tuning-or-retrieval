\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How exactly is the group baseline computed in GRPO, and what stops degenerate normalization when rewards are identical?
Question:
In Section 4.1, GRPO replaces the critic with a group-based baseline and normalizes rewards within each group: for outcome supervision, \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\). If the reward model produces identical or near-identical scores \(r_i\) for all \(G\) sampled outputs on a question, then \(\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r}) \approx 0\). How do you handle this in practice to avoid exploding gradients or division by zero? Do you add an \(\epsilon\) floor to the standard deviation, or skip such groups? Also, do you apply any robust statistics (e.g., median/IQR) to mitigate outliers in \(\mathbf{r}\)?
Answer:
Short answer: you avoid divide-by-zero / exploding gradients by making the normalization numerically stable (e.g. a small epsilon floor on the denominator or falling back to a zero advantage for that group). In practice GRPO’s authors also rely on design choices that make exact ties uncommon (large group size, process supervision, iterative reward-model updates), and you can optionally use robust preprocessing (median/IQR, clipping, or ranking) if outliers or ties are a concern.

Explanation and practical options grounded in the paper’s design. Section 4.1 defines outcome normalization as
$\widetilde r_i = \dfrac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)}$.
If $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)\approx 0$ (identical or near-identical rewards across the group), dividing by a tiny number would amplify noise and produce huge/saturated advantages. The usual, safe remedies are:

1) Numerical epsilon floor (simple and standard). Replace the denominator by $\max(\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r),\epsilon)$ so
$\widetilde r_i = \dfrac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\max(\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r),\epsilon)}$.
Typical $\epsilon$ values are in the range $10^{-6}$–$10^{-3}$ depending on reward scale. This prevents division-by-zero and caps the magnitude of $\widetilde r_i$.

2) Fallback to zero-advantage (safe semantics). If $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)<\delta$ treat the whole group as uninformative and set $\widetilde r_i=0$ for all $i$. That makes the group contribute no policy gradient (reasonable when the reward model can’t discriminate the samples).

3) Robust/alternative normalizations to mitigate outliers or ties. Instead of mean/std you can use median and IQR:
$\widetilde r_i = \dfrac{r_i-\mathrm{median}(\mathbf r)}{\max(\mathrm{IQR}(\mathbf r),\epsilon)}$,
or winsorize/clip extreme $r_i$ before normalizing. Another option is to convert group scores to ranks or soft ranks (ordinal information) and use those as advantages; this avoids sensitivity to scale and heavy tails.

Why these fixes fit GRPO’s setting. The paper already reduces degenerate behavior by sampling relatively large groups (they use $G=64$), by using process supervision (which produces more fine-grained reward variance than outcome-only), and by iteratively retraining the reward model so it becomes better at discriminating outputs over time. Those choices make exact identical scores uncommon in practice, but numerical guards (epsilon or zero-fallback) are still important for stability.

Which to pick? The simplest safe choice is the epsilon floor on the standard deviation (option 1); if you care about throwing away uninformative groups, use the zero-advantage fallback (option 2). If you see outliers or heavy-tailed reward distributions, try median/IQR or rank-based advantages. All of these are consistent with the GRPO philosophy (group-relative advantages and variance reduction) and are commonly used to ensure stable RL fine-tuning of LLMs.
