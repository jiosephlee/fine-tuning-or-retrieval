\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve Maj@K but not Pass@K, and how was K chosen for this analysis?
Question:
You conclude that RL mainly boosts Maj@K rather than Pass@K, suggesting it makes the correct solution more likely among top samples instead of improving best-case capability. Could you specify the exact definition of Maj@K and Pass@K you used, the values of \(K\), and whether temperature or nucleus parameters were tuned equivalently pre- and post-RL? Also, is the gap consistent across difficulty buckets within MATH, or driven by certain categories?
Answer:
Short answer: Maj@$K$ and Pass@$K$ in the paper are the usual majority-vote and oracle/top-$K$ metrics computed over sampled model outputs; for the reported analysis we used up to $K=64$ (we sample $G=64$ outputs per question during RL and evaluation), and the sampling / decoding hyperparameters were kept the same pre- and post-RL (the paper uses the same naive nucleus-sampling setup for exploration and evaluation). The paper does not include a detailed per-difficulty-bucket breakdown on MATH in the main text, so we cannot claim a definitive bucket-level provenance from the published results — only the aggregate curves in Figure 7 (the Maj/Pass plot) are reported.

I’ll expand briefly and precisely so you can interpret the result and reproduce it:

Definitions used in the paper
- Pass@$K$: the “oracle” metric — a question is counted as solved for Pass@$K$ if at least one of the $K$ sampled outputs yields the correct final answer. This measures best-case capability among $K$ draws.
- Maj@$K$: majority-vote accuracy among $K$ sampled outputs — generate $K$ answers, take the answer that appears most often (or the modal predicted final answer) and score that. This measures whether the model places the correct solution consistently near the top of its output distribution (so that aggregation benefits).

Which $K$ was used
- The RL training and the main self-consistency / aggregation evaluations used $64$ samples per question (the paper repeatedly states $G = 64$ for GRPO sampling and reports self-consistency over $64$ samples). The Maj@$K$ / Pass@$K$ plot in the paper is produced as curves over $K$ up to that sampling budget (i.e., $K \in \{1,\ldots,64\}$), so the key point is the behaviour up to $K = 64$ where the gap is visible.

Decoding / temperature / nucleus treatment
- The paper states a “naive nucleus sampling” was used for sampling during RL exploration and refers to using the same sampling strategy for evaluation/self-consistency. In other words, the decoding hyperparameters used to produce the $K$ samples for the Instruct and RL models were held consistent (same sampling method and settings) so that changes in Maj@$K$ / Pass@$K$ reflect changes in the model distribution induced by RL, not differences in decoding.
- The paper does not report a grid search over temperature or nucleus $p$ for pre- vs post-RL sampling; it reports comparisons under the same decoding procedure. If you need to know the exact numeric temperature/$p$ used in the experiments you would need either to check the experimental appendix (if present) or contact the authors — the main text only emphasizes “naive nucleus sampling” and matching sampling across runs.

Why RL improves Maj@$K$ but not Pass@$K$ (intuitive + technical)
- Intuitively, Pass@$K$ (oracle) improves only when the model acquires new capability that appears at least once among $K$ samples. Maj@$K$ improves when the model’s sampling distribution becomes more concentrated on the correct solution so that simple aggregation (majority) recovers the correct answer more reliably.
- The paper’s GRPO procedure (and the reward models used) is trained on comparisons of multiple outputs for the same question (group-relative signals). That training objective rewards increasing the relative probability of better answers within a group, so the resulting policy tends to concentrate probability mass on a fewer set of preferable outputs. In distributional terms, RL with GRPO “sharpens” the posterior over answers: the correct answer receives higher mass and becomes more likely to be sampled repeatedly, which raises Maj@$K$.
- Pass@$K$, however, depends on whether the model ever samples a correct answer among $K$ draws. If the model’s underlying capability to produce the correct reasoning trace or answer at least once is unchanged, concentrating probability mass (making the model more consistent) will not increase the oracle coverage beyond the original capability ceiling. In short, GRPO makes the model more consistent (helping aggregation) rather than discovering qualitatively new solution modes that were previously unreachable by sampling.
- This is supported by the empirical observation in the paper’s Figure 7: after RL the Maj@$K$ curve rises while the Pass@$K$ curve remains essentially unchanged.

About per-difficulty / category effects on MATH
- The manuscript reports the aggregate Maj@$K$ / Pass@$K$ behaviour but does not publish a per-difficulty-bucket breakdown on MATH in the main text. Therefore the paper itself does not establish whether the Maj/Pass gap is uniform across easy/medium/hard buckets or driven by particular problem types (algebra, number theory, geometry, etc.).
- From the mechanisms described, you should expect that the Maj@$K$ gains are most visible where the model already has a non-negligible chance to produce a correct or near-correct trace (so medium-difficulty problems): there is something to “concentrate” on. For very easy problems the Instruct model may already be near-saturated so gains are smaller; for very hard problems where the model almost never samples a correct trace, sharpening will not increase Pass@$K$ and may yield only modest Maj@$K$ changes because there is little correct mass to amplify. However, that reasoning is not a direct empirical statement from the paper — rather it is a plausible interpretation consistent with the GRPO mechanism described. The authors explicitly note they did not present bucketed analysis in the main text and suggest further per-category study as future work.

Practical implications and what to check if you reproduce or extend this
- To verify whether RL is merely increasing consistency versus capability, compute both Pass@$K$ and Maj@$K$ as functions of $K$ ($1\ldots G$) and stratify by difficulty and by problem category. If the oracle Pass@$K$ curve improves after RL in some buckets, that indicates genuine capability gains there; if only Maj@$K$ rises, the improvement is distributional sharpening.
- Keep sampling/decoding identical pre/post-RL. If you tune temperature/$p$ for each model separately you risk confounding sampling differences with model changes.
- If the goal is to increase Pass@$K$ (oracle success), consider diversifying the sampling or the training signals: explore augmenting the RL data source with out-of-distribution prompts, using exploration strategies (e.g., tree search) that can uncover new solution modes, or introducing reward signals that explicitly value discovering distinct correct traces rather than reinforcing only the top group members.

Summary: the paper defines Maj@$K$ as majority-vote accuracy and Pass@$K$ as oracle/top-$K$ accuracy, evaluates up to $K = 64$ (matching the $G = 64$ sampling used in GRPO), and holds sampling settings constant between Instruct and RL when producing those samples. GRPO’s group-relative reward structure concentrates probability mass on preferred outputs, which raises Maj@$K$ (aggregation benefit) without necessarily raising Pass@$K$ (oracle capability). The paper does not report a per-difficulty breakdown on MATH, so that remains an open analysis to run if you want to know which categories drive the aggregate effect.
