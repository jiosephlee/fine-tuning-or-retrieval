\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why add the KL regularizer outside the reward in GRPO instead of a per-token KL penalty like PPO?
Question:
Equation (3) for PPO uses a per-token KL penalty inside the reward, \(r_t = r_\varphi(\cdot) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{ref}}\). In Equation (4) for GRPO, you directly add a KL divergence term \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta || \pi_{ref}]\) to the objective rather than folding it into the token-level reward. Conceptually and practically, why is this preferable? Does moving the KL outside the advantage computation change the credit assignment over time steps or the stability properties that PPO relies on? Any observed differences in variance or learning dynamics?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO moves the $\mathrm{KL}$ term outside the per-token reward because it makes the advantage computation simpler and cleaner when you use group-relative baselines (no learned critic), preserves the comparative reward signal coming from a preference-style reward model, and reduces implementation/memory complexity. Per-token $\mathrm{KL}$ inside the reward (as in PPO) mixes a sequence-level regularizer into token-level advantages and can bias credit assignment and complicate / increase variance of advantage estimates; GRPO avoids that and the paper reports better empirical behavior (GRPO outperforms simpler baselines and GRPO+process-supervision improves further).

Why the placement matters (intuitively and technically)
- Per-token $\mathrm{KL}$-in-reward (PPO style). PPO typically subtracts a term
  $r_t = r_\phi(\dots) - \beta \log\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_t)}\right)$
  and then builds token-level advantages (via a learned value function) from those token rewards. That folds a global sequence-regularizer (penalizing divergence from the reference) into every token reward. Intuitively this “shapes” the reward at each time step: even when the reward model gives only a single sequence-level score, the per-token $\mathrm{KL}$ creates a varying token-wise signal that interacts with advantage estimation and the learned value baseline. This is fine when you have a well-trained value function estimating token-level returns, but it complicates advantage computation and can bias updates toward preserving low-$\mathrm{KL}$ tokens independent of the reward-model comparisons you actually care about.

- $\mathrm{KL}$ as a separate term (GRPO). GRPO keeps the $\mathrm{KL}$ penalty as an explicit regularizer subtracted from the surrogate objective (Equation (4) / Eq. (GRPO-obj)). Advantages $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ are computed purely from the group-normalized reward signals (either outcome or process supervision) and are not polluted by the per-token $\mathrm{KL}$. Concretely the paper motivates this because GRPO uses a group baseline (the average reward across $G$ sampled outputs for the same question) instead of a learned value function. Folding a per-token $\mathrm{KL}$ into the reward would “complicate the calculation of $\hat{A}_{i,t}$” (paper, §Reinforcement Learning) because the advantage is estimated relative to other outputs in the group; keeping $\mathrm{KL}$ outside avoids altering those relative comparisons.

Effects on credit assignment and stability
- Credit assignment: moving $\mathrm{KL}$ outside preserves the intended credit signal coming from the reward model (especially important for process supervision where rewards are step-level). When $\mathrm{KL}$ is injected per-token into $r_t$ it can dilute or distort which tokens/steps are credited for a high reward because a token’s immediate reward now mixes reward-model signal and a divergence penalty. By keeping the $\mathrm{KL}$ separate, GRPO ensures the advantage (which drives token-level policy gradients) reflects the reward-model comparisons among group outputs, so credit for correct/incorrect reasoning steps remains aligned with the reward model’s judgments.
- Stability and variance: GRPO removes the need for a separate value network (reducing memory and a source of estimation error). Using a group mean/std baseline (normalization within each group of $G$ samples) is a low-variance, natural baseline for comparative reward models; it reduces variance coming from learning a value function and matches the comparative nature of reward models trained on pairwise/group data. Also, because $\mathrm{KL}$ is a global penalty on the policy in the objective rather than noisy token-level reward noise, advantage estimates are less contaminated and can have lower variance. The paper explicitly designed GRPO this way to “avoid complicating the calculation of $\hat{A}_{i,t}$” and to align with reward models trained on comparisons (see §Group Relative Policy Optimization).

Practical differences reported in the paper
- Memory / compute: GRPO avoids training a value network, substantially reducing memory cost compared to PPO (paper, §From PPO to GRPO).
- Empirical behavior: GRPO outperforms simpler online-RFT baselines and GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) beats GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS), showing the advantage of (1) algorithmically converting reward-model scores into informative per-step advantages and (2) keeping the $\mathrm{KL}$ as a separate regularizer. The paper reports large gains on metrics like GSM8K / MATH after GRPO training (see §Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL).
- Hyperparameter handling: because $\mathrm{KL}$ is an explicit objective term, one can tune its coefficient $\beta$ as a straightforward regularizer on the whole sequence/policy rather than indirectly affecting per-token rewards and advantage magnitudes, which simplifies hyperparameter reasoning.

A compact intuition: Treat the reward model as producing comparative sequence-level (or step-level) judgments. If you then want to (a) turn those comparisons into token-level learning signals via group-based advantages and (b) softly constrain how far the policy moves from the reference, it’s cleaner to keep the constraint ($\mathrm{KL}$) as a separate penalty on the policy objective. That way the token-level advantages reflect only the reward-model comparisons (better credit assignment) and the $\mathrm{KL}$ cleanly controls policy drift without introducing extra noise into those advantages.

Bottom line: GRPO’s design choice to add $\mathrm{KL}$ outside the reward is a purposeful trade: it simplifies and stabilizes advantage estimation when you do not train a value function (use group baselines), better preserves the comparative reward signal, lowers memory/computation, and empirically yields superior learning dynamics in the paper’s math-reasoning experiments.
