\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What is the expected residual contamination risk under 10-gram exact matching, and how was it audited?
Question:
For decontamination, you remove any page containing a 10-gram that exactly matches a benchmark substring (or exact match for 3–9 grams). This can miss paraphrases or LaTeX-normalization variants common in math. Did you run any post-hoc audits (e.g., approximate matching, hash-based fuzzy matching on numbers/equations) to estimate residual leakage into training? Any observed anomalies where model outputs mirrored benchmark solutions verbatim despite the 10-gram filter?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s decontamination used exact substring matching ($10$-grams for long benchmarks; exact $3$–$9$-gram matching for shorter texts) plus URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication, and an iterative domain/URL-level seeding step. The authors do not report running a post-hoc fuzzy/approximate matching audit (e.g., Levenshtein/SimHash on normalized LaTeX, numeric-expression hashing, or embedding-based similarity checks) in the text of the paper, nor do they report finding any instances where the model verbatim-reproduced benchmark solutions that escaped the exact $10$‑gram filter.

Why residual contamination still can exist, intuitively and technically. Exact $10$‑gram matching guarantees removal only of substrings that match character-for-character. Paraphrases, re-tokenizations, LaTeX macro normalization, small edits (spacing, punctuation, symbol name changes), and solution rephrasings will evade exact matching. Mathematical expressions are especially fragile to exact-string approaches: the same formula can be written in different LaTeX (macro-expanded vs. macro-shorthands), with whitespace changes, with Unicode vs. ASCII symbols, or with algebraic simplifications that change token sequences while preserving semantics. Thus the true residual risk is non‑zero and concentrated in (a) near-duplicates that differ by small edits, (b) paraphrased solutions, and (c) LaTeX/notation variants.

What the paper did to reduce risk. Beyond the exact $n$‑gram filter it explicitly describes: they applied URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication on Common Crawl prior to selection, iteratively improved the fastText classifier by adding domain-specific URLs and annotations, and removed any web page containing an exact benchmark substring per the $10$‑gram / $3$–$9$‑gram rule. Those steps reduce the chance that exact copies remain and reduce repeated near-duplicates, but they do not eliminate approximate matches or semantic paraphrases.

What the paper does not report (so should be assumed absent unless authors say otherwise). The manuscript does not describe any post-hoc approximate/fuzzy audits such as: normalized-LaTeX hashing, number/equation canonicalization followed by similarity search, embedding-based nearest-neighbor checks between training pages and benchmark problems, locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)/MinHash on token $n$‑grams, or manual sampling targeted at near-duplicates. It likewise does not report observed anomalous cases where the trained model produced verbatim benchmark solutions that would indicate leakage beyond what the $10$‑gram filter would catch.

Practical implications and recommendations (educational/accessible): in practice, exact $10$‑gram filtering is a reasonable and computationally cheap first line of defense and catches literal copies. However, because mathematical text is often formulaic and susceptible to small edit variants, a conservative estimate is that approximate matches and paraphrases could survive at measurable but probably low rates, especially when the training corpus is very large ($120$B math tokens). To quantify and bound residual leakage you should run targeted post-hoc audits: (1) normalize LaTeX (expand macros, remove whitespace/preamble variance) and hash expressions to find near-duplicates; (2) compute sentence- or problem-level embeddings for benchmark items and run nearest-neighbor search against your candidate corpus to catch paraphrases; (3) use fuzzy string matching (Levenshtein, $n$‑gram Jaccard, SimHash) to detect near-matches; (4) sample model generations on held-out benchmark prompts and automatically check for long verbatim spans (e.g., match length $>20$ characters) or exact answers—then manually inspect flagged cases; (5) check model Pass@K vs. memorization signals (e.g., identical long solution text across many seeds) to distinguish memorized verbatim outputs from convergent reasoning. Those audits are the standard ways to estimate residual leakage beyond exact matching.
