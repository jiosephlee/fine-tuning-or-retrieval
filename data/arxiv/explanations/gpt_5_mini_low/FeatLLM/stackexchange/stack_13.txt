\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How does FeatLLM handle samples that match zero rules or all rules?
Question:
Since features are binary per rule, edge cases can occur. If a sample activates zero rules for all classes, logits become zero and softmax is uniform—do you treat this as an abstention or low-confidence prediction? Conversely, if the sample activates almost all rules for multiple classes, do you cap contributions or regularize weights to avoid overconfident predictions? Is there any calibration step applied after ensembling to improve probability quality?
Answer:
Short answer: FeatLLM handles the zero-rules / all-rules edge cases implicitly via its linear logit model and ensembling; a sample that activates no rules for any class produces zero logits and therefore a uniform (low‑confidence) softmax prediction (the paper does not treat that as a formal “abstention”). Conversely, there is no explicit hard cap on the contribution of many active rules — class logits are the (nonnegative) weighted sum of active rule features, so many active rules can produce large logits and a confident prediction, but the learned weights, positive projection, tuning procedure, and ensembling are used to limit pathological overconfidence in practice. The paper does not report any additional post‑hoc calibration step after ensembling.

Explanation grounded in the paper
The model in FeatLLM converts each LLM-generated rule into a binary feature and for class $k$ computes
$\text{logit}_{k}^{i} = \max(w_{k}, 0)\cdot z_{k}^{i}$,
then $p^{i} = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_{1}^{i}, \dots, \text{logit}_{c}^{i}])$ (Eq. $(1)$ in the paper). A few direct consequences follow.

1) Zero‑rules activated for a sample:
If for a given sample all binary rule vectors $z_{k}^{i}$ are zero (no rules matched for any class), every class logit is $0$. After softmax this yields a uniform distribution across classes. The paper treats this outcome as a (naturally) low‑confidence prediction rather than a special abstention mechanism; there is no separate abstention policy described. Intuitively, the model is saying “none of the learned rule‑features apply, so I have no evidence to prefer any class.”

2) Many/all rules activated:
If a sample activates many rules, the logit is the sum of the corresponding (projected) nonnegative weights. There is no explicit clipping of logits or a hard cap on a rule’s contribution in the published method. Instead, the approach relies on the training dynamics and architectural choices to avoid pathological behavior: weights are learned by minimizing cross‑entropy on the few-shot training samples, weights are projected to the nonnegative subspace (so features can only increase class logit), and ensembling with randomized rule generation / bagging is used. Together these reduce the chance that noisy or spurious rule sets produce consistently overconfident wrong predictions. In analysis the authors also demonstrate that tuning learns to downweight irrelevant rules (they report filtering rules with learned weights below a threshold like $0.1$ when inspecting feature quality).

3) Regularization / capping:
The paper does not describe any explicit $L_{2}$/$L_{1}$ regularizer, explicit weight clipping, or a hard cap on summed contributions. Regularization is therefore implicit via the few‑shot training procedure, the positive projection, and early/ cross‑validated epoch selection (they use Adam, learning rate $0.01$, train up to $200$ epochs and pick epoch by k‑fold CV). The ensemble/bagging design is also an implicit regularizer: averaging probabilistic outputs from multiple randomized rule sets smooths predictions and mitigates single‑trial overconfidence.

4) Calibration after ensembling:
No dedicated calibration step (for example temperature scaling or Platt scaling) is reported in the paper. The final prediction is the average of probabilities produced by each ensemble trial. Averaging probabilities across diverse trials tends to improve calibration compared to single deterministic models, but the paper does not perform or report any additional post‑hoc calibration experiments.

Practical implications / what to do if you need different behavior
If you need explicit abstention for zero‑rule samples, add a simple rule: if all logits equal zero (or max probability below a threshold), return abstain. If overconfidence from many active rules becomes a problem in your domain, you can add $L_{2}$ regularization on weights, apply a softmax temperature on logits, clip logits, or run a post‑hoc calibration method (temperature scaling / isotonic regression) on a held‑out calibration set. These are standard remedies; FeatLLM’s paper did not apply them but the model structure admits them naturally.

Summary
FeatLLM defaults to uniform (low‑confidence) predictions when no rules fire, and relies on learned nonnegative weights, cross‑entropy tuning, and ensembling to prevent spurious high confidence when many rules fire. There is no explicit abstention mechanism, weight capping, or post‑hoc calibration reported in the paper, although the authors do inspect and filter low‑weight rules in analysis and rely on ensemble averaging to improve robustness.
