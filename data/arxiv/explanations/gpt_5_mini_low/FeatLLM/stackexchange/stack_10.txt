\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How are few-shot examples chosen for the prompt, and does example order bias results?
Question:
You mention randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations because order affects LLM responses. How are the specific examples selected from the k-shot pool—randomly, or with some coverage heuristic (e.g., class balance, feature diversity)? Given that different orders and selections induce different rules, did you evaluate strategies for selecting exemplars that reduce variance or improve performance (e.g., clustering-based prototypes or informative selection), versus pure random choice?
Answer:
Short answer: for the experiments in the paper the $k$-shot demonstrations are drawn randomly (subject to basic class-presence constraints used for cross‑validation), and the only deliberate non‑random choices made to control variance are (1) randomizing the order of those examples in the prompt and (2) using bagging + ensembling across many LLM trials. The paper does not evaluate exemplar‑selection heuristics such as class‑balanced sampling, clustering/prototype selection, or information‑theoretic exemplar picking.

Why that matters and what the paper does about it
LLMs are sensitive to both which exemplars are shown and the order in which they are shown. Intuitively, different exemplars emphasize different local regularities or spurious correlations in a tiny $k$-shot set, and a different order can nudge the LLM’s chain‑of‑thought toward different reasoning paths. The paper addresses those sources of variance in two practical ways. First, it explicitly randomizes the order of few‑shot demonstrations (citing known prompt‑order effects) so that any one ordering does not systematically bias results. Second, it runs the whole rule‑generation pipeline multiple times with bagging (random subsets of features/instances) and a nonzero temperature for LLM decoding, producing diverse rule sets, and then ensembles them (they used $T = 20$ trials and extract $R = 10$ rules per class in the reported setting). This ensemble/bagging strategy is the paper’s main mechanism to reduce variance and improve robustness: incorrect or spurious rules in some trials are averaged out while common, stable rules survive across trials.

Concrete statements from the paper you can rely on
- Sampling: “For the $k$-shot learning experiments, only $k$ ($< N$) labeled samples are randomly sampled to train the model.” The cross‑validation setup also ensures the training fold contains at least one example of each class.  
- Order: the prompt construction “randomize[s] the order of few‑shot demonstrations based on the observation that the order can impact the query outcome of LLM.”  
- Variance control: they use bagging + ensembling, set LLM temperature to $0.5$, and report that ensemble consistently improves performance (ablation shows removing ensemble degrades AUC). The Appendix contains additional analysis on rule diversity across trials.

What the paper does not do (and reasonable next steps)
The paper does not compare different exemplar selection strategies such as class‑balanced sampling, diversity/coverage heuristics, clustering‑based prototype selection, influence‑score or gradient‑based informative sampling, or optimized ordering (e.g., retrieval of nearest neighbours, representative exemplars, or ordering by difficulty). It also does not report experiments that directly contrast e.g. random exemplars versus prototype exemplars or tuned selections. Exploring such strategies is a natural future direction: one could test class‑aware sampling, $k$-means prototypes on feature space, Max‑Min diversity selection, or exemplar selection based on model uncertainty, and measure (i) mean performance, (ii) variance across trials, and (iii) rule stability (fraction of rules reproduced across trials). Given the paper’s finding that ensembling saturates around $T \approx 20$, a promising experiment would be to test whether smarter exemplar selection reduces required $T$ for the same performance/variance.

Practical takeaway
If you’re reproducing or extending FeatLLM, use random $k$-shot sampling and randomize prompt order as the paper does, and rely on bagging+ensemble to stabilize outputs. If you want to reduce API calls or variance further, trying class‑balanced or prototype exemplar selection is a sensible next experiment—but the paper itself reports only the random sampling + bagging/ensembling approach, not comparisons with sophisticated exemplar selection methods.
