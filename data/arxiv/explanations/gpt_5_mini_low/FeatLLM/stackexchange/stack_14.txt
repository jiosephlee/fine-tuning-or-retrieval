\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Details on ensembling: averaging probabilities vs. logits and calibration effects
Question:
The paper states that final predictions average class probabilities \(\mathbf{p}^i\) across T trials. Did you compare against averaging logits or stacking (e.g., training a meta-learner on the ensemble outputs)? Averaging probabilities can under- or over-calibrate depending on diversity. Did you measure calibration metrics (e.g., ECE, Brier score) and try temperature scaling or Dirichlet calibration on top of the ensemble?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper only reports averaging class probabilities across the $T$ ensemble trials (it states “the prediction is made by averaging the probability for each class $\mathbf{p}^i$ computed with each weight $\mathbf{w}[t]$”). It does not report experiments that compare averaging logits vs. averaging probabilities, nor does it report stacking/meta‑learners or calibration evaluations (ECE, Brier) or post‑hoc calibration (temperature scaling, Dirichlet).  

Why this matters (intuitively and technically). Averaging probabilities and averaging logits are not the same. Averaging logits corresponds to taking the mean of pre‑softmax scores and then applying softmax, which is equivalent to a geometric‑like averaging in probability space and can preserve stronger relative class preferences from individual members. Averaging probabilities is an arithmetic mean of class likelihoods; if the ensemble members are over‑ or under‑confident in different ways, probability averaging can under‑ or over‑calibrate the final output. Stacking (training a small meta‑learner on the $T$ outputs) can learn to correct systematic biases and reweight models based on validation performance; post‑hoc calibration (temperature scaling or Dirichlet calibration) directly optimizes calibration on a held‑out set and typically improves ECE/Brier without changing ranking.

Specific considerations tied to FeatLLM’s design that affect calibration choices. FeatLLM trains per‑trial linear weights and forms per‑trial logits as
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$,
so logits are non‑negative and then converted with softmax. Two consequences follow. First, forcing non‑negative weights biases logits upward and removes the ability of any rule to *decrease* a class score; that structural bias can skew confidences and interact with ensemble averaging. Second, because each trial’s logit baseline is constrained, the scale and spread of logits across trials may be limited, which changes how averaging logits vs. probabilities behaves. Both observations make calibration particularly relevant: a calibration layer can correct global over/under‑confidence introduced by the non‑negativity and the limited shot regime.

Practical experiments I would recommend (concise recipe you can run on the paper’s setup)
1. Compute calibration metrics for the current probability‑averaged ensemble: Expected Calibration Error (ECE, with reasonable binning like 15–20 bins) and Brier score on a held‑out validation fold used in the paper’s $k$‑fold process. Report reliability diagrams.  
2. Compare three simple aggregation strategies: (A) average probabilities (current), (B) average logits then softmax, (C) majority‑vote (for hard labels) as a baseline. Evaluate accuracy/AUC plus ECE/Brier.  
3. Try stacking: use a small validation set to train a logistic regression (or a shallow neural net) that takes either per‑trial logits or per‑trial probabilities as features and outputs calibrated probabilities. Evaluate generalization on test folds. Stacking often outperforms naive averaging when ensemble members have complementary strengths.  
4. Apply temperature scaling to the final logits (fit a single scalar temperature on the validation set minimizing negative log likelihood); this is simple and low‑risk. For multiclass, fit temperature on the vector of logits before softmax. Evaluate ECE/Brier after scaling.  
5. Try Dirichlet calibration if multiclass calibration is still poor: Dirichlet calibration fits a richer mapping from logits to probabilities and often beats scalar temperature for multiclass problems. Use the validation set for fitting.  
6. Optionally, calibrate each trial separately (fit temperatures per trial) and then average resulting probabilities; or average logits (possibly after per‑trial scaling) before softmax. Compare these variants.

Which aggregation + calibration I expect to help for FeatLLM. Because per‑trial logits are non‑negative and of limited dynamic range, averaging logits may restore sharper class differences if trials consistently favor the same class; however if some trials are strongly miscalibrated (overconfident), averaging probabilities can dilute that effect. In practice stacking or temperature/Dirichlet calibration on top of the ensemble is the most reliable fix for calibration problems: temperature scaling is cheap and often yields immediate ECE/Brier improvements; Dirichlet or a small meta‑learner will further help when different trials have systematic, class‑dependent biases.

A few implementation notes tied to the paper’s constraints. FeatLLM is designed for low‑shot regimes where validation data is scarce; calibration needs a held‑out set, so use $k$‑fold splits (as the paper already does) or use cross‑validated temperature fitting to avoid losing too many training shots. Also report both discrimination (AUC/accuracy) and calibration (ECE/Brier) because a method can keep AUC but improve expected calibration. Finally, because the LLM temperature was already changed to increase rule diversity, you may find trial diversity helpful for ensemble calibration but also creating heteroskedastic confidences — another reason to fit a calibration layer.

Summary: the paper only averages probabilities and does not report comparisons with logit averaging, stacking, or calibration metrics. Given FeatLLM’s non‑negativity constraint on weights and low‑shot setting, evaluating ECE/Brier and trying simple fixes (temperature scaling, Dirichlet calibration, or stacking on logits) is a compact, practical next step that I would expect to improve calibration without harming discrimination.
