\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How is k-fold cross-validation used with very small k-shot training sets?
Question:
You select epochs using k-fold cross-validation to avoid overfitting in the linear model trained on rule features. In very low-shot scenarios (e.g., 4 or 8 shots), how do you define folds without collapsing class coverage? The paper says k is 2 or 4, ensuring each fold has at least one example per class. Does this procedure become unstable for multi-class datasets with class imbalance? Did you compare against leave-one-out or stratified repeated holdouts to reduce variance in model selection?
Answer:
Short answer: With $4$–$8$ shot regimes you must choose folds so each validation split still contains every class (the paper enforces this by using $k=2$ or $4$ so each fold has at least one example per class). That approach is simple and practical, but it is higher-variance and can become unstable for multi-class, highly imbalanced settings. The paper does not report explicit comparisons to leave‑one‑out or repeated stratified holdouts; instead it relies on (1) small $k$ ($2$ or $4$) with stratification to preserve class coverage and (2) bagging/ensembling and multiple random train/test draws to reduce variance.

Why the paper uses $k=2$ or $4$ (intuition grounded in the text)
The model inside FeatLLM is very low‑shot ($k$ much smaller than dataset size). The authors state they set $k$ to small values ($2$ or $4$) “guaranteeing that the training set includes at least one example of each class.” With only a few labeled points, larger $k$ can make some validation folds lack examples of certain classes, breaking both metric computation and model fitting for class‑specific weights. Hence the conservative choice of small $k$.

Why that can be unstable for multi‑class, imbalanced data
When classes are many or strongly imbalanced, even a fold with “at least one example” per class is a weak estimate: the validation result depends heavily on that one or two examples and therefore has high variance. Model selection (selecting epoch) based on such a noisy validation curve can flip between epochs, causing instability. This is unavoidable in extreme few‑shot regimes: the smaller the validation set per class, the less reliable the error estimate.

Alternatives and tradeoffs (what you can do)
- Stratified $k$-fold (what the paper effectively uses): always prefer stratified folds so class proportions are preserved as much as possible. For tiny $k$, stratification simply enforces each fold has at least one example per class (what the authors did).  
- Repeated stratified holdout (Monte‑Carlo CV): repeat many random stratified train/val splits and average validation metrics. This lowers variance relative to a single small $k$-fold at the cost of more evaluations. It is a practical alternative when LLM queries aren’t needed at every split (FeatLLM only needs few LLM calls up front, so repeated light-weight tuning/eval is often feasible). The paper does not report this comparison, but it is a sensible option.  
- Leave‑one‑out (LOO): LOO can reduce bias of the validation estimate but in few‑shot multi‑class problems it is often unusable because some classes may not appear if you leave one out of an already tiny class, and LOO has high variance when classes are rare. Also LOO is expensive if you must retrain model for each held out sample — for FeatLLM the downstream linear weight tuning is cheap, so LOO is computationally possible, but the estimate will still be noisy when classes are rare. The paper does not compare against LOO.  
- Nested or pooled validation across ensemble trials: because FeatLLM creates many rule/feature sets via ensemble/bagging, you can pool validation outcomes across ensemble members or use ensemble‑level validation to stabilize epoch selection. The paper’s ensemble/bagging design implicitly reduces sensitivity to one unstable epoch choice.  
- Regularization and conservative early stopping: with tiny validation sets it helps to reduce model capacity, use stronger regularization, or choose fewer training epochs ($200$ epochs with cross‑validation for epoch selection is what the paper uses, but in practice you may prefer smaller max epochs and early stopping thresholds to avoid reacting to noisy validation fluctuations).

What the paper actually did and what it did not
The paper explicitly used $k = 2$ or $4$ so that each fold contains at least one example per class, and it used $k$-fold cross‑validation for epoch selection in the linear model. It does not report experiments comparing this procedure to leave‑one‑out or to repeated/stratified holdout schemes. To control variance across the whole evaluation, the authors repeated the entire experiment multiple times with different random choices of train/test splits ($3$ repetitions) and used ensembling/bagging across rule generations to increase robustness.

Practical recommendation
If you face a multi‑class, imbalanced few‑shot problem, start with stratified $k$-fold with the largest $k$ that still guarantees at least one example per class (as the paper did). If instability remains, try repeated stratified holdouts (average several repeats) or pool validation metrics across the FeatLLM ensemble trials. If computational budget permits, try LOO for the downstream linear tuning as a sanity check, but expect noisy estimates when classes are rare. Finally, favor stronger regularization and rely on FeatLLM’s ensemble/bagging (which the paper shows helps) to reduce the risk of unstable model selection.

In short: the paper’s $k=2$ or $4$ stratified choice is a pragmatic compromise for few‑shot settings; it is susceptible to high variance in imbalanced multi‑class cases, and the paper mitigates this via ensembling and multiple experiment repeats rather than reporting comparisons to LOO or repeated stratified holdouts.
