\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What prevents label leakage through feature names or descriptions?
Question:
Because FeatLLM includes detailed feature names and descriptions in the prompt, and some tabular datasets contain columns that are proxies for the label (or include subtle task hints), how do you ensure there is no leakage (e.g., a feature named "disease_present")? Do you sanitize feature names/descriptions or mask obviously label-correlated strings? In the synthetic datasets, was any care taken to avoid textual cues that trivially reveal the target?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not perform explicit sanitization or masking of feature names/descriptions. Instead, FeatLLM relies on its prompt design, two-step reasoning, ensemble/bagging, and the downstream weight-tuning to reduce the impact of trivial label proxies; the experimental setup also uses datasets chosen to avoid memorization (including recent UCI additions and synthetic tasks). The authors report empirical evidence (spurious-column experiments and feature-quality analyses) that show the pipeline largely avoids trivial leakage in their evaluated datasets, but they do not claim a formal guarantee against a column that literally names the label.

Explanation (intuitive + technical, tied to the paper)

1) No explicit name-mangling in the method
The Methods and Experiments sections describe that each dataset comes with natural-language feature names and optional definitions and that those are included in the “basic information” part of the prompt. There is no step in the paper that says the authors systematically rename, mask, or remove features that are string-identical to the label (for example converting "disease_present" to "feature_12"). Thus FeatLLM purposely uses the human-readable metadata as input to the LLM rather than sanitizing it.

2) Why that does not automatically imply trivial leakage in their experiments
The paper uses several defenses implicit in the design that reduce the chance that an obviously label-correlated string (or other spurious textual cue) will trivially make the task trivial or leak labels into rule generation:

- Two-step reasoning instruction. The prompt forces the LLM to first infer general relationships between features and the task without looking at the few-shot examples, and only then use examples to derive rules. This encourages rules grounded in prior knowledge and causal tendencies, not mere rote pattern-matching from example serialization that could exploit a literal label-name column.

- Rule format + parsing + downstream fitting. The LLM generates explicit rules which are parsed to binary features. Those binary rule-features are then passed to a simple linear model with non-negative weights $\text{Eq. (1)}$. The model is trained from the held-out few-shot examples to learn per-rule importance; rules that are not predictive in the few-shot data get small weights (and the paper thresholds weights to filter weak rules in the feature-quality analysis). That tuning step filters out many spurious or noisy rules even if they appear in the generated set.

- Ensemble + bagging. FeatLLM repeats rule generation with randomized prompts (temperature, demonstration order) and bagging. That means a single accidental or memorized label-proxy rule from one trial is unlikely to dominate the ensemble. The paper shows that performance improves and stabilizes with $\sim 20$ ensembles, suggesting robustness to occasional noisy rules.

3) Datasets choice and synthetic tasks
The paper explicitly states that two datasets were recently added to UCI after Sept 2023 and two datasets are synthetic, so they were not in the LLM pretraining corpus used (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613). This reduces the chance that the LLM already memorized a mapping. The synthetic datasets were constructed so they could not have been memorized and thus trivial textual memorization is not an evaluation concern for those tasks. The experimental “effect of adding spurious correlations” shows that when unrelated columns are attached, FeatLLM experienced the least performance degradation relative to baselines and that only about $1$--$2\%$ of generated rules used noisy columns—evidence that the method does not trivially latch onto irrelevant textual cues in practice on their benchmarks.

4) What remains a practical concern and recommended safeguards
The paper admits (and the experiments imply) that FeatLLM can still learn spurious correlations in some settings (see the appendix note that attaching closely related columns can cause misunderstanding). Concretely, if a dataset really contains a column whose name or description literally states the label (e.g., "disease_present", "target_flag") or contains near-deterministic proxies, then any method that ingests names could find that as an easy rule. The paper does not claim to guard against that class of leakage.

If you are replicating or applying the method and worry about leakage, reasonable practical safeguards (not claimed in the paper but consistent with its spirit) are: remove or obfuscate obviously label-named columns, withhold explicit label-descriptive text from the feature descriptions during rule-generation, run a quick check for near-deterministic columns (correlation / mutual information with the label) and optionally drop them or mask their names, and examine generated rules for exact-name matching. The paper’s downstream tuning and ensemble will help, but explicit name-sanitization is the most direct protection against trivial label leakage.

Summary
The paper does not sanitize feature names; instead it depends on LLM prompt design (two-step reasoning), parsing + downstream weight tuning, and ensembling/bagging to avoid generating and relying on trivial label-proxy rules, and it uses datasets (including recent and synthetic ones) chosen to reduce memorization risk. Empirically this appears effective on their benchmarks, but there is no formal guarantee—if your dataset contains an explicit label-name feature, you should sanitize or validate it before running FeatLLM.
