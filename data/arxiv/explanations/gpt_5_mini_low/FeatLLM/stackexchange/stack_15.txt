\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How fair is the comparison to TabLLM when using T0 instead of GPT-3.5?
Question:
In experiments, FeatLLM uses GPT-3.5 for rule generation, while TabLLM is restricted to public checkpoints (T0). Since base model capability can significantly affect downstream performance, how do you ensure a fair comparison? Did you attempt a variant where FeatLLM uses the same open-source LLM as TabLLM, or a variant where TabLLM uses the strongest permissible model? Can you quantify the expected performance delta attributable to backbone differences rather than method design?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper acknowledges the mismatch and follows the original TabLLM experimental constraint (TabLLM is only evaluated with publicly released checkpoints such as T0), while FeatLLM uses GPT-3.5 for rule generation. The authors state (main text and Implementation details) that GPT-3.5 was used for FeatLLM and for the in‑context baseline, whereas TabLLM was run with T0 “following the original setting.” They also note (several times, and in the Appendix) that FeatLLM is backbone‑agnostic and that additional experiments with different LLM backbones are reported in the Appendix. However, the paper does not present a fully controlled head‑to‑head where (a) FeatLLM and TabLLM are forced to use the same LLM, or (b) TabLLM is given access to GPT‑3.5 (which TabLLM’s original constraints disallow). Thus the published comparison is not perfectly controlled for backbone capability; the authors mitigate this by designing method‑level ablations and feature‑quality analyses that show components of FeatLLM matter beyond raw model capability.

Why the choice was made (intuitive): TabLLM’s original design and reproducibility constraint is that it only uses publicly released checkpoints (e.g., T0). The paper preserves that setup to compare against the published TabLLM recipe. Meanwhile, FeatLLM was evaluated under the realistic condition of API access to a stronger closed‑weight model (GPT‑3.5), since FeatLLM’s operational mode requires only API‑level access and is intended to be used with modern API LLMs. So the experimental design reflects two different practical usage paradigms rather than a pure “algorithmic” fairness check.

What this implies for fairness and interpretation: any performance gap between FeatLLM and TabLLM can be decomposed into at least two contributors: (1) methodological innovations (rule extraction, parsing, linear tuning, ensembling/bagging), and (2) backbone model capability (GPT‑3.5 vs T0). The paper does report a substantial average improvement (roughly $10\%$ on average across datasets) of FeatLLM over alternatives including TabLLM, but it does not claim that all of that improvement is due solely to the method and explicitly recognizes that backbone choice matters.

How to quantify the backbone contribution (recommended controlled experiments)
1. Replace FeatLLM’s GPT‑3.5 with the same public checkpoint used by TabLLM (e.g., T0) and rerun FeatLLM end‑to‑end. This yields a direct measure of method-only gains (FeatLLM with T0 versus TabLLM with T0).
2. If licensing allows, run TabLLM with the same stronger backbone (GPT‑3.5) or with another stronger open model (e.g., Llama‑based or Vicuna) to see how much TabLLM’s performance improves solely from a better backbone.
3. Measure intermediate quantities that reflect LLM quality rather than final classifier AUC: (a) rule quality metrics used in the paper (e.g., average AUROC of generated binary features vs target, fraction of features with AUROC $> 0.5$ before/after tuning), and (b) syntactic/parse failure rates when converting rules to executable features. These metrics isolate how much a backbone helps at rule generation and parsing, independent of the downstream classifier.
4. Run a paired‑ablations style analysis: for each dataset and random seed, collect $(\text{FeatLLM\_GPT3.5}, \text{FeatLLM\_T0}, \text{TabLLM\_T0})$. The performance differences yield estimates of the backbone effect ($\text{FeatLLM\_GPT3.5} - \text{FeatLLM\_T0}$) and the method effect ($\text{FeatLLM\_T0} - \text{TabLLM\_T0}$). Repeating across datasets gives a distribution of deltas.

Practical expectations (qualitative, grounded in the paper): the authors claim FeatLLM produces a high fraction of informative rules (Table on feature quality) and show ablations where removing reasoning guidance or tuning degrades performance substantially. That indicates a nontrivial portion of the gain comes from the method pipeline. But because GPT‑3.5 is stronger at reasoning and code generation than many public checkpoints, you should expect a non‑negligible positive delta from using GPT‑3.5 for rule generation: stronger LLMs typically produce clearer, less noisy rules, fewer parse errors, and better prior knowledge injection, which directly improves the downstream features and thus final AUC. The paper does not provide a single numeric “backbone-only” delta; the reported aggregate improvement ($\sim 10\%$ average) is the net effect of method + backbone. The authors do mention additional backbone experiments in the Appendix, so those results (if available in the Appendix) are the closest existing evidence in the paper for how performance varies with backbone.

Conclusion and best practice: the comparison in the paper follows reproducibility and original baseline constraints rather than enforcing identical backbones. To fully disentangle method vs backbone, run the controlled experiments proposed above (FeatLLM with TabLLM’s checkpoint and TabLLM with the stronger LLM where allowed) and inspect intermediate rule‑quality metrics. That will quantify the backbone contribution and give a fairer attribution of the $10\%$ average improvement reported.
