\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How do you measure and filter "useful" rules before and after tuning?
Question:
The paper reports the fraction of rules with AUROC > 0.5 against the target ("Before tuning") and then filters rules with learned weights below 0.1 ("After tuning"). Why AUROC > 0.5 as the usefulness threshold and 0.1 for weights—are these data-dependent? Also, AUROC on very low-shot data can be high-variance; did you use any cross-validated or bootstrapped estimate to assess rule usefulness robustly before final filtering?
Answer:
Short answer: $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ and $\text{weight} > 0.1$ are heuristics rather than theoretically fixed cutoffs. In the paper the $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ cutoff is used as the intuitive “better than random” test for a binary (rule vs. target) relationship, and the $0.1$ weight threshold was chosen empirically after inspecting the histogram of learned weights. The authors did not report a dedicated cross‑validated or bootstrapped per‑rule $\mathrm{AUROC}$ procedure in the paper; instead they reduce variance and improve robustness by ensembling/bagging, repeating experiments with different train/test splits, and using $k$-fold CV to select epochs for training the linear model.

More detail and interpretation grounded in the paper
- Why $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$? $\mathrm{AUROC}$ is a natural way to measure how well a binary feature (a rule firing or not) ranks positive vs. negative cases. A value of $0.5$ is the value of a random classifier, so counting rules with $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ is a simple “useful vs. not-useful” heuristic: rules that do no better than random are unlikely to help. This choice is explicitly what the paper reports (“ratio of useful features with an $\mathrm{AUROC}$ higher than $0.5$”). It is a coarse, interpretable threshold rather than a statistical test; it is therefore a pragmatic, not a hard, theoretically optimal choice.

- Why weight threshold $0.1$? After the LLM generates binary rule features the paper trains a linear model with non‑negative weights (Eq. (1)). The authors state that the threshold of $0.1$ was set “taking into consideration the overall histogram of weights.” That is, $0.1$ is an empirical cutoff derived by inspecting the distribution of learned weights across experiments and choosing a value that separates near‑zero (uninformative) weights from ones that contribute meaningfully. So this cutoff is data/experiment dependent in practice — it’s a practical rule of thumb motivated by the observed weight distribution in their runs.

- Are these choices data‑dependent? Yes. $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ is a generic baseline (and thus minimally data‑dependent), but how useful that threshold is will depend on class balance, noise, and the low‑shot regime. The $0.1$ weight cutoff is explicitly empirical and depends on the learned weight histogram for the datasets and shot levels in the paper. The paper does not claim these thresholds are universal; they were chosen for interpretability and shown to work across the evaluated datasets.

- What about $\mathrm{AUROC}$ variance in very low‑shot settings? The paper acknowledges low‑shot instability and addresses robustness in several ways: rules are generated multiple times with bagging and LLM temperature randomness, an ensemble averages across $T=20$ trials (they use $T=20$), experiments are repeated three times with different train/test splits and averages/stds are reported, and $k$-fold cross‑validation is used to pick training epochs for the linear model. However, the paper does not report using per‑rule cross‑validated $\mathrm{AUROC}$ estimates, bootstrap confidence intervals, or permutation tests to quantify $\mathrm{AUROC}$ uncertainty for individual rules. In short, the authors rely on ensemble/stability and aggregate reporting rather than explicit per‑rule bootstrapped uncertainty estimates.

Practical implications and recommendations (if you want more robust filtering)
If you are concerned about noisy $\mathrm{AUROC}$ estimates in your low‑shot setting, consider these simple extensions (all consistent with the paper’s pipeline):
- Estimate per‑rule $\mathrm{AUROC}$ with cross‑validation or bootstrap to get confidence intervals and only mark a rule “useful” if its lower CI bound exceeds $0.5$ (or another calibrated threshold).
- Use permutation testing (shuffle labels) to get an empirical null distribution of rule $\mathrm{AUROC}$ and compute $p$-values or FDR corrections to control false discoveries among many rules.
- Replace a hard weight cutoff with a stability criterion: keep rules that are repeatedly assigned non‑negligible weight across ensemble trials (frequency threshold), which leverages the paper’s bagging/ensembling mechanism.
- Regularize the linear model (L1 or group sparsity) so that irrelevant rules are shrunk toward zero more aggressively; choose regularization strength by CV even in low‑shot (nested CV or Bayesian priors can help).
- Use rank‑based filtering (e.g., top-$k$ rules by median weight or median $\mathrm{AUROC}$ across bootstrap samples) rather than absolute thresholds if weight scales vary across datasets.

Summary
The $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ and $\text{weight} > 0.1$ cutoffs in the paper are practical, interpretable heuristics: $\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$ tests “better than random,” while $0.1$ is an empirical weight threshold set after inspecting the weight histogram. The paper mitigates low‑shot variance through ensembling, bagging, repeated trials, and $k$-fold CV for training, but it does not report bootstrapped or cross‑validated per‑rule $\mathrm{AUROC}$ estimates — adding those would be a sensible way to make the “useful rule” filtering more statistically robust.
