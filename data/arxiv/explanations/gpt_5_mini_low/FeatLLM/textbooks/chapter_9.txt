\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 9: Experimental Setup

# Datasets
The evaluation of FeatLLM covers a diverse collection of thirteen tabular datasets chosen to represent a range of domains, difficulty levels, feature dimensionalities, and likelihoods of being seen during LLM pretraining. The datasets fall into four groups.

First, a set of commonly used UCI and benchmark tabular datasets (referred to below as “UCI classics”) are included: Adult (predict whether an individual earns over \$50,000), Bank (predict whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit), Blood (predict whether donors will return), Car (predict car quality), Communities (predict crime rate in a region), and Credit-g (predict whether an individual is a good or bad credit risk). These datasets vary in the number of features and complexity; some such as Communities have over 100 attributes and thus stress prompt-size limitations.

Second, a group of health-focused datasets is used to evaluate performance in sensitive, real-world prediction problems: Diabetes (predict presence of diabetes), Heart (predict occurrence of coronary artery disease), and Myocardial (predict chronic heart failure). These datasets were selected both for domain relevance and because clinical prior knowledge encoded in LLMs can be especially useful for few-shot learning.

Third, two more recent UCI datasets—Cultivars (predict grain-yield level of a soybean cultivar) and NHANES (predict age group from a person’s record)—are included. These datasets were donated to the UCI repository after September 2023 and are explicitly chosen to reduce the likelihood that their data or attributes were included during LLM pretraining.

Finally, two synthetic datasets were constructed to further guard against memorization by the LLM backbone: Sequence-type, which classifies a sequence as arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz; and Solution-mix, which predicts whether the concentration percentage of a mixture from four solutions exceeds 0.5. Because these synthetic datasets are generated algorithmically and do not correspond to natural texts, they are unlikely to have been part of the LLM’s pretraining data.

Across these thirteen datasets the paper evaluates FeatLLM in low-shot regimes (e.g., $k$-shot settings with $k \ll N$). Each dataset provides natural language names and definitions for attributes; these feature descriptions are leveraged by FeatLLM when constructing prompts and extracting rules. The experimental write-up reports per-dataset statistics (size, number of attributes, task type) in the main paper (Table references) and uses this variety to stress-test FeatLLM’s robustness to different feature counts and domains.

# Rationale for including recent and synthetic datasets
A central design choice in the evaluation is to include datasets that the LLM backbone is unlikely to have seen in pretraining. The two recent UCI datasets (Cultivars and NHANES) were added after the LLM’s cutoff date for public data availability, and the two synthetic datasets (Sequence-type and Solution-mix) are algorithmically constructed. This selection reduces the chance that high performance is due to memorization of table contents or dataset-specific heuristics learned during pretraining. By evaluating FeatLLM on these datasets alongside standard benchmarks and health datasets, the experiments aim to demonstrate that the method extracts task-relevant rules from few-shot examples and LLM prior knowledge, not merely recalling examples seen during LLM training.

# Baselines
The experiments compare FeatLLM to nine baselines spanning conventional machine learning methods, semi-supervised / transfer methods that assume access to unlabeled data or multi-task pretraining, and recent LLM-based approaches for tabular learning. These baselines are:

- Logistic regression (LogReg): a linear classifier commonly used as a simple parametric baseline.
- XGBoost: a gradient-boosted tree ensemble that is a strong off-the-shelf tabular learner.
- RandomForest: a bagged tree ensemble that serves as a robust nonparametric baseline.
- SCARF: a semi-supervised method that leverages unlabeled data for augmentation and training.
- STUNT: a few-shot method that assumes access to a large unlabeled dataset for augmentation or pretraining.
- TabPFN: a pretrained Bayesian neural network for tabular tasks that uses synthetic training distributions to provide few-shot inference without task-specific training.
- In-context learning: direct in-context prompting of the LLM by serializing few-shot examples into the prompt and obtaining predictions without parameter tuning.
- TABLET: a method that augments LLM in-context prompts with additional hints derived from an external classifier (rule-sets and prototypes) to improve inference quality.
- TabLLM: an LLM-based approach that uses parameter-efficient tuning (IA3 in the referenced study) to adapt a language model to tabular tasks.

These baselines provide a spectrum of comparisons: classical supervised learners (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest), methods that exploit additional unlabeled data or synthetic pretraining (SCARF, STUNT, TabPFN), and LLM-based strategies that either use in-context inference directly or require tuning (In-context, TABLET, TabLLM). It is worth noting that some baselines, such as SCARF and STUNT, assume access to large unlabeled datasets and therefore are not directly comparable in settings where such data are unavailable; the paper excludes those baselines from comparisons when the experimental condition forbids unlabeled data.

# LLM backbone and API settings
FeatLLM is designed to be LLM-agnostic, but the experiments reported in the paper use GPT-3.5 (specifically the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 API) as the backbone LLM for rule generation and for parsing LLM outputs into executable code. The chosen API settings in the reported experiments are a temperature of 0.5 and top-p (nucleus sampling) set to the default value of 1. These settings introduce controlled randomness: temperature provides stochasticity to encourage diverse rule generation across ensemble trials, while top-p left at 1 means nucleus sampling is not further restricted. The paper notes that FeatLLM requires only API-level inference access (no fine-tuning), which makes it applicable to modern LLMs that do not expose training checkpoints or tuning APIs.

Although GPT-3.5 is used in the primary experiments, FeatLLM’s architecture (extract rules once, convert to binary features, then use a small downstream model at inference time) is intentionally method-agnostic so alternative LLM backbones can be substituted. Additional experiments with different LLM backbones are discussed in the Appendix of the paper.

# Hyperparameters: rules per class, ensemble size, and training details
Two hyperparameters are highlighted as most influential to FeatLLM’s performance: the number of rules extracted per class per LLM query and the number of ensemble trials (bagged models) combined at prediction time.

- Number of rules per class: In the reported experiments FeatLLM extracts $R=10$ rules for each class in a single LLM query. The paper fixes this number to avoid both underfitting (too few rules) and overly large prompts (too many rules), striking a balance appropriate for low-shot settings. Hyper-parameter sensitivity experiments reported in the Appendix show performance is relatively stable around this choice, and $R=10$ was selected as a practical trade-off between prompt length and predictive information.

- Ensemble size (number of trials): FeatLLM repeats the rule-extraction, parsing, binary-feature construction, and downstream weight training process $T$ times and ensembles the resulting models by averaging predicted probabilities. In the main experiments $T=20$ trials are used. Increasing $T$ improves performance up to a point; the paper reports diminishing returns beyond approximately 20 trials. The ensemble uses two sources of randomness to increase diversity across trials: sampling feature/instance subsets (bagging) and setting a non-zero LLM temperature so the LLM produces diverse rule sets.

Training details for the downstream model are deliberately lightweight. After binary features are produced, FeatLLM fits a simple linear model (one per class) without bias to convert the rule indicators into logits. Formally, for class $k$ the binary-rule feature vector for sample $i$ is $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, and the model maintains a trainable weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$. The logit for class $k$ is computed as

logit_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,

and probabilities are obtained by applying a Softmax over the class logits:

\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}([\,\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i\,]).

Projecting $\mathbf{w}_k$ into the non-negative orthant via $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ ensures features cannot reduce a class likelihood. The model optimizes the cross-entropy loss on the few-shot training samples. Implementation details reported in the paper specify training the linear model with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs, using $k$-fold cross-validation to select the optimal epoch (see next section). The number of API calls required for FeatLLM is small: typical experimental settings use on the order of 30 LLM queries per full training run, making the approach practical under API access limits.

# Cross-validation and early stopping for hyperparameter and epoch selection
To avoid overfitting in the few-shot regime and to choose training hyperparameters (notably the number of training epochs for the downstream linear model), FeatLLM uses $k$-fold cross-validation on the available few-shot training set. The value of $k$ is chosen so that each fold maintains at least one example of every class; in practice $k$ is set to either 2 or 4 depending on dataset size and the shot count. During training of the linear model, FeatLLM records performance on cross-validation folds to determine the appropriate stopping epoch (early stopping). This cross-validation procedure also guides hyperparameter selection such as the exact number of epochs and any dataset-specific choices when fitting the downstream model.

Beyond model-fitting, cross-validation is used to evaluate variance due to training/test splits: the main reported metrics (e.g., AUC) are averaged over three experimental runs with different random splits of training and testing data, and standard deviations are reported to capture variability.

# Additional implementation notes
Several practical choices are reported in the experiments. Prompt engineering for rule extraction comprises three structured parts—basic information (task and feature descriptions plus serialized demonstrations), a two-step reasoning instruction (prior-knowledge inference followed by data-conditioned rule generation), and strict response formatting instructions to make subsequent parsing robust. Parsing of the natural-language rules into executable code is itself performed via the LLM: the inferred rules and a function specification are provided to the LLM, which outputs Python code that is then executed (via Python’s exec()) to create the binary features. The paper emphasizes that this two-stage use of the LLM—first to propose rules, then to produce parsing code—reduces brittle hand-written parsers and exploits the LLM’s strength at interpreting noisy natural language.

Temperature for rule generation is set to 0.5 in the main experiments to balance determinism and diversity across ensemble trials. The bagging strategy subsamples features or instances in each trial to address prompt-size limitations (datasets with many features) and to increase ensemble diversity. The final prediction is the average of predicted class probabilities across the $T$ trained trials.

Taken together, these experimental choices—dataset selection (including recent and synthetic datasets), a broad and representative set of baselines, a clear LLM backbone and API configuration, controlled hyperparameter settings ($R=10$, $T=20$ in main results), and cross-validation for epoch selection—constitute the evaluation protocol used to validate FeatLLM’s ability to extract informative, generalizable rule-based features for few-shot tabular learning.
