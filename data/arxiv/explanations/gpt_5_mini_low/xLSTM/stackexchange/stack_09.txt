\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How critical is the zero-mean assumption for keys/values in the covariance update rule?
Question:
The paper assumes LayerNorm before key/value projections so that keys/values have zero mean, then cites optimality properties of the covariance rule. What breaks if the mean-shift is nonzero (e.g., due to learned biases or distribution drift)? Does a nonzero mean introduce a spurious rank-1 component that degrades retrieval via increased cross-talk? Would explicit mean-centering per batch or per sequence be preferable to relying on LayerNorm?
Answer:
Short answer: the zero-mean assumption is important for the covariance update to behave like the ideal outer-product memory described in the paper. If keys or values carry a nonzero mean (from biases, learned offsets, or distribution drift) you effectively inject a persistent rank-1 term into the matrix memory which (a) biases retrieval and (b) reduces signal-to-noise, so retrieval quality and the separability optimality the paper cites degrade. Practically you should keep LayerNorm (or another explicit mean-centering) before the key/value projections; if that is not possible, remove or correct the mean via simple explicit fixes (mean-subtraction, projecting out dominant components, or removing biases).

Why this happens (intuitive + math): the mLSTM covariance update is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
and retrieval uses $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}$. If $\mathbf{k}_t=\bar{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_t$ with a persistent mean $\bar{\mathbf{k}}$ and zero-mean fluctuation $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_t$, then every update contributes a component $i_t\mathbf{v}_t\bar{\mathbf{k}}^\top$. Over time these add up into a large, slowly changing rank-1 matrix
$$
\sum_t i_t\mathbf{v}_t\bar{\mathbf{k}}^\top = \left(\sum_t i_t\mathbf{v}_t\right)\bar{\mathbf{k}}^\top,
$$
which acts like a strong, non-signal-aligned memory entry. When you later compute $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}$ this term produces a spurious contribution proportional to $(\sum_t i_t\mathbf{v}_t)(\bar{\mathbf{k}}^\top\mathbf{q})$. Two concrete consequences follow.

1) Bias and cross-talk. If $\bar{\mathbf{k}}^\top\mathbf{q}$ is nonzero for many queries $\mathbf{q}$, the rank-1 part leaks information into retrievals that should be orthogonal, causing cross-talk and systematic bias. In other words, the memory no longer cleanly separates stored pairs, because a global key-direction pollutes everything.

2) Reduced signal-to-noise and suboptimality. The optimality statement the paper cites (covariance updates maximize separability / SNR under zero-mean assumptions) relies on the stored outer-products being centered around zero so different pairs act like independent directions. A dominant mean term increases the noise floor or can swamp weak genuine signals, reducing the effective capacity and degrading retrieval accuracy.

How it interacts with the paper’s normalizer and denominator. The mLSTM also keeps a normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t=\sum f\cdots i_t \mathbf{k}_t$ and uses $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}|,1\}$ in the denominator. A nonzero mean shifts $\mathbf{n}_t$ too, so the stabilizing denominator can become large or structured in a way that changes scaling across queries and effectively masks or rescales correct retrievals unpredictably. The normalization helps some pathological cases but does not remove structured rank-1 contamination.

Practical remedies (grounded in the paper’s choices)

- Keep the LayerNorm before the key/value projections, as the paper does. Pre-projection LayerNorm enforces approximate zero-mean keys/values and is the simplest, low-cost solution consistent with the model design in the paper.

- Remove or tie biases on key/value projections. The paper’s equations include biases $\mathbf{b}_k,\mathbf{b}_v$; if you keep them, they create obvious mean shifts. Either set these biases to zero or ensure they are canceled by preceding normalization.

- Explicit mean-centering. Subtract a mean estimate from $\mathbf{k}_t$ and/or $\mathbf{v}_t$ per sequence or per batch:
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_t=\mathbf{k}_t-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{k}_s
$$
Per-sequence centering most closely matches the assumption the covariance rule relies on. Per-batch centering is cheaper and often effective, but be aware it can leak batch statistics and interact with distribution shifts.

- Running or exponential moving mean / drift correction. If distribution drift is a concern across long training runs or nonstationary data, maintain a running mean and subtract it, or periodically re-center keys/values.

- Project out dominant principal components. If you observe a dominant direction that corrupts retrieval, remove the top singular vector from keys (and/or values). This is a slightly heavier operation but targets exactly the rank-1 contamination problem.

- Learn a correction or gating. You can let a gate downweight a global mean component (learn a scalar or vector gate on $\bar{\mathbf{k}}$ direction), but this adds complexity and must be carefully regularized.

Recommendation in one sentence: follow the paper and keep LayerNorm (or explicit mean-subtraction per sequence) before key/value projections; if you see retrieval degradation or strong dominant singular vectors during training, remove the mean or project out the top component rather than relying on implicit cancellation.

Monitoring tip: track the norm and top singular value of accumulated $\mathbf{C}_t$ (or of the sequence of $\mathbf{k}_t$ vectors). If a single direction captures a large fraction of energy, you have mean-induced rank-1 contamination and should apply one of the remedies above.

This explanation stays within the paper’s design: the covariance update is optimal under zero-mean keys/values (as they state), and nonzero mean creates exactly the rank-1 leakage described; LayerNorm or explicit centering is the intended and pragmatic way to preserve the covariance rule’s desirable properties.
