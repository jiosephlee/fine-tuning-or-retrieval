\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 9: Positioning xLSTM in the Landscape

# Linear attention variants: Synthesizer, Linformer, Linear Transformer, Performer, SGConv, Hyena
Linear-attention and related methods arise from a shared motivation: the quadratic dependence of standard Transformer attention on context length makes very long-context modeling expensive. The paper situates xLSTM in a landscape where many approaches have attempted to replace or approximate full softmax attention with alternatives that scale linearly in sequence length. These methods differ in what they trade off—exactness of token–token interactions, representational richness, or computational form—but they share the objective of making long-context computation tractable.

Synthesizer removes the explicit token–token interaction by learning synthetic attention weights; it thus sidesteps quadratic pairwise computation at the cost of foregoing direct content-based attention. Linformer replaces attention with a low-rank factorization, approximating the attention matrix to reduce complexity. Linear Transformer and similar linearization approaches directly rewrite the attention computation so that the softmax-form attention can be evaluated with kernels that allow linear-time accumulation across the sequence. Performer uses positive random-feature approximations to the softmax kernel, again enabling linear-time approximation of attention. SGConv and Hyena take a different route: they replace attention by convolutional operators or structured long convolutional filters, providing fast alternatives that aim to capture long-range dependencies through carefully designed filtering.

Within the paper’s framing, these linear attention variants are important points of comparison because xLSTM, like these methods, promises linear computation in the context length. However, the paper emphasizes that xLSTM approaches the linearity challenge from a recurrent-memory perspective (extended LSTM memories and gating) rather than by approximating the Transformer attention mechanism. Thus, xLSTM joins the family of models that target linear complexity but does so by reviving and extending recurrent-memory mechanisms rather than by modifying attention kernels.

# State Space Models: S4, DSS, GSS, S5, BiGS, H3, Mamba
State Space Models (SSMs) are another prominent family targeting long-range sequence modeling with linear complexity. The paper lists several SSM variants that have recently become popular: Structured State Space (S4), Diagonal State Space (DSS), Gated State Space (GSS), S5, Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS), H3, and Mamba. These models employ linear dynamical systems or carefully parameterized state-transition mechanisms to accumulate information from long contexts while keeping computation linear in sequence length.

The paper positions xLSTM relative to SSMs in two ways. First, both SSMs and xLSTM aim for linear computational scaling and are designed to handle long contexts efficiently. Second, the authors highlight a structural distinction: many SSMs do not implement memory mixing in the same way that recurrent models with hidden–hidden connections do. The paper argues that memory mixing—internal recurrent connections that allow the stored state to interact and be remixed over time—enables state-tracking behaviors that SSMs (and many attention-free linear models) struggle with. Consequently, while SSMs are strong competitors on long-context benchmarks and are reflected among the baselines in the experiments, xLSTM claims an advantage through its extended gating and memory structures that preserve or reintroduce memory-mixing capacity where beneficial.

# RNN revivals: LRUs, HGRN/HGRN2, RWKV
There has been renewed interest in recurrent architectures for large-scale language modeling. The paper lists several RNN-based or RNN-inspired architectures that have shown competitive performance: Deep Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs), Hierarchically Gated Linear RNNs (HGRN and HGRN2), and RWKV. These approaches attempt to reconcile recurrence with modern training and scaling techniques, sometimes by linearizing updates or adding gating structures inspired by LSTMs.

xLSTM is presented as part of this revivalary thread but with distinct engineering and algorithmic choices. Like LRUs, HGRN variants, and RWKV, xLSTM emphasizes linear complexity in context length and modern residual stacking. Where xLSTM differs is in (i) the introduction of exponential gating with normalization and stabilization, and (ii) the modified memory structures—scalar sLSTM and matrix mLSTM—that explicitly target the two limitations the authors ascribe to classic LSTMs: inability to revise storage decisions and limited scalar storage capacity. In experiments, xLSTM is compared directly to RWKV and HGRN-family models; the paper reports favorable performance for xLSTM in perplexity, extrapolation, and domain benchmarks, positioning it as a competitive member of the modern RNN family.

# Gating as a unifying theme across modern sequence models
One of the conceptual pivots of the paper is the observation that gating—originally central to LSTM design—has reappeared across a broad range of modern sequence models. The paper enumerates several contemporary methods that use gating mechanisms: HGRN, HGRN2, Gated Linear Attention (GLA), Gated State Space (GSS) models, Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS), MEGA, RWKV, and Mamba, among others. This trend suggests that selective control of information flow (i.e., gating) remains a powerful inductive bias for long-range sequence learning.

xLSTM leverages this insight directly. It extends gating in two ways. First, with exponential gating for input and optionally forget gates, xLSTM makes storing and erasing information multiplicative in an exponential sense, which the authors argue enables more decisive and revisable storage behavior. Second, xLSTM pairs gating with explicit normalizer states and stabilization mechanisms so that the numerical consequences of exponential gating are handled safely. The paper’s ablation studies attribute a substantial portion of xLSTM’s performance gains to these gating choices, reinforcing the view that gating is a unifying and practically important mechanism across modern sequence architectures.

# Covariance update ancestry: fast weights, Retention, RWKV-5/6
The paper traces the matrix-memory idea in mLSTM back to a long lineage of covariance-style updates and fast-weight mechanisms. The covariance update rule—adding an outer product of value and key vectors to a memory matrix—has roots in Bidirectional Associative Memories and early fast-weight ideas. The paper explicitly connects mLSTM’s matrix memory and covariance update to Fast Weight Programmers and later methods that apply a decay on past weights and a learning-rate-like scaling of new outer products.

More recent contemporary models also rely on similar updates or functional analogues. Retention and RWKV variants (including RWKV-5 and RWKV-6 mentioned in the paper) use mechanisms that can be interpreted as accumulating key–value statistics over time; Linear Transformer variants and HGRN2 also relate to these fast-weight/covariance ideas. In xLSTM, the authors adopt a covariance update for the matrix memory and align the forget gate with decay and the input gate with a learning-rate-like multiplier. They also include a normalizer state (a weighted sum of keys) so that retrieval is normalized and stable, with safeguards such as taking absolute values and lower-bounding the denominator, following precedents in retention-style methods.

# Most related to xLSTM: Retention, RWKV, HGRN2
The paper identifies Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 as the models most closely related to xLSTM. Each shares some combination of gating, linear-time accumulation, or key–value style memory with xLSTM. However, the paper emphasizes contrasts that make xLSTM distinctive.

Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 have in common a focus on linear complexity and on mechanisms that accumulate or gate information over long contexts. Yet, according to the paper, they differ from xLSTM in how they implement memory mixing and in the precise gating and memory update rules. In particular, the authors claim that the sLSTM variant of xLSTM permits memory mixing through recurrent hidden–hidden connections, enabling solutions to state-tracking problems that some of these other models cannot handle. The mLSTM variant shares with Retention and RWKV-like methods the covariance-style accumulation, but xLSTM integrates this update into an LSTM-style gated framework, including exponential input gating and a normalizer state, plus stabilization techniques to avoid numerical issues.

Empirically, the paper reports that xLSTM outperforms these closely related methods on several benchmarks in language modeling, sequence extrapolation, and associative-recall-style tasks, suggesting that the combination of exponential gating, matrix memory, and residual stacking yields practical advantages over the most related recent architectures.

# Expressivity and state tracking: why memory mixing matters
A core theoretical and empirical point in the paper is that memory mixing—the ability for internal stored states to be remixed via recurrent hidden–hidden interactions—is crucial for certain classes of sequence problems, particularly those requiring state tracking. The paper cites results and experiments showing that models lacking memory mixing (many Transformers and SSMs) cannot solve particular formal languages or state-tracking tasks that RNNs with recurrent mixing can handle.

Memory mixing allows previously stored information to be transformed and combined with newly arriving inputs and other stored components across time steps. This capacity is important for algorithms that require updating summaries of past events in a way that depends on both past memory contents and current inputs (for example, tracking counters, nested parentheses, or carrying the best-so-far match in nearest-neighbor-like tasks). The paper demonstrates experimentally that xLSTM variants with memory mixing (the sLSTM blocks) can solve formal language tasks and state-tracking problems that models without such recurrent mixing fail on. In contrast, mLSTM, by design, drops hidden–hidden mixing to achieve parallelizability; it compensates for lack of recurrent mixing with a higher-capacity matrix memory and parallel-friendly covariance updates.

Thus, the paper positions expressivity along a trade-off: memory mixing tends to increase expressive power for state-tracking tasks, while removing mixing enables parallelization and different scaling properties. xLSTM explores this trade-off by offering both sLSTM (with mixing and exponential gates) and mLSTM (matrix memory, parallelizable), and by permitting hybrid architectures (e.g., xLSTM[$a$:$b$] mixing ratios) so that practitioners can tune between expressivity and parallel speed.

# Concluding positioning remarks
The paper’s central claim is that by extending LSTM with exponential gating, stabilizers, a normalizer state, and a matrix memory update rule, xLSTM brings together the strengths of several recent trends: the linear-context efficiency of SSMs and linear attention variants; the gating-driven selective memory of modern gated models; and the higher-capacity associative storage of fast-weight/covariance updates. At the same time, xLSTM preserves, in its sLSTM variant, the classical LSTM advantage of memory mixing for state-tracking problems. Empirically, the authors report that xLSTM competes favorably with Transformers, SSMs, and recent RNN revivals on language modeling, extrapolation, and associative recall, thereby arguing for xLSTM’s place as a reconciliatory architecture that revives LSTM ideas while adopting the modern practices that enabled current Large Language Models.
